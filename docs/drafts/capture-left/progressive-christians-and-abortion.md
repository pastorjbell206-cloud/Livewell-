There is a moral instinct that runs through the best of progressive Christianity, and it deserves to be named with gratitude before anything else is said. It is the conviction that the powerful never get to decide which human beings count. It hears Proverbs as a command and not a sentiment: "Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute" (Proverbs 31:8, ESV). It has spent its energy on the prisoner nobody visits, the migrant nobody wants, the disabled child nobody planned for, the poor woman whose name never reaches the newspaper. It remembers that the American church once read its Bible in defense of slavery, and it has resolved never again to let the comfortable majority draw the circle of human worth tightly around itself. That instinct is not a secular import. It is one of the church's oldest gifts to the world, and the left has often carried it when the right set it down.

On one question the instinct goes quiet. The progressive Christian who will not let anyone be called disposable, who distrusts every argument that grades human beings by their usefulness, who hears in "unwanted" the oldest excuse of the powerful, tends to fall silent at the edge of the womb. Not all do; some progressive Christians have held a consistent ethic of life for decades, often lonely in both parties. Where the silence falls, it is rarely cruel. It is usually tender, protective of women, wary of a movement that has so often looked like a party with a cross on its lapel. But it is a silence, and it sits in exactly the place where the left's own moral grammar ought to be loudest.

That is the claim of this essay, and it is a claim about consistency more than about politics. The historic Christian conviction that the child in the womb is a human life owed protection is not the property of the right. In its origins it was the ethic of a powerless minority standing against the prerogative of powerful men, and in its fullest modern statement it indicts the right as sharply as the left. The progressive Christian is not being asked to adopt someone else's cause. She is being asked to finish her own sentence.

I should say plainly where this essay stands before it says anything else. It is written by a pastor, from inside a conservative church world that has been far more comfortable naming the left's blind spot than its own, and it does not exempt that world or its writer. The American evangelical world spent decades defending the unborn with a vigor it never brought to the mother in the next pew, the child after birth, or the man on death row, and that failure is part of why so many thoughtful Christians stopped listening. And if you are reading this as a woman who has had an abortion, or as a man who pressed for one, or who paid for one and walked away, nothing here is written to make you smaller. There is a word for you near the end, and it is not a trap. If tonight is heavier than you can carry, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, and tell a pastor or a counselor you trust.

## The case for choice, stated by those who make it

The pro-choice Christian position deserves its strongest form, and its strongest form is not the caricature of a woman who thinks a pregnancy is nothing. It is made by people who have thought harder than most about what pregnancy costs and who has always been made to pay.

The first strand is bodily autonomy. Judith Jarvis Thomson's "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) remains its most rigorous statement because it grants the opponent's premise. Suppose, she argued, the fetus is a person from conception. It still doesn't follow that anyone is obligated to sustain another person with her own body, month after month, at real risk to her health and life. Her famous illustration asks you to imagine waking up with your kidneys connected to a famous violinist who will die unless you stay attached for nine months, and she asked whether you would be obliged to stay. Her defenders add that the law does not compel even a parent to give a kidney to a dying child, however much we might admire the parent who did. Why, then, should the law compel the far larger bodily gift of pregnancy? The argument is serious, and its force is that it refuses to hide behind uncertainty about the fetus.

The second strand is theological and comes from inside the church. Beverly Wildung Harrison, who taught Christian ethics at Union Theological Seminary, argued in *Our Right to Choose: Toward a New Ethic of Abortion* (1983) that the church's teaching on abortion was formed almost entirely by men who never bore its costs, inside a tradition that did not trust women as moral agents. She contended that a genuinely Christian ethic of procreation must take seriously the lives women actually live, the poverty and violence that shape their choices, and their standing before God as persons capable of grave moral decisions. Behind her argument stood clergy who had already acted on it. In 1967, six years before *Roe*, ministers and rabbis in New York, led by Howard Moody of Judson Memorial Church, formed the Clergy Consultation Service on Abortion, moved by the injuries and deaths that illegal abortion was causing among the women they served. Whatever one concludes, that was not a movement of the careless.

The third strand is the hard cases: pregnancy after rape or incest, a pregnancy that threatens the mother's life, a diagnosis that the child will not survive birth. These are not rhetorical devices. They are Tuesdays in hospitals, and any Christian who talks about abortion without having sat with them has not yet earned the right to a conclusion.

The fourth strand is an indictment of the church itself, and it is the most damning because it is true. Ann Fessler's *The Girls Who Went Away* (2006) gathers the testimony of women who, in the decades before *Roe*, were sent away when they became pregnant outside marriage, often to maternity homes, many of them run by religious charities, and pressed to surrender their children and never speak of it again. The church that now speaks for the unborn was, within living memory, a place where pregnant girls were hidden and shamed. Add the right's long record on the born: the defense of the child in the womb joined, too often, to indifference toward the mother's wages, the child's health care, the prisoner's execution. Even the conservative conviction has a shorter history than its defenders like to admit. In 1971 the Southern Baptist Convention, meeting in St. Louis, resolved to support legislation permitting abortion in cases of rape, incest, severe fetal deformity, and likely damage to the mother's emotional, mental or physical health. Evangelical certainty on this question hardened later in the 1970s, and it hardened in tandem with a political coalition.

Every one of these strands contains something true. The body is not a public utility. Women have been treated by the church as problems to be managed rather than persons to be heard. The hard cases are real. The right has been inconsistent. A Christian who concedes none of this has not understood the argument. Thomson's strand stands apart from the others, because it grants what the child is and asks only what the mother owes, and it deserves an answer on its own terms; the site's fuller treatment, [What Does the Bible Say About Abortion?](/writing/christians-and-abortion), takes it up. The other strands, however much truth they carry, tend to step around the question this essay presses, which is what the child is.

## What the left already believes about who counts

The progressive moral imagination tells a story about history, and it is a good story. Moral progress, in this account, is the widening of the circle. Again and again the powerful drew the boundary of full humanity around themselves and placed others outside it, and again and again the conscience of a people was converted to see the excluded as neighbors. The enslaved, the colonized, women, the disabled, the stranger: each was at some point described as less than fully human, and each exclusion rested on a claim about capacities. They were said to lack reason, or independence, or the ability to speak for themselves. The progressive has learned to distrust that argument in every form.

Look closely at the arguments usually offered for why the unborn child does not yet count as one of us, and the same shape appears. Most people who make them are not trying to exclude anyone; they are trying to protect a woman in a hard place. But the reasons have a familiar form. The child is not yet viable, and so depends entirely on another. The child cannot yet feel, or think, or remember, or speak. The child is not wanted. These are claims about capacities and circumstances, and they are exactly the kind of claims the progressive has taught the rest of us to suspect when they are used to sort human beings into those who count and those who don't.

The sharpest form of this point has come from within the disability-rights movement, and from people who are not opponents of legal abortion. In a 1999 essay for the *Hastings Center Report*, Erik Parens and Adrienne Asch set out what has come to be called the expressivist objection to selective abortion after prenatal testing. Asch, a bioethicist who was blind and who supported abortion rights, argued that when a pregnancy is ended because the child will have a disability, a single trait is allowed to stand for the whole person, and the choice communicates a judgment about the worth of lives like hers. The argument does not need a theology to work. It needs only the progressive's own conviction that no human being should be reduced to the characteristic the majority fears.

Jesus met a version of this question from a lawyer who wanted to know where his obligations ended: "But he, desiring to justify himself, said to Jesus, 'And who is my neighbor?'" (Luke 10:29, ESV). The parable that follows refuses the question as asked. Jesus does not supply a boundary. He tells a story in which the man in the ditch is identified only by his need, and then turns the question around: "Which of these three, do you think, proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers?" (Luke 10:36, ESV). The neighbor is not the one who qualifies. The neighbor is the one within reach. The whole force of the story is against drawing the circle by capacity, status or convenience.

The distinction that decides this question is the one the progressive already uses everywhere else: the difference between what a human being is and what a human being can presently do. Dignity grounded in capacity rises and falls with the capacity, which is why the sleeping, the comatose, the newborn and the severely disabled have always been vulnerable wherever that grounding prevailed. Dignity grounded in what a being is, a living member of the human family bearing, as Genesis puts it, the image of God, does not rise and fall. It belongs to the weakest exactly as fully as to the strongest. The progressive Christian has insisted on the second kind of dignity for the prisoner, the migrant and the child with Down syndrome. The only question is whether it stops at birth, and if it does, what principle stops it.

The criterion of being wanted is the hardest to see clearly, because wanting a child is such a good thing. But it is worth asking where else the progressive Christian would accept it. An unwanted immigrant is not less of a person. An unwanted elderly parent is not less of a person. The whole prophetic tradition is a protest against a world where the worth of the poor depends on whether the powerful want them around. "Give justice to the weak and the fatherless; maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute. Rescue the weak and the needy; deliver them from the hand of the wicked" (Psalm 82:3–4, ESV). The psalm assumes that the weak have a claim on us precisely when no one wants them.

None of this dissolves the hard cases, and an honest essay should say so. It does not tell a woman carrying a child conceived in violence that her suffering is small. What it does is refuse the move that turns the hard case into a general principle, and it asks the progressive Christian to notice that the principle usually invoked, that a human being's standing depends on capacity or on being wanted, is one she has spent her moral life opposing.

## The oldest Christian ethic was the radical one

The historical claim that the conviction belongs to the right can be tested, and it fails the test. The Christian refusal of abortion is far older than any American party, and when it first appeared it stood against the powerful, not with them.

In the Roman world a newborn was not automatically received into the family. Under the old principle of *patria potestas*, the male head of the household held extraordinary authority over his children, and the exposure of unwanted infants, leaving them outside to die or to be taken by strangers, was legal and common. Abortion was practiced too, and sometimes at the demand of a man rather than the choice of a woman. A papyrus from Oxyrhynchus in Egypt, dated to 1 BC, preserves a letter from a man named Hilarion, away working in Alexandria, to his wife Alis at home. It is affectionate, asks after their little boy, promises to send money, and then instructs her that if the child she is carrying is a boy she should keep it, and if it is a girl she should put it out. The tenderness and the instruction sit in the same letter. That was the world into which the church was born.

The church's first manual of instruction, the Didache, dated by most scholars to the late first or early second century, sets two prohibitions side by side in its list of the commandments of the way of life: you shall not murder a child by abortion, and you shall not kill one that has been born. The Greek phrase for the first, *ou phoneuseis teknon en phthora*, uses the ordinary word for a child, *teknon*. The unborn and the newborn are named with the same noun and protected by the same command. Michael Gorman's *Abortion and the Early Church* (1982) surveys the pagan, Jewish and Christian evidence of the period and argues that the Christian refusal was distinctive not because no pagan ever objected, but because the Christian witness was uniform, and because it placed abortion and exposure together as a single act against a single kind of victim.

Listen to what the early apologists thought they were defending. Justin Martyr, writing to the emperor around 155, told him that Christians had been taught that exposing newborns is wicked, and among his reasons was the fate of the exposed: most of them, he said, were gathered up and raised for prostitution. Athenagoras, around 177, told the emperors that Christians regard the child in the womb as a living being under God's care and could not consistently treat the same being as a thing to be destroyed, and in the same chapter of his plea he explained that Christians stayed away from the gladiatorial shows because they judged that watching a man put to death was close to killing him. The *Epistle to Diognetus* described Christians as marrying and having children like everyone else, but refusing to cast out their offspring. O. M. Bakke's *When Children Became People* (2005) traces the larger change this represented: a movement that insisted the smallest and least useful members of a household were persons before God, with claims on the whole community.

Read that history with the progressive's own categories and it looks very different from the story we usually tell. The ancient Christian ethic of life was the protest of people without power against the prerogative of the head of the household to decide which of his children would live. It set limits on men. It rescued, at least in principle, the girl Hilarion told his wife to put out. It was joined, in the same authors, to a refusal of the arena's violence. It was, in the plainest sense of the word, a defense of the voiceless.

And it was joined, from very early, to mercy. The church's discipline was severe at first; an earlier rule had barred women who procured abortions from communion until the hour of death. The Council of Ancyra in 314 revisited that rule and judged it too harsh, prescribing instead a period of penance with a road back to the table. The early church held the conviction and the way home in the same hand. It did not soften the first to make room for the second, and it did not close the second to prove it meant the first.

## Bernardin's garment, cut from both sides

The modern form of this argument has a date and an author, and its author was no partisan of the right. On December 6, 1983, Cardinal Joseph Bernardin of Chicago gave the Gannon Lecture at Fordham University under the title "A Consistent Ethic of Life: An American-Catholic Dialogue." He had chaired the committee that drafted the American bishops' 1983 pastoral letter on nuclear war, *The Challenge of Peace*, and he had come to believe that the church's teaching on abortion, war, capital punishment and the care of the poor were not separate causes that happened to share a sponsor. They were applications of one conviction about the dignity of human life, and each would be credible only if held together with the others. The image attached to his argument was the seamless garment, the tunic of Christ at the foot of the cross that the soldiers would not tear (John 19:23).

What makes Bernardin useful here is that his argument cuts in both directions and was meant to. To the right he said that a defense of the unborn which shrugs at the poor mother, the hungry child, the condemned prisoner and the civilian in the path of a bomb is not a defense of life. It is a defense of one life chosen for its political convenience. To the left he said the same thing from the other side: a concern for the poor, the prisoner and the victim of war that exempts the child in the womb has cut the garment at the one place where cutting costs its own coalition something. Bernardin was careful to say that consistency does not mean equivalence. The issues differ in kind and in gravity, and the direct taking of an innocent life is not morally the same as a debate over welfare policy. What consistency requires is accountability, every question answering to the same principle rather than to the platform of a party. What that looks like for a church on the far side of birth is the subject of [What Does It Mean to Be Pro-Life After Birth?](/writing/the-consistent-pro-life-ethic-womb-to-tomb), and the right should read it before it reads anything here.

The American church largely declined the invitation. Within a few years each side had learned to use half the garment against the other. The right cited abortion to excuse its indifference to everything that happens after birth, and the left cited poverty, war and the death penalty to excuse its silence about what happens before it. Each took one real priority and spent it to buy an exemption from the rest. That is the precise shape of the capture this site has named on the right for years, and it is the same shape on the left. When a conscience is sorted by the party that will reward it, the unborn become a right-wing issue and the poor a left-wing issue, and the Christian who belongs to either side learns to feel the one and forget the other. The instrument has to be the same for both, and so does the grief.

It matters to say what kind of question this is. That the child in the womb is a human life made in the image of God and owed protection has been the conviction of the church across its traditions from the Didache forward, and this essay stands where that historic witness stands. How that conviction should be expressed in law, what policies best protect both mother and child, how the church should weigh its public voice against its local work: those are questions on which faithful Christians disagree, and this essay leaves them open. The progressive Christian is not being asked to join a political movement. She is being asked whether her own conviction about the voiceless has an unexplained exception.

## A word to those who have already chosen

Some who read this are not weighing an argument. They are carrying a memory. You may have ended a pregnancy years ago and never told anyone, or told only the person who drove you home. You may be a man who asked for it, or insisted on it, or paid and left. You may be a parent who arranged it for a frightened daughter. The conviction this essay defends is real, and it would be a lie to tell you it does not touch what you did. It would be a worse lie to tell you it is the last word about you.

The church has a long record of failing you twice: first by giving you no reason to believe you could come to it pregnant, and then by giving you no reason to believe you could come to it afterward. Some of the women Fessler interviewed were hidden by churches. Some women sat through sermons on this subject that left them certain they were the only one in the room, which they almost never were. That is the church's sin, and the church needs to confess it before it asks anyone else to confess anything.

What the gospel says to you is not that nothing was lost. It says that what was lost is known to God, that your grief is permitted, and that the cross is large enough for this. "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9, ESV). The promise does not say he will forgive the lesser sins. It says all unrighteousness. David wrote Psalm 51 after a man was dead by his order, and the psalm does not pretend otherwise; it ends in the confidence that "a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Psalm 51:17, ESV). Paul, who had consented to killing, wrote that "there is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV). The church at Ancyra, seventeen centuries ago, looked at its own severity and chose to open the door wider. It was right to.

If this is a grief you have never spoken, speak it to someone safe: a pastor who will listen before he talks, a counselor, a friend who can keep a confidence. Many churches and pregnancy centers now offer quiet groups for exactly this grief. If the weight tonight feels like more than you can survive, call or text 988. You are not the exception to grace. No one is.

## The circle with no floor but God's

The progressive Christian instinct is right, and the argument of this essay depends on its being right. The circle of human worth should keep widening. The powerful should never get to decide who is disposable. The voiceless should have someone who opens a mouth for them. The only question is where the widening stops, and whether the answer is a principle or a convenience.

For the right, the consistent ethic means following the child out of the womb and into the neighborhood, the school, the clinic, the court and the prison, and it means receiving the mother as a person rather than a problem. For the left, it means following the same conviction inward, to the smallest and most hidden human being, the one who cannot march, vote, post or be seen. Neither side gets to cut the garment where its coalition finds the cutting cheap. And neither side, if it is Christian, gets to hold this conviction without also holding the mercy the church held with it from the beginning.

Matthew tells a story at the start of his Gospel that the church reads every Christmas and rarely lingers over. A ruler who felt threatened by a child ordered the killing of the boys of Bethlehem, and Matthew reaches back to Jeremiah for the only words that fit: "A voice was heard in Ramah, weeping and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted, because they are no more" (Matthew 2:18, ESV). The Christ child survived that night as a refugee, carried by his parents into Egypt, and he grew up to say that whatever was done to the least of his brothers was done to him. He had himself been, not long before, the least of all: hidden in a young woman's body, unseen, unable to speak, entirely dependent, the Word made flesh at the size of a seed.

That is the weight the church carries into this question. Not a slogan and not a platform, but Rachel's refusal to be comforted, and a Savior who was once as small as the smallest person this argument concerns. The left was right to ask who has been left outside the circle. The unborn child is still standing there, and so, too often, is the woman who carried her. The church that finally lets both of them in will have to repent to do it, on the right and on the left, and I don't know yet whether we will.
