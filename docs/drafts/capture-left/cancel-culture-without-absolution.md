The pattern is familiar enough now that it hardly needs a particular case. Something a person said or did, sometimes last week and sometimes fifteen years ago, surfaces and circulates. A crowd gathers around it, most of whom will never meet the person and some of whom never read past the first sentence. A statement follows, carefully worded, taking responsibility and promising to listen and learn. The statement is judged insufficient, and a second statement is judged worse than the first. The job goes, or the book contract, or the pulpit, or the invitation. Then comes the strangest part, the part that ought to interest anyone who has read the Gospels: nobody can say what would make it end. There is no sentence the accused can speak, no restitution he can make, no length of time he can wait, after which the matter is closed. The file stays open. The name stays a byword.

This essay is not a complaint about accountability, and it will spend real time defending accountability, because the church has earned the defense being made against it. It is an argument about the shape of a moral system. Call-out culture, which many progressive churches have absorbed into their own practice, has kept almost the whole Christian grammar of sin: the conviction that wrongs are real, that they stain, that they must be named, that the guilty ought to confess. What it has discarded is the one word that made that grammar bearable, which is absolution. It is an economy of guilt with confession and no forgiveness, penance and no end, accusation and no advocate. The right runs its own version with different sins on the list. And the Christian claim, which the church has often failed to live and must recover, is that there is only one place in the universe where accusation actually ends.

No one writes about this from outside it. Anyone who has spent time online, or in church politics, or at a dinner table where an absent person's failures were being turned over with pleasure, knows the feeling of adding a stone to the pile and calling it conscience. That feeling is not confined to one side. Nor is it confined to other people.

## The accountability the church owed and refused

Start with why this culture exists, because it did not come from nowhere, and a good deal of it came from the church's own failure.

In January 2002 the *Boston Globe*'s Spotlight team began publishing its investigation into the Archdiocese of Boston, showing that church leaders had known about priests who abused children and had moved them from parish to parish rather than removing them. In October 2017 the reporting on Harvey Weinstein turned the phrase "Me Too" into a movement, and within weeks women in churches began telling their own stories under the name #ChurchToo. In February 2019 the *Houston Chronicle* and the *San Antonio Express-News* published "Abuse of Faith," documenting abuse by leaders and volunteers in Southern Baptist churches and the ease with which accused men had moved on to new congregations. In May 2022 the independent report by Guidepost Solutions found that staff of the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee had kept a private list of accused ministers for years while resisting the reforms survivors were asking for. That record crosses traditions. It is Catholic and Protestant, liturgical and free church, and evangelical churches cannot read it as someone else's story.

In each case the private process had failed. Victims had gone to pastors, elders, bishops and committees, and had been told to forgive, to be quiet, to think of the ministry, to follow Matthew 18 and go to the man privately, which meant going alone to the person who had harmed them. The public call-out, for many of them, was the first tool that worked. It was not their first choice. It was what remained when every institution that should have heard them had closed its door.

Scripture does not treat exposure as a sin. "Take no part in the unfruitful works of darkness, but instead expose them" (Ephesians 5:11, ESV). Paul tells Timothy that leaders who persist in sin should be rebuked "in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear" (1 Timothy 5:20, ESV). The prophets named kings to their faces, and Israel wrote the sins of its greatest king into its Scriptures for every generation to read. When powerful people use the language of grace to avoid consequences, bringing the matter into the light is not a failure of love. It is love refusing to be used.

So the first thing to say about accountability is that the church needs more of it, not less, and that anyone who reads this essay as permission to protect an abuser has read it backward. If you have been harmed by someone in a church, you do not owe the institution your silence. In the United States, the National Sexual Assault Hotline is 1-800-656-4673, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, and the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline can be reached by calling or texting 988. Tell the police when a crime has been committed. Tell someone outside the institution if the institution will not listen.

The argument of this essay begins only after all of that has been granted. Accountability was never the problem. The problem is what happens to a moral system that has learned how to accuse and forgotten how to absolve.

## An economy of guilt with no way to pay

Friedrich Nietzsche, who had no sympathy for Christianity, saw something about guilt that Christians often miss. In the second essay of *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) he argued that the moral idea of guilt grew out of the much older, material idea of debt; the German word *Schuld* carries both meanings. Before anyone felt guilty, someone owed someone else. Punishment, in his account, began as a form of repayment: the creditor who could not recover his money was compensated with the satisfaction of seeing the debtor suffer. Nietzsche meant this as an unmasking of morality's cruel origins. Read now, it works as a description of the economy the call-out has built.

That economy has debts. Every offense, from a crude joke to a real abuse of power, creates an obligation that the offender owes. It has currency: public confession, the statement, the apology in its approved form. It has creditors: the crowd, often acting on behalf of real victims but also, often, on behalf of itself. What it does not have is any mechanism for closing an account. There is no court whose sentence ends. There is no one with authority to declare a debt paid, because authority has been dispersed into a crowd that has no single voice and no memory except the permanent one the internet provides. Confession, in this system, does not lead to release. It functions as evidence. The apology becomes the proof of guilt, and a second apology becomes proof that the first one was insincere.

A distinction matters here, and without it the argument collapses into a defense of impunity. Accountability is one thing and condemnation is another. Accountability says a wrong was done, the one who did it must own it, make what restitution he can, and accept the consequences, which may include losing a position of trust for good. Condemnation says the one who did it is now defined by it and can never be anything else. A man who abused his office should not have the office back, and a church that forgives him does not owe him a pulpit. But losing an office is a consequence. Being turned permanently into the worst thing you did is a verdict on your soul, and no crowd has the standing to deliver it.

Joshua Mitchell, a political theorist at Georgetown, makes a related argument in *American Awakening* (2020). He reads identity politics as a transformed version of American Protestantism that has kept its moral categories, stain and innocence, transgression and purity, while losing the figure who made those categories survivable. In the Christian account, the stain of sin is real, and it is borne away by Christ, the one innocent victim who takes it on himself. Without Christ, Mitchell argues, the stain still has to go somewhere. It is purged by locating it in someone else and expelling them. The call-out becomes a ritual of purification in which the community keeps itself clean by casting out the unclean, and the need for a new transgressor is never satisfied, because the stain it is trying to remove is finally everyone's.

René Girard spent a career on that mechanism. In *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning* (1999) he described the way human communities have always relieved their internal tension by uniting against a single victim, believing him guilty and feeling, after his expulsion, a strange peace. What made the Gospels unique, Girard argued, is that they tell the story from the side of the victim and reveal that the crowd was wrong; the Passion narratives expose the mechanism instead of hiding it. Our modern concern for victims is, in his account, a Christian inheritance. But he warned that this concern can itself be turned into a weapon, so that a society persecutes in the name of victims, using the language of their innocence to justify a new round of expulsions. The crowd no longer believes it is killing a god's enemy. It believes it is protecting the vulnerable. The satisfaction afterward feels the same.

## The right keeps its own ledger

This is a pillar about the capture of the church by the left, and the progressive church has often absorbed this economy into its public posture. It should be said that some of the sharpest criticism of call-out culture has come from the left itself. Loretta Ross, a veteran of the Black feminist and reproductive-justice movements, argued in a 2019 *New York Times* essay titled "I'm a Black Feminist. I Think Call-Out Culture Is Toxic" that public shaming often did more harm than good to the people and movements it claimed to serve, and she urged activists toward what she called calling in: correction offered with patience and, where possible, in private, in the hope of keeping the person rather than expelling them. Many progressive readers already share the diagnosis this essay is making, and some of them made it first. But an honest essay has to use the same instrument on both sides, because the right runs a ledger of its own, and American Christianity had one long before anyone was cancelled on the internet.

The Puritans built one in Massachusetts, and one of the great American novels was written to indict it. Nathaniel Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* (1850) is set in seventeenth-century Boston, where Hester Prynne is made to wear the letter A on her breast as a permanent public mark of her adultery. The community's law has a punishment and no absolution; the letter is meant to define her until she dies. The novel's deepest irony is that her fellow sinner, the minister Arthur Dimmesdale, stands in the pulpit admired and undiscovered while she bears the mark alone, and his hidden guilt consumes him far more thoroughly than her public shame consumes her. Hawthorne saw that a community organized around exposure produces two kinds of people: the marked and the hidden. Neither kind is healed.

The Salem trials of 1692 reveal the same economy at its most perverse. The twenty people put to death, nineteen of them by hanging, were people who refused to confess to witchcraft or who took back a confession they had made. Those who confessed, however falsely, and held to it were spared. A system built on accusation had made confession the currency of survival and had therefore made lying the safe course and truth-telling fatal. Every economy of accusation drifts in this direction. When the only way to end the pressure is to say what the crowd demands, the words stop meaning anything, and those who refuse to say them are punished for their honesty.

The contemporary right has its own purity tests, and conservative churches enforce them. There is the charge of disloyalty to the party or the leader, and pastors who would not bless a candidate from the pulpit have found it used to drive them out. There is the charge of having gone "woke," often attached to anyone who raises the subject of racial injustice or abuse, and used less as an argument than as an excommunication. There are the theological shibboleths that sort the faithful from the compromised, and the whisper networks that can end a ministry without any process at all. Purity culture, which taught a generation of young evangelicals that sexual sin left a permanent stain, turned the body into a ledger where one entry could never be erased. The words differ from the progressive list. The machinery is identical: a community secures its own sense of righteousness by marking the ones who fall outside it.

Alan Jacobs names the social mechanism beneath both in *How to Think* (2017). Borrowing a phrase from the anthropologist Susan Friend Harding, he describes the "Repugnant Cultural Other," the group whose members we cannot imagine as reasonable people, and he draws on C. S. Lewis's 1944 lecture "The Inner Ring" to show how much of our thinking is really an effort to secure our place among the people we want to belong to. The call-out, on this reading, is often less about the one called out than about the caller's membership. To join the condemnation is to prove you are inside the ring. To hesitate is to risk being the next name. Most of us, left or right, have felt that pull and given in to it.

## What the church knew about confession

Christianity is not squeamish about sin. It is the one tradition in the West that has insisted most relentlessly that every human being is a sinner, that sin is serious enough to require the death of God's Son, and that it must be confessed. So the difference between the church's practice and the crowd's is not that the church takes sin less seriously. It is that the church knows what confession is for.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote *Life Together* in 1938, after the Gestapo closed the underground seminary at Finkenwalde where he had trained pastors for the Confessing Church, and it was published in 1939. Its chapter on confession is one of the most searching things written on the subject in the last century. Bonhoeffer observed that a religious community can become a place where no one is permitted to be a sinner, and that in such a community everyone must hide their sin from themselves and from each other, so that its members live in a lonely pretense of righteousness. The break comes, he argued, when one person confesses to another. Confession to a brother or sister breaks through to community, because the sin brought into the light loses its power to isolate. And the one who hears the confession does not hear it as a judge. He hears it as a fellow sinner who has seen his own sin under the cross, and who can therefore speak God's forgiveness without contempt.

Set that beside the call-out and the difference is plain. In the call-out, confession is extracted by accusers, heard by a crowd that stands outside the sin, and followed by no word of release. In Christian confession, the admission is offered, heard by someone who knows himself to be guilty too, and answered with a word that ends the matter before God. The first isolates the sinner more completely than the sin did. The second restores him to the community.

The New Testament gives a case that sounds strikingly modern. Some wrong in the Corinthian church, serious enough that Paul had written in grief about it, had led the congregation to discipline the man responsible. Paul then writes: "For such a one, this punishment by the majority is enough, so you should rather turn to forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. So I beg you to reaffirm your love for him" (2 Corinthians 2:6–8, ESV). Notice what Paul does not say. He does not deny the offense or the discipline. He says the punishment by the majority, the public consequence, is enough. It has an end. And he warns that a community that keeps punishing past the point of repentance may destroy the person it was meant to restore. Elsewhere he makes the goal explicit: "Brothers, if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness. Keep watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted" (Galatians 6:1, ESV). The aim of exposure, in the church, is restoration, and the one who does the restoring must keep watching himself.

Repentance in this account is not cheap. When Zacchaeus met Jesus, his response was concrete: "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8, ESV). Real repentance includes restitution to the ones who were harmed. It does not demand that victims resume trust, and it does not return abusers to positions where they can harm again. Forgiveness is not the restoration of office. It is the refusal to let the worst thing a person did become the only thing he is.

## The accuser thrown down

The prophet Zechariah saw a vision that could serve as a portrait of the call-out. Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the Lord, dressed in filthy garments, and beside him stands Satan, the Hebrew word meaning "the accuser," ready to make the case against him. The accusation is not false. The garments really are filthy. But the Lord answers the accuser, not the accused: "The LORD rebuke you, O Satan! The LORD who has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you! Is not this a brand plucked from the fire?" (Zechariah 3:2, ESV). Then the angel orders the filthy garments removed and says to Joshua, "Behold, I have taken your iniquity away from you, and I will clothe you with pure vestments" (Zechariah 3:4, ESV).

The accusation ends in that scene not because it is refuted but because it is answered by someone with the authority to take the iniquity away. That is the thing no crowd can do. A victim can forgive what was done to her, and that forgiveness is costly and real, but she cannot forgive what was done to someone else. The crowd cannot forgive at all, because it is not the one who was wronged. Every sin, in the end, is against God before it is against anyone else, which is why David, after a crime that had destroyed a family and killed a man, could pray, "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight" (Psalm 51:4, ESV). He was not minimizing what he had done to Uriah and Bathsheba. He was naming the only creditor who could close the whole account.

Nietzsche saw this too, and he saw it with contempt. In the same essay in which he traced guilt to debt, he described what he regarded as Christianity's stroke of genius: God himself paying the debt, the creditor sacrificing himself for the debtor. He meant it as a mockery of a religion that had made guilt infinite in order to offer an infinite payment. But the skeptic's analysis concedes the structure. If guilt is debt, then only the creditor can cancel it, and a system without a creditor who forgives will keep collecting forever.

Paul describes the cross in exactly those terms. God has forgiven us all our trespasses, he writes, "by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross" (Colossians 2:14, ESV). The record is not destroyed by pretending it was never written. It is nailed to the cross, where the debt is paid by the one to whom it was owed. That is why the New Testament can say, "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Romans 8:33, ESV), and why the book of Revelation announces that "the accuser of our brothers has been thrown down, who accuses them day and night before our God" (Revelation 12:10, ESV). Accusation does not end because sin turns out not to matter. It ends because sin has been borne.

This is what the church has to offer a culture that has learned to accuse and forgotten how to absolve. Not a softer standard, and not an end to consequences. The Christian church, of all communities, ought to be the place where wrongs are named more honestly than anywhere else, where victims are believed and protected, where the powerful are held to account, and where, after the truth has been told and restitution made and the consequences accepted, a sinner can hear that the matter is closed before God. A church that can only accuse has become one more crowd. A church that can only absolve has become a shelter for abusers. The church at its best has always held both.

That leaves most of us with an uncomfortable inventory. Each of us carries a list of names, some public figures and some people we once knew, whose worst moment has become the whole of who they are in our minds. Some of them did real harm, and the harm should not be minimized. Some of them should never again hold the trust they betrayed. But the question the cross puts to the one holding the list is not whether those people are guilty. It is whether the one holding the list has any intention of letting God be the one who closes the account, or whether he means to keep collecting on it himself, a debt he could never repay standing unacknowledged behind him the whole time.
