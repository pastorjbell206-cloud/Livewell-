The people deconstructing their faith are usually right about why. That is not the line most pastors lead with, so I am leading with it. If you were handed a Christianity that was equal parts Jesus and a voting bloc, that policed your hemline and ignored your hunger, that protected the man behind the pulpit and disbelieved the woman in the pew, that taught you to fear questions because the questions had answers nobody could survive being asked — then the impulse to tear it down is not rebellion. It is closer to repentance. The online deconstruction wave did not invent the wound. It found a microphone for a pain the church had spent decades keeping quiet.

So I am not going to defend the building that hurt you. I have torn one down myself. At nineteen I built an atheism brick by brick to keep God at arm's length, and I defended it the way you defend a house you are afraid to leave. Tearing it down was the most honest thing I had done up to then. I have held the hammer. I still do. This is not a warning from a man who never doubted. It is a report from one who doubted enough to learn that doubt, by itself, builds nothing.

The claim underneath the whole essay. **Faith deconstruction can be the most faithful thing a person does — Jesus deconstructed the Pharisees on purpose and relentlessly — but deconstruction that never rebuilds is not freedom. It is demolition. And the swept, empty house does not stay empty. Something always moves in, and what moves in is rarely kinder than what you evicted.**

## The word meant something, and we lost half of it

Start with the word, because we have forgotten what it was for.

*Deconstruct* is borrowed, and the lender meant something we have misplaced. It comes from Jacques Derrida, the French philosopher, who introduced it in the late 1960s — *Of Grammatology* appeared in 1967 — and in his hands it was never destruction. It was a method of reading. A way of taking a text apart to see what it was secretly built on, what it had buried in its own foundations, what it leaned on while pretending it stood on its own. The point of taking the thing apart was to read it more truly, not to leave it in pieces on the floor. Derrida spent his life inside the texts he deconstructed, not in some cleared field beyond them. He was not swinging a wrecking ball. He was a man on his knees in the basement, running his hand along the load-bearing walls.

Somewhere on the road from the seminar room into our group chats, the word lost its second half.

The wave that crested around 2015 — the hashtag, the deconstruction podcasts, the exvangelical testimony threads, the entire architecture of people narrating their exit in real time on TikTok — kept the taking-apart and quietly dropped the reading-truly. I want to be careful, because the wound driving all of it was real, and much of it the church spent years hiding. But somewhere the method became a mood. We turned a way of reading into a permanent condition of suspicion, and we called the suspicion freedom.

There is a difference between renovating a house and standing in a field where a house used to be. Both start with demolition. That is the trap.

In the early days the two are indistinguishable — the same walls coming down, the same dust in the same air, the same neighbors asking what on earth you are doing in there. You can be three years into deconstructing your faith and genuinely not know whether you are building something truer or simply tearing down something false and intending to live, from now on, in the open. The dust is identical. Only the intention is different, and intention is invisible — even to yourself. **Especially to yourself.**

## A field is not a home

Here is what I have watched, pastorally, across fifteen years, in people I love.

The demolition feels like clarity for about two years. There is a real exhilaration in it — the relief of putting down a god who was never God, the rush of finally saying the forbidden thing, the company of others swinging their hammers beside you on the same timeline. It is a genuine joy and I will not mock it. But a field is not a home, and a person cannot live in suspicion forever, because suspicion is a posture and not a place.

Suspicion can tell you what to tear down. It cannot tell you what to do on a Tuesday in February when the marriage is hard and the father is dying and you need something to stand on that you did not invent that morning. Suspicion has no Tuesday. So the ones who only deconstructed, who never rebuilt, rarely end up as triumphant free thinkers. They end up tired. They end up with a sophisticated account of everything they no longer believe and no account at all of what they would die for. No one runs on that for long.

The philosophers who thought hardest about doubt knew this, and they were not pastors trying to keep anyone in the pews.

Michael Polanyi, a chemist before he was a philosopher, watched the twentieth century try to ground all knowledge in universal doubt and concluded the project was incoherent. You cannot doubt everything at once. To doubt one thing, he argued in *Personal Knowledge* in 1958, you have to stand on a hundred things you are not doubting at the moment. The man who claims to have suspended all his commitments has only hidden them from himself. There is no view from nowhere. There is no clean field. The mind that believes it has cleared the ground of every belief is standing, without knowing it, on ground it never examined — which is the most dangerous ground there is, because it is load-bearing and unseen.

Alasdair MacIntyre drove the same stake deeper. In *After Virtue*, published in 1981, he argued that human reasoning is not a free-floating faculty that works best stripped of every inherited loyalty. It is tradition-constituted. We think *from somewhere*, inside a story and a community and a set of practices that taught us what the questions even are. A person who imagines he has reasoned his way out of every tradition into pure, unencumbered freedom has merely become fluent in a newer tradition without noticing it is one — usually the thin, dissolving tradition of the late-modern West, which trains its children to believe they have no tradition at all. The unreconstructed deconstruction does not rescue you from this. It delivers you straight into it.

## Doubt always feels braver than belief — and that feeling is rigged

Charles Taylor named the water we are all swimming in.

In *A Secular Age*, in 2007, he described the immanent frame — a way of living in which meaning is generated entirely within history, with nothing breaking in from outside it. We did not choose it. We were born into it the way a fish is born into water. And inside that frame, doubt always feels braver than belief, subtraction always feels more honest than construction, and the person tearing down feels like a truth-teller while the person building feels like a rube who has not yet been disillusioned.

Taylor's point cuts against the feeling. The choice to stop in the empty field is not the neutral, default, grown-up position. **It is itself a faith — a faith in nothing in particular — and it is no less a leap than the one it replaced.** The field is not the absence of a story. The field is a story that tells you to call it an absence.

This is the part the field cannot admit, because the field has a stake in your not knowing it. A faith taken apart and left open does not become a neutral clearing. It gets occupied by whatever is strongest in the neighborhood. The spirit of the age moves in. The anxieties of the feed move in. A new and unexamined orthodoxy moves in — and you will defend it more fiercely than the one you left, precisely because you are sure you have left all orthodoxies behind, which makes the new one invisible and therefore unopposed.

I keep returning to the man in the parable who swept his house clean. Jesus tells it almost as a warning. An unclean spirit goes out of a man, and the man cleans the house, sweeps it, puts it in order — and leaves it empty (Matthew 12:43–45). Empty. And because it is empty, the spirit comes back with seven others more wicked than itself, and the last state of that man is worse than the first. I do not think Jesus is preaching about deconstruction. But the shape of the warning is the shape of our moment. An emptied house does not stay empty. The seven worse tenants do not arrive looking worse. They arrive looking like liberation, like the obvious opinions of decent people, like the only sane way to think — which is exactly how the first set arrived, before you learned what they cost.

We do not get to choose whether we will worship. We only ever choose what.

## The exiles were told to rebuild before they could see the end

The Israelites in exile had every reason to deconstruct.

The temple was rubble — literal rubble, not metaphor — and the theology that had promised *God will never let Jerusalem fall* lay in the ash with it. The certainties of their fathers were gone. If anyone ever earned the right to demolish a faith and live in the open, it was the generation Jeremiah wrote to in 587 before Christ, dragged a thousand miles into Babylon to grieve a God who had apparently lost.

And what does the prophet send them? Not *your old faith was a lie, walk free*. He tells them to build houses and live in them, to plant gardens and eat what they grow, to marry and raise children, to seek the welfare of the very city that holds them captive (Jeremiah 29:5–7). He tells the deconstructed to reconstruct — in exile, with no temple, on foreign soil, before they could possibly see how it ends.

They did not get their old certainties back. They got something better and harder. They got a faith that had passed through the fire and learned to stand without the props, and that faith produced, eventually, the synagogue and the canon and some of the deepest theology Israel ever wrote. None of it would have come if they had refused to build. Read the record: the ones who would not reconstruct assimilated into Babylon and dissolved, and history does not even keep their names.

A century later Nehemiah went back to the actual rubble of the actual walls, and the work of reconstruction had a detail the romance of demolition always forgets. The builders worked with a tool in one hand and a weapon in the other (Nehemiah 4:17), because there were people invested in keeping the ruins ruined. Rebuilding is contested. Someone always profits from the field staying empty. That is worth knowing before you mistake the resistance you feel for proof you should quit.

There is a counterfeit reconstruction as dangerous as the empty field, and it usually arrives faster. It is the person who tears down the faith that hurt them and immediately rebuilds, on the same foundation, the same kind of certainty pointed the opposite way — the same contempt, the same tribe, the same refusal to be questioned, simply repainted. That is not reconstruction. That is the swept house redecorated for new tenants who are no gentler than the old ones.

## Faithful deconstruction has a model, and it is not Derrida

Jesus deconstructed the Pharisees' system constantly and on purpose.

He took apart their reading of the Sabbath, their fences around the law, their accounting of clean and unclean, their confidence that they could see. He pulled down a religious structure that policed the hemline and ignored the hunger — the original of every counterfeit you were handed — and he did it relentlessly, and the people who loved the old building hated him for it. But he never tore down to leave a field. He tore down to build a temple they could not see yet, raised in three days, made of his own body. **His demolition always had a destination. That is the line between the prophet and the cynic, and it is a thin, sharp line, and most of us have stood on the wrong side of it while feeling most righteous.**

There is a verse that has become the patron saint of everyone caught between the house and the field, and it earns its place. A father brings his suffering son to Jesus and is asked whether he believes. He answers with the truest sentence in the Gospels: "I believe; help my unbelief" (Mark 9:24). Notice what he does not say. He does not say *I have torn down my belief and will live in my unbelief and call it courage*. He does not say *I have resolved my doubt and have no more questions*. He holds both in the same breath, in the same body, and he holds them toward Christ — not in a field at a safe distance, but kneeling in front of the only One who could do anything about either. That is reconstruction in a single line. It is not the end of doubt. It is doubt brought home and given somewhere to stand.

Thomas is the other patron, and the church has slandered him for centuries by calling him a doubter as if doubt were the sin. Thomas said he would not believe the resurrection unless he put his hand in the wounds (John 20:24–29). He wanted evidence. He wanted the demolition of an easy, secondhand faith of people who had not been in the room. Jesus did not rebuke the demand. He walked through a locked door and offered him the wounds. *Put your finger here.* The risen Christ met the deconstruction by handing over the load-bearing wall and saying, test it, lean on it, put your weight where the nails went. Thomas did, and what came out of him was not a field. It was the highest confession in the Gospel: "My Lord and my God." Doubt was not the enemy. Doubt was the way in. But it had to walk all the way to the wounds.

Augustine, who tore down more than most before he built anything, gives the *Confessions* — finished around 397 after Christ — its strange double motion. Every page is an accounting of what he had believed and abandoned: the Manichees, the astrologers, the rhetoric, the ambition. And every page is also a building, a slow construction of praise on the ruin of all of it. He did not deconstruct his way to nothing. He deconstructed his way to God, and the wreckage was the road. "You have made us for yourself," he wrote, "and our heart is restless until it rests in you." That restlessness is the engine. It is the thing the unreconstructed deconstruction tries to medicate by calling the field home — and the field never quiets it, because the field is precisely the absence of the rest the heart was made to find.

Lesslie Newbigin spent forty years as a missionary in India and came home to a Britain that had quietly stopped believing while he was gone. He thought longer than almost anyone about faith in a world that treats doubt as the only honest posture, and his answer — sharpened in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* in 1989 — was that there is no commitment-free standpoint from which to evaluate the faith. Every knower stands somewhere. Every act of knowing rests on things held without proof, trusted in order to see anything at all. The imagined field, where you have cleared away all belief and now reason in pure suspended air, is not humility. It is the proudest illusion the modern world sells, and it sells it cheap. The choice is never between commitment and the absence of commitment. It is only ever between commitments. You are going to build on something. The unreconstructed deconstruction is dishonest exactly there, because it claims to have built on nothing while standing, the whole time, on the floor of an unexamined age.

## What I would say to the one in the dust

Keep tearing down what is false. Do not stop short out of fear.

The half-deconstructed faith — where you have seen the rot but lack the nerve to name it — is its own misery, and I am not sending anyone back into the house that did the harm. Tear out the rot. But decide, while the hammer is still swinging, whether this is renovation or razing. Decide whether there is anything you are tearing *toward* and not only away from. The goal of taking a faith apart was never the taking apart. Derrida knew that, in the basement, on his knees, with his hand on the wall. The goal was to find what was load-bearing — what could actually carry the weight of a life — and to build again on that, and on nothing less.

There is one wall I have found that does not come down no matter how hard I have swung. It is a man on a cross who refused to defend himself, and an empty tomb three days later that I cannot explain away and have spent thirty years trying. Everything else in my faith I have taken apart and rebuilt, some of it more than once, and I expect to do it again before I die. But that wall has held my full weight every time I threw myself against it. It held the weight of my father's absence. It held the night I was certain there was no God. It is still holding.

Church hurt is real. The wounds are real, and the One who carries his own scars into a locked room to be touched is not asking you to pretend otherwise. He is asking you not to die of exposure in a field while you wait for proof you have already been offered.

You can tear everything else down. I have. The field has no roof, and the rain is coming, and it always comes, and a hammer was never a shelter and was never meant to be one.
