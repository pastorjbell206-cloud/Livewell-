title: Deconstruction Without Reconstruction Is Demolition
pillar: Capture by the Left

I want to start by saying that the people taking their faith apart are usually right about why. That is not the concession most pastors lead with, so I am leading with it. If you grew up handed a Christianity that was equal parts Jesus and a political party, that policed your hemline and ignored your hunger, that protected the man behind the pulpit and disbelieved the woman in the pew, that taught you to fear questions because the questions had answers nobody could survive being asked — then the impulse to tear it down is not rebellion. It is closer to repentance. You are not walking away from God. You are walking away from a counterfeit, and the One you are walking toward has been waiting in the rubble the whole time. I have torn things down too. I tore down the atheism I built at nineteen to keep God at arm's length, and the tearing was the most honest thing I had ever done.

So this is not an essay scolding you for deconstructing. It is an essay about what happens next, and what happens next is the whole question.

Because *deconstruct* is a borrowed word, and we have forgotten what it was for. It comes from Jacques Derrida, the French philosopher, and in his hands in the 1960s it was never an act of destruction. It was a method of reading — a way of taking a structure apart to see what it was secretly built on, what it was hiding, what it had buried in its foundations. The point of taking the thing apart was to understand it more truly. Derrida was not swinging a wrecking ball. He was a man on his knees in the basement of a building, examining the load-bearing walls. Somewhere on the way into our churches the word lost its second half. We kept the taking-apart and dropped the seeing-truly. We turned a method of reading into a permanent condition of suspicion, and called the suspicion freedom.

There is a difference between renovating a house and standing in a field where a house used to be.

Both involve demolition. That is the trap. In the early days they look identical — the same walls coming down, the same dust, the same neighbors asking what on earth you are doing in there. The renovation and the demolition are indistinguishable for the first season, and that is exactly why so many people cannot tell which one they are in. You can be three years into tearing your faith apart and genuinely not know whether you are building something truer or simply destroying something false and intending to live, from now on, in the open field. The dust is the same. Only the intention is different, and intention is invisible, even to yourself, especially to yourself.

Here is what I have watched, pastorally, over fifteen years, in people I love. The demolition feels like clarity for about two years. There is real exhilaration in it — the relief of putting down a god who was never God, the rush of finally saying the thing you were not allowed to say, the community of others swinging their hammers beside you. It is genuine joy and I will not mock it. But a field is not a home, and a person cannot live in suspicion forever, because suspicion is a posture and not a place. It can tell you what to tear down. It cannot tell you what to do on a Tuesday in February when your marriage is hard and your father is dying and you need something to stand on that you did not invent that morning. Suspicion has no Tuesday. And so the people who only deconstructed, who never rebuilt, do not usually end up as triumphant freethinkers. They end up tired. They end up with a sophisticated account of everything they no longer believe and no account at all of what they would die for, and a human being cannot run on that for long. We were not built to live in a field.

The Israelites in exile had every reason to deconstruct. The temple was rubble — literal rubble, not metaphorical — the theology that said *God will never let Jerusalem fall* had fallen with it, and the certainties of their fathers were ash in their mouths. If anyone earned the right to demolish a faith and live in the field, it was the generation Jeremiah wrote to. And what does the prophet tell them? Not *your old faith was a lie, walk free*. He tells them to build houses, plant gardens, marry, multiply, seek the welfare of the city of their captivity (Jeremiah 29:5–7). He tells the deconstructed to reconstruct, in exile, with no temple, on foreign soil, before they can see how it ends. The exiles did not get their certainties back. They got something better and harder. They got a faith that had been through the fire and learned to live without the props, a faith that produced, eventually, the synagogue and the canon and the deepest theology Israel ever wrote. But it only happened because they built. The ones who refused to build assimilated and vanished. History does not even record their names.

I keep thinking about the man in the parable who swept his house clean. Jesus tells it almost as a warning (Matthew 12:43–45): an unclean spirit goes out of a man, and the man cleans the house, sweeps it, puts it in order — and leaves it empty. Empty. And because it is empty, the spirit comes back with seven worse than itself, and the last state of that man is worse than the first. I do not think Jesus is talking about deconstruction, but the structure of the warning is the structure of our moment. An emptied house does not stay empty. A swept room does not stay swept. Whatever you evicted will be replaced — the only question is by what — and a faith taken apart and left open does not become a neutral field of freedom. It becomes occupied by whatever was strongest in the neighborhood: the spirit of the age, the anxieties of the feed, a new and unexamined orthodoxy that you will defend more fiercely than the one you left, precisely because you think you have left all orthodoxies behind. Nature abhors a vacuum. So does the soul. We do not get to choose whether we worship. We only get to choose what.

So to the one in the dust, I have only this, and I say it as a man holding a hammer of his own. Keep tearing down what was false. Do not stop short out of fear; the half-deconstructed faith, where you have seen the rot but lack the nerve to name it, is its own kind of misery, and I am not sending you back into the house that hurt you. But decide, while you swing the hammer, whether you are renovating or razing. Decide whether there is anything you are tearing *toward* and not only away from. Because the goal of taking a faith apart was never the taking apart. Derrida knew that, in the basement, on his knees, looking at the walls. The goal was to find what was load-bearing — what could actually hold the weight of a life — and to build again on that, and on nothing less.

There is one wall I have found that does not come down no matter how hard I have swung. It is a man on a cross who refused to defend himself, and an empty tomb three days later that I cannot explain away and have spent thirty years trying. Everything else in my faith I have taken apart and rebuilt, some of it more than once, and I expect to do it again before I die. But that wall has held my full weight every time I have thrown it against it. It held the weight of my father's absence. It held the weight of the night I was sure there was no God. It is still holding.

You can tear everything else down. I have. Just do not stop in the field. The field has no roof, and the rain is coming, and it always comes, and a hammer is no shelter.
