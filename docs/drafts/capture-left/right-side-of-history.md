The phrase arrives like a verdict already settled. *The right side of history.* It is meant to end the argument before the argument begins, and most of the time it works. I have used it. I have stood in a room and felt the warm certainty of believing that the future agreed with me, that time itself was a kind of ally, that to be on the moving edge of the present was to be near to God. It is a good feeling. That is the first thing to admit. It is a very good feeling, and good feelings have always been the cheapest disguise sin wears.

Where did we get the idea that history has a side?

Not from Scripture. We got it from the nineteenth century. We got it from Hegel, who taught the educated West to read time as Spirit unfolding toward freedom, and from the engineers of progress who followed him and believed, with real sincerity, that the arc of the human story bent upward on its own. The phrase as we use it now is older than a meme and younger than the gospel. It is the secularized residue of a Christian hope — the conviction that the story is going somewhere — with the God who guarantees the destination quietly removed. We kept the arc. We threw away the hand that bends it.

And so we are left holding a confidence we cannot account for. If history is merely the sum of what survives, then "the right side of history" means nothing more than the winning side, and the winning side has, across the centuries, included the slave market and the conquistador and the eugenicist. Each of them was certain the future belonged to them. Each of them, in their moment, was modern. The Belgian administrators in the Congo in the 1890s believed they stood on the advancing edge of civilization. The future did not redeem them. The future buried them and moved on, and called the next thing progress too.

Scripture does not measure faithfulness by which way the wind is blowing.

This is the hinge, so let me set it down plainly. The biblical word for the future is not *progress*. It is *judgment*, and then *mercy*, and the two are never pried apart. The prophets did not ask Israel to read the trend lines. Amos did not poll the room. He stood in the wreckage of a prosperous, confident, upwardly mobile nation and said the day they were longing for would be darkness and not light (Amos 5:18). He was on the wrong side of history. He was on the right side of God. There is a difference, and the whole of the Old Testament is an argument that the difference is everything.

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the place where my own tribe — the one I am inside, the one that reads essays like this one — likes to reach for the eject button. We hear a critique of "the right side of history" and we assume it is cover for the right side of the culture war. It is not. The selfsame error lives on the right, dressed differently. The Christian who insists that a vanished America was God's chosen order is making the identical mistake in reverse: he has fused the kingdom to a moment in time, except his moment is behind him instead of ahead. Nostalgia and progressivism are the same idolatry facing opposite directions. Both have decided that a season of human history *is* the will of God, and both will defend that season with a passion they no longer spend on the cross.

So this is not a partisan essay. It is the harder thing. It is written to the people I agree with, by a man who agrees with them, about the way our agreement has started to carry a weight that belongs only to Christ.

Here is what the capture looks like from the inside. It does not look like apostasy. It looks like relevance. You begin with a genuine conviction — that the poor are neglected, that the stranger is despised, that the church has been complicit in real cruelties, and on all of this you are right, gloriously, biblically right; Micah 6:8 is not a suggestion. But somewhere in the defending of these convictions a substitution occurs, so quietly you never sign the papers. The reason you hold the conviction shifts. You held it because the God who set his image in every person commands it. Now you hold it because it is what decent, forward-looking people believe, and you have grown frightened of being mistaken for the other kind. The conviction stays. Its root is severed. And a conviction cut off from its root will keep its color for a while, the way a cut flower does, and then it will begin, without your noticing, to die.

I have watched it die in myself. I have caught myself measuring a sermon not by whether it was true but by whether it would age well — whether, ten years out, I would be embarrassed to have preached it. That is a real question and not a stupid one. But notice the judge I had installed. The future. The applause of a generation not yet born. I had quietly replaced the God before whom I will actually stand with a tribunal of imagined descendants whose approval I craved, and I had done it while feeling more righteous, not less.

Charles Taylor named the air we breathe. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he calls it the immanent frame — a way of inhabiting the world in which all meaning is generated within history, with nothing breaking in from outside it. We did not choose the immanent frame. We were born into it the way a fish is born into water. And inside it, "the right side of history" is the only transcendence still permitted. It is heaven for people who have stopped believing in heaven. It lets you feel the pull of something larger than yourself, something that vindicates your sacrifices and condemns your enemies and promises that the dead will not have died in vain — all the freight the resurrection used to carry — without the inconvenience of a God who might also judge you. That is the appeal. It is most of the appeal. It offers eschatology without accountability.

But the resurrection is not the arc of history bending upward. The resurrection is a corpse standing up in a borrowed garden on a Sunday no trend predicted. It is the opposite of inevitability. The first witnesses were not riding the momentum of their age; their age had just executed their Lord, and the right side of history, that week, was a smooth stone rolled across a tomb and a squad of imperial soldiers and the settled opinion of every serious person that the matter was closed. Then the matter was not closed. The future did not produce the resurrection. The resurrection produced the future. And it did so against the grain of everything the present could see.

Which means the church was never promised the wind at its back. We were promised a cross to carry and a Spirit to carry it by, and these are not the same as the approval of the coming generation. Jesus said the world would hate his people (John 15:18–19), and he did not specify which side of the ballot the hatred would come from, because he knew the world is not a party. The world is an arrangement of human pride, and human pride has a left wing and a right wing and they take turns flying.

So what do we do with this. Not retreat — I am not writing you toward retreat, and the retreat would itself be a verdict about which way history is going. We keep feeding the hungry. We keep standing with the stranger. We keep saying the hard true things our churches have avoided about race and money and power, because Scripture said them first and louder than we ever have. But we do these things facing the other direction. We do them before God and not before the future. We do them as obedience and not as positioning. And we hold them loosely enough that if the wind shifts — and it will, the wind always shifts — we will still be standing where the text put us, instead of where the breeze left us.

The test is simple and it is brutal. Take the conviction you are proudest of. Now imagine history turns against it. Imagine your grandchildren are taught that you were a fool, that the future you bet on lost. Would you still hold it? If the only thing keeping the conviction alive is your confidence that tomorrow will agree with you, then it was never faith. It was forecasting. And forecasting is not a fruit of the Spirit.

I do not know which way history is going. Neither do you. We have both pretended to, and we have both used the pretending to avoid the harder question, which is not where the world is headed but where we are standing. The prophets did not bend with the arc. Most of them were broken on it, and called blessed afterward by a God who keeps a different record than the one we are all so anxious to be written into.

There is only one side worth being on. It was never the side of history. History is going to die.
