> [PERSONAL STORY — James to supply, if he wants one: a specific moment he caught himself preaching or deciding by the standard of "how this will age" rather than whether it was true. The essay works without it, but a true scene at the hinge below would carry it.]

The phrase arrives like a verdict already settled. *The right side of history.* It is meant to end the argument before the argument begins, and most of the time it works. I have used it. I have stood in a room and felt the warm certainty of believing that the future agreed with me — that time itself was a kind of ally, that to stand on the moving edge of the present was to stand near to God. It is a good feeling. That is the first thing to admit. It is a very good feeling, and good feelings have always been the cheapest disguise sin wears.

So before this goes one sentence further, understand where it comes from. It comes from inside. I am not standing outside my own tribe throwing stones through the window. I am sitting in the room I am describing. The critique landed on me before I ever turned it on anyone else, and if it stops landing on me while I write it, I have lost the thread.

Where did we get the idea that history has a side?

Begin with the honest answer, the one that does the phrase justice before it does it harm. The first famous mouth to say it was fighting actual slavery. Theodore Parker, the abolitionist preacher, stood in a Boston pulpit in 1853 and said he could not see far down the moral universe, that the arc was long and his eye reached only a little way, but that from what he could see he was sure it bends toward justice. A century later Martin Luther King Jr. took that sentence and carried it into the streets of Birmingham. In their mouths it was not a slogan. It was a confession of providence. Parker and King were not saying the future is automatically good. They were saying God is good, and God is not finished, and the God who is not finished will not finally be mocked. There was a hand in that arc. The arc bent because Someone was bending it.

That is where we got it. That is also what we did to it.

Because the phrase did not stay in the pulpit. It passed through the nineteenth century and came out the other side stripped of its God. Hegel had already taught the educated West to read time as Spirit unfolding toward freedom — history as a rational process climbing, stage by stage, toward its own fulfillment. His *Phenomenology* appeared in 1807, and for two centuries his confidence has been the groundwater of the modern imagination, watering thinkers who never read him and would not have agreed with him if they had. The engineers of progress who followed believed, with real sincerity, that the human story bent upward on its own steam. The arc no longer needed a hand. The arc was a law of nature, like gravity, like compound interest. And "the right side of history" became what it is now: the secularized residue of a Christian hope, the conviction that the story is going somewhere, with the God who guarantees the destination quietly removed.

We kept the arc. We threw away the hand that bends it.

And so we are left holding a confidence we cannot account for. If history is only the sum of what survives, then "the right side of history" means nothing grander than the winning side, and the winning side has, across the centuries, included the slave market and the conquistador and the eugenicist. This is not rhetoric. Look at the record. The Belgian administrators carving up the Congo in the 1890s believed they stood on the advancing edge of civilization, and they had the science and the maps and the serious opinion of their day to prove it. The future did not redeem them. The future buried them and called the next thing progress.

Sit longer with the example closest to the bone, because it is the one my own tribe least wants to hear. A hundred years ago the people most certain they were on the right side of history were the eugenicists, and they were not, by the standards of their moment, cranks. They were the progressives. They were the educated, the scientific, the forward-looking — the ones who read the trend lines and intended to help history along. In 1927 the Supreme Court of the United States, in *Buck v. Bell*, ruled eight to one that the state could sterilize a young woman named Carrie Buck against her will, and Oliver Wendell Holmes, as fine a liberal mind as the century produced, wrote the sentence that should haunt everyone who has ever reached for our phrase: "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." They thought they were curating the future. They thought the arc ran through them. Tens of thousands of Americans were sterilized on the strength of that certainty, and the certainty felt, from the inside, exactly like ours feels now. Righteous. Modern. Ahead.

Scripture does not measure faithfulness by which way the wind is blowing.

This is the hinge, so I will set it down without decoration. The biblical word for the future is not *progress*. It is *judgment*, and then *mercy*, and the two are never pried apart. The prophets did not read the trend lines. Amos did not poll the room. He stood in the wreckage of a prosperous, confident, upwardly mobile nation — a nation sure that the day of the Lord would be its vindication — and told them that day would be darkness and not light, that God hated their festivals and would not look at their offerings, that what God wanted was justice rolling down like water (Amos 5:18–24). By every available metric Amos was on the wrong side of history. The market disagreed with him. The court prophets disagreed with him. The future, as his contemporaries projected it, had no room for him in it. He was on the wrong side of history and the right side of God, and the entire prophetic tradition is an argument that the difference between those two is the difference between a religion and an idol.

The church learned this lesson once, at cost, and then forgot it, the way the church forgets everything. When Rome fell in 410 the pagans blamed the Christians: the old gods had been abandoned, and look, the eternal city had burned. Rome had been the right side of history for a thousand years. Augustine answered them with twenty-two books. *The City of God*, finished around 426, is the longest refusal in Christian literature of the idea that any earthly order — even the best one, even the one you were born into and love — carries the weight of ultimate hope. There are two cities, Augustine said, built by two loves: the earthly city by the love of self reaching even to contempt of God, the City of God by the love of God reaching even to contempt of self. They are tangled together in every nation and every age and every heart, and they will not be sorted out by an election or a century or a trend. Rome was not the kingdom. Rome was never the kingdom. And the Christian who wept for Rome as though the kingdom had fallen with it had confused his two cities.

We make the same confusion. We have only swapped the city.

There is even a name for the habit when historians fall into it. In 1931 Herbert Butterfield called it the Whig interpretation of history — the reflex of reading the past as one long approach march toward the enlightened present, sorting the dead into those who helped us arrive and those who got in the way. It is bad history, Butterfield argued, because it flatters the historian. It turns the past into a mirror in which the present admires itself. And what Butterfield diagnosed in the guild of historians, the church has now baptized in the language of faith. We have become Whigs of the soul. We read Scripture, we read the saints, we read the councils, and we quietly grade them all by how nearly they anticipated us. Reinhold Niebuhr saw where that pride was taking a confident America and named it, in 1952, *the irony of American history* — a nation so sure of its own innocence and its own forward motion that it could not see the evil it was capable of doing in the very act of doing good. Niebuhr was writing to a country. He could have been writing to a congregation.

I want to be exact about the temptation, because this is the place my own people reach for the eject button. We hear a critique of "the right side of history" and we assume it is cover for the right side of the culture war. It is not. The identical error lives on the right, dressed in different clothes. The Christian who insists that a vanished America was God's chosen order is making the same mistake in reverse: he has fused the kingdom to a moment in time, except his moment sits behind him instead of ahead. Nostalgia and progressivism are the same idolatry facing opposite directions. Both have decided that a season of human history *is* the will of God. Both will defend that season with a passion they no longer spend on the cross. The progressive worships a future that has not arrived; the reactionary worships a past that never was; and the God of Scripture stands outside them both, unimpressed by the calendar, asking the same question he asked in Eden. Where are you.

So this is not a partisan essay. It is the harder thing. It is written to the people I agree with, by a man who agrees with them, about the way our agreement has begun to carry a weight that belongs only to Christ.

Consider how the capture actually proceeds, because it never announces itself. It does not look like apostasy. It looks like relevance. You begin with a genuine conviction — that the poor are neglected, that the stranger is despised, that the church has been complicit in cruelties it still will not confess — and on all of this you are right, gloriously and biblically right. Micah 6:8 is not a suggestion. But somewhere in the defending of these convictions a substitution occurs, so quietly that you never sign the papers. The reason you hold the conviction shifts. You held it because the God who set his image in every person commands it. Now you hold it because it is what decent, forward-looking people believe, and you have grown afraid of being mistaken for the other kind. The conviction stays. Its root is severed. And a conviction cut from its root keeps its color for a while, the way a cut flower does, and then it begins, without your noticing, to die in the vase you were so proud to display it in.

I have watched it die in myself. I have caught myself weighing a sermon not by whether it was true but by whether it would age well — whether, ten years on, I would be embarrassed to have preached it. That is a real question and not a foolish one. But look at the judge I had quietly installed on the bench. The future. The applause of a generation not yet born. I had replaced the God before whom I will actually stand with a tribunal of imagined descendants whose approval I craved, and I had done it while feeling more righteous, not less. That is the signature of this particular sin. It does not arrive as compromise. It arrives as conscience.

Charles Taylor gave the air a name. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he calls it the immanent frame — a way of inhabiting the world in which all meaning is generated within history, with nothing breaking in from outside it. We did not choose the immanent frame. We were born into it the way a fish is born into water, and most of us will die without once having seen the water we swam in. Inside that frame, "the right side of history" is the only transcendence still permitted. It is the last respectable way to feel the pull of something larger than yourself. It lets you believe your sacrifices will be vindicated, that your enemies will be judged, that the dead will not have died for nothing — all the freight the resurrection used to carry — without the inconvenience of a God who might also judge you. That is the appeal. That is most of the appeal. It offers eschatology without accountability. It is heaven for people who have stopped believing in heaven.

But the resurrection is not the arc of history bending upward. The resurrection is a corpse standing up in a borrowed garden on a Sunday no trend predicted. It is the opposite of inevitability. The first witnesses were not riding the momentum of their age; their age had just executed their Lord. The right side of history, that particular weekend, was a smooth stone rolled across a tomb, a squad of imperial soldiers, and the settled judgment of every serious person in Jerusalem that the matter was closed. Then the matter was not closed. The future did not produce the resurrection. The resurrection produced the future — broke it open from the far side, against the grain of everything the present could see, and seeded a new creation in the middle of the old one while the old one was still congratulating itself on having won.

This is why Lesslie Newbigin, who spent forty years as a missionary in India and came home to a Britain that had quietly stopped believing, insisted the gospel is not the crown on the modern story of progress but a rival story altogether. The kingdom does not arrive as our achievement. It arrives as God's gift, and it arrives, again and again, through people the trend lines had already written off. N. T. Wright has spent a career saying the same thing in a different key: the new creation is not the upward extension of this age but God's interruption of it, and the resurrection of Jesus is the first hard fact of it. Which means the church was never promised the wind at its back. We were promised a cross to carry and a Spirit to carry it by, and these are not the same as the approval of the coming generation. Jesus said plainly that the world would hate his people (John 15:18–19), and he did not specify which wing of the world the hatred would fly in from, because he knew the world is not a political party. The world is an arrangement of human pride, and human pride has a left wing and a right wing, and they take turns flying.

What, then, do we do with this. Not retreat. I am not writing you toward retreat, and the retreat would itself be a verdict about which way history is going — a quiet bet that the wind has turned against us and we had better get indoors. No. We keep feeding the hungry. We keep standing with the stranger. We keep saying the hard, true things our churches have spent generations avoiding about race and money and power, because Scripture said them first and louder than we ever have. The content of the obedience does not change. What changes is the direction we are facing while we obey. We do these things before God and not before the future. We do them as obedience and not as positioning. And we hold them loosely enough that when the wind shifts — and it will shift, the wind always shifts — we will still be standing where the text put us, instead of where the breeze left us.

The test is simple, and it is brutal, and I have failed it more than once. Take the conviction you are proudest of. The one that makes you feel, when you hold it, like one of the good ones. Now imagine history turns against it. Imagine your grandchildren are taught that you were a fool, that the future you bet your name on lost, that the room you were so glad to be standing in emptied out and the door was locked behind you. Would you still hold it? If the only thing keeping the conviction alive is your confidence that tomorrow will agree with you, then it was never faith. It was forecasting. And forecasting is not a fruit of the Spirit.

Hebrews keeps a different ledger than the one we are all so anxious to be written into. The eleventh chapter runs through the heroes of faith and then says the thing we skip: that many of them did not receive what was promised, that they were stoned and sawn in two and wandered in deserts wearing skins, that the world was not worthy of them (Hebrews 11:36–38). By every metric available in their lifetimes they lost. They were on the wrong side of history. The God who keeps the real record called them faithful anyway, and the calling is the only verdict that will finally hold.

I do not know which way history is going. Neither do you. We have both pretended to, and we have both used the pretending to dodge the harder question — which is not where the world is headed but where we are standing, and before whom. The prophets did not bend with the arc. Most of them were broken on it, and called blessed afterward by a God who measures by a different rule than the one we keep checking our reflection in.

There is only one side worth being on. It was never the side of history. History is going to die — and the One who walked out of his own grave will still be standing in the wreckage, asking the only question that ever mattered, the one no trend can answer for you.
