Say the phrase out loud and feel what it does. *The right side of history.* It doesn't argue. It announces. It is the rare sentence that ends a debate instead of starting one, and that is precisely why it has become the most quoted line in American moral life — said from podiums and pulpits, printed on corporate statements the week the wind shifts, posted by the millions every time a consensus turns. It feels less like an opinion than like a weather report on the future. And it feels, when you are the one saying it, *wonderful.*

That is the first thing to admit, because I have felt it too. I have stood in a room certain that time itself was on my side, that to stand on the leading edge of the present was to stand near the heart of God. It is a very good feeling. Good feelings have always been the cheapest disguise sin puts on.

The claim is blunt, and the rest of this essay is me earning it. **"The right side of history" is not a neutral phrase. It is a piece of theology — a complete, working faith with its own god, its own salvation, and its own final judgment — and the American church, left and right, adopted it without ever deciding to.** Once you see the religion inside the phrase, you cannot unsee it. And you have to choose which faith you actually hold.

## The phrase has a noble birth — and that is the trap

Start by being fair to it, because the version worth refuting is the strong one.

The most famous mouth to say it was fighting actual slavery. Theodore Parker, the abolitionist preacher, told a Boston congregation in 1853 that he could not see far down the moral universe, that his sight reached only a little way, but that from what he could see he was sure it bends toward justice. A hundred years later Martin Luther King Jr. carried that sentence into Birmingham and made it the heartbeat of a movement. In their mouths it was not a slogan. It was a confession of providence. There was a *hand* in that arc. The arc bent because Someone was bending it.

That is the version everyone half-remembers. That is the trap. We kept Parker's confidence and quietly fired Parker's God.

Because the phrase did not stay in the pulpit. It passed through the nineteenth century and came out the other side stripped of the one thing that made it true. Hegel had already taught the educated West to read time itself as Spirit climbing toward freedom — history as a rational escalator, going up on its own. His *Phenomenology of Spirit* landed in 1807, and for two centuries his confidence has been the groundwater under thinkers who never read him. By the time the phrase reached us it meant something Parker never said: not that God bends the arc, but that the arc bends itself, automatically, and our only job is to guess its direction and lean.

We kept the arc. We threw away the hand that bends it.

## What the phrase actually worships

Strip a sentence down to what it promises and you find its religion.

"The right side of history" promises that your sacrifices will be vindicated. That your enemies will face a reckoning. That the dead who fought for the cause will not have died for nothing. That there is a verdict coming, and it will fall on the just side. Read that list again. That is not politics. **That is the freight the resurrection used to carry** — judgment, vindication, the moral arc of the cosmos resolving in favor of the good — repackaged for people who can no longer say the word *God* in public without flinching.

Charles Taylor gave the condition a name. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he calls it the immanent frame: a way of living in which all meaning is generated inside history, with nothing breaking in from outside it. We were born into that frame the way a fish is born into water, and inside it, "the right side of history" is the only transcendence still allowed at the table. It lets you feel the pull of something cosmic and vindicating — without a God who might also judge *you.* It is eschatology with the accountability surgically removed.

It is heaven for people who have stopped believing in heaven.

And here is the modern accelerant Parker never had to reckon with: the platforms. The phrase thrives now because the internet runs on it. The algorithm rewards the certainty, not the doubt. A consensus can form, harden, and reverse in a single news cycle, and the timeline rewards whoever called it first and loudest. We have built a machine that pays out in status every time you correctly predict which way the crowd will move — and then we baptized the machine and called the payout righteousness. The "right side of history" stopped being a long arc you trusted God to bend. It became a leaderboard you check hourly.

## The problem the phrase cannot survive

Here is the question that breaks it, and there is no good answer.

If history has a "right side," who told you which side it is — *before* history finished? You are claiming to read a verdict that has not been handed down. And the record of people who were certain they had read it correctly is the most damning record there is.

A century ago the people most sure they stood on the right side of history were not the villains of the story. They were the progressives. They were the educated, the scientific, the forward-looking — and a great many of them were eugenicists. This is the sentence my own tribe least wants to hear, so I will say it slowly. In 1927 the Supreme Court of the United States ruled, eight to one, that the state could sterilize a young woman named Carrie Buck against her will, and Oliver Wendell Holmes — as fine a progressive mind as the century produced — wrote the line that should haunt everyone who reaches for our phrase: *"Three generations of imbeciles are enough."* They believed they were curating the future. They believed the arc ran straight through them. Tens of thousands of Americans were sterilized on the strength of a certainty that felt, from the inside, exactly like ours feels now. Righteous. Modern. Ahead.

The future did not redeem them. The future buried them and called the next thing progress.

So did the architects of a hundred other "inevitable" futures. The Belgian administrators in the Congo in the 1890s knew they stood on the advancing edge of civilization. They had the science, the maps, and the serious opinion of their day. History was not their judge. History was their alibi, right up until it wasn't.

**"The right side of history" just means the winning side, and the winning side has included monsters in every century, each one convinced it was modern.**

## What Scripture means by "the future"

Now the harder turn, the one that costs me as much as anyone.

The biblical word for the future is not *progress.* It is *judgment*, and then *mercy*, and the two are never pried apart. The prophets did not read trend lines. Amos did not poll the room. He stood in a prosperous, confident, upwardly mobile nation — a nation sure the day of the Lord would be its vindication — and told them that day would be darkness, not light, that God hated their festivals and would not glance at their offerings, that what God wanted was justice rolling down like water (Amos 5:18–24). By every available metric Amos was on the wrong side of history. He was on the right side of God. The entire prophetic tradition is an argument that the gap between those two is the only gap that finally matters.

The church learned this once, at the cost of an empire, and then forgot it. When Rome fell in 410 the pagans blamed the Christians for abandoning the old gods. Augustine answered with twenty-two books. *The City of God*, finished around 426, is the longest refusal in Christian literature of the idea that any earthly order — even the best one, even the one you were born into and love — can carry the weight of ultimate hope. Two cities, Augustine said, built by two loves, tangled together in every nation and every heart, and not to be sorted out by an election or a century or a trend. Rome was never the kingdom. The Christian who wept for Rome as though the kingdom had fallen with it had simply confused his two cities.

We make the identical confusion. We have only swapped the city for a date on the calendar.

And the resurrection — the hinge of the whole Christian claim — is the precise opposite of "the right side of history." It is not the arc bending upward. It is a corpse standing up in a borrowed garden on a Sunday no trend predicted. The first witnesses were not riding the momentum of their moment; their moment had just executed their Lord. The right side of history, that weekend, was a sealed tomb, a squad of imperial soldiers, and the settled verdict of every serious person in Jerusalem that the matter was closed. Then the matter was not closed. **The future did not produce the resurrection. The resurrection produced the future** — broke it open from the far side, against the grain of everything the present could see. This is what Lesslie Newbigin spent forty years insisting to a Britain that had quietly stopped believing, and what N. T. Wright has spent a career arguing since: the kingdom does not arrive as our achievement riding the escalator up. It arrives as God's gift, and it keeps arriving through the people the trend lines had already written off.

## This is not a critique of the left. It is a critique of the church.

Brace for the move my readers always want me to make here — the pivot where I reveal this was secretly about the other side all along. I am not making it, and I am also not letting my own side off.

The identical idol lives on the right, dressed in different clothes. The Christian who insists a vanished America was God's ordained order has made exactly the same mistake in reverse: he has fused the kingdom to a moment in time, except his moment sits behind him instead of ahead. **Nostalgia and progressivism are the same idolatry facing opposite directions.** The progressive worships a future that has not arrived; the reactionary worships a past that never was; and both will defend their chosen moment with a devotion they no longer spend on the cross. The God of Scripture stands outside both, unimpressed by the calendar, asking the same question he asked in the garden. *Where are you.*

So this is the harder essay. It is written to the people I agree with, by a man who agrees with them, about the way our agreement started carrying a weight that belongs only to Christ.

Watch how the capture actually happens, because it never announces itself. It does not feel like apostasy. It feels like relevance. You begin with a real conviction — that the poor are neglected, the stranger despised, the church complicit in cruelties it still will not confess — and on all of it you are right, biblically and gloriously right. Micah 6:8 is not a suggestion. But somewhere in defending those convictions a substitution occurs, so quietly you never sign the papers. The *reason* shifts. You held the conviction because the God who set his image in every person commands it. Now you hold it because it is what decent, forward-looking people believe, and you have grown afraid of being mistaken for the other kind. The conviction stays. Its root is cut. And a conviction cut from its root keeps its color for a while, the way a cut flower does, and then it begins, without your noticing, to die in the very vase you were so proud to display it in.

The tribunal I had installed. I have caught myself weighing a sermon not by whether it was true but by whether it would age well — whether, ten years out, I would be embarrassed to have preached it. A real question. But look at the judge I had seated on the bench. The future. The applause of a generation not yet born. I had swapped the God before whom I will actually stand for a jury of imagined grandchildren whose approval I craved — and I had done it while feeling more righteous, not less. That is this sin's signature. It does not arrive as compromise. It arrives as conscience.

## The test, and the verdict

Here is the test. It is simple, and it is brutal, and I have failed it.

Take the conviction you are proudest of — the one that makes you feel, when you hold it, like one of the good ones. Now imagine history turns against it. Imagine your grandchildren are taught you were a fool, that the future you bet your name on lost, that the room you were so glad to stand in emptied and the door locked behind you. Would you still hold it?

If the only thing keeping the conviction alive is your confidence that tomorrow will agree with you, then it was never faith. **It was forecasting. And forecasting is not a fruit of the Spirit.**

Hebrews keeps a different ledger than the one we are all so anxious to be written into. Its eleventh chapter runs the roll of the faithful and then says the part we skip: that many of them did not receive what was promised, that they were stoned and sawn in two and wandered in deserts in animal skins, that the world was not worthy of them (Hebrews 11:36–38). By every metric available in their lifetimes, they lost. They were on the wrong side of history. The God who keeps the real record called them faithful, and that verdict is the only one that will finally hold.

I do not know which way history is going. Neither do you. We have both pretended to, and we have both used the pretending to dodge the only question that was ever ours to answer — not where the world is heading, but where we are standing, and before whom.

There is one side worth being on. It was never the side of history.

History is going to die. The One who walked out of his own grave will be standing in the wreckage when it does — asking the question no trend can answer for you.
