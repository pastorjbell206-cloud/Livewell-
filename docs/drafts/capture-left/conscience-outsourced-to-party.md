title: The Conscience We Outsourced to the Party
pillar: Capture by the Left

There is a question I can no longer answer about most of my own positions, and I suspect you cannot answer it about yours, and the fact that we cannot is the whole subject. The question is this: how do I know I believe this? Not whether it is true — that is a different and easier question. How did I come to hold it? Trace any of your convictions back far enough and you arrive at a fork, and at that fork you either reasoned your way to a position from something prior, or you received it, pre-formed, from a tribe whose approval you needed and whose disapproval you feared. Most of what I believe, when I am honest enough to trace it, I received. I did not think it. I caught it, the way you catch an accent, by living among people who already had it.

We talk constantly about the church captured by the political right — the pulpit that became a precinct, the cross fused to the flag. It is a real capture and I have written about it. But capture is not a partisan event. It is a human one. And the left's version is harder to see precisely because the left tells itself a story about being the side of independent thought, the side that questions, the side that is not a herd. The herd that believes it is not a herd is the most thoroughly herded of all, because it has disabled the one alarm that might have warned it.

Here is the mechanism, and I want to be technical for a moment because the precision matters. Jonathan Haidt, in *The Righteous Mind* (2012), assembled the research showing that moral reasoning is mostly post-hoc — that we arrive at our judgments by fast intuition and then recruit reasons afterward, like a press secretary defending a decision the boss already made. The intuitions themselves are tuned by the group. We are, Haidt argued, *groupish* — built to bind ourselves into moral teams and to experience the team's enemies as not merely wrong but unclean. This is not a flaw in a few weak minds. It is the standard architecture of every human mind, mine included, and the spiritual danger of it is that the binding feels exactly like conviction from the inside. The man who has outsourced his conscience to his tribe does not experience it as outsourcing. He experiences it as having finally seen the truth. The takeover is invisible to the one taken over. That is what makes it a capture and not a choice.

The party — and I mean the whole apparatus, the movement, the consensus of the people you cannot bear to disappoint — offers a service the human heart finds almost irresistible. It will tell you what to think about questions you have not studied. You will have a settled, fervent opinion about a policy you could not explain for ninety seconds, a foreign conflict you could not locate, a person you have never met, and the opinion will arrive with the emotional force of something you reasoned out at cost, when in fact you did nothing but absorb the ambient certainty of your people. This is a tremendous relief. Thinking is laborious and lonely; belonging is warm and instant. The party does your moral homework and hands it back with your name on it, and the gratitude you feel for being spared the work, you mistake for the strength of your convictions.

And then comes the part that should frighten anyone who follows a crucified Lord: the conscience that has been outsourced cannot be recalled when the party goes wrong. This is the lesson of the German church in the 1930s, the lesson Bonhoeffer's life was, the lesson the Barmen Declaration of 1934 tried to sound when it insisted that the church owes its allegiance to one Word of God and not to the *Führer* or the *Volk* or the spirit of the age. The pastors who had outsourced their conscience to the nation could not get it back when the nation demanded the unthinkable, because they had no conscience left that stood outside the nation to judge it. The faculty had atrophied from disuse. They were not, most of them, evil men. They were captured men, and a captured man cannot resist his captor, because resistance requires a self that is not owned, and he had signed his over.

I am not comparing any party to the Nazis. Hear that plainly, because it is the lazy reading and I refuse it. I am saying the mechanism is identical at every scale, that the same atrophy happens in a Michigan suburb as in a Berlin parish, just quieter, with lower stakes and the same lost faculty. The conscience you outsource for small things will not be available for large ones. The muscle you do not use does not come back when you need it. And the test is never the easy disagreement. The test is the moment your own side asks you to call evil good — to cheer something, excuse someone, hate a category of person, bury a truth that is inconvenient to the cause — and you have to ask whether you have any conscience left that can say no to your own people. Most of us have never tried. We have never once disappointed our tribe on something that mattered to it, and we have mistaken that perfect record of agreement for integrity, when it is the surest evidence that the capture is complete.

Daniel kept a window open toward Jerusalem and prayed three times a day toward a city that no longer existed, in defiance of a law signed by a king he served loyally in every lawful thing (Daniel 6:10). That is the picture I cannot get out of my head. He was not a rebel; he was the best administrator the empire had. But there was a window in him that opened toward an authority higher than the empire, and when the empire and the higher authority collided, the window decided. He had not outsourced the window. The whole drama of Daniel is that one man kept a conscience the kingdom could not reach, and the kingdom found out, and it cost him a night with the lions, and he counted it cheap. We have closed the window. We have bricked it over with the consensus of our people and hung a curtain of righteousness across the brick, and we pray now toward the party, three times a day, and call it faith.

So how do you tell whether you have a conscience or a subscription? I have found one test that does not lie, and it is the one I least want to take. Name the issue on which you disagree with your own side. Not a safe one — not a matter of tone or tactics where the disagreement still flatters you as the reasonable one in the room. A real one. A place where the people whose respect you need would be genuinely disappointed in you, where you would pay a social price, lose a little standing, get the look. If you cannot name one — if your conscience and your tribe's platform are a perfect, seamless overlay with no daylight anywhere between them — then you do not have a conscience on these matters. You have a membership. And a membership is not the same as a soul, though it is far more comfortable, and it never once keeps you up at night.

Paul told the Romans not to be conformed to the pattern of this world but to be transformed by the renewing of the mind (Romans 12:2), and we have read that verse a thousand times as a warning against obvious worldly sins, the vices on the old list. But conformity is rarely a vice you choose. It is a pattern you absorb, a shape the world presses you into while you sleep, and the renewing of the mind is the slow, lonely, deliberate work of recovering a conscience that answers to God before it answers to your people. It cannot be done in a crowd. The crowd is the thing it has to be done against. And it is the hardest work I know, because the world that presses us into its shape now is not the obvious world of the old sermons. It is the world of the people we love and admire and cannot bear to lose, which is the only world that has ever had the power to shape anyone.

I do not exempt myself from a word of this. I have caught myself reaching for an opinion I had not earned, defending a position because my people held it and the alternative would have cost me a table I wanted to keep my seat at. I have closed my own window and prayed toward the consensus. The frightening thing is how good it felt — how much it felt like conviction, like courage even, while it was the precise opposite.

Keep the window open. It is the loneliest discipline in the Christian life, and it will cost you a seat at some table you wanted, and the lions are not always a metaphor. But a conscience you can still hear over the cheering of your own crowd is the only one that will be there when the crowd is wrong. And the crowd is going to be wrong. Yours and mine. It always has been. That is why the window faces a different city.
