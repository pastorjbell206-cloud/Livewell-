There is a question I can no longer answer about most of my own positions, and I suspect you cannot answer it about yours, and the fact that neither of us can is the whole subject. The question is this. How do I know I believe this? Not whether it is true. That is a different and easier question. How did I come to hold it? Trace any of your convictions back far enough and you arrive at a fork, and at that fork you either reasoned your way to a position from something prior, or you received it, pre-formed, from a tribe whose approval you needed and whose disapproval you feared. Most of what I believe, when I am honest enough to trace it, I received. I did not think it. I caught it, the way you catch an accent, by living among people who already had it.

We talk constantly about the church captured by the political right. The pulpit that became a precinct. The cross fused to the flag, the sanctuary that learned to applaud a man before it would weep over a sin. It is a real capture and I have written about it and I will write about it again, because it is killing the witness of the American church in front of a watching world that already suspected we did not mean any of it. But capture is not a partisan event. It is a human one. And the left's version is harder to see precisely because the left tells itself a story about being the side of independent thought, the side that questions, the side that is not a herd. The herd that believes it is not a herd is the most thoroughly herded of all, because it has disabled the one alarm that might have warned it.

So this is not a piece about them. It is a piece about us, and the us is the harder word to say.

Let me be technical for a moment, because the precision is the mercy here. Jonathan Haidt, in *The Righteous Mind* (2012), assembled the research showing that moral reasoning is mostly post-hoc — that we arrive at our judgments by fast intuition and then recruit reasons afterward, like a press secretary defending a decision the boss already made. The intuitions themselves are tuned by the group. We are, Haidt argued, *groupish* — built to bind ourselves into moral teams and to experience the team's enemies as not merely wrong but unclean. This is not a flaw in a few weak minds. It is the standard architecture of every human mind, mine included, and the spiritual danger of it is that the binding feels exactly like conviction from the inside. The man who has outsourced his conscience to his tribe does not experience it as outsourcing. He experiences it as having finally seen clearly. The takeover is invisible to the one taken over. That is what makes it a capture and not a choice.

The party — and I mean the whole apparatus, the movement, the consensus of the people you cannot bear to disappoint — offers a service the human heart finds almost irresistible. It will tell you what to think about questions you have not studied. You will hold a settled, fervent opinion about a policy you could not explain for ninety seconds, a foreign conflict you could not locate on a map, a person you have never met and never will, and the opinion will arrive with the emotional force of something you reasoned out at cost, when in fact you did nothing but absorb the ambient certainty of your people. This is a tremendous relief. Thinking is laborious and lonely. Belonging is warm and instant. The party does your moral homework and hands it back with your name on it, and the gratitude you feel for being spared the work, you mistake for conviction.

There is an older name for what the party has become, and it is worth saying out loud. In 1967 the sociologist Robert Bellah published an essay called "Civil Religion in America," and he was not writing as a critic but as a diagnostician. He noticed that the nation had its own gods and its own scriptures, its martyrs and its high holy days, a whole apparatus of the sacred running underneath the formal religion of the churches and often louder than it. Bellah thought a civil religion could be a restraining good. But he saw the hazard plainly, and the hazard is the point: when the civil faith and the church faith fuse, the church no longer has anything to say to the nation that the nation did not already believe about itself. The sacred canopy becomes a mirror. And a mirror cannot rebuke you. It can only show you, in flattering light, the face you brought to it.

This is where the substitution happens, and it never announces itself. You begin with a genuine conviction. That the poor are neglected, that the stranger is despised, that the church has been complicit in cruelties it still will not name — and on much of this you are right, biblically and gloriously right, and the prophets were saying it before your party was born. But somewhere in the defending of these convictions the root gets swapped while the flower stays the same color. You held the conviction because the God who set his image in every person commands it. Now you hold it because it is what decent, forward-looking people believe, and you have grown afraid of being mistaken for the other kind. The conviction stays. Its root is severed. And a conviction cut from its root keeps its color for a while, the way a cut flower does, and then it begins, without your noticing, to die in the vase you were so proud to set it in. The faith was supposed to judge the politics. Now the politics tells the faith what it is allowed to feel.

And then comes the part that should frighten anyone who follows a crucified Lord. The conscience that has been outsourced cannot be recalled when the party goes wrong.

This is the lesson of the German church in the 1930s, and it is the lesson precisely because the men who failed it were not monsters. When the National Socialists came to power in 1933 a movement rose inside the Protestant church that called itself the *Deutsche Christen*, the German Christians, and they were not a fringe. They won church elections. They wanted a Christianity scrubbed of its Jewish roots and aligned with the blood and soil of the *Volk*, a faith that would baptize the new order and bless the *Führer* as God's gift to the nation. They believed they were being relevant. They believed they were reading the moment. Against them a remnant formed the Confessing Church, and in May of 1934, at Barmen, they issued a declaration, drafted largely by Karl Barth and signed by men including Dietrich Bonhoeffer's allies in the resistance, that said one thing with terrible clarity: Jesus Christ is the one Word of God whom the church must hear and obey in life and in death, and the church cannot and will not recognize any other lord, any other event or power or figure, as a source of its proclamation alongside this one Word. That sentence was not abstract theology. It was a refusal. It was a church drawing the line the *Deutsche Christen* would not draw, saying the state's ideology will not become our creed, the *Volk* is not our gospel, the *Führer* is not our Word.

The German Christians could not draw that line because they had nothing left to draw it with. They had handed their conscience to the nation years before, in a thousand small surrenders, and when the nation finally demanded the unthinkable they had no conscience standing outside the nation to judge it. The faculty had atrophied from disuse. They were not, most of them, evil men. They were captured men, and a captured man cannot resist his captor, because resistance requires a self that is not owned, and they had signed theirs over. Bonhoeffer's whole life was an argument against that surrender, and it ended on a scaffold at Flossenbürg in April of 1945, weeks before the war's end, because he had kept a conscience the Reich could not reach and the Reich found out.

I am not comparing any American party to the Nazis. Hear that plainly, because it is the lazy reading and I refuse it. I am saying the mechanism is identical at every scale, that the same atrophy happens in a Michigan suburb as in a Berlin parish, just quieter, with lower stakes and the same lost faculty. The conscience you outsource for small things will not be available for large ones. The muscle you do not use does not come back when you need it.

And here is where I have to turn the same knife on my own tribe's mirror image, because if I do not, this whole essay is a cheap shot wearing a clerical collar. The right made the identical error, and it made it in public, with a mailing list. In 1979 Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority, and the move was not, in its own telling, a surrender of the faith to a party. It was a rescue of the nation by the faith. But what actually happened over the decades that followed is that a platform learned to set the boundaries of the Christian conscience — that a voter guide came to function as a catechism, that a man's standing in the kingdom could be measured by his standing in a coalition, that the cross was asked to ratify a politics it was never consulted about. The German Christians fused the faith to the *Volk*. A great deal of American religion fused it to a party and a flag. The direction differs. The idolatry is identical. Both took a human arrangement — a movement, a nation, a coalition — and let it occupy the throne where Christ alone may sit. Barth would have recognized the right's version on sight, because it is the *Deutsche Christen* error in English. The left simply tells the same lie in a different accent, and prides itself on being too enlightened to lie at all.

So now to the harder room, the one where the prophets did their actual work.

Scripture is not neutral on the crowd. We have sentimentalized the word *community* until it means only warmth, but the Bible is wary of the crowd in a way we have edited out of our reading. Exodus 23:2 says it without decoration: do not follow the crowd into wrongdoing. The Hebrew is blunter than our translations — do not go after a multitude to do evil. It is a commandment, set down in the middle of the law about courts and justice, and it assumes the most ordinary fact about human beings, which is that we will do in a crowd what we would never do alone, and we will feel righteous doing it because everyone around us is doing it too. The crowd is not presented as a neutral container for conviction. It is presented as a pressure, a current, a thing that pulls, and the law has to command us to stand against it because standing against it is not natural. It is the opposite of natural. The herd is the default. The conscience is the exception, and it has to be commanded into being.

Paul says it again in the key of the new covenant. Do not be conformed to the pattern of this world, he writes to the Romans, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind (Romans 12:2). We have read that verse a thousand times as a warning against the obvious worldly sins, the vices on the old list. But conformity is rarely a vice you choose. The Greek is *syschematizo* — do not let yourself be pressed into the world's mold — and a mold does not ask permission. It is a shape the world presses you into while you sleep, and the renewing of the mind is the slow, lonely, deliberate work of recovering a conscience that answers to God before it answers to your people. It cannot be done in a crowd. The crowd is the thing it has to be done against. And the world that presses us into its shape now is not the obvious world of the old sermons, the world of liquor and dice. It is the world of the people we love and admire and cannot bear to lose, which is the only world that has ever had the power to shape anyone.

Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon, in *Resident Aliens* (1989), put their finger on what the church forgot when it became chaplain to whichever political order it preferred. The church, they argued, is not meant to be a department of the nation's moral life, a values-supplier for one party or the other. It is meant to be a colony — an outpost of a different country, with a different citizenship and a different Lord, living in the empire as resident aliens, loyal in every lawful thing and bought by no one. The moment the church becomes the religious wing of a political movement, left or right, it has stopped being a colony and become a constituency, and a constituency exists to be counted, not to be a conscience. It has nothing to say that its party did not already say. Lesslie Newbigin, who gave forty years to India as a missionary and came home to a Britain that had quietly stopped believing, said the same in another register: the gospel is not the chaplain of any culture's project but a rival announcement, a different account of what is finally real, and a church that loses its nerve to be different has nothing left to offer the culture but agreement. Agreement is not witness. Agreement is the sound a mirror makes.

This is why the early church could say the sentence that founded its whole posture toward power. When the authorities ordered the apostles to stop preaching, Peter answered: we must obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29). Not God in addition to men. Not God once we have checked with our coalition. God rather than men, when the two collide, because there is an authority that stands outside every human authority and judges it, and a church that has lost access to that outside authority has nothing left to obey but the room. The book of Revelation pressed the same blade against the same wound. The beast of Revelation 13 is not a cartoon. It is the political order divinized, the state that demands not merely your taxes and your obedience but your worship, the mark that says you may not buy or sell — you may not belong, you may not eat — unless you bow. John wrote it to a church being asked to put a pinch of incense on the emperor's altar and call it a formality. It was never a formality. It was a question about who owns your conscience, and the whole of Revelation is the refusal to answer the way the empire wanted.

Set against all of this is one man at a window.

Daniel kept a window open toward Jerusalem and prayed three times a day toward a city that no longer existed, in defiance of a law signed by a king he served loyally in every lawful thing (Daniel 6:10). That is the picture I cannot get out of my head. He was not a rebel. He was the best administrator the empire had, the man the king trusted with the realm, and there is no record of him refusing a single lawful thing the empire ever asked of him. But there was a window in him that opened toward an authority higher than the empire, and when the empire and the higher authority collided, the window decided. He had not outsourced the window. And before Daniel there were three men in chapter three who would not bow to the golden image when the whole assembly fell on its face at the sound of the music — who stood while everyone they knew was kneeling, who told the most powerful man on earth that their God was able to deliver them but that even if he did not, even then, they would not serve the king's gods or worship the image he had set up (Daniel 3:17–18). *Even if he does not.* That is the sentence the captured conscience can never say, because the captured conscience has already decided that belonging is survival, and these men had decided that belonging to God was survival and the furnace was just the weather.

We have closed the window. We have bricked it over with the consensus of our people and hung a curtain of righteousness across the brick, and we pray now toward the party, three times a day, and call it faith. We have heard the music and we have knelt with the assembly and we have told ourselves that kneeling is humility, when it was only fear wearing humility's coat.

So how do you tell whether you have a conscience or a subscription? I have found one test that does not lie, and it is the one I least want to take. Name the issue on which you disagree with your own side. Not a safe one — not a matter of tone or tactics where the disagreement still flatters you as the reasonable one in the room. A real one. A place where the people whose respect you need would be genuinely disappointed in you, where you would pay a price, lose a little standing, get the look. If you cannot name one — if your conscience and your tribe's platform are a perfect, seamless overlay with no daylight anywhere between them — then you do not have a conscience on these matters. You have a membership. And a membership is not the same as a soul, though it is far more comfortable, and it never once keeps you up at night.

I do not exempt myself from a word of this. I have caught myself reaching for an opinion I had not earned, defending a position because my people held it and the alternative would have cost me a table I wanted to keep my seat at. I have closed my own window and prayed toward the consensus and felt the warmth of the crowd at my back and named the warmth courage. The frightening thing is how good it felt, how much it felt like conviction, like bravery even, while it was the precise opposite — while it was the surrender itself, dressed as the resistance to surrender.

The test is never the easy disagreement. The test is the moment your own side asks you to call evil good — to cheer something, excuse someone, despise a category of person, bury a truth that is inconvenient to the cause — and you have to find out whether you have any conscience left that can say no to your own people. Most of us have never tried. We have never once disappointed our tribe on something that mattered to it, and we have mistaken that perfect record of agreement for integrity, when it is the surest evidence that the capture is complete. A man who has never disagreed with his own side does not know whether he can.

Keep the window open. It is the loneliest discipline in the Christian life, and it will cost you a seat at some table you wanted, and the lions are not always a metaphor. The men at Barmen lost their pulpits and some of them lost their lives. Daniel lost a night with the lions and counted it cheap. But a conscience you can still hear over the cheering of your own crowd is the only one that will be there when the crowd is wrong.

And the crowd is going to be wrong. Yours and mine. It always has been. That is the whole reason the window faces a different city.
