Ask yourself one question about your strongest political conviction, and notice how hard it is to answer honestly. Not *is it true.* That is the easy question, and it lets you off the hook. The hard question is *how did I come to hold it.* Trace it back far enough and you reach a fork. At that fork you either reasoned your way to the position from something prior, or you received it — pre-formed, ready to wear — from a tribe whose approval you needed and whose disapproval you feared. Be honest about which. Most of what I believe, when I trace it, I caught the way you catch an accent: by living among people who already had it.

That is the subject. Not whether your politics are right. Whether they are yours.

We talk constantly about the church captured by the political right. The pulpit that became a precinct. The cross fused to the flag, the sanctuary that learned to applaud a man before it would weep over a sin. That capture is real, I have named it before, and I will name it again, because it is killing the witness of the American church in front of a world that already suspected we never meant any of it. The other capture gets almost none of the attention. Put it on the table first.

**Capture is not a partisan event. It is a human one. And the left's version is more dangerous precisely because the left believes it is the side that thinks for itself.** The herd that is certain it is not a herd is the most thoroughly herded of all, because it has disabled the one alarm that might have warned it.

So this is not a piece about them. It is a piece about us. *Us* is the harder word.

## Your brain was built to outsource this

The precision matters here, because precision is where the mercy hides.

Jonathan Haidt, in *The Righteous Mind* (2012), gathered the research showing that moral reasoning runs mostly backward. We arrive at a judgment by fast intuition, then hire reasons after the fact — like a press secretary defending a decision the boss already made. And the intuitions are tuned by the group. We are, Haidt argued, *groupish*: built to bind ourselves into moral teams and to feel the team's enemies as not merely wrong but unclean.

This is not a defect in a few weak minds. It is the standard wiring of every mind, mine included. And the spiritual danger is exactly this: the binding feels, from the inside, identical to conviction. The man who has handed his conscience to his tribe does not experience it as handing anything over. He experiences it as having finally seen clearly.

**The takeover is invisible to the one taken over. That is what makes it a capture and not a choice.**

Consider what the party offers — and I mean the whole apparatus, the consensus of the people you cannot bear to disappoint. It offers to think for you about questions you have never studied. You will hold a fervent opinion about a policy you could not explain for ninety seconds, a foreign war you could not find on a map, a person you have never met. And the opinion arrives with the weight of something you earned at cost, when in fact you only absorbed the ambient certainty of your people. That is a tremendous relief. Thinking is slow and lonely. Belonging is warm and instant. The party does your moral homework and hands it back with your name on it, and the gratitude you feel for being spared the labor, you mistake for conviction.

## When the faith stops judging the politics

There is an older name for what the party has become.

In 1967 the sociologist Robert Bellah published "Civil Religion in America," not as a critic but as a diagnostician. He noticed the nation had its own gods and scriptures, its martyrs and high holy days, an entire apparatus of the sacred running underneath the religion of the churches and often louder than it. Bellah thought a civil religion could even be a restraining good. But he saw the hazard, and the hazard is the whole point: when the civil faith and the church faith fuse, the church has nothing left to say to the nation that the nation did not already believe about itself. The sacred canopy becomes a mirror. And a mirror cannot rebuke you. It can only show you, in flattering light, the face you brought to it.

Watch how the fusion happens, because it never announces itself. You begin with a real conviction — that the poor are neglected, the stranger despised, the church complicit in cruelties it still will not name — and on much of this you are right, biblically and gloriously right, and the prophets were saying it before your party was born. But somewhere in the defending of it, the root gets swapped while the flower keeps its color. You held the conviction because the God who set his image in every person commands it. Now you hold it because it is what decent, forward-looking people believe, and you have grown afraid of being mistaken for the other kind.

The conviction stays. Its root is cut. And a conviction cut from its root keeps its color for a while, the way a cut flower does, and then it dies, without your noticing, in the very vase you were so proud to set it in. **The faith was supposed to judge the politics. Now the politics tells the faith what it is allowed to feel.**

## The church that had nothing left to say no with

And then comes the part that should frighten anyone who follows a crucified Lord. A conscience you have outsourced cannot be recalled when the party goes wrong.

This is the lesson of the German church in the 1930s, and it is the lesson precisely because the men who failed it were not monsters. When the National Socialists took power in 1933, a movement rose inside the Protestant church calling itself the *Deutsche Christen*, the German Christians. They were not a fringe. They won church elections. They wanted a Christianity scrubbed of its Jewish roots and aligned with the blood and soil of the *Volk* — a faith that would bless the new order and crown the *Führer* as God's gift to the nation. They thought they were being relevant. They thought they were reading the moment.

Against them a remnant formed the Confessing Church, and in May 1934, at Barmen, they issued a declaration drafted largely by the theologian Karl Barth. It said one thing with terrible clarity: Jesus Christ is the one Word of God the church must hear and obey in life and in death, and the church will not recognize any other event, power, or figure as a second source of its proclamation alongside that one Word. That was not abstract theology. It was a refusal — a church drawing the line the German Christians could not draw. The state's ideology will not become our creed. The *Volk* is not our gospel. The *Führer* is not our Word. (Dietrich Bonhoeffer stood with the Confessing Church and helped shape its resistance, though he was not himself among the Barmen signatories; the line he kept cost him his life on a scaffold at Flossenbürg in April 1945, weeks before the war's end.)

The German Christians could not draw that line because they had nothing left to draw it with. They had handed their conscience to the nation years earlier, in a thousand small surrenders, and when the nation finally demanded the unthinkable there was no conscience standing outside the nation to judge it. The faculty had atrophied from disuse. They were not, most of them, evil men. They were captured men, and a captured man cannot resist his captor, because resistance requires a self that is not owned, and they had signed theirs away.

I am not comparing any American party to the Nazis. Hear that plainly, because it is the lazy reading and I refuse it. I am saying the mechanism is identical at every scale — that the same atrophy happens in a Michigan suburb as in a Berlin parish, only quieter, lower stakes, the same lost faculty. **The conscience you outsource for small things will not be there for the large ones. The muscle you never use does not come back when you need it.**

## The right made the identical mistake, with a mailing list

Now I have to turn the knife on my own tribe's mirror image, because if I do not, this whole essay is a cheap shot wearing a clerical collar.

The right made the same error, and it made it in public. In 1979 Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority, and in its own telling the move was not a surrender of the faith to a party. It was a rescue of the nation by the faith. But what actually happened across the decades that followed is that a platform learned to set the boundaries of the Christian conscience. A voter guide began to function as a catechism. A man's standing in the kingdom got measured by his standing in a coalition. The cross was asked to ratify a politics it was never consulted on.

The German Christians fused the faith to the *Volk*. A great deal of American religion fused it to a party and a flag. The direction differs. The idolatry is identical. Both took a human arrangement — a movement, a nation, a coalition — and seated it on the throne where Christ alone may sit. Barth would have recognized the right's version on sight, because it is the *Deutsche Christen* error in English.

The left simply tells the same lie in a different accent, and prides itself on being too enlightened to lie at all. That self-congratulation is not a defense against the capture. It is the capture's most effective cover, because a man who is certain he is immune never checks.

## Scripture is not sentimental about the crowd

We have sentimentalized the word *community* until it means only warmth. The Bible is warier of the crowd than we are, and we have edited the wariness out of our reading.

Exodus 23:2 says it without decoration: do not follow the crowd into wrongdoing. The Hebrew is blunter than our translations — do not go after a multitude to do evil. It is a commandment, set down in the middle of the law about courts and justice, and it assumes the most ordinary fact about us: that we will do in a crowd what we would never do alone, and feel righteous doing it because everyone around us is doing it too. The crowd is not a neutral container for conviction. It is a current, a pressure, a thing that pulls — and the law has to *command* us to stand against it, because standing against it is not natural. The herd is the default. The conscience is the exception, and it has to be commanded into being.

Paul says it again in the key of the new covenant. Do not be conformed to the pattern of this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind (Romans 12:2). We have read that a thousand times as a warning against the obvious vices. But conformity is rarely a vice you choose. The Greek is *syschematizo* — do not let yourself be pressed into the world's mold — and a mold does not ask permission. The renewing of the mind is the slow, lonely work of recovering a conscience that answers to God before it answers to your people, and it cannot be done in a crowd, because the crowd is the thing it has to be done against. The world that presses us into its shape now is not the world of the old sermons, of liquor and dice. It is the world of the people we love and admire and cannot bear to lose, which is the only world that has ever had the power to shape anyone.

Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon, in *Resident Aliens* (1989), named what the church forgot when it became chaplain to whichever order it preferred. The church is not a department of the nation's moral life, a values-supplier for one party or the other. It is a colony — an outpost of a different country, with a different citizenship and a different Lord, living in the empire as resident aliens, loyal in every lawful thing and bought by no one. The moment the church becomes the religious wing of a political movement, left or right, it stops being a colony and becomes a constituency. And a constituency exists to be counted, not to be a conscience. Lesslie Newbigin, who gave forty years to India as a missionary and came home to a Britain that had quietly stopped believing, said the same in another register: the gospel is not the chaplain of any culture's project but a rival announcement, a different account of what is finally real. A church that loses its nerve to be different has nothing left to offer the culture but agreement. Agreement is not witness. Agreement is the sound a mirror makes.

This is why the early church could say the sentence that founded its whole posture toward power. Ordered to stop preaching, Peter answered: we must obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29). Not God in addition to men. Not God once we have checked with the coalition. God *rather than* men, when the two collide — because there is an authority that stands outside every human authority and judges it, and a church that has lost access to it has nothing left to obey but the room. The book of Revelation pressed the same blade against the same wound. The beast of Revelation 13 is no cartoon. It is the political order divinized — the state demanding not merely your taxes and obedience but your worship, the mark that says you may not buy or sell, may not belong, may not eat, unless you bow. John wrote it to a church being asked to drop a pinch of incense on the emperor's altar and call it a formality. It was never a formality. It was a question about who owns your conscience.

## One man at a window

Set against all of it is one man at a window.

Daniel kept a window open toward Jerusalem and prayed three times a day toward a city that no longer existed, in defiance of a law signed by a king he served loyally in every lawful thing (Daniel 6:10). He was not a rebel. He was the best administrator the empire had, the man the king trusted with the realm, and there is no record of him refusing a single lawful thing the empire ever asked. But there was a window in him that opened toward an authority higher than the empire, and when the empire and that authority collided, the window decided. He had not outsourced the window.

And before Daniel there were three men in chapter three who would not bow to the golden image when the whole assembly fell on its face at the sound of the music — who stood while everyone they knew knelt, who told the most powerful man on earth that their God was able to deliver them, but that even if he did not, even then, they would not serve the king's gods or worship the image (Daniel 3:17–18). *Even if he does not.* That is the sentence the captured conscience can never say, because the captured conscience has already decided belonging is survival. These men had decided belonging to God was survival, and the furnace was only the weather.

We have closed the window. We have bricked it over with the consensus of our people, hung a curtain of righteousness across the brick, and we pray now toward the party, three times a day, and call it faith. We have heard the music and knelt with the assembly and told ourselves that kneeling is humility, when it was only fear wearing humility's coat.

So how do you tell whether you have a conscience or a subscription? I have found one test that does not lie, and it is the one I least want to take. Name the issue on which you disagree with your own side. Not a safe one — not a matter of tone or tactics where the disagreement still flatters you as the reasonable one in the room. A real one. A place where the people whose respect you need would be genuinely disappointed in you, where you would pay a price, lose a little standing, get the look. If you cannot name one — if your conscience and your tribe's platform are a perfect, seamless overlay with no daylight anywhere between them — then you do not have a conscience on these matters. You have a membership. And a membership is not the same as a soul, though it is far more comfortable, and it never once keeps you up at night.

I do not exempt myself from a word of this. I have caught myself reaching for an opinion I had not earned, defending a position because my people held it and the alternative would have cost me a table I wanted to keep my seat at. I have closed my own window and prayed toward the consensus and felt the warmth of the crowd at my back and named the warmth courage. The frightening thing is how good it felt — how much it felt like conviction, like bravery even, while it was the precise opposite. While it was the surrender itself, dressed as resistance to surrender.

The test is never the easy disagreement. The test is the moment your own side asks you to call evil good — to cheer something, excuse someone, despise a category of person, bury a truth that is inconvenient to the cause — and you have to find out whether you have any conscience left that can say no to your own people. Most of us have never tried. We have never once disappointed our tribe on a thing that mattered to it, and we have mistaken that perfect record of agreement for integrity, when it is the surest evidence the capture is complete.

Keep the window open. It is the loneliest discipline in the Christian life, and it will cost you a seat at some table you wanted, and the lions are not always a metaphor. The men at Barmen lost their pulpits and some of them lost their lives. Daniel lost a night with the lions and counted it cheap.

And the crowd is going to be wrong. Yours and mine. It always has been. That is the whole reason the window faces a different city.
