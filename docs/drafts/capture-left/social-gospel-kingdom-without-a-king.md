Walk into certain old downtown churches on a weekday and you will find the building busier than it is on Sunday. There's a food pantry in the basement, a recovery group in the parlor, a tenants' association meeting in the fellowship hall and a banner on the lawn announcing the congregation's stand on the question of the season. The work is real. People are fed who would not otherwise be fed. And yet a visitor who stays through Sunday morning sometimes notices something harder to name. The word "kingdom" is everywhere in the sermon and the prayers, but the King appears mostly as an example, a teacher whose values the congregation shares. He does not seem to be ruling anything. He seems to be endorsing.

The easy response, and the one many evangelicals reach for, is contempt: that's what happens when a church trades the gospel for politics. The contempt is cheap, and it's also bad history, because the movement behind that downtown church began as one of the most serious recoveries of biblical faith in American history. The man who launched it saw something the evangelical churches had lost and would spend half a century refusing to see. If his heirs drifted, the drift was not from nothing into nothing. It was from a true kingdom into a kingdom that had quietly lost its King, and the mechanism of that drift is worth understanding precisely because it is not only a liberal temptation.

The claim of this essay is that Walter Rauschenbusch was right about the scope of the kingdom of God and that his movement lost its grip on the source of it. He saw, against a privatized piety, that God's reign claims wages and housing and the conditions of a child's life. His heirs, within a generation, came to treat that reign as a project human beings build rather than a gift the King brings. The way back is not to shrink his kingdom to fit inside a soul. It is to set his kingdom on the foundation he himself never fully abandoned and his followers let go.

## Hell's Kitchen, 1886

Rauschenbusch was born in Rochester, New York, in 1861, the son of a German Baptist seminary professor, and raised in a devout, conversionist piety. In 1886 he took the pulpit of the Second German Baptist Church on West 45th Street in New York, at the edge of the neighborhood called Hell's Kitchen. He served there eleven years. The congregation was poor and immigrant, and the conditions were those of industrial New York at its worst: crowded tenements, men thrown out of work by every downturn, disease that moved through the buildings faster than any pastor could visit. He later recalled that what stayed with him most from those years was the funerals of small children, and the question that would not leave him afterward was why they had to die. In 1888 an illness left him almost entirely deaf. He kept preaching.

What changed in him was not his faith but his sense of its reach. He had been taught that the gospel's business was the rescue of individual souls, and he still believed souls needed rescuing. But he could not square a gospel that saved a man's soul on Sunday with a silence about the tenement and the wage that were killing his children on Monday. He read the prophets again and found that they were not chaplains to the prosperous. Amos had cried, "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24, ESV), and he had cried it against a people who kept crowded festivals while they sold the needy "for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6, ESV). Jeremiah had said of King Josiah, "He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 22:16, ESV). Knowing God and doing justice for the poor were, in that sentence, the same act seen from two sides.

He read the Gospels again too, and found at their center the phrase the church he grew up in rarely preached. "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ESV). Jesus did not announce a way for souls to escape the world. He announced that God was acting as King within it, and he taught his disciples to pray, "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10, ESV). In 1892 Rauschenbusch and a few friends formed the Brotherhood of the Kingdom to pursue that prayer. In 1897 he left the pastorate to teach at Rochester Theological Seminary, and in 1907 he published *Christianity and the Social Crisis*, which surprised everyone, its author included, by becoming a national bestseller.

Its argument was that the church had lost the social meaning of the kingdom. The prophets had spoken to the nation's order, Jesus had announced God's reign over the whole of life, and the church had shrunk that message into private salvation and future bliss while the industrial economy ground people down. In *Christianizing the Social Order* (1912) he pressed further, and in *A Theology for the Social Gospel* (1917), delivered as lectures at Yale the year before his death, he tried to give the movement a doctrine. The book opens with a sentence that states its whole ambition: "We have a social gospel. We need a systematic theology large enough to match it and vital enough to back it."

## The strongest case for Rauschenbusch

Before saying where the social gospel went wrong, it has to be said plainly where it was right, because the evangelical reader tends to skip this part and the skeptic has noticed.

First, Rauschenbusch did not abandon sin. The caricature of the social gospel is a movement that thought people were basically good and needed only better conditions. That describes many of his successors. It does not describe him. *A Theology for the Social Gospel* has a long treatment of what he called the kingdom of evil: sin that is passed from one generation to the next not only through individual hearts but through institutions, customs and interests that outlive the people who serve them. He saw that a man could be personally kind and still profit from and defend an arrangement that crushed his neighbors, and that the arrangement would teach his children to do the same. He called these forces superpersonal. The church had a theology for the sins of the drunkard and the adulterer and almost none for the sins of the landlord and the monopolist, and he meant to supply one.

Second, he did not abandon the cross. In the same book he asked who killed Jesus, and answered that no single villain did. Jesus was crucified by a combination of ordinary public sins: religious bigotry, graft joined to political power, the corruption of justice, the mob spirit, militarism and class contempt. Those same sins, he argued, are alive in every society, and whoever shares in them stands, in some measure, at the foot of that cross. That is not a full doctrine of the atonement, and it is not the whole of what the New Testament says the cross achieved. But it is not a moral example floating free of history either. It is an attempt to say that the sins which crucified Christ are the sins of every social order, including ours.

Third, and here the evangelical has to hear it, he was right about what the evangelical churches were doing. In the decades after his death, American fundamentalism retreated from social concern almost entirely. The sociologist David Moberg described that retreat in *The Great Reversal* (1972): a movement whose nineteenth-century grandparents had fought for abolition and temperance and poor relief now treated care for the poor as a liberal distraction from saving souls. It was an evangelical, not a liberal, who first said so in public. In *The Uneasy Conscience of Modern Fundamentalism* (1947), Carl F. H. Henry told his own movement that it had abandoned the social dimension of the gospel and left the field to the liberals, and that a fundamentalism with nothing to say about war, race and labor had misunderstood the kingdom Jesus preached. Henry did not become a social gospeler. He wanted the same breadth on a different foundation. But he conceded the charge.

And the fruit was real. When a young Martin Luther King Jr. was a seminary student at Crozer, he read *Christianity and the Social Crisis*. In *Stride Toward Freedom* (1958) he wrote that the book left a lasting mark on his thinking and gave him a theological basis for the social concern he already felt. He also wrote that Rauschenbusch had fallen into the era's faith in inevitable progress and had come close to identifying the kingdom of God with a particular social and economic system. Both halves of King's judgment matter. The movement gave him something true. It also carried something false, and he could see it.

## How a kingdom loses its King

The false thing entered through the door of optimism. Rauschenbusch wrote in the last golden hour of Victorian confidence, when educated Protestants on both sides of the Atlantic believed history was moving, by God's providence and human effort together, toward a better world. The Dutch theologian W. A. Visser 't Hooft, later the first general secretary of the World Council of Churches, wrote his doctoral dissertation on the movement, published in 1928 as *The Background of the Social Gospel in America*, and he traced how much of its hope had been borrowed from the American faith in progress rather than drawn from the New Testament. *Christianizing the Social Order* shows the borrowing plainly. Rauschenbusch argued there that the family, the church, education and political life had already been substantially christianized, and that the economic order was the great remaining territory still to be won. It is a remarkable sentence to read with the twentieth century behind us. The book appeared two years before the guns of August.

Once the kingdom is conceived as a society growing steadily toward God's will, three shifts follow almost by gravity, and each of them appears in the movement's second generation.

The first is a shift in who brings the kingdom. In the Gospels the kingdom is God's act. It is given, received and entered. "Fear not, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom" (Luke 12:32, ESV). "Whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child shall not enter it" (Mark 10:15, ESV). Daniel's vision of the kingdom that ends the empires is a stone "cut out by no human hand" (Daniel 2:34, ESV). Rauschenbusch still believed God was at work in history, but his language kept sliding toward the kingdom as a task, something to be built, extended and achieved. His heirs let go of the qualifications. By the 1920s, in some of the modernist theology taught at places like the University of Chicago Divinity School, the kingdom of God had drawn very close to a Christianized democracy, and the work of building it could be done by anyone of goodwill, with or without the King.

The second is a shift in who Jesus is. If the kingdom is humanity organized according to the will of God, and human beings are the ones organizing it, then Jesus becomes its founder and its finest example: the one who first saw the vision clearly and lived it most fully. He remains honored. But an honored founder is not a reigning Lord. The resurrection becomes less necessary, because a founder's ideals can live on without his body. The ascension becomes an embarrassment, because a King at the right hand of the Father implies that the kingdom has a throne somewhere other than our committees. And the return of Christ in judgment, which the creed of 381 confesses in the same breath as his endless kingdom, drops out entirely, because a kingdom that grows by our effort does not need anyone to come and finish it.

The third is a shift in what sin is. Rauschenbusch had held personal and social sin together, however unevenly. His successors increasingly located sin in structures and ignorance, which meant it could be cured by reform and education. Reinhold Niebuhr, who had been formed by the social gospel and pastored auto workers in Detroit, turned on its optimism in *Moral Man and Immoral Society* (1932), arguing that collective self-interest is far more stubborn than the reformers imagined and that the reformer's own group is not exempt from it. His brother's verdict five years later has become the movement's epitaph, and it is worth reading slowly because every clause names one of those shifts. In *The Kingdom of God in America* (1937), H. Richard Niebuhr described the liberal Protestantism the social gospel had fed into: "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross."

Niebuhr's book tells a longer story than that sentence, and the story is the diagnosis. He traced the idea of the kingdom of God through three American movements. The Puritans held to the sovereignty of God. The awakenings recovered the reign of Christ in the converted heart. The social gospel reached for the coming kingdom, God's reign extending over society. Each idea, he argued, was true, and each needed the other two. The coming kingdom cut loose from God's sovereignty and Christ's present reign was no longer the kingdom of God at all. It was the idea of progress wearing the church's vocabulary. That is the kingdom without a King. It keeps the word "kingdom," the passion for justice and even much of the language of Jesus. What it loses is anyone who reigns. (What Jesus himself meant by the kingdom, and the century of argument over it, is traced more fully in [What Is the Kingdom of God?](/writing/what-is-the-kingdom-of-god-and-why-it-changes-everything); this essay stays with one movement's story.)

## The same instrument, turned both ways

It would be convenient to stop there and let the evangelical reader enjoy the verdict. That would be dishonest, because the mechanism that emptied the social gospel is not a liberal mechanism. It is a human one, and the American right has run it with at least as much enthusiasm.

The social gospel's heirs identified the kingdom with a Christianized democracy of the progressive kind. A generation later, another wing of American Protestantism identified it with a Christian America of the conservative kind. The content differs. The grammar is identical. In both cases the kingdom becomes a social order we can recognize, measure and vote for, and Christ becomes the patron of a program his followers already knew they wanted. In both cases the King's actual demands, when they cut against the program, are quietly set aside. The progressive version tends to set aside personal holiness, repentance and judgment. The conservative version tends to set aside the Sermon on the Mount, love of enemies and the warnings to the rich. Each accuses the other of politicizing the gospel. Each is right about the other.

There is an older name for this, and it's idolatry. The prophets did not usually accuse Israel of worshiping nothing. They accused Israel of worshiping something it could manage. An idol is a god whose will happens to coincide with ours and who can be carried where we are going. A kingdom without a King is precisely that sort of god. It asks for effort, sometimes heroic effort, and the effort is often admirable. It never asks for surrender, because there is no one there to surrender to.

No one who leads a church stands outside this. A pastor who preaches the kingdom week after week lives with a particular temptation: to measure the kingdom's arrival by things he can count and report, by programs launched and causes advanced and people served. Those things matter. But they can quietly become the kingdom itself in a pastor's imagination, and the moment they do, prayer becomes a formality before the real work and the King becomes the name at the top of the letterhead. The downtown church with the banner on the lawn and the suburban church with the flag in the sanctuary are closer relatives than either would like, and most of us who lead churches have a foot in one or the other.

The distinction that holds all of this together is between the kingdom's scope and its source. Rauschenbusch was right about the scope. God's reign claims the tenement, the wage, the courtroom and the child's lungs, and a gospel that has nothing to say about them has shrunk the kingdom to fit inside a private life. His heirs lost the source. The kingdom comes from the King, by his act, on his terms and in his time, and a movement that treats it as our construction project will eventually build something that looks like us. The fundamentalist retreat kept the source and shrank the scope. The social gospel's drift kept the scope and lost the source. American Christianity has spent a century choosing one error to avoid the other.

## The kingdom he was right to want

The way back is not to be found by splitting the difference. It lies in recovering the older foundation under the kingdom Rauschenbusch wanted, and that foundation is older than either the social gospel or its fundamentalist opponents.

Begin with the prayer he loved. "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" is a petition, not a plan. It is addressed to someone. It asks God to do what we cannot do, and it binds the one who prays to obey in the meantime. That double shape is the whole matter. The kingdom is on earth, so the Christian who prays for it cannot be indifferent to bread, justice and the conditions of the poor. The kingdom comes from heaven, so the Christian who works for it cannot mistake his work for its arrival. "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain" (Psalm 127:1, ESV). The psalmist does not tell builders to put down their tools. He tells them whose house it is.

Then recover the King's own manner of reigning. Jesus told his disciples that the rulers of the nations lord it over their subjects, "but it shall not be so among you," because "even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:43-45, ESV). The kingdom does not advance by seizing the machinery of power, whether that machinery is a legislature or a denomination's social statements. It advances the way its King did, by service and suffering and a death that God vindicated. The inscription over the cross, "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" (John 19:19, ESV), was meant as mockery. It was also the most accurate political statement anyone made that week. This is how this King reigns, and a kingdom that forgets it will reach for the tools of every other kingdom.

Other Christians have held the scope and the source together, and their example shows it can be done. In 1880, at the founding of the Free University of Amsterdam, the Dutch theologian and statesman Abraham Kuyper said, in words that have been quoted ever since, that there is not a single square inch of human existence over which Christ, sovereign over all, does not claim ownership. That is Rauschenbusch's scope, stated by a Calvinist who never doubted Christ's deity, resurrection or return. Lesslie Newbigin, a missionary bishop who spent decades in India before returning to a Britain he found harder to reach, described the church in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) as the sign, instrument and foretaste of the kingdom. A sign points beyond itself. An instrument is used by another hand. A foretaste is a first bite of a meal someone else is preparing. None of the three words lets the church imagine that the kingdom is its achievement, and all three require it to live now as though the King were already reigning.

What would that look like on an ordinary weekday? It would look a great deal like the busy downtown church, with one difference that changes everything. The food pantry would still be open, and the people who staffed it would understand themselves as servants of a King who fed five thousand and who will one day spread a table for the nations. The advocacy for the poor would continue, and it would be carried out with the humility of people who know their own side is also under judgment, and who can therefore tell the truth about their allies as readily as about their opponents. The worship on Sunday would not be a pep rally for the week's causes. It would be the place where the congregation confesses that the kingdom is not theirs, receives it again at the Lord's Table and prays for its coming. And the preaching would dare to name the King's demands that the congregation's politics finds inconvenient, because a King who never contradicts you is not reigning over you.

The same pattern would reform the church on the right. Its members would keep their conviction that the kingdom comes from God and not from government, and they would lose their excuse for indifference to the poor, because the King who brings the kingdom described his own mission in Isaiah's words: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor" (Luke 4:18, ESV). A church that has the source without the scope has confessed the King and ignored his commands. A church that has the scope without the source has obeyed a list of commands and forgotten who gave them. Both are called to the same repentance.

Rauschenbusch died in July 1918, with his country at war against the land of his parents, his health broken and his confident hopes for the age badly shaken. His biographer Christopher Evans titled his life with a phrase of Rauschenbusch's, *The Kingdom Is Always but Coming* (2004). It is a truer sentence than much of what his movement said afterward. The kingdom is always coming because it is never ours to finish. It will arrive in its fullness only when the One who announced it returns to complete it, and the New Testament's last word on the matter is not an achievement but an announcement: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (Revelation 11:15, ESV).

That sentence judges the social gospel's heirs, who forgot the King, and it judges the fundamentalists who forgot the world, and it judges every pastor who has confused his program with the reign of God. It also gives back what each side lost. The world is not abandoned; it becomes his kingdom. The kingdom is not our construction; it becomes his. Rauschenbusch stood beside the small coffins in Hell's Kitchen and knew that God could not be indifferent to them, and he was right. What he could not finally supply, and what his heirs stopped looking for, is a King who had already gone down into death himself and come back out of it, and who will one day wipe the tears from those mothers' faces with his own hand. We keep the pantry open in the meantime. We keep it open because he is coming, and not in his place.
