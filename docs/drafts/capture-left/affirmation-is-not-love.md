title: Affirmation Is Not Love

My third son came to me at fourteen, certain about something that was going to hurt him. I will not put the particulars on a page; they are his and not mine to spend. What I remember is the fork in the half-second before I answered, and how clearly I saw both roads. One road was to tell him what he wanted to hear, to mirror his certainty back to him, to call that mirroring love. The other was to love him enough to be, for a moment, the one person in his life who would not simply agree. I have taken the first road more times than I will admit, because the first road is paved with his smile and the second road is paved with his anger, and I am a man who would rather be smiled at. That is the confession this essay is built on. I have called my cowardice love because the cowardice felt warm.

We have built a whole spirituality on that warmth.

The word doing the work is *affirmation*, and it has slid, quietly, into the seat where *love* used to sit. To affirm a person now means to confirm them — to ratify whatever they have concluded about themselves and to treat any hesitation as a kind of violence. Love, in this arrangement, is agreement. To love you is to say yes to you. To question you is to harm you. And because no decent person wants to harm anyone, and because the people I run with are decent to the bone, we have quietly agreed to a love that has lost the power to say anything but yes.

But yes is not the most loving word in the language. It is only the easiest.

Philip Rieff named where this came from before most of us were born. In *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966) he described a culture shifting its highest value from salvation to well-being — from being made righteous to feeling all right — and he predicted that under such a culture the role of the priest would slowly be replaced by the role of the therapist, whose job is not to tell you the truth about yourself but to help you feel at peace with the self you have. Rieff was not against therapy; neither am I, and I have sat in the chair and been helped. He was warning about what happens when the therapeutic stops being a tool and becomes a theology — when feeling all right becomes the whole of the good, and any word that disturbs my peace, even a true one, gets reclassified as harm.

Charles Taylor gave it the deeper name. He called it the ethic of authenticity: the modern conviction that each of us has an original way of being human that we discover inside ourselves, and that the highest moral task is to be true to it, and that anyone who calls it into question is committing the one unforgivable sin — they are denying you the right to be yourself. Inside that ethic, the most loving thing a person can do is affirm, because the self is sovereign and the self has already spoken, and who am I to legislate over your soul. It is a powerful ethic. It is also, when you set it beside the gospel, almost exactly upside down.

Because the gospel does not affirm the self. It loves the self enough to tell it the truth, and the first truth it tells is that the self, left to its own discovery, will get lost. *There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way of death* (Proverbs 14:12). That verse is offensive to the ethic of authenticity in every word, because it says the inner certainty I am most sure of — the way that *seems right* — can be the very thing killing me, and that I am not equipped to know the difference from the inside. We are not, any of us, reliable narrators of our own lives. The drunk is certain he can drive. The bitter man is certain he is owed. I am certain, most mornings, that my motives are cleaner than they are. A love that only ever affirms my self-perception is a love that has agreed to lie to me whenever I am most deceived, which is precisely when I most need it not to.

Look at how Jesus loved, because he is the standard and not our instincts. He loved the rich young ruler — Mark says so explicitly, *Jesus looked at him and loved him* — and the very next thing the love did was tell him the one thing he did not want to hear, that he had to sell everything, and the man went away grieving (Mark 10:21–22). The love and the hard word were the same act. They were not in tension. Jesus did not affirm the man into the kingdom; he told him a truth costly enough that the man left, and Jesus let him leave, because love that will not let you leave is not love, it is control, and love that will only let you stay if you stay unchallenged is not love either, it is flattery. The Samaritan woman at the well met a man who told her everything she had ever done (John 4:29), and she ran to town not crying *he affirmed me* but *come see a man who told me the truth* — and that was the joy. To be fully known and not condemned. Not affirmed. Known. They are not the same, and we have lost the distinction.

I have to turn this back on the church I am defending myself against, because the other side of the room has its own counterfeit and it is uglier than ours. There is a Christianity that has confused truth-telling with cruelty, that wields the hard word the way a man wields a hammer he enjoys, that mistakes the wreckage it leaves for faithfulness because someone got hurt. That is not what I am arguing for, and I want to slam that door before anyone walks through it carrying a quote of mine. The opposite of affirmation is not condemnation. Truth without love is not the truth about anything; it is just aggression with a verse stapled to it, and Paul said a man with all knowledge and no love is a clanging gong, a noise (1 Corinthians 13:1–2). The cruel literalist and the cowardly affirmer have made the identical error from opposite ends: both have torn truth and love apart, and they are not meant to come apart, they were one thing in Jesus and they are meant to be one thing in us.

What I am after is the harder, narrower road. *Speaking the truth in love*, Paul calls it (Ephesians 4:15), and the genius of the phrase is that it refuses to let me keep either word without the other. I do not get to be a truth-teller who skips the love; that is the literalist's lie. I do not get to be a lover who skips the truth; that is mine. The two have to arrive together or neither has arrived, and most of us are good at one and use it as an excuse to neglect the other. I am good at love. I use it to excuse my silence. I tell myself I am being gentle when I am being afraid.

The deepest argument against affirmation-as-love is the cross, and I will end there. If affirmation were love — if the most loving thing God could do were to confirm us as we are and call our self-discovery good — then the cross is an atrocity, a needless brutality, a Father killing his Son for no reason, because we did not need fixing, only celebrating. The cross only makes sense if there was something in us so wrong that it could not be affirmed, only redeemed; something that love could not ratify, only die for. *God demonstrates his love for us in this: while we were still sinners, Christ died for us* (Romans 5:8). Not: while we were already fine. While we were still sinners. The love met us exactly where the affirmation would have lied.

My son is grown now. The conversation went the way I described — I took, that day, the harder road, though I have not always — and I would like to tell you it resolved into something tidy. It did not. He was angry for a long time. But years later he said a thing I keep folded in my chest: that I was the one who did not just tell him what he wanted to hear, and that somewhere in the anger he had filed that away as evidence that I meant it.

That is the difference. Affirmation tells you what you want to hear and calls it love. Love tells you the truth and is willing to be hated for it, and waits, and is sometimes — not always, but sometimes — believed in the end. I would rather be believed in the end than smiled at tonight. Most days I would rather be smiled at tonight. That is the war, and I do not always win it, and neither do you, and the gospel knew that about both of us before we said a word.
