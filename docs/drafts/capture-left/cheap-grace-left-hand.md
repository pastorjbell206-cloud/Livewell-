Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote the words in 1937, in a Germany where the church had mostly made its peace with a monster, and the words have never stopped accusing the people who quote them. *Cheap grace is the deadly enemy of our church.* He wrote it in *The Cost of Discipleship*, and he defined it with a precision that still cuts: cheap grace is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ living and incarnate. It is the preaching of forgiveness without requiring repentance. It is, he said, grace we bestow on ourselves. Grace as a principle, a system, a doctrine you can hold in your hand instead of a Person who holds you. Grace that costs the recipient nothing because it cost the giver, in our telling, nothing either — though it cost God his Son.

We have done a strange thing with that warning. We have decided it belongs to the right.

And it does belong to the right; let me grant the obvious before I take it away. There is a recognizable American cheap grace that lives most comfortably on the conservative side of the room — the altar call with no aftermath, the eternal-security teaching that becomes a license, the businessman who is forgiven on Sunday for what he will do again on Monday and has been assured it does not matter because grace. The man who treats the blood of Christ as a transaction that cleared his account and changed nothing else, who got the fire insurance and went home. That is cheap grace and Bonhoeffer's words land on it like a hammer, and the progressive Christian quoting them is not wrong to aim there.

But Bonhoeffer's hand was not the only hand grace had, and the left has a cheap grace of its own, and it is harder to see because it does not look like grace at all. It looks like justice.

Here is the progressive version, and I have administered it, so I am not describing strangers. Cheap grace on the left is forgiveness extended in the abstract to everyone except the people actually standing in front of you. It is a grace that flows freely toward the distant and the categorical — the marginalized in general, the oppressed as a class, the sympathetic figure who confirms your politics — and freezes solid toward the particular sinner whose sin is the wrong kind. It is the warmest possible language about mercy attached to the most merciless possible practice toward the unforgiven. We have a grace for victims and a wrath for villains and we are very confident about which is which, and we call the wrath justice so we never have to notice that we have simply moved the cheapness to the other hand. The right gives cheap grace to itself. The left gives cheap grace to its allies and no grace at all to its enemies. Both have made grace cheap. They have only chosen different people to be cheap toward.

Watch the second move, because it is subtler and it is mine. Cheap grace on the left is also grace bestowed on the self through identification with the right cause. Bonhoeffer's exact charge was that cheap grace is the grace we bestow on ourselves — and the progressive does this not by saying *I am forgiven, so I can do as I please*, but by saying, underneath, *I am on the right side, so I am not the problem*. The cause launders the conscience. To stand with the oppressed becomes a way of standing outside the dock — of being, by virtue of my politics, one of the good ones, the awake ones, the ones the future will thank. And once I am one of the good ones, the call to repent quietly excuses me, because repentance is for the captured, the complicit, the people who have not yet seen what I have seen. I have absolved myself by my alignment. That is grace without the cross. That is grace I bestowed on myself. It is the exact thing Bonhoeffer named, wearing the one costume he did not live to see it wear.

The tell, in both cases, is the same: a forgiveness that costs the forgiver nothing. Real grace is expensive to the one who extends it. It cost God the cross; it costs me, when I extend it, the surrender of my right to be the one who was wronged, the death of my satisfaction in someone else's guilt. *Forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors* (Matthew 6:12) — the as is a hinge and a horror, because it ties the mercy I receive to the mercy I am willing to spend, and the unforgiving servant in Matthew 18, hauled back before the king after being forgiven a fortune and then throttling a man over pennies, is the most uncomfortable parable in the Gospels for anyone who has ever felt righteous about their grievances. I have throttled men over pennies while bearing a forgiven fortune. I have done it in the name of justice, which is the most respectable way to do it.

This is where the costly version stings, so let me hold it where it hurts. Costly grace, turned left, looks like extending real forgiveness to the people my politics has taught me to hate. Not the abstract enemy. The actual one. The relative who voted the way that frightens me. The pastor whose theology I find cruel. The figure from the past whose statue came down — and whether or not the statue should have come down, the question of whether I can pray *Father, forgive him* without my teeth grinding is a different question, and a more revealing one, and the harder of the two. Costly grace means I do not get to keep an enemy I am permitted to despise without limit. The cross took that permission away from me. Jesus did not say *love your neighbor and feel however you want about your enemy*. He said *love your enemies, pray for those who persecute you* (Matthew 5:44), and he said it is no great thing to love those who love you, even the tax collectors manage that — even the people on the right side of history manage to love the others on the right side of history. The test of grace was never the ally. It was always the enemy.

I have to be fair to the impulse underneath, because it is not nothing. The fear behind the left's cheap grace is a real and holy fear: that "forgiveness" has been weaponized by the powerful to silence the wounded, that *just forgive and move on* has been said to abused women and exploited workers and a whole race of people as a way of protecting the abuser and burying the wound. That fear is earned. The church has used a counterfeit grace to muzzle real pain, and the rage against that counterfeit is closer to God than the counterfeit was. Bonhoeffer himself would have stood with the wounded against the cheap absolution. But the answer to a grace that protects the powerful is not a grace that simply changes which people get protected. The answer is costly grace, which protects no one from the truth, the abuser or the avenger, and offers everyone the same impossible mercy — the mercy that names the sin in full, refuses to bury the wound, and still, with the wound open, with the sin named, opens a door the offender did not earn. That is harder than cheap forgiveness and harder than righteous wrath. It is the only thing the cross actually purchased.

So I will keep quoting Bonhoeffer. But I am trying to stop quoting him as a weapon and start hearing him as a mirror, the way he wrote it, the way he died for it — in a Flossenbürg gallows in April 1945, having extended his own life as a kind of grace to a country that had earned none. He paid for the costly grace he preached. That is why his words still cut. Most of us are quoting a martyr to indict the other side while keeping, in our own left hand, the very thing he died saying was the enemy of the church.

Cheap grace has a left hand. I know, because I have been writing checks with it for years, and the signature at the bottom is my own.
