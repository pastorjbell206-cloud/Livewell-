Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote the words in 1937, in a Germany where the church had mostly made its peace with a monster, and the words have never stopped accusing the people who quote them. *Cheap grace is the deadly enemy of our church.* He wrote it in *The Cost of Discipleship*, and he defined it with a precision that still cuts: cheap grace is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ living and incarnate. It is the preaching of forgiveness without requiring repentance. It is, he said, grace we bestow on ourselves. Grace as a principle, a system, a doctrine you can hold in your hand instead of a Person who holds you. Grace that costs the recipient nothing because it cost the giver, in our telling, nothing either — though it cost God his Son.

We have done a strange thing with that warning. We have decided it belongs to the right.

And it does belong to the right; grant the obvious before it gets taken away. There is a recognizable American cheap grace that lives most comfortably on the conservative side of the room — the altar call with no aftermath, the eternal-security teaching that becomes a license, the businessman who is forgiven on Sunday for what he will do again on Monday and has been assured it does not matter because grace. The man who treats the blood of Christ as a transaction that cleared his account and changed nothing else, who got the fire insurance and went home. That is cheap grace and Bonhoeffer's words land on it like a hammer, and the progressive Christian quoting them is not wrong to aim there.

But Bonhoeffer's hand was not the only hand grace had, and the left has a cheap grace of its own, and it is harder to see because it does not look like grace at all. It looks like justice.

To see it, go back to the room Bonhoeffer was actually writing in, because the slogan-version of him has eaten the man. By 1937 the German Protestant church had largely folded itself into the Reich. The German Christians, the *Deutsche Christen*, had decided that Hitler's Germany was a fresh revelation of God's will in history, that the arc of the nation was bending toward something glorious, and that the church should align itself with the movement of the moment. Against them stood the Confessing Church, which in the Barmen Declaration of 1934 — drafted largely by Karl Barth — said the one thing the age would not let it say: that Jesus Christ, not the spirit of the times, is the one Word of God the church is bound to hear. Bonhoeffer's cheap grace was not first an idea about lazy individuals getting saved without changing. It was an indictment of a whole church that had granted itself absolution for its alignment — that had decided, because it stood with the right cause of the right moment, that it was no longer the thing in need of repentance. The Confessing Church was a minority. It lost most of its public fights. Bonhoeffer ended in a Gestapo cell. The lesson he paid for is the one we have turned into a bumper sticker: a church can sell grace cheap not only by forgiving too easily but by aligning so confidently with a cause that it forgets it is still in the dock.

Hold that, and the left's version comes into focus. Cheap grace on the left is forgiveness extended in the abstract to everyone except the people actually standing in front of you. It is a grace that flows freely toward the distant and the categorical — the marginalized in general, the oppressed as a class, the sympathetic figure who confirms your politics — and freezes solid toward the particular sinner whose sin is the wrong kind. It is the warmest possible language about mercy attached to the most merciless possible practice toward the unforgiven. We have a grace for victims and a wrath for villains and we are very confident about which is which, and we call the wrath justice so we never have to notice that we have simply moved the cheapness to the other hand. The right gives cheap grace to itself. The left gives cheap grace to its allies and no grace at all to its enemies. Both have made grace cheap. They have only chosen different people to be cheap toward.

Watch the second move, because it is subtler and it is mine. Cheap grace on the left is also grace bestowed on the self through identification with the right cause. Bonhoeffer's exact charge was that cheap grace is the grace we bestow on ourselves — and the progressive does this not by saying *I am forgiven, so I can do as I please*, but by saying, underneath, *I am on the right side, so I am not the problem*. The cause launders the conscience. To stand with the oppressed becomes a way of standing outside the dock — of being, by virtue of my politics, one of the good ones, the awake ones, the ones the future will thank. And once I am one of the good ones, the call to repent quietly excuses me, because repentance is for the captured, the complicit, the people who have not yet seen what I have seen. I have absolved myself by my alignment. That is grace without the cross. That is grace I bestowed on myself. It is the exact thing Bonhoeffer named, wearing the one costume he did not live to see it wear.

The tell, in both cases, is the same: a forgiveness that costs the forgiver nothing. Real grace is expensive to the one who extends it. It cost God the cross; it costs me, when I extend it, the surrender of my right to be the one who was wronged, the death of my satisfaction in someone else's guilt. *Forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors* (Matthew 6:12) — the *as* is a hinge and a horror, because it ties the mercy I receive to the mercy I am willing to spend.

And then there is the parable nobody wants attached to their politics. A servant is forgiven ten thousand talents — a debt no laborer could clear in a hundred lifetimes — and walks straight out of his own pardon to seize a man who owes him a hundred denarii by the throat, demanding payment, deaf to the same plea he had just made and been granted (Matthew 18:23–35). The king's verdict on him is the most frightening sentence in the chapter: should you not have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you. The horror of the parable is not that the servant was a hypocrite. It is that he could not feel the contradiction. He held an erased fortune in one hand and throttled a man over pennies with the other, and the two never met in his conscience, because he had filed the man he was choking under a different category than the one he himself had been forgiven under. That is the precise mechanism of cheap grace on the left. I have throttled men over pennies while bearing a forgiven fortune. I have done it in the name of justice, which is the most respectable way to do it.

This is where the theology has to slow down, because the easy answer would be to say *the left should just forgive more*, and that answer is shallow and Bonhoeffer would have hated it. The harder truth is that forgiveness and justice are not opponents to be balanced but a single act the cross holds together, and we keep tearing them in half and grabbing the half that flatters us. Miroslav Volf, a Croatian theologian who wrote *Exclusion and Embrace* in 1996 out of the wreckage of the Balkan wars — out of being asked, by Jürgen Moltmann, whether he could embrace a Serbian fighter, a *četnik*, the soldiers who had burned his people's villages — saw this more clearly than anyone in our generation. Volf's argument is not that we should forgive instead of pursuing justice. It is that the will to embrace the other must precede the judgment about the other, or the judgment becomes a weapon and the self becomes God. We sin, Volf says, not first by hating the wicked but by drawing the boundary of the human community to leave the enemy outside it, so that the violence we then do feels like cleansing. Exclusion comes dressed as righteousness. It always has. And the embrace Volf calls for is not cheap — it does not pretend the *četnik* did nothing, it does not bury the burned village. It makes room, while the wound is open, for the one who caused it. That is the costliest thing a wounded person can do, and Volf insists it is the only thing the crucified God actually models, because on the cross God embraced the very humanity that was killing him.

Reinhold Niebuhr saw the political form of the danger a generation earlier, and he saw it inside the side he belonged to. Niebuhr was a man of the left — a socialist, a labor preacher in industrial Detroit before he was anything else — and the great burden of his work, from *Moral Man and Immoral Society* in 1932 onward, was to warn his own people about the self-righteousness that hides inside a just cause. The groups most certain of their own virtue, he argued, are the most dangerous, because they baptize their will to power as the will of God and lose the one thing that might restrain them: the knowledge that they too stand under judgment. Niebuhr was not telling the left to abandon justice. He spent his life pursuing it. He was telling the left that a movement which cannot imagine its own sin has already become the thing it opposes, and will do, in the name of its righteousness, what its enemies did in the name of theirs. He could have been writing to a congregation. He was, in fact, writing to me.

Walter Brueggemann would push it one layer further down, into the language we pray. Brueggemann spent a career arguing that the prophetic imagination is not the same as moral certainty — that the prophets did not stand over Israel as the righteous over the wicked but stood *inside* the people, grieving a sin they shared, holding the dialectic of judgment and hope that the powerful always want to pry apart. The danger of a prophetic posture is that it can curdle into a permanent indictment of everyone but the prophet. And Brueggemann's reading of the Psalms keeps a door open we usually want shut: the imprecatory psalms, the ones that howl *break their teeth, O God* (Psalm 58), are not models for our wrath but the place we are commanded to *bring* our wrath — handed up to God, surrendered to the only One who can judge without sin, instead of executed by our own hand. The rage is real. The rage is even prayed. But it is prayed *to* God, which means it is taken *out of* my hands. That is the whole point of *Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord* (Romans 12:19). It is not a denial that vengeance is owed. It is a relocation of who gets to take it. Paul does not say the debt is forgiven into thin air. He says I do not get to be the collector, and then he says the unbearable next thing: if your enemy is hungry, feed him (Romans 12:20). I had to give up the wrath and then hand the enemy bread.

This is the hinge, so set it down without decoration. Costly grace, turned left, looks like extending real forgiveness to the people my politics has taught me to hate. Not the abstract enemy. The actual one. The relative who voted the way that frightens me. The pastor whose theology I find cruel. The figure from the past whose statue came down — and whether or not the statue should have come down, the question of whether I can pray *Father, forgive him* without my teeth grinding is a different question, and a more revealing one, and the harder of the two. Costly grace means I do not get to keep an enemy I am permitted to despise without limit. The cross took that permission away from me. Jesus did not say *love your neighbor and feel however you want about your enemy*. He said *love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you* (Luke 6:27–28), and he said it is no great thing to love those who love you — even sinners love those who love them, even the tax collectors manage that, even the people on the right side of history manage to love the others on the right side of history. The test of grace was never the ally. It was always the enemy.

And there is one more demand, the one we never preach, because it cuts the wrong direction. We think the burden of reconciliation falls on the offender — that he must come, contrite, and earn his way back before we owe him anything. Jesus puts the burden somewhere that should stop us cold. *If you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there and go. First be reconciled to your brother* (Matthew 5:23–24). Not: if you have something against him. If *he* has something against *you*. The worshiper is sent away from the altar not to collect a debt but to settle one he may not even think he owes. Grace that costs the forgiver everything also costs the forgiven the pretense of innocence — and the left's cheap grace, like the right's, runs on the conviction that I am the one with the legitimate grievance and never the one who left a gift at the altar while a brother stood somewhere holding a wound with my name on it.

Be fair to the impulse underneath, because it is not nothing. The fear behind the left's cheap grace is a real and holy fear: that "forgiveness" has been weaponized by the powerful to silence the wounded, that *just forgive and move on* has been said to abused women and exploited workers and a whole race of people as a way of protecting the abuser and burying the wound. That fear is earned. The church has used a counterfeit grace to muzzle real pain, and the rage against that counterfeit is closer to God than the counterfeit was. Bonhoeffer himself would have stood with the wounded against the cheap absolution; he stood with the persecuted Jews of Germany while most of the church looked for a way to call its silence prudence. But the answer to a grace that protects the powerful is not a grace that simply changes which people get protected. The answer is costly grace, which protects no one from the truth, the abuser or the avenger, and offers everyone the same impossible mercy — the mercy that names the sin in full, refuses to bury the wound, and still, with the wound open, with the sin named, opens a door the offender did not earn. That is harder than cheap forgiveness and harder than righteous wrath. It is the only thing the cross actually purchased.

There is an older brother in one of the parables who never gets the grace, and it is because he refuses it, not because it was withheld. The prodigal comes home and the father runs (Luke 15). The fatted calf, the robe, the ring, the music — all of it lands on the son who burned the inheritance in a far country, and the elder brother stands out in the field and will not come in. His complaint is not wrong on the facts. He really did stay. He really did work. The younger son really did squander everything. Every line of his grievance is true, and the truth of it is exactly what hardens him, because he has built his whole standing on being the one who did it right, and a father who throws a feast for the one who did it wrong has demolished the economy the elder brother lived by. He cannot enter the joy without surrendering his ledger. And here is the part of the parable that should stop every progressive Christian, myself first: the elder brother is the one the story leaves outside. Not the sinner. The righteous one. The one who was, by every metric, on the correct side, and who stayed there, in the dark, in the field, because the grace shown to his enemy was an offense he would not forgive. I have stood in that field. I have called it justice. The father came out and pleaded with him too, which is the mercy in it, but the parable does not tell us whether he went in.

So I will keep quoting Bonhoeffer. But I am trying to stop quoting him as a weapon and start hearing him as a mirror, the way he wrote it, the way he died for it — hanged at Flossenbürg on the ninth of April, 1945, two weeks before the Americans reached the camp, on the personal order of a regime that wanted him gone before the end it could already see coming. He had a chance to stay safe in New York in 1939 and came back to Germany to share the fate of his people. He paid for the costly grace he preached, in full, in his own body, to a country that had earned none of it. That is why his words still cut. Most of us are quoting a martyr to indict the other side while keeping, in our own left hand, the very thing he died saying was the enemy of the church.

I do not get to keep the enemy. That is the part I keep trying to negotiate around. I will extend grace to the distant and the categorical all day, because it costs me nothing and it confirms my standing among the people whose approval I want. The grace that costs is the grace I owe the man across the table whose politics frightens me, the one I have filed under *villain* so that I never have to file him under *neighbor*, the one whose hunger I am commanded to feed before I have finished deciding whether he deserves to eat.

Cheap grace has a left hand. I know, because I have been writing checks with it for years, and the signature at the bottom is my own.
