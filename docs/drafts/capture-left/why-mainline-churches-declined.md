Most American towns of any age have one. It sits on Main Street or just off the square, built of stone in the 1880s or brick in the 1920s, with a steeple that was once the tallest thing in sight and a sign that names a denomination most people under forty could not describe. The endowment keeps the lights on. On Sunday about forty people gather in a sanctuary built for four hundred, most of them past seventy, most of them kind, and many of them carrying a quiet grief they rarely say aloud: their children were raised in this building, confirmed at this rail, married at this altar, and almost none of them come anymore. They didn't storm out. They drifted, and the drift never reversed.

Evangelical preachers have told the story of that building for fifty years, usually as a sermon illustration, and usually in the same form. The mainline churches abandoned the Bible, embraced liberal theology and progressive politics, and emptied as a result, while the churches that held to the gospel grew. The illustration ends with a warning and, more often than not, a trace of satisfaction. It's the satisfaction that should give us pause, because some of what the illustration says is true and a good deal of it is not, and the parts that are not true have been flattering the people telling it.

This essay makes three claims. The decline of the Protestant mainline has several honest explanations, and the most powerful of them has little to do with theology. The churches that told themselves they were growing because they were faithful are now shrinking too, which ought to end the gloating. And beneath the demographics lies a question that numbers can only point toward, the question Dean Kelley asked in 1972 and that every church, liberal or conservative, must eventually answer: is there anything we will not give up?

## What happened, in numbers we can trust

The word "mainline" was never theological. It described the old, established Protestant bodies that sat at the center of American public life for most of the nation's history, the churches whose ministers prayed at inaugurations and whose members ran the banks and the colleges: the Methodists, Presbyterians, Episcopalians, Lutherans of the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, the United Church of Christ, the Disciples of Christ and the American Baptists. In 1955 the sociologist Will Herberg described, in *Protestant-Catholic-Jew*, an America in which belonging to one of three faiths was nearly part of being a good citizen. Within the Protestant third, these churches set the tone. Their longer institutional story, from the ecumenical high tide to the theology taught in their seminaries, is told in [The Rise and Fall of Mainline Protestantism](/writing/the-rise-and-fall-of-mainline-protestantism); this essay asks a narrower question about why they emptied, and what the answer asks of everyone else.

Their membership peaked in the mid-1960s and then turned downward, and the turn has never reversed. When the Methodist Church merged with the Evangelical United Brethren in 1968 to form the United Methodist Church, the new body counted roughly eleven million American members. The Presbyterian bodies that later reunited as the Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.) counted more than four million members in the mid-1960s. The PC(USA) reported about 1.1 million in 2023. In 2019 the United Methodist Church began a formal process that allowed congregations to leave over its disputes about sexuality, and by the end of 2023 roughly a quarter of its American congregations had done so.

The broadest measure comes from the Pew Research Center's three large national religion studies, each surveying more than 35,000 Americans, conducted in 2007, 2014 and 2023-24. In 2007, 18.1 percent of American adults identified with a mainline Protestant church. In 2014 the figure was 14.7 percent. In the most recent study it was 11 percent. Across two generations, the churches that once spoke for American Protestantism have become one of its smaller branches.

Those are the numbers most sermon illustrations stop at. The next ones are less comfortable. The same Pew studies found that evangelical Protestants made up 26.3 percent of American adults in 2007, 25.4 percent in 2014 and 23 percent in 2023-24. The Southern Baptist Convention, the largest evangelical body in the country, reported its peak membership of about 16.3 million in 2006. By 2023 it reported fewer than 13 million, a loss of more than three million people in less than twenty years. The evangelical decline began later and has been slower, and evangelicals still hold a larger share of the population. But the story of faithful churches growing while unfaithful ones shrank is no longer the story the numbers tell.

## The explanations that are true

Before any theological verdict, the other explanations deserve their full strength, because they are serious and the people who offer them know what they are talking about.

The first is demography. In 2001 the sociologists Michael Hout, Andrew Greeley and Melissa Wilde published a study in the *American Journal of Sociology* called "The Demographic Imperative in Religious Change in the United States." Using decades of survey data, they asked why conservative Protestant denominations had gained on the mainline across the twentieth century. Their answer was that roughly three quarters of the shift came from birth rates. Women in conservative denominations had more children and had them earlier. Mainline women, who were more educated and more affluent, had fewer and later. The researchers found little evidence that large numbers of mainline Protestants had switched to stricter churches because they wanted firmer doctrine. The conservative churches, in their account, had not so much won converts from the mainline as outbred it.

That finding should humble anyone who has preached the sermon illustration. If most of the gap between the mainline and the evangelical churches came from the maternity ward, then evangelical growth was in large part a fact about family size, not a verdict on faithfulness. And it predicts exactly what we now see. As evangelical birth rates have fallen toward the national average, evangelical churches have begun to shrink as well.

The second explanation is what might be called the paradox of victory. In 1995 the sociologist N. J. Demerath argued in the *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion* that liberal Protestantism had lost its members precisely because it had won the culture. The values it championed through the twentieth century, individual dignity, freedom of conscience, tolerance, democratic participation and the critical reading of inherited authority, became the common sense of educated America. Once those values could be had without the church, the church became optional. The historian David Hollinger made a related case in *After Cloven Tongues of Fire* (2013). The ecumenical Protestants, he argued, pressed their own tradition toward cosmopolitan openness on race, empire and other religions, and their children carried those commitments out into secular life. On this reading the mainline was not so much defeated as absorbed. Its ideals succeeded, and the institution that carried them lost its reason to exist.

The third explanation is structural. Robert Wuthnow showed in *The Restructuring of American Religion* (1988) that after the Second World War the deepest divisions in American Protestantism stopped running between denominations and started running through them, liberal against conservative within each body. The old denominational loyalties that once held families in a church for generations weakened, and people sorted themselves by conviction and politics instead. The mainline, with its broad and internally divided membership, felt that sorting most acutely.

And there is a fourth point that the progressive defender of the mainline is right to make. Size is not faithfulness. In the 1950s and 1960s mainline churches were among the earliest white institutions to support the civil rights movement, often at the cost of members who left over it. They were right about segregation when many Southern evangelical churches were defending it from the pulpit. If a church lost members because it told the truth about race, the loss is not a mark against it. Jesus himself watched a crowd walk away after a hard teaching (John 6:66). A church can shrink because it is faithful. Anyone who measures a church's obedience by its attendance has adopted the market's theology, not the New Testament's.

All four of these explanations are true, or largely true, and together they account for much of what happened. What they cannot fully explain is the building on Main Street, and why the children of those forty faithful people did not go to a different church when they left. They mostly did not go anywhere.

## What Dean Kelley actually saw

The book most often cited in the sermon illustration is also the one most often misread. Dean Kelley was not an evangelical. He was a Methodist minister who worked for the National Council of Churches, the ecumenical body of the mainline itself, as its specialist on religious liberty. In 1972 he published *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing*, and the title made him famous with exactly the readers he was not trying to please.

Kelley's argument was not that conservative doctrine as such attracts people. It was sociological, and it was about seriousness. Religion, he argued, exists to make life meaningful in ultimate terms, and people judge whether a religion can do that by how seriously its members take it. Groups that demand a great deal, that hold clear beliefs, expect members to live by them and treat membership as costly, convey that their message matters. Groups that ask little, tolerate any belief and treat membership as easy convey the opposite, however admirable their tolerance. Kelley called the first kind strict. He thought strict groups would grow and lenient ones would decline, whether their theology was conservative or not. By his logic a demanding liberal church could flourish and an undemanding conservative one would not.

Twenty-two years later the economist Laurence Iannaccone gave the argument its most rigorous form in an *American Journal of Sociology* article with the plain title "Why Strict Churches Are Strong" (1994). A church, he observed, is a collective good. Its worship, fellowship and care are produced by its members together. When membership costs nothing, people can enjoy the benefits while contributing little, and a congregation full of such members produces a thin and unconvincing religious life. Demanding groups screen out those who will not commit, and the ones who remain produce a richer common life for everyone. Strictness, on this account, is not cruelty. It is the price of a congregation that has something to offer.

The most telling evidence came from inside the mainline itself. In *Vanishing Boundaries* (1994), the sociologists Dean Hoge, Benton Johnson and Donald Luidens tracked down hundreds of adults who had been confirmed as teenagers in Presbyterian churches during the 1950s and 1960s. They wanted to know why so many had left. The answers were not dramatic. Few had been driven out by conflict or scandal. The strongest predictor of whether someone was still active in church, they found, was belief, specifically whether the person held that Christ was uniquely the way to God and that faith made a claim on life. Those who had kept that conviction had tended to stay in church, in the mainline or elsewhere. Those who had lost it saw no reason to be anywhere on Sunday morning. The researchers called the prevailing outlook "lay liberalism": a sincere, tolerant, morally serious outlook that could find no reason why church mattered. The boundaries between the church and the surrounding culture had not been attacked. They had simply faded until there was nothing on the other side of them.

There are dated turning points that show how the fading happened, and they should be read as symptoms, not as the whole story. In 1932 a commission of laymen from several mainline denominations, chaired by the Harvard philosopher William Ernest Hocking, published *Re-Thinking Missions*. It urged missionaries to put service ahead of preaching for conversion and to work alongside the other great religions as partners in a common search for truth. Several mission boards distanced themselves from it, but its outlook spread. In 1966 the Episcopal House of Bishops censured James Pike, who had just stepped down as bishop of California, for public denials of the Trinity and the virgin birth, but did not try him, and the censure changed little. In 1998 Bishop John Shelby Spong published *Why Christianity Must Change or Die*, which set aside the historic doctrines of God, the incarnation and the resurrection. Many mainline pastors and members never believed what Pike and Spong taught, and many held the creed with real devotion. But the institutions proved unable to say that such teaching was outside the faith. That inability is the thing Kelley was pointing at.

This is where the distinction that matters most has to be drawn. There is a difference between the openness of a church's welcome and the openness of its confession. A church can and should throw its doors wide, to the sinner and the doubter, the poor and the outsider, to people from every class and color and politics. That openness is the gospel's own, and at their best the mainline churches practiced it better than many evangelical churches did. The mainline's error was to believe that open doors required an open creed, that a welcoming church must also be a church that will not say what it believes. It turned out to be the other way around. A church with nothing at its center has nothing to welcome people into. The door stays open and the room behind it slowly empties, because an open door into an empty room is not a welcome.

## The evangelical in the mirror

Here the evangelical reader will be tempted to nod along, and that nod is the most dangerous moment in this essay.

Consider what the numbers actually show. If Hout, Greeley and Wilde are right, the evangelical churches spent decades attributing to their faithfulness growth that came largely from their birth rates. We preached the sermon illustration as though we had won an argument. We had mostly had larger families. That is a sobering thing to learn about one's own boasting, and it should make any evangelical slow to explain another tradition's decline by its unfaithfulness when our own growth was so much less spiritual than we claimed.

Consider, too, what happened to belief inside evangelical churches. In *Soul Searching* (2005), Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton reported the findings of a national study of American teenagers and gave a name to the faith most of them actually held, across nearly every tradition: moralistic therapeutic deism. God exists, wants people to be nice and fair, wants them to be happy and feel good about themselves, does not need to be involved in life except when a problem arises, and takes good people to heaven. Smith and Denton found that faith among evangelical teenagers too. It is lay liberalism by another name, without the mainline's social conscience, and it grew in churches with conservative doctrinal statements on the wall. The boundaries Hoge and his colleagues watched vanish in the mainline were vanishing in evangelical youth groups at the same time. We had only kept the statements.

And consider what evangelical churches have in fact refused to give up. Kelley's question was about seriousness, about what a church holds so firmly that it will lose members rather than let it go. Many evangelical churches can answer that question, and the honest answer is not always the creed. Some will not give up the flag in the sanctuary. Some will not give up a political alliance, whatever that alliance demands of their witness. Some will not give up a style of worship, a cultural identity or a place in the community's social order. Those are strict commitments in Kelley's sense, and they have held congregations together. But a church that will lose members over its politics and not over the resurrection has kept its boundaries and moved them to the wrong place. It has done the mainline's error in reverse, keeping a firm fence and forgetting what the fence was built to protect.

The letter to the church at Sardis ought to be read aloud in every congregation that has told the mainline's story as a warning about other people: "I know your works. You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead" (Revelation 3:1, ESV). Sardis had a reputation. It was presumably busy and respected. The risen Christ did not care about the reputation. The same Lord told the church at Ephesus, which had excellent doctrine and had tested false teachers carefully, that it had abandoned the love it had at first, and warned, "If not, I will come to you and remove your lampstand from its place, unless you repent" (Revelation 2:5, ESV). A lampstand can be removed from a church with sound doctrine. Paul's warning to the Corinthians covers every tradition at once: "Therefore let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12, ESV).

Pastors in growing or stable churches should hear this first, because the elder brother in Jesus' parable is the figure we are most likely to become. He stayed home. He kept the rules. When his brother came back, he stood outside the party and said to his father, "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command" (Luke 15:29, ESV). The parable ends with the younger son inside and the older one outside, and Jesus leaves it there on purpose. The evangelical who reads the mainline's decline with satisfaction has taken his place in the yard.

## What a church cannot give up

So what should a church refuse to give up? The question is harder than it sounds, because the answer is not "everything." A church that treats every custom, every political preference and every second-order conviction as sacred will fracture over trivia and crush its people with burdens Christ never laid on them. American Protestants have divided over far less than the creed. Kelley's strictness can become plain rigidity, and Iannaccone's costly membership can become a sect's control. The task is not to hold everything tightly. It is to know what the center is and to hold that center with open hands and a firm grip at the same time.

Paul told the Corinthians what the center was: "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4, ESV). The phrase "of first importance" does the work. Not everything in the Christian faith carries the same weight. The death of Christ for sins, his burial and his bodily resurrection are the load-bearing wall. The church spent its first four centuries saying the same thing at greater length, and the creeds that came out of those centuries, the Apostles' and the Nicene, are its summary: the Father who made heaven and earth, the Son who became flesh, died and rose, the Spirit who gives life, the church, forgiveness, the resurrection of the body and the life of the world to come. Jude called it "the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (Jude 3, ESV). A church that holds that faith can give up nearly everything else. It can lose its building, its endowment, its standing in the town, its political access and its cultural prestige, and still be the church. A church that gives up that faith has nothing left to give anyone, however many programs it runs.

The mainline's deepest loss, in the end, was that it gave up the center to keep the prestige, and then lost the prestige anyway. Not everywhere and not in every pulpit, but often enough to shape the institutions, it softened the resurrection so as not to embarrass educated members, blurred the uniqueness of Christ so as not to offend its neighbors, and quieted the call to conversion so as to stay respectable, and the educated members and the neighbors and the respectable classes went elsewhere regardless. Jesus described that trade once: "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matthew 16:25, ESV). The evangelical temptation runs the other way. It keeps the creed on paper and gives up the cross in practice, trading the costly way of Jesus for political power and cultural victory. It is the same trade in different currency. Both have tried to save their lives.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer published *Discipleship* in 1937, in a German church that had largely given up its center to keep its place in the nation, and he called the result cheap grace: grace without the cross, forgiveness without repentance, a church that asks nothing because it has nothing it is willing to suffer for. He was writing about Lutherans under Hitler, not American Presbyterians or Southern Baptists. But the diagnosis travels. A church becomes cheap when there is nothing it would rather lose members than surrender.

Salt is Jesus' image for what happens then. "You are the salt of the earth, but if salt has lost its taste, how shall its saltiness be restored? It is no longer good for anything except to be thrown out and trampled under people's feet" (Matthew 5:13, ESV). Salt does not lose its saltiness by being hated. It loses it by becoming indistinguishable from what surrounds it. The mainline became indistinguishable from the educated, tolerant, decent culture it had helped create. Much of evangelicalism has become indistinguishable from a political movement and a consumer market. The mechanism is the same. The results are only arriving at different speeds.

Here is the verdict, and it cuts in every direction. A church that has nothing it will not give up has nothing to give.

There are still congregations in the mainline that hold the center with great courage, sometimes at the cost of their denominations' approval, and there are evangelical churches that have let go of the flag and the party and kept only Christ. Both kinds are usually small. Both kinds usually look, from the outside, like the building on Main Street. Their future does not depend on the next Pew study. It depends on whether, when the question comes, they will answer the way Peter did when the crowd walked away and Jesus asked the twelve whether they wanted to leave too: "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:68, ESV). The forty people in the stone church on the square are, some of them, still saying that. Their children are not in the pews. The question for every church in America, the one that is shrinking fast and the one that is shrinking slowly, is whether we have anything left to say to those children that they could not hear anywhere else, and whether we would lose everything else before we stopped saying it.
