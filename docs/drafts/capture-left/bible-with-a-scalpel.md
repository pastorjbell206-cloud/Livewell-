The sentence usually arrives in a church hallway or a comment thread, and it comes in two versions. The first says: Jesus never said a word about that, so neither should we. The second says: the Bible tells us to submit to the governing authorities, so that settles it. The first is most often heard from Christians on the left and the second from Christians on the right, and they are usually aimed at each other. They are also, underneath, the same move. Each takes one part of Scripture, holds it up as the whole, and uses it to silence the rest.

The skeptic has noticed. A reason thoughtful people often give for dismissing Christianity is that Christians plainly pick and choose. They quote Leviticus on sex and ignore it on shellfish. They quote Jesus on love and ignore him on money. If the book can be made to say anything, the skeptic reasons, it says nothing, and the believers are simply dressing up their own preferences in borrowed authority. The skeptic is not wrong about what he has seen. The question is whether picking and choosing is the only way to read the Bible, or whether there is a way of reading that lets the whole book speak, including the parts the reader would rather not hear.

This essay takes up the most serious form of the picking and choosing, the kind that is not lazy or cynical but principled. The red-letter and trajectory readings of Scripture begin from something true: the Bible does move, and Jesus Christ is its center. But there is a difference between reading the whole canon through Christ as its key and using Christ as a knife to cut away whatever the reader's age finds hard. The first is the church's oldest way of reading. The second is its oldest heresy, and the right and the left both practice it.

## The knife has a long history

The first Christian to take a blade to the Bible did it in Rome around the year 144. Marcion was a wealthy shipowner from Pontus who became convinced that the God of the Old Testament, the Creator who gave the law and judged the nations, could not be the Father of Jesus Christ. The Jewish Scriptures, in his view, belonged to a lesser and harsher god. So he produced a Bible without them: an edited version of Luke's Gospel and ten letters of Paul, trimmed of passages that tied Jesus too closely to Israel's God. The church in Rome expelled him. But his question never went away, and his method has had heirs in every century.

Around the year 200, Tertullian of Carthage, a lawyer turned theologian, described two ways of corrupting Scripture, and the distinction still helps. In *The Prescription Against Heretics* he observed that Marcion had openly used the knife rather than the pen, cutting out what did not suit him. Valentinus, the Gnostic teacher, seemed to keep the whole text but bent its meaning until it said what he wanted. One method removes the passage. The other keeps the passage and empties it. Every generation of Christians has used both, and it is worth watching for both in ourselves.

The knife reappeared in American hands. Thomas Jefferson, sometime around 1820, sat in his study at Monticello with copies of the New Testament in Greek, Latin, French and English and a razor. He cut out the passages he considered the genuine teaching of Jesus and pasted them into a small book he called *The Life and Morals of Jesus of Nazareth*. The miracles, the resurrection and the claims to divinity stayed on the floor. In a letter to John Adams in 1813 he had described the task as extracting the real words of Jesus from the surrounding material, which he thought as easy to tell apart as diamonds in a dunghill. Jefferson believed he was rescuing Jesus from his followers. He did not notice that the Jesus he rescued looked remarkably like an eighteenth-century Virginia gentleman of enlightened views.

Protestants should not imagine this is only a liberal habit. Martin Luther, in the preface to his 1522 German New Testament, called the letter of James an epistle of straw compared with the books he thought showed Christ most clearly, because it seemed to him to teach justification by works. Luther did not remove James from the Bible, and he later softened his language. But he had articulated a principle that has shaped Protestant reading ever since: a canon within the canon, some books and passages treated as the real Scripture, others as lesser or suspect.

Then, in 1899, the New York publisher Louis Klopsch produced a New Testament with the words of Jesus printed in red ink. It was a devotional innovation and a commercial success, and it quietly taught generations of readers to treat one set of words as more central than the rest. Red ink did not make those words more inspired. But it made them look that way.

## The best case for a center

Before any criticism, the readings this essay questions deserve their strongest statement, because they respond to a real failure in the church and they rest on a real truth in the text.

The Red Letter Christians, a loose movement of evangelicals led by Tony Campolo and Shane Claiborne, took the name in the middle of the 2000s. Their stated aim, laid out in Campolo's *Red Letter Christians* (2008) and in *Red Letter Revolution* (2012), which the two wrote together, was not to discard the rest of the Bible. They said plainly that they did not regard the red letters as more inspired than the black. Their claim was that the words of Jesus are the lens through which the rest of Scripture must be read, and that American evangelicalism had in practice done the opposite, reading Jesus through a political program and ignoring his teaching when it proved inconvenient. They pointed to the Sermon on the Mount, with its commands to love enemies, turn the other cheek, refuse anxious hoarding and give to the one who asks, and asked why a movement that claimed to take the Bible literally took those words so lightly.

The complaint was fair. Some dispensational teachers, following the notes of the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), had assigned the Sermon on the Mount's primary application to a future kingdom age rather than to the church now. Many more evangelicals simply ignored it in practice. A tradition that could build a theology of national blessing from 2 Chronicles and treat Jesus' teaching on wealth as a figure of speech had no standing to lecture anyone else about selective reading.

And the red-letter instinct rests on the New Testament's own claim. "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2, ESV). The risen Jesus, on the road to Emmaus, taught two discouraged disciples to read their Bibles this way: "And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27, ESV). To the religious scholars of his day he said, "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me" (John 5:39, ESV). Christ is the center. That is not a progressive invention. It is apostolic.

The trajectory reading rests on a second truth: the canon moves. The Bible is not a flat collection of equally timeless rules. It is a story across more than a thousand years, and within that story God's people are led from one arrangement to another. Mark notes, in an aside after Jesus' teaching about what defiles a person, "(Thus he declared all foods clean.)" (Mark 7:19, ESV). Peter's vision in Acts 10 and the council of Acts 15 carried that movement into the welcome of Gentiles without circumcision. Jesus himself explained Moses' permission for divorce as a concession: "Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8, ESV). Some laws in the Bible, by Jesus' own account, regulate a fallen situation without endorsing it.

The most careful evangelical form of this argument is William Webb's *Slaves, Women and Homosexuals* (2001). Webb proposed what he called a redemptive-movement hermeneutic. Read a biblical command against its ancient setting, he argued, and ask which direction it moves. Picture three points: the surrounding culture, the biblical text, and the ultimate ethic toward which the text is pointing. Where the text moves away from the harshness of the surrounding culture, the reader should follow that movement further than the text itself could go in its own time. Webb developed eighteen criteria for telling which elements of a text are culturally bound and which transcend culture. Slavery was his clearest case. The Old Testament's laws on servitude were gentler than those of its neighbors, and Paul could tell Philemon to receive the runaway Onesimus "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 16, ESV). The texts do not command abolition. But they point toward it, and the church was right, eventually, to follow where they pointed. Richard Longenecker had made a similar argument in *New Testament Social Ethics for Today* (1984), reading Paul's declaration that in Christ "there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female" (Galatians 3:28, ESV) as a principle whose implications the early church only began to work out.

The strongest historical evidence for this way of reading is the American argument over slavery itself. As Mark Noll showed in *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis* (2006), the defenders of slavery in the 1850s often had the more straightforward proof texts. Abraham held slaves, the law of Moses regulated slavery, and the apostles told slaves to obey their masters. The abolitionists argued from the spirit and direction of Scripture as a whole, from the image of God in every person and the logic of the gospel. On the most urgent moral question of the century, the readers who looked for the Bible's trajectory were right and the readers who stood on the plain sense of isolated verses were catastrophically wrong. Anyone who dismisses trajectory reading has to reckon with that.

Webb's method also showed restraint that its popular imitators often lack. When he applied his criteria to the biblical texts on same-sex relations, he concluded that they showed no movement toward greater permissiveness. The biblical texts were, if anything, more restrictive than the surrounding cultures, and so his method did not support revising them. On women's roles in church and home, he found real movement away from the patriarchy of the ancient world, and his readers still disagree about how far that movement goes. That is a second-order question on which faithful churches differ, and this essay leaves it there, open, as the site's fuller treatment in [Can Women Be Pastors?](/writing/women-in-ministry) does.

## Where the knife slips

The truth in these readings is real. But each carries a danger that its best practitioners guard against and its popular forms often do not.

The first danger is that the reader supplies the destination. Webb's model depends on discerning the ultimate ethic toward which the text is moving. But the text does not state that ethic; the reader infers it. And the reader who infers it lives in a particular age with its own confident sense of where history is going. Wayne Grudem's critique of Webb, published in the *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* in 2004, pressed exactly this point: the method invites readers to treat the New Testament's ethic as a waypoint on the way to a better one that we can see and the apostles could not. Once that door is open, the question becomes who decides what the better ethic is. In practice the answer is usually the reader's own culture. The nineteenth-century liberal Protestants who saw the Bible moving toward the progressive ideals of their own day were confident they had found its direction. The twentieth century was unkind to their confidence.

The same danger shows in how quickly careful scholars can reach opposite conclusions with similar tools. Richard Hays, one of the most respected New Testament scholars of his generation, argued in *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* (1996) that the New Testament's witness on same-sex relations was consistent and should be kept. In 2024, with his son Christopher, he published *The Widening of God's Mercy*, arguing from the Bible's pattern of widening inclusion that the church should now affirm those relationships. Webb, using his own trajectory method, had concluded that no such movement existed in those texts. The question itself receives a full treatment in [Is the Christian View of Sexuality Homophobic?](/writing/christian-sexual-ethic), with both cases at full strength, and this essay draws no new verdict on it. The point here concerns method. When the same kind of reading can carry serious scholars to opposite destinations, the destination is not being supplied by the text alone.

The second danger belongs to the red-letter reading. The words of Jesus do not only cut in one direction. He was stricter than Moses on divorce and stricter than the law on lust: "You have heard that it was said, 'You shall not commit adultery.' But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:27-28, ESV). He spoke of judgment plainly and often: "And do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28, ESV). He made claims about himself that no tolerant teacher of ethics would make: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6, ESV). And he spoke of the Scriptures of Israel with a reverence that leaves no room for Marcion: "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17, ESV). A reader who takes all the red letters seriously will find them at least as hard on the progressive conscience as on the conservative one. The reader who keeps only some of them is no longer reading the red letters. He is reading himself.

The third danger is historical. The red letters do not come to us from some source independent of the rest of the New Testament. They come through the Gospels, which were written, preserved and gathered by the same apostolic church that wrote and preserved the letters of Paul. Paul's earliest letters are older than any of the Gospels in their written form. The movement's leaders say they do not rank the red letters above the rest, but setting Jesus against Paul is common in the popular versions of their idea. To set Jesus against Paul, as though the red letters were the real Jesus and the black letters a later distortion, is to trust the church's witness about Jesus while distrusting the same church's witness about what Jesus meant. That is not a historical judgment. It is a preference.

Beneath all three dangers lies the distinction this essay turns on. Christ can be the key to the canon or he can be a knife used on it. A key opens every room in the house. It does not remove rooms. When the church reads the Old Testament through Christ, it finds the sacrifices fulfilled, the food laws completed, the promises to Abraham opened to the nations, and all of it still Scripture, still speaking, still to be heard. When a reader uses Christ as a knife, he cuts away whatever his age finds hard and calls the remainder the words of Jesus. The first reading submits to the whole. The second picks from it. The test is simple and uncomfortable: does your center ever tell you something you did not want to hear?

## The right has its own blade

If this essay stopped here, it would be one more tribal argument, and the reader on the right would finish it with his certainties intact. That would be dishonest, because the most famous knife in American history has been wielded by conservatives, and its name is Romans 13.

"Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God" (Romans 13:1, ESV). The verse is true, and it belongs in the canon. But watch how it has been used. In 1850, when Congress passed the Fugitive Slave Act, requiring citizens of free states to assist in returning people who had escaped slavery, some Northern ministers preached obedience to it from this chapter. In 1930s Germany, many Protestant churchmen appealed to Romans 13 to urge loyalty to the new regime, and it took the Barmen Declaration of 1934, drafted largely by Karl Barth for the Confessing Church, to insist that the state has a limited, God-given task and may not claim to be the total order of human life. In June 2018, the Attorney General of the United States cited Romans 13 to defend the enforcement policy that separated migrant children from their parents at the border.

Each time, the verse was lifted out of its setting and used to silence everything around it. The chapter before it tells Christians, "Bless those who persecute you," and, "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:14, 21, ESV). Paul's description of the ruler as "God's servant for your good" (Romans 13:4, ESV) assumes a ruler who punishes evil and rewards good, and the verses that follow sum up the whole law in love of neighbor: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (Romans 13:8, ESV). The same New Testament records the apostles standing before the council that ordered them to stop preaching and answering, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29, ESV). And it closes with Revelation 13, where the state, unmoored from God, becomes a beast demanding worship. A reader who keeps Romans 13:1 and cuts away Romans 12, Acts 5 and Revelation 13 has done exactly what Marcion did. He has simply used a different blade.

The right has other knives. It has often kept the Old Testament's promises of national blessing and set aside the Sermon on the Mount. It has kept the verses on sexual holiness and muted the far more numerous verses on wealth, the poor and the stranger. It has kept Paul's household codes and passed quickly over the parts of the same letters that told masters their own Master was in heaven. The instrument is the same one the left uses. It cuts away whatever the tribe's age finds hard and keeps whatever the tribe already believed. The only difference is which age and which tribe.

No preacher is outside this, and the one writing here is not. Every preacher has favorite texts and texts he quietly avoids, and the avoidance usually follows the same line as his congregation's comfort and his own. The test that exposes the left's selective reading exposes the pulpit as well. The passages a preacher would most like to skip are usually the ones he most needs to preach.

## Reading the whole

If picking and choosing is not the answer, and flattening the Bible into a list of equally timeless rules is not the answer either, what is? The church has an answer, and it is older than any of the modern debates.

Around the year 180, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, faced Gnostic teachers who used the Bible's own words to build a very different religion. He offered an image that has never been bettered. Imagine, he wrote, a beautiful mosaic of a king, made by a skilled artist from precious stones. Someone takes it apart and rearranges the same stones into the picture of a dog or a fox, and then claims it is the original image of the king. Every stone is genuine, but the picture is false. What lets a reader tell the difference, Irenaeus argued, is the rule of faith, the summary of the apostles' teaching that every baptized Christian received: one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became flesh for our salvation; and the Holy Spirit, who spoke through the prophets. The rule of faith does not replace Scripture. It is the shape of the whole, handed down so that no one could rearrange the stones and call the fox a king.

Augustine added two further guides in *On Christian Teaching*, begun in 397. The first was the rule of love. Anyone who thinks he has understood Scripture, Augustine wrote, but whose understanding does not build up the double love of God and neighbor, has not yet understood it. The second was that obscure passages should be read in light of clearer ones. The Reformers gave that second principle its classic Protestant form. The Westminster Confession of 1646 put it this way: "The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself." When one passage is unclear, the reader goes to others that speak more plainly. Scripture interprets Scripture. No single verse is allowed to silence the rest, and no reader's age is allowed to be the judge over the text. What that looks like at the reading desk, verse by verse and genre by genre, is set out in [How to Read the Bible in Context, and Let It Read You](/writing/how-to-read-the-bible-without-making-it-say-what-you-want).

In the twentieth century, Brevard Childs at Yale argued in *Biblical Theology of the Old and New Testaments* (1992) that the church must read each passage within the whole canon as the church received it, because the canon's final shape is itself part of the witness. The Old Testament is not simply background for the New. It remains Christian Scripture, speaking in its own voice even as it is read in light of Christ. And Hays's *Moral Vision* offered three focal images for reading the New Testament's ethics as a whole: community, cross and new creation. Whatever one makes of his later conclusions, the method points in the right direction. A passage is read within the whole story, not lifted out of it.

These guides do not make interpretation easy. Faithful Christians still disagree about second-order questions, and they should be allowed to. But they change what the reader is doing. The reader who uses the knife stands over the text, deciding what may stay. The reader who follows the rule of faith and lets Scripture interpret Scripture stands under the text, with the whole church across the centuries beside him, including the voices of believers whose age he does not share and whose blind spots differ from his. That company is one of the best protections against the blind spots of any single generation, including our own.

Practically, it means reading the passages you would cut. The progressive Christian needs to sit a long time with Jesus' words on judgment, sexual holiness and his own uniqueness. The conservative Christian needs to sit just as long with his words on wealth, enemies and the stranger, and with the whole of Romans 12 and 13 rather than a single verse. Both need to read the Old Testament as Scripture and not as an embarrassment or a political manual. Both need to ask the same question of every conclusion they reach: does this reading build up love for God and neighbor, or does it simply confirm what I already believed?

There is a final reversal, and the Bible supplies it. We began with readers taking a blade to Scripture. The letter to the Hebrews describes the direction the blade is meant to go: "For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and of spirit, of joints and of marrow, and discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart" (Hebrews 4:12, ESV). The Scripture is the scalpel. We are the ones on the table. Marcion, Jefferson and every reader since who has trimmed the book to fit his age, whether with a progressive knife or a conservative one, has tried to reverse the operation, standing over the text as its surgeon when the text was meant to open us. The skeptic is right that picking and choosing makes the Bible say nothing. He is wrong that it is the only way to read. There is another way, and it is harder. It is to let the whole book speak, especially the parts that cut, and to trust that the hand holding the blade is the same hand that was pierced for us. The cut is for healing. It still has to be allowed to cut.
