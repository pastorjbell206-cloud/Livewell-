There is a kind of discomfort that almost never gets named from a pulpit, because the people who feel it are rarely in the room long enough to name it. A man who works nights at a distribution center, or a woman who cleans offices and never finished high school, walks into a church on a Sunday morning. No one is rude. The greeters are warm. But within twenty minutes the visitor has learned a great deal about who the church is for. It is in the vocabulary of the sermon, the causes on the bulletin board, the assumptions about what everyone did last summer, the particular accent of the jokes. Nothing on the sign says that this is a congregation of the educated and the comfortable. Nothing needs to.

The early church had the opposite reputation. Around the year 175 a pagan philosopher named Celsus wrote an attack on Christianity, preserved for us because Origen answered it line by line around 248. Among his sneers was that the Christians made their converts among wool-workers, cobblers and laundry-workers, the most uneducated and rustic of people, and that they whispered their teaching to children and foolish women in the back rooms of shops. It was meant as a humiliation. It reads now like a description of what the church once was and too rarely is.

This essay is about how that happened, and it begins with the part of the American church that has most identified itself with justice for the poor: the educated, professional-class progressive congregation. My argument is that such congregations have often traded the gospel's offense for cultural respectability, keeping the moral commitments their class already admires and quietly letting go of the claims that embarrass it. But the argument does not stop there, because the suburban evangelical church has made the same trade in a different currency, keeping its doctrine while abandoning the crossing of class lines that the doctrine requires. Both have become respectable. Both have made it harder for the poor to sit down. And any essay on a platform that writes for thoughtful, educated readers, as this one does, has to admit that respectability is its own temptation too.

## What the educated church does well

The case for the educated congregation is strong, and it should be made in the form its own members would sign.

These churches have given a great deal to the country. American mainline Protestants built colleges, hospitals, seminaries and settlement houses. They took learning seriously at a time when anti-intellectualism was spreading through other parts of the church, and their willingness to read Scripture with historical honesty, asking who wrote a text, when, and to whom, recovered a great deal that proof-texting had buried. Their clergy were present in the civil rights movement in numbers that embarrassed much of the white evangelical world; after the violence on the Edmund Pettus Bridge on March 7, 1965, ministers from across the country answered the call to Selma, and one of them, the Unitarian Universalist minister James Reeb, was beaten on a Selma street on March 9 and died two days later.

The professional class also brings real competence to the church. It knows how to govern with process, to write safeguarding policies that actually protect children, to keep honest books, to run a food bank so that food reaches the hungry, to build a refugee resettlement program that works. It tends to distrust demagogues, to respect evidence, and to feel obligations toward strangers it will never meet. These are not small virtues, and a church without them is often at the mercy of a strongman in the pulpit.

Even respectability, rightly understood, is a biblical good. Paul tells Timothy that an overseer "must be above reproach, the husband of one wife, sober-minded, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach" (1 Timothy 3:2, ESV), and adds that "he must be well thought of by outsiders, so that he may not fall into disgrace, into a snare of the devil" (1 Timothy 3:7, ESV). Peter urges the scattered churches to keep their "conduct among the Gentiles honorable" (1 Peter 2:12, ESV). The New Testament has no romance about disorder. It wants a church whose life commends the gospel to people watching from outside.

So the distinction matters. There is a respectability that is the fruit of holiness: sobriety, honesty, faithfulness in marriage, diligence at work, kindness to the stranger, the kind of life that makes outsiders think well of Christ. And there is a respectability that is the price of admission: conformity to the tastes, manners, vocabulary, politics and sensibilities of a particular social class, so that belonging to the church requires first belonging to the class. The first is a gift of the Spirit. The second is a wall. The trouble is that from inside a comfortable congregation they look almost exactly alike.

A second distinction runs alongside the first. The gospel carries an offense of its own. Paul calls it "Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). There is also an offense that belongs not to the gospel but to the church: the offense a poor visitor feels at being made to understand that he is out of place. A faithful church offends in the right place and nowhere else. The respectable church tends to reverse it. It removes the offense of the cross and keeps the offense of class.

## How churches climb

The pattern is old, and its most careful American analyst saw it nearly a century ago. H. Richard Niebuhr's *The Social Sources of Denominationalism* (1929) argued that the divisions of American Protestantism tracked the divisions of American society, class, race, region and national origin, far more closely than they tracked doctrine. His account of how churches change over time has become a standard piece of the sociology of religion. Movements begin among the disinherited, with fervor, discipline and a sharp sense of separation from the world. Their discipline makes their members sober, diligent and thrifty. Their children prosper. Prosperity brings respectability, and the movement that began as a church of the poor becomes a church of the middle class, with educated clergy, fine buildings and a faith adjusted to the comforts of its members. Then a new movement arises among the poor it has left behind, and the cycle begins again. Niebuhr regarded the whole arrangement as a moral failure: the church had accommodated itself to the caste lines of the world instead of dissolving them.

John Wesley had seen the same thing from inside his own movement. Methodism began in the eighteenth century with open-air preaching to miners and laborers, and by the end of his life Wesley was worried about what it was becoming. In "Thoughts upon Methodism" (1786) he described the paradox that troubled him: true religion makes people industrious and frugal, industry and frugality make them rich, and riches bring pride, love of the world and the slow death of the religion that produced them. He did not know how to stop the cycle except by urging his people to give away everything they could. In America, Methodism went from circuit riders on horseback to the largest Protestant body in the country, and within a few generations to stately brick churches in the best part of town.

The late twentieth century gave the cycle a new twist, because the class that rose was a new kind of class. Christopher Lasch, in *The Revolt of the Elites and the Betrayal of Democracy* (1995), described the professional and managerial elites who had come to dominate American life: highly educated, mobile, attached more to their careers and their networks than to any particular place, confident in their own enlightenment, and quietly contemptuous of the provincial, the religious and the working class. Lasch, who was no conservative in the usual sense, argued that these elites had withdrawn from the common life of the nation while claiming to speak for its victims, and that they had come to treat religion as a matter of private taste or therapy rather than a source of judgment on their own lives. David Brooks gave the same class a gentler portrait in *Bobos in Paradise* (2000), describing educated Americans who had fused the moral seriousness of the counterculture with the ambitions of the professional world, so that status came to be displayed less through wealth than through refined taste and correct opinion.

Michael Sandel added the crucial moral diagnosis in *The Tyranny of Merit* (2020). The meritocracy, he argued, teaches those who rise that they have earned their place, and teaches those who fall behind that they have earned theirs. It produces hubris at the top and humiliation below, and it has made prejudice against the less educated one of the few prejudices still widely tolerated among people who pride themselves on tolerance. Sandel traces this ethic back to Protestant debates about grace and merit, and he notices that the Reformation's insistence that salvation is a gift, not an achievement, was slowly inverted into a work ethic in which success became a sign of deserving. That observation should stop every Christian reader. Meritocracy is works-righteousness applied to social class. It is exactly the thing the gospel of grace was meant to overturn.

## The trade the progressive congregation made

When a congregation is made up almost entirely of that class, the faith tends to be reshaped around its sensibilities. That is not true of every progressive congregation, and some of them confess the creed with more seriousness than their critics do. But where the pattern appears, it is visible in what is kept and what is let go.

What is kept, and often kept with real devotion, are the moral commitments the educated class already honors: care for the refugee, concern for the environment, opposition to racism, the protection of minorities. Many of these commitments are deeply biblical, and nothing here is an argument against them. What tends to go are the parts of the faith that embarrass educated people. The bodily resurrection of Jesus becomes a metaphor for hope. Miracles become stories about community. Judgment, wrath and hell disappear entirely. Personal sin, especially the sins of the respectable, such as pride, ambition, greed and contempt, is rarely named, while the sins of other classes and other political tribes are named often. Evangelism becomes something slightly vulgar, done by other kinds of Christians. And the sexual ethic of the historic church, which asks the educated as much as anyone to give up something they want, is revised in step with the consensus of the class. Those who made that revision would say, often sincerely, that they did it for reasons of Scripture and conscience, and their case deserves its own hearing, which it receives in [Is the Christian View of Sexuality Homophobic?](/writing/christian-sexual-ethic) The narrower point here is that conviction and class conformity become very hard to tell apart when every revision happens to cost the class nothing.

H. Richard Niebuhr, again, saw where this leads. In *The Kingdom of God in America* (1937) he summarized the liberal Protestantism of his day in a sentence that has never been improved: "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross." Every piece of that sentence is a respectable subtraction. Wrath offends modern sensibilities, sin offends self-regard, judgment offends autonomy, and the cross offends everything. Take them away and what remains is a faith that no educated person could object to, and that no poor person, facing death, disease and injustice without the buffers money provides, could possibly lean on.

The apostle Paul was offered the same bargain in Athens and declined it. Standing before the Areopagus, the most distinguished intellectual council in the ancient world, he made his case with care and courtesy, quoting their own poets. Then he reached the resurrection, and the room changed: "Now when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked. But others said, 'We will hear you again about this'" (Acts 17:32, ESV). He could have left out the embarrassing part. He did not. The respectable church, faced with the same mocking, too often decides that the resurrection can be discussed later.

There is a second loss, subtler than the doctrinal one. The professional-class congregation very often serves the poor with genuine generosity, but it serves them across a line. The poor are the objects of its ministry, the recipients of its programs, the subject of its advocacy, but they are not its members, its elders, its preachers or its friends. They come on Tuesday for the food pantry and do not come on Sunday for worship, and no one quite asks why. The difference between serving the poor and belonging with them is the difference between charity and church. The New Testament knows only the second.

## The suburb made the same trade

This essay belongs to a series on the capture of the church by the left, and the progressive congregation is its subject. But it would be a false and flattering essay if it stopped there, because the evangelical church, especially the suburban evangelical church, has made its own version of the same trade, and it did it deliberately.

In *Understanding Church Growth* (1970), the missiologist Donald McGavran set out what became known as the homogeneous unit principle: people are more readily converted when they do not have to cross barriers of race, language or class to become Christians. McGavran drew it from years of watching whole communities come to faith together in India, and there is truth in it; people do hear the gospel more easily from those who share their world. But the American church growth movement turned the observation into a strategy. Congregations were planted and designed for a target demographic, usually the young suburban family, and the music, the preaching, the architecture and the programs were built to make that demographic comfortable. It worked. Many suburban churches grew very large. They grew, in large part, by making sure that the person in the next seat looked, earned and voted like you.

The suburban evangelical church kept the doctrines the progressive church let go: the resurrection, the authority of Scripture, the reality of sin and judgment. But it often let go of what those doctrines require of a community. It preached grace while forming its members in a meritocratic ethic, family by family, school by school, in which success was treated as a sign of God's favor and failure as a sign of poor choices. Richard Reeves, in *Dream Hoarders* (2017), described how the American upper middle class protects its position through exclusionary zoning, legacy admissions, informal internships and other quiet mechanisms that keep opportunity within the family. Those mechanisms are defended as vigorously in evangelical suburbs as in progressive ones. The churches in those suburbs rarely say a word about them. The poor, in many such congregations, exist mainly as a destination for short-term mission trips.

So the two respectable churches mirror each other. One keeps the class and drops the doctrine. The other keeps the doctrine and drops the crossing of class lines that the doctrine demands. Both have made it possible to be a faithful member without ever sitting at the table with someone from the other side of the tracks. And this writer cannot stand outside either. The pull toward respectability is strongest exactly where the faith is argued before educated unbelievers, and it is easy to let the wish to be thought intelligent shape the faith itself: to write for the admired reader, to trim the parts of the creed that sound embarrassing in educated company, to be pleased when the skeptic calls you serious and uneasy when the poor man in the back row doesn't follow the argument. A platform that aims to meet the thoughtful skeptic has to watch for this in itself. It is possible to want the skeptic's respect more than his soul.

## The church James and Paul knew

The New Testament speaks to this with unusual directness. James writes:

> My brothers, show no partiality as you hold the faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory. For if a man wearing a gold ring and fine clothing comes into your assembly, and a poor man in shabby clothing also comes in, and if you pay attention to the one who wears the fine clothing and say, "You sit here in a good place," while you say to the poor man, "You stand over there," or, "Sit down at my feet," have you not then made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts? Listen, my beloved brothers, has not God chosen those who are poor in the world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom, which he has promised to those who love him? But you have dishonored the poor man. Are not the rich the ones who oppress you, and the ones who drag you into court? Are they not the ones who blaspheme the honorable name by which you were called? (James 2:1–7, ESV)

The word translated "partiality" means, literally, receiving the face: judging a person by the surface, by what can be seen. James is not addressing a church that hates the poor. He is addressing a church that has simply absorbed the world's way of seeing, in which the man with the gold ring and fine clothing is obviously the more important visitor. The seating arrangement is the sermon. Where you put people in the assembly tells them what you believe about their worth. And James refuses the quiet assumption beneath it: that the respectable are the natural leaders of the church and the poor its natural clients. God, he says, has chosen the poor to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom. The inheritance is theirs first.

Paul writes to Corinth, a church with real social mixture, and he tells them to look around the room:

> For consider your calling, brothers: not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth. But God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong; God chose what is low and despised in the world, even things that are not, to bring to nothing things that are, so that no human being might boast in the presence of God. (1 Corinthians 1:26–29, ESV)

"Not many" is not "none." Wayne Meeks, in *The First Urban Christians* (1983), showed that the Pauline churches drew from a broad cross-section of urban society, from slaves to householders with some wealth, and that part of what made them strange was that people of such different standing belonged to one body. Pliny the Younger, writing to the emperor Trajan around 112, reported with some alarm that the Christians in his province included people of every age, every rank and both sexes. The scandal of the early church was not that it was poor. It was that it was mixed, and that in its gatherings the ordinary ranking of persons was suspended.

That mixture was hard to sustain, and Corinth shows how quickly it broke down. When the church gathered for the Lord's Supper, some ate well while others went without. Gerd Theissen, in *The Social Setting of Pauline Christianity* (1982), argued that the division ran along social lines, with wealthier members eating their own better food before the poorer ones, who had worked all day, could arrive. Paul's response is among the sharpest in his letters: "What! Do you not have houses to eat and drink in? Or do you despise the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing?" (1 Corinthians 11:22, ESV). A meal that sorted people by class was not, he said, the Lord's Supper at all. The table of the Lord is where the respectable and the poor receive the same bread, as equally undeserving guests. When it becomes a place where class distinctions are displayed, it has stopped being the table of the Lord.

The purpose Paul gives for God's choice is the heart of it: "so that no human being might boast in the presence of God." That is the end of meritocracy, stated in one clause. Whatever the educated have earned, whatever the successful have built, whatever the respectable have achieved, none of it counts as currency before God. And a church that lives by that truth will not be able to maintain the quiet sorting by education, income and taste that both our respectable churches have made their normal life.

## The seat by the door

The respectable church, on the left and on the right, faces a choice that is harder than it sounds. It can go on being admired by the people it most resembles. Or it can become the kind of place where the man in shabby clothing is given the good seat, where the woman who cleans offices is an elder and not only a client, where the resurrection is preached without embarrassment to the lawyer and the laborer together, and where the table of the Lord seats people who would never otherwise eat together.

That second church will be less respectable. It will be noisier, less efficient and harder to manage. It will embarrass its educated members in front of their colleagues, both by what it believes and by who sits beside them. It will require the suburban family to cross lines that its whole way of life was built to avoid, and the progressive professional to receive from the poor instead of only giving to them. It will look, in other words, much more like the church Celsus mocked.

The respectable have always had one advantage in the church: they can afford to believe they are not beggars. The gospel removes that advantage. At the table everyone holds out empty hands, and the hands of the respectable are no fuller than anyone else's. The one who was crucified outside the city, among criminals, with nothing respectable left about him, is the one the church gathers to remember. Every congregation that bears his name has to decide whether it will make room at that table for people it would not otherwise have chosen, and every member of it, including this writer, has to decide whether he can bear to be seated there as one more guest who deserves nothing. The question is still open, in the progressive congregation and the suburban one alike, and the seat by the door is still empty.
