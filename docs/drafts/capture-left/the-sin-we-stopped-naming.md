Ask a certain kind of church a hard question about the doctrine of sin and watch what happens. You will not get silence. You will get fluency. We can name sin at a scale our grandparents never managed — systems, structures, the long arithmetic of history, the way an economy grinds the poor without any single villain pulling the lever. That is a real skill, and most of it is true. But notice the word that has gone quiet underneath all the noise. Sin in the singular. Sin in the second person. Sin as something a person did. Sin as something I did.

I am inside the church I am describing. I helped the word go quiet.

So let me be fair to my own side before I turn on it, because the version worth telling is the strong one. For a long stretch of American Christianity, *sin* meant a short list of private vices — what you drank, what you watched, who you slept with — while the public sins went unnamed from the pulpit. The segregated congregation. The exploited worker. The war blessed without a tremor of conscience. The word had been shrunk until it fit inside other people's bedrooms and never reached our own balance sheets. A generation reacted, and the reaction was right. We enlarged the word. We said sin is bigger than your private habits — it scales, it hides in systems, it poisons the air a whole society breathes. Amos knew this. He stood in a prosperous nation and condemned an entire economy rather than a few greedy men, and told them God hated their festivals and would not glance at their offerings until justice rolled down like water. Recovering that was not a fad. It was obedience.

The claim, stated before it is earned. **Progressive Christianity recovered structural sin, which was a genuine gain, and then lost personal sin, which was a catastrophe — and the recovered half, cut loose from the lost half, has quietly become the most respectable form of self-righteousness the modern church has produced.** Once you see the trade, you cannot unsee it. Sin becomes always what *they* do, what the system does, what the dead did. Never what I am doing at this desk.

## The optimism that outran the evidence

This has happened before, almost beat for beat, and the last time it happened it did not end the way the optimists expected.

The American mainline did not abandon the doctrine of sin in one dramatic act of unbelief. It mislaid it slowly, across the long Protestant afternoon between the Civil War and the First World War, and it mislaid it for reasons that felt like growing up. The confidence of the nineteenth century had trained the educated West to read the human story as a climb — Darwin misread as moral progress, industry misread as providence, a missionary empire misread as the kingdom arriving on schedule. Liberal Protestantism breathed that air. The harsher furniture of the old faith — wrath, judgment, the bondage of the will, the rot native to every heart — came to seem like the cramped theology of a frightened age the species had outgrown. Sin, where it survived in the preaching, softened into ignorance, or maladjustment, a residue of bad environment that better schools and better laws would slowly wash out.

H. Richard Niebuhr fixed the whole drift in one sentence that still draws blood. Looking back in 1937, in *The Kingdom of God in America*, he described the gospel liberalism had built: "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross." Read it slowly. Every clause is a thing quietly removed. No wrath, so no judgment. No sin, so no need of a cross. A religion of uplift with the offense at its center surgically taken out. Niebuhr was not sniping from outside the church. He was a churchman grieving from inside it, naming what his own tradition had done to itself while it believed the whole time it was maturing.

His older brother had drawn the same blade five years earlier and aimed it at the optimism underneath. In 1932 Reinhold Niebuhr published *Moral Man and Immoral Society*, and the title carried the argument. Individuals, he allowed, can sometimes be brought to consider an interest beyond their own. But groups — nations, classes, movements — almost never can. The larger the collective, the more naked its egotism and the more total its self-deception. He had watched a Christian civilization march singing into the trenches of 1914, and pastored autoworkers in Henry Ford's Detroit, and seen what a churchgoing industrialist did to the men on the line. Human evil does not evaporate under sunlight. It organizes. It scales. It learns to wear the face of the common good.

But Niebuhr's deepest charge was not that the liberals were naive about systems. **It was that they had grown naive about the self.** By moving evil out into circumstances and structures — into everything outside the will — the modern reformer had quietly exempted his own heart from the diagnosis. And a reformer who believes his own heart is clean is the most dangerous man in any movement. He has no brake. He has audited the sin in everyone but the auditor. Niebuhr would later call original sin the one Christian doctrine empirically verifiable by a glance at the morning paper, and the one the modern church was most determined to be rid of. We got rid of it. Then the century showed us, in the camps and the purges and the ovens of supposedly Christian Europe, exactly what we had set down.

That is the warning we inherited. We learned half of it.

## The half we kept

We were the children of that correction, and we took the part of it that flattered us.

We seized Reinhold Niebuhr's insistence that sin is structural — that it scales, that it hides in systems, that a society can be guilty in ways no single citizen intends — and we built a whole theology of justice on it, and the theology was largely true. But we let go of the half his brother named, the half that costs. We kept *immoral society*. We lost *moral man* — which in Niebuhr's hands never meant the individual was clean. It meant the individual was the only place where the disease can actually be confessed. We took the doctrine that indicts the system and dropped the doctrine that indicts the one analyzing it. We ran the exact play Niebuhr had warned the liberals against, and we ran it in his name.

Here is the mechanism, and I am describing my own heart, not a stranger's. When sin is only structural, it is never mine. A structure has no soul to examine at midnight. A system cannot kneel. If the locus of sin is the institution, the economy, the inherited arrangement of power, then I am no longer a sinner — I am, at worst, a beneficiary, and at best an ally, and either way the verb has slid off of me and onto something I can study from a clean distance. I get to keep the moral weight of the word while shedding its personal cost. **I get to be against sin without ever being a sinner. It is the most comfortable position in the history of moral thought. We found it. We called it justice.**

Forty years after Reinhold's book, a Kansas psychiatrist noticed the silence and said the obvious thing out loud. In 1973 Karl Menninger published *Whatever Became of Sin?* — a bracing book from a secular clinician who had watched the culture, and the churches inside it, retire the word entirely. Sin had become sickness, or crime, or syndrome, or social pathology; it had become anything that could be assigned to a cause outside the patient and treated without a verdict. Menninger's worry was not theological prudery. It was that a society which can no longer say *I have sinned* has lost the one category that takes responsibility seriously — it has traded the chance of repentance for the comfort of explanation. He was writing to a whole culture. He could have been writing to my congregation, and to me.

## The bend in the will

To see why the personal can never be quietly swapped for the structural, go back to the oldest map of the diseased heart in the Christian tradition.

The Augustinian stream of theology — and the phrase gets pinned on both Augustine and Luther, so hold the attribution loosely — gave the condition its enduring name: *incurvatus in se*, the soul curved in on itself, the will bent inward so that even its loves and its labors and its justice finally bend back toward the self and serve it. This is the engine under everything else. It is not first a matter of bad rules or bad systems, though it builds those by the thousand. It is the original torque in the will, the gravity that turns a person into his own horizon. And the point we have lost is that this curve is not out there in the institution. It is in here, in the one doing the looking — including the one looking with the sharpest eye for injustice. You do not escape the curve by becoming an excellent critic of other people's curve. The critic is curved too. The reformer is curved.

Sixteen centuries later, Cornelius Plantinga built the same truth into a phrase made for our exact evasion. In *Not the Way It's Supposed to Be* (1995), he defined sin as "culpable shalom-breaking" — the willful vandalizing of the way God meant things to flourish. Hold both words, because together they block our trick. *Shalom-breaking* is as wide as the world; it reaches every fractured system, every wrecked economy, every structure that grinds the poor, and it licenses all the structural analysis the prophets ever demanded. But *culpable* keeps a name attached. It keeps a will in the sentence. It will not let the breaking float free of the breaker. Plantinga's whole burden was that the modern church had grown fluent in the breaking and gone silent on the culpability — could describe the wreckage at length and never locate a hand, least of all its own, on the hammer. That is our condition exactly. We have an eloquent account of broken shalom and no one in it who is culpable but the absent and the dead.

## The Pharisee changed parties

Scripture will not let me off this lightly, and the place it pins me is a parable I have preached without flinching.

Two men went up to the temple to pray (Luke 18:9–14). The Pharisee was not wrong about anything visible. He really did tithe. He really did fast. He really was, by every public standard of his day, on the right side. His sin was not in his analysis. It was in his pronoun. *God, I thank you that I am not like other people* — and that is the sentence the modern progressive heart composes in the dark, word for word, only with the list updated. Thank you that I am not like the racists, the colonizers, the fundamentalists, the ones who do not see, the ones still asleep in the pews I walked out of. We keep a running litany of the people we are grateful not to be, we recite it daily, and we have taught ourselves to call it conviction. The other man kept no list. He could not see past himself far enough to compile one. He beat his chest and said *God, be merciful to me, a sinner* — not *be merciful to the unjust structures*, true as that prayer would have been — and Jesus said it was that man, not the structurally literate one, who went home justified.

Notice what indicts us. It is not that the Pharisee faked his virtues. He had them. It is that real virtue became the very ground he stood on to look down. The most religiously serious man in the story goes home unforgiven, and he goes home unforgiven for the one sin that hides perfectly inside being right.

David did not write Psalm 51 about a system either. He wrote it after a night with Bathsheba and a murdered husband, and the line that breaks it open — *against you, you only, have I sinned* — is not a denial that Uriah was wronged. It is the discovery that every horizontal cruelty is the visible end of a vertical betrayal. Paul builds the same trap on purpose. The opening of Romans springs on the reader who is nodding along — first the obvious pagans, then the cultured types who judge them, until the door shuts on everyone: *there is no distinction, for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God* (Romans 3:22–23). No distinction. Not between Jew and Greek, and not between the awake and the asleep, the early adopter and the holdout. And John, writing to a church that already considered itself saved and past the warning, still says it flatly: *if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us* (1 John 1:8). We. Not them. Under all of it sits Jeremiah's verdict on the organ we trust most to certify our innocence: *the heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?* (Jeremiah 17:9). The instrument we use to declare ourselves clean is the instrument most committed to the lie.

## Count the pronouns

There is a tell, and I have learned to listen for it in my own preaching. Listen to how a church talks about sin and count the pronouns.

A church that has kept the doctrine in full will say *we* and mean it, will put the preacher inside the sentence, will leave the room a little quieter than it came in. A church that has lost it will say *they* with great fluency — the systems, the powerful, the other tribe, the unenlightened — and will leave the room not quieter but more certain, more pleased, surer of the line between itself and the guilty. The first is the sound of conviction. The second is the sound of a verdict being read over someone who is not in the room to answer it. I have preached both, and I can feel the difference in my own chest as the words leave it. One costs me something. The other feels wonderful. **Good feelings have always been the cheapest disguise sin puts on.**

This is the place to be exact, because the modern progressive sin does not look like sin. It looks like discernment. It looks like being awake while others sleep. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), named the frame we now all live inside — a way of being in which meaning is generated within history and nothing breaks in from beyond it. Strip the transcendent judge out of that frame and the moral hunger does not die. It relocates. The need to be justified, to stand among the righteous and not the damned, does not evaporate when belief in a final reckoning thins out; it goes looking for a tribunal closer to hand. And the tribunal it finds is the moral consensus of one's own enlightened circle, before which one is acquitted by holding the correct opinions about everyone else's guilt. Call it justification by sociology. You are righteous because you are not like *them*. The Pharisee did not vanish when hell got embarrassing. He moved his prayer indoors, into a frame with no God at the front of it, and went on thanking the room that he was not like other people.

## Justice without repentance

A church that cannot say *I have sinned* can still say a great many impressive things.

It can analyze. It can advocate. It can build a moral vocabulary of real sophistication and aim it at the powerful with courage. What it cannot do is repent. And a church that cannot repent has mislaid the one move the gospel requires — the first word of Jesus' public ministry: *repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near*. Repentance is not analysis. It is not even regret. It is the will, curved inward all its life, being wrenched the other way. You cannot repent of a structure. You can only denounce it. And denunciation, however true, leaves the denouncer exactly where he was — cleaner, in his own eyes, than when he started.

So we built a justice without repentance, and justice without repentance is the old self-righteousness wearing this decade's clothes. The Pharisee did not disappear when the megachurch declined and the culture turned. He read better books. He changed parties. He learned the language of systems and complicity and found in it the perfect hiding place — a sin large enough to indict the whole world and shaped precisely so it could never indict him. He kept the prayer. He only updated the list.

Now hear the other ditch, because it is real and people dive into it with relief. There is a way of recovering personal sin that quietly retires the social kind — that says the only real sin is in the individual heart, so stop talking about systems, stop talking about race, stop talking about the poor, just get people saved. That is not the gospel either. That is the gospel with its arms cut off. Reinhold Niebuhr never said structural sin was a fiction. He said structural and personal sin are the same disease at different scales, and you cannot treat the one while pretending you are immune to the other. The slaveholder and the abolitionist who despised him both needed the cross. They needed it differently. They needed it equally.

So the recovery I am after is not smaller than the one my tradition made. It is larger. A doctrine of sin wide enough to indict the system *and* sharp enough to indict the man analyzing it — me, at this desk, writing this sentence with the quiet satisfaction of someone who has figured something out about everyone else.

Because the trap is closing again, even here. The temptation in writing an essay called *The Sin We Stopped Naming* is to name it in everyone but the author — to make the unnaming itself the new thing I am gratefully not guilty of. *God, I thank you that I am not like the progressives who forgot to repent.* There is no exit from this hall of mirrors that does not run through the first person. I forgot how to say *I have sinned*. Not we, comfortably. Not they, fluently. I.

That is the sin we stopped naming. Not the abstract one — that one we name constantly, at volume, with footnotes. The one with my name on it. And we built a whole theology, sincere and intelligent and partly true, to keep from having to say it out loud.

The publican said five words and went home justified. We have written the libraries and gone home certain. The God who keeps the only record that holds was never impressed by the libraries. He has always been listening for the five words. The church that recovered every sin but its own still has not learned to say them — which means the doctrine of sin, in the end, names the one naming it, and waits.
