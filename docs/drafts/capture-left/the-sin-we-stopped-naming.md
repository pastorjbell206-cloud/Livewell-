There is a word that has gone quiet in a certain kind of church, and I belong to that kind of church, and I have helped it go quiet. The word is *sin*. Not the concept — the concept is everywhere, louder than ever, attached to systems and structures and the long arithmetic of history. We can name sin at scale with real eloquence. What has gone quiet is sin in the singular and the second person. Sin as something a person does. Sin as something I did. We have kept the word for the powerful and the dead and lost it for ourselves, and a doctrine of sin that exempts the person holding it has stopped being the Christian doctrine of sin and become something else. It has become a weapon, and a weapon points outward.

I understand how we got here, because I made the trip.

The doctrine of sin had been abused. That is true and it has to be said first. For a long time, in a wide swath of American Christianity, "sin" meant a short list of private vices — what you drank, what you watched, who you slept with — while the enormous public sins went unnamed from the pulpit, the segregated congregations and the exploited workers and the wars blessed without a tremor of conscience. The word *sin* had been shrunk until it fit comfortably inside other people's bedrooms and never reached our own balance sheets. So a generation reacted. We enlarged the word. We said sin is bigger than your private habits; it is woven into systems, it is structural, it is the air. And that was true. That was a real recovery of something the prophets knew, that Amos knew when he condemned a whole economy and not just a few greedy men.

But a recovery can curdle into an evasion, and ours did.

Here is the mechanism, and I am describing my own heart. When sin is only structural, it is never mine. Structures have no soul to examine at midnight. A system cannot kneel. If the locus of sin is the institution, the economy, the inherited arrangement of power, then I am no longer a sinner — I am, at worst, a beneficiary, and at best an ally, and either way the verb has moved off of me and onto something I can analyze from a safe distance. I get to keep the moral seriousness of the word while shedding its personal weight. I get to be against sin without ever being a sinner. It is the most comfortable position in the history of moral thought, and we found it, and we called it justice.

Reinhold Niebuhr saw the trap a long time before us. Writing in the 1930s and 40s, watching the optimistic liberal Protestantism of his day, he said the genius of the Christian doctrine of original sin was that it was the one Christian doctrine empirically verifiable by a glance at any newspaper — and the one the modern church was most desperate to abandon. He warned that when you locate evil entirely in social systems, you produce reformers who are certain of their own innocence, and a reformer certain of his own innocence is the most dangerous man in any revolution. He has no brake. He has analyzed the sin in everyone but himself.

Scripture will not let me off this lightly. The publican in the temple does not pray *Lord, be merciful to the unjust structures* (Luke 18:13). He beats his breast and says *be merciful to me, a sinner*, and Jesus says that man went home justified and the respectable, structurally-aware Pharisee did not. The Pharisee was not wrong about the structures. He was wrong about himself. And the most chilling line in the parable is not the boast — it is *God, I thank you that I am not like other people*, because that is the exact sentence the progressive heart composes in the dark. Thank you that I am not like the racists, the colonizers, the fundamentalists, the ones who do not see. We have a litany of people we are grateful not to be, and we recite it daily, and we call it conviction.

David did not write Psalm 51 about a system. He wrote it about a night with Bathsheba and a murdered husband, and the line that breaks it open is *against you, you only, have I sinned* (Psalm 51:4) — not because the wrong against Uriah was nothing, but because David finally saw that every sin against a neighbor is first a sin against God, that the horizontal cruelty was the visible end of a vertical betrayal. That is the doctrine of sin in full. It is personal. It is Godward. It is unbearable until it is forgiven, and it cannot be forgiven until it is owned in the first person.

There is a tell, and I have learned to watch for it in my own preaching. Listen to how a church talks about sin and count the pronouns. A church that has kept the doctrine in full will say *we* and mean it, will put the preacher inside the sentence, will leave the room a little quieter than it came. A church that has lost it will say *they* with great fluency — the systems, the powerful, the other tribe — and will leave the room not quieter but more certain, more pleased, more sure of the line between itself and the guilty. The first is the sound of conviction. The second is the sound of a verdict being read about someone who is not present. I have preached both, and I can feel the difference in my own chest as the words leave it: one costs me something, and one feels wonderful.

A church that cannot say *I have sinned* can still say a great many impressive things. It can analyze. It can advocate. It can produce a moral vocabulary of real sophistication. What it cannot do is repent, and a church that cannot repent has lost the one move that the gospel actually requires, the move John the Baptist demanded in the wilderness and Jesus made the first word of his ministry — *repent, for the kingdom is near* (Mark 1:15). We have a justice without repentance. And justice without repentance, I have come to believe, is just the old self-righteousness wearing this decade's clothes. The Pharisee did not stop existing when the megachurch declined. He read better books. He changed parties. He kept the prayer.

I want to be careful not to swing the pendulum into the ditch on the other side, because that ditch is real and I have seen people fall into it gratefully. There is a way of recovering personal sin that quietly retires the social kind — that says, in effect, *the only real sin is in the individual heart, so stop talking about systems, stop talking about race, stop talking about the poor, just get people saved.* That is not the gospel either. That is the gospel with its arms cut off. Niebuhr's point was never that structural sin is fake; it was that structural sin and personal sin are the same disease at different scales, and you cannot treat one while pretending you are immune to the other. The slaveholder and the abolitionist who despised him both needed the cross. They needed it differently. They needed it equally.

So the recovery I am after is not smaller. It is total. It is a doctrine of sin large enough to indict the system *and* personal enough to indict the man analyzing the system — me, at this desk, writing this sentence with the quiet satisfaction of a man who has figured something out about everyone else.

Because that is the trap closing again, even now, even here. The temptation in writing an essay called *The Sin We Stopped Naming* is to name it in everyone but the author, to make the unnaming itself the new thing I am gratefully not guilty of. *Thank you, God, that I am not like the progressives who forgot to repent.* The doctrine of sin is a hall of mirrors and there is no exit that does not run through the first person. I forgot how to say *I have sinned*. Not we. Not they. I.

That is the sin we stopped naming. It was never the abstract one. It was always the one with my name on it, and we built an entire theology, sincere and intelligent and partly true, to keep from having to say it out loud.

The publican said four words and went home justified. We have written libraries and gone home certain. I would trade the libraries.
