There is a word that has gone quiet in a certain kind of church, and I belong to that kind of church, and I have helped it go quiet. The word is *sin*. Not the concept — the concept is everywhere, louder than it has been in a generation, attached to systems and structures and the long arithmetic of history. We can name sin at scale with real eloquence. What has gone quiet is sin in the singular and the second person. Sin as something a person does. Sin as something I did. We kept the word for the powerful and the dead and lost it for ourselves, and a doctrine of sin that exempts the person holding it has stopped being the Christian doctrine of sin and become something else. It has become a weapon. And a weapon points outward.

I understand how we got here, because I made the trip.

Begin with the thing that has to be said first, because it is true and because the rest is a lie without it. The doctrine of sin had been abused. For a long stretch of American Christianity, *sin* meant a short list of private vices — what you drank, what you watched, who you slept with — while the enormous public sins went unnamed from the pulpit. The segregated congregation. The exploited worker. The war blessed without a tremor of conscience. The word had been shrunk until it fit comfortably inside other people's bedrooms and never once reached our own balance sheets. So a generation reacted, and the reaction was holy in its origin. We enlarged the word. We said sin is bigger than your private habits — it is woven into systems, it is structural, it is the very air a society breathes. And that was true. That was a real recovery of something the prophets always knew, something Amos knew when he stood in a prosperous nation and condemned a whole economy rather than a few greedy men, when he said God hated their festivals and would not look at their offerings until justice rolled down like water.

But a recovery can curdle into an evasion. Ours did.

Here is the mechanism, and I am describing my own heart and not a stranger's. When sin is only structural, it is never mine. Structures have no soul to examine at midnight. A system cannot kneel. If the locus of sin is the institution, the economy, the inherited arrangement of power, then I am no longer a sinner — I am, at worst, a beneficiary, and at best an ally, and either way the verb has slid off of me and onto something I can analyze from a clean distance. I get to keep the moral seriousness of the word while shedding its personal weight. I get to be against sin without ever being a sinner. It is the most comfortable position in the history of moral thought. We found it. We called it justice.

To see how old this evasion is, and how exactly it has happened before, you have to go back further than our own argument — back to the last time a confident Protestant church talked itself out of the doctrine of sin and discovered, too late, what it had set loose.

## The Optimism That Outran the Evidence

The American mainline did not lose the doctrine of sin in a single act of unbelief. It mislaid it slowly, across the long Protestant afternoon between the Civil War and the First World War, and it mislaid it for reasons that felt like maturity. The confidence of the nineteenth century had taught the educated West to read the human story as an ascent — Darwin misread as moral progress, industry misread as providence, the missionary expansion of a Christian empire misread as the kingdom arriving on schedule. Liberal Protestantism breathed that air and grew toward it. The harsher furniture of the older faith — wrath, judgment, the bondage of the will, the depravity native to every heart — came to seem like the cramped theology of a frightened age the world had outgrown. Sin, where it survived in the preaching at all, became ignorance, or maladjustment, a residue of bad environment that better schools and better laws would steadily wash out.

H. Richard Niebuhr fixed the whole drift in a single sentence that still draws blood. Looking back in 1937 at the gospel that liberalism had constructed, he wrote that it preached "a God without wrath" who "brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross." Read it slowly. Every clause is a thing quietly removed. No wrath, so no judgment. No sin, so no need of a cross. A religion of uplift and improvement and the steady bettering of the species, with the offense at its center surgically taken out. Niebuhr was not mocking from outside the church. He was a churchman grieving from inside it, naming what his own tradition had done to itself while believing the whole time that it was growing up.

His older brother had drawn the blade five years earlier, and aimed it not at theology but at the optimism underneath it. In 1932 Reinhold Niebuhr published *Moral Man and Immoral Society*, and the title carried the argument. Individuals, he conceded, can sometimes be brought to consider an interest beyond their own. But groups — nations, classes, races, movements — almost never can; the larger the collective, the more naked its egotism and the more total its self-deception. The liberal Protestant hope that an educated, well-meaning Christian society would simply reason and reform its way toward the kingdom struck Niebuhr as sentimentality with the lights off. He had watched a Christian civilization march singing into the trenches of 1914. He had pastored autoworkers in Henry Ford's Detroit and seen what a benevolent, churchgoing industrialist did to the men on the line. The evidence was in, and the evidence was that human evil does not evaporate under sunlight. It organizes. It scales. It wears the face of the common good.

Niebuhr's deepest charge was not that liberalism was naive about systems. It was that liberalism had grown naive about the self. By relocating evil into circumstances and structures — into everything outside the will — the modern reformer had quietly exempted his own heart from the diagnosis. And a reformer who believes his own heart is clean is the most dangerous man in any revolution. He has no brake. He has audited the sin in everyone but the auditor. Niebuhr would later call the doctrine of original sin the one Christian teaching empirically verifiable by a glance at the morning paper — and the one the modern church was most determined to be rid of. We were rid of it. Then the century showed us, in camps and purges and the ovens of supposedly Christian Europe, exactly what we had set down.

That is the warning we inherited and did not learn. We learned half of it.

## The Half We Kept

We were the children of that correction, and we took the part of it that flattered us. We seized Reinhold Niebuhr's insistence that sin is structural — that it scales, that it hides in systems, that a society can be guilty in ways no single citizen intends — and we built a whole theology of justice on it, and the theology was largely true. But we let go of the other half, the half his brother named, the half that costs. We kept *immoral society*. We lost *moral man*, which in Niebuhr's hands had never meant that the individual was clean — it meant the individual was the only place where the disease can actually be confessed. We took the doctrine that indicts the system and dropped the doctrine that indicts the one analyzing the system. We performed the precise maneuver Niebuhr had warned the liberals against, and we performed it in his name.

Forty years after Reinhold's book a Kansas psychiatrist noticed the silence and asked the obvious question out loud. In 1973 Karl Menninger published *Whatever Became of Sin?* — a strange and bracing book from a secular clinician who had watched the culture, and the churches inside it, retire the word entirely. Sin had become sickness, or crime, or syndrome, or social pathology; it had become anything that could be assigned to a cause outside the patient and treated without a verdict. Menninger's worry was not theological prudery. It was that a society which can no longer say *I have sinned* has lost the one category that takes responsibility seriously — has traded the possibility of repentance for the comfort of explanation. He was writing to a whole culture. He could have been writing to my congregation, and to me.

## The Augustinian Bend

To see why the personal can never be quietly traded for the structural, you have to go back to the oldest map of the diseased heart in the Christian tradition, and to the man who drew it.

Augustine gave the condition its enduring name. Sin, he taught, is the soul curved in on itself — *incurvatus in se*, the will bent inward so that even its loves and its labors and its justice finally bend back toward the self and serve it. This is the engine under everything else. It is not first a matter of bad rules or bad systems, though it builds those by the thousand; it is the original torque in the will, the gravity that turns a man into his own horizon. And the point Augustine pressed, the point we have lost, is that this curve is not out there in the institution. It is in here, in the one doing the looking, including the one looking with the sharpest eye for injustice. You do not escape *incurvatus in se* by becoming an excellent critic of other people's curve. The critic is curved too. The reformer is curved. The prophet, off duty, is curved. A doctrine of sin that cannot find the bend in its own author has not found sin at all; it has found a club.

Sixteen centuries later Cornelius Plantinga set the same truth in a sentence built for our exact evasion. In *Not the Way It's Supposed to Be*, his 1995 study of a doctrine he watched the church misplacing, he defined sin as "culpable shalom-breaking" — the willful vandalizing of the way God meant things to flourish. Hold those two words together, because together they refuse our trick. *Shalom-breaking* is as wide as the world; it reaches every fractured system, every wrecked economy, every structure that grinds the poor, and it licenses all the structural analysis the prophets ever demanded. But *culpable* keeps a name attached. It keeps a will in the sentence. It will not let the breaking float free of the breaker. Plantinga's whole burden was that the modern church had grown fluent in the breaking and gone silent on the culpability — could describe the wreckage at length and never locate a hand, least of all its own, on the hammer. That is our condition exactly. We have an eloquent account of broken shalom and no one in it who is culpable but the absent and the dead.

## The Pharisee's Prayer Has a New Translation

Scripture will not let me off this lightly, and the place it pins me is a parable I have preached without flinching.

Two men went up to the temple to pray (Luke 18:9–14). The Pharisee was not wrong about the structures. He really did tithe; he really did fast; he really was, by the visible standard, on the right side of every public question of his day. His sin was not in his analysis. It was in his pronoun. *God, I thank you that I am not like other people* — and there is the sentence the modern progressive heart composes in the dark, word for word, only with the catalogue updated. Thank you that I am not like the racists, the colonizers, the fundamentalists, the ones who do not see, the ones still asleep in the pews I left. We keep a litany of the people we are grateful not to be, and we recite it daily, and we have learned to call it conviction. The publican kept no such list. He could not see past himself far enough to compile one. He beat his breast and said *God, be merciful to me, a sinner* — not *be merciful to the unjust structures*, true as that prayer would have been — and Jesus said it was that man, not the structurally literate one, who went down to his house justified.

Read the parable again and notice what indicts us. It is not that the Pharisee fabricated his virtues. He had them. It is that virtue, real virtue, became the very ground he stood on to look down. The most religiously serious man in the story is the one who goes home unforgiven, and he goes home unforgiven for the one sin that hides perfectly inside being right.

David did not write Psalm 51 about a system either. He wrote it after a night with Bathsheba and a murdered husband, and the line that breaks it open — *against you, you only, have I sinned* (Psalm 51:4) — is not a denial that Uriah was wronged. It is the discovery that every horizontal cruelty is the visible end of a vertical betrayal, that the wreckage a man leaves in the world is the outflow of a will already curved away from God. That is the doctrine of sin in full. Personal. Godward. Unbearable until it is forgiven, and incapable of being forgiven until it is owned in the first person.

Paul leaves no tribe outside the indictment, and he built the letter that way on purpose. The whole opening movement of Romans is a trap that springs on the reader who is nodding along. First the obvious pagans, then the cultured despisers who judge them, until the door shuts on everyone: *there is no distinction, for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God* (Romans 3:22–23). No distinction. Not between Jew and Greek, and not between the woke and the benighted, the early adopter and the holdout, the one on the right side of history and the one history is about to bury. And John, writing to a church that had presumably already been saved and presumably already knew better, still has to say it flatly: *if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us* (1 John 1:8). We. Not them. The first-person plural, aimed at the congregation that thought it was past the warning. And under all of it Jeremiah's old verdict on the organ we trust most to tell us we are clean: *the heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?* (Jeremiah 17:9) — which is to say the very instrument we use to certify our own innocence is the instrument most committed to the lie.

## Count the Pronouns

There is a tell, and I have learned to listen for it in my own preaching. Listen to how a church talks about sin and count the pronouns. A church that has kept the doctrine in full will say *we* and mean it, will put the preacher inside the sentence, will leave the room a little quieter than it came. A church that has lost it will say *they* with great fluency — the systems, the powerful, the other tribe, the unenlightened — and will leave the room not quieter but more certain, more pleased, more sure of the line between itself and the guilty. The first is the sound of conviction. The second is the sound of a verdict being read over someone who is not in the room to answer it. I have preached both, and I can feel the difference in my own chest as the words leave it. One costs me something. The other feels wonderful.

This is the place to be exact about what the modern progressive sin actually is, because it does not look like sin. It looks like discernment. It looks like being awake while others sleep. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), described the frame we all now live inside — a way of being in the world in which all meaning is generated within history and nothing breaks in from beyond it. Strip the transcendent judge out of that frame and the moral hunger does not die; it relocates. The need to be justified, to stand among the righteous and not the damned, does not evaporate when belief in a final assize thins out. It goes looking for a tribunal closer to hand. And the tribunal it finds is the moral consensus of one's own enlightened circle, before which one is acquitted by holding the correct positions about everyone else's guilt. It is justification by sociology. You are righteous because you are not like *them*. The Pharisee did not stop existing when the doctrine of hell got embarrassing. He moved his prayer indoors, into a frame with no God at the front of it, and went on thanking the room that he was not like other people.

## Justice Without Repentance

A church that cannot say *I have sinned* can still say a great many impressive things. It can analyze. It can advocate. It can produce a moral vocabulary of genuine sophistication and deploy it against the powerful with real courage. What it cannot do is repent. And a church that cannot repent has mislaid the one move the gospel actually requires — the move John the Baptist demanded in the wilderness and Jesus made the first word of his public ministry: *repent, for the kingdom of heaven is near*. Repentance is not analysis. It is not even regret. It is the will, curved inward all its life, being wrenched the other way; it is the first-person confession that the problem reaches my own hands. You cannot repent of a structure. You can only denounce it. And denunciation, however true, leaves the denouncer exactly where he was — cleaner, in his own eyes, than when he started.

So we built a justice without repentance, and justice without repentance is the old self-righteousness wearing this decade's clothes. The Pharisee did not vanish when the megachurch declined and the culture turned. He read better books. He changed parties. He learned the language of structures and systems and complicity, and he found in it the perfect hiding place — a sin large enough to indict the whole world and shaped precisely so that it could never indict him. He kept the prayer. He only changed the list.

Now hear the other ditch, because it is real and people fall into it with relief. There is a way of recovering personal sin that quietly retires the social kind — that says, in effect, the only real sin is in the individual heart, so stop talking about systems, stop talking about race, stop talking about the poor, and just get people saved. That is not the gospel either. That is the gospel with its arms cut off. Reinhold Niebuhr's point was never that structural sin is a fiction. It was that structural and personal sin are the same disease at different scales, and you cannot treat the one while pretending you are immune to the other. The slaveholder and the abolitionist who despised him both needed the cross. They needed it differently. They needed it equally.

So the recovery I am after is not smaller than the one my tradition made. It is larger. It is a doctrine of sin wide enough to indict the system *and* sharp enough to indict the man analyzing the system — me, at this desk, writing this sentence with the quiet satisfaction of someone who has figured something out about everyone else.

Because that is the trap closing again, even here, even now. The temptation in writing an essay called *The Sin We Stopped Naming* is to name it in everyone but the author — to make the unnaming itself the new thing I am gratefully not guilty of. *God, I thank you that I am not like the progressives who forgot to repent.* The doctrine of sin is a hall of mirrors, and there is no exit that does not run through the first person. I forgot how to say *I have sinned*. Not we, comfortably. Not they, fluently. I.

That is the sin we stopped naming. It was never the abstract one — that one we name constantly, at volume, with footnotes. It was always the one with my name on it. And we built an entire theology, sincere and intelligent and partly true, to keep from having to say it out loud.

The publican said five words and went home justified. We have written the libraries and gone home certain. The God who keeps the only record that holds was never impressed by the libraries. He has always been listening for the five words. And the church that recovered every sin but its own still has not learned to say them — which means the doctrine of sin, in the end, names the one naming it, and waits.
