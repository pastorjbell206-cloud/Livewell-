## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** The resurrection is a historical claim, and I examined it as an enemy

**Body:**

Christianity is the one religion that cannot retreat into metaphor. Paul
said so himself, writing around the year 54: if Christ has not been raised,
the whole thing is worthless and the preachers should be pitied. No
fallback. No good-teacher consolation prize. A specific man, dead on a
Friday, alive on a Sunday — or nothing.

This week's essay treats that claim the way I treated it when I was an
atheist: as a question of evidence. It starts with the facts nearly all
serious scholars grant, believing and unbelieving alike. It dates the
earliest resurrection creed to within a few years of the event — no time
for legend. It takes the rival explanations seriously — hallucination,
fraud, the wrong tomb — because they deserve it, and shows where each one
rescues one corner of the evidence and leaves the rest exposed. And it names
the move underneath most educated unbelief, the one I made for years:
ruling the miracle out before examining anything, and calling the ruling
rigor.

Not a proof. History does not deliver proofs about anything. The claim is
narrower and harder: the best available explanation of facts that do not go
away when you stop believing.

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

I did not come to the evidence for the resurrection as a friend of it. I
came as an enemy, certain the whole thing was a legend that grew the way
legends do — slowly, comfortably, long after anyone could check.

Then I looked at the dates. The earliest statement of the resurrection is
older than any gospel — a creed already circulating, in fixed form, while
the eyewitnesses were still alive to be asked.

"I had thought my unbelief was the result of looking. It was the result of
not looking, dressed up as looking."

This week's essay is the looking. [link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

For years I held it as an axiom: no evidence could establish a miracle, so
any natural explanation — however strained — wins by default.

Then I noticed what the axiom does. It rules the resurrection out before
examining the evidence, by assuming the very thing in question and calling
the assumption rigor.

"I no longer think it is reasoning; I think it is a decision dressed as
reasoning."

The evidence has to be weighed on the table, not swept off it in advance.

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

Two travelers on the road out of Jerusalem, the first Easter, walking beside
a stranger they do not recognize. Their honest summary of the weekend is in
the past tense: "we had hoped he was the one to redeem Israel." Had hoped.

That is not what propaganda sounds like. That is what dead hope sounds like
— and some of you are carrying it tonight. A prayer that was not answered. A
person who did not stay. A faith that was supposed to hold and did not.

The record of that first weekend is not a parade of certainty. It is
confused women, dismissive men, and mourners being dragged, resistant, back
toward a hope they had already buried. Whatever you decide about the
resurrection, know this much: the story has room in it for people whose hope
has died. It starts with them. [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — somewhere in your congregation is a man who has sat through years
of Easters while privately assuming the historians settled this against us
long ago. He has never read Wright, never weighed the creed in 1 Corinthians
15; he inherited a verdict and called it research. I know, because I did the
same from the other side — I was the skeptic who had decided the answer
without doing the work the question deserved. The essay I published this
week builds the case from facts skeptical scholars already grant, takes the
rival explanations at full strength, and concedes the limit out loud: "You
may examine all of this and still conclude it did not happen. That is an
intellectually respectable place to stand, and I will not pretend
otherwise."

That concession is why you can hand it over without wincing. Give it to the
engineer who thinks apologetics is salesmanship, the son-in-law who reads
history, the college student who has only ever heard the legend theory. It
does not corner anyone. It puts the evidence on the table and trusts the
reader with it — which is more respect than most of them have ever been
shown by a church. [link]
