## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** The care runs one direction and never comes back

**Body:**

A man stands at the back door on Sunday and shakes two hundred hands. He
remembers the surgery, the layoff, the wayward son, and files each one away
to pray over on Monday. Then he drives home, and no one asks him anything.

This week's essay is about that man. Pastoral loneliness is not a
personality flaw in a few unlucky men. It is the predictable output of a
vocation built so the pastor is always the one who checks and never the one
checked on. It was not always so. The desert fathers, the Celtic soul
friend, the Puritan circle of brethren all assumed the shepherd was also a
sheep, and that somebody was appointed to know it. We kept the giving and
dropped the receiving, inside a single century.

The essay sits a long while with Elijah under the broom tree, where God's
first response to a burned-out man was sleep and bread — not a rebuke, not
a plan. And it is honest about my own hands in the problem: I have praised
another man's exhaustion because praising it was easier than caring for
him.

If you are the man at the back door, this was written so you would hear
your own name in a caring sentence this week. If you love a pastor, it will
tell you what to ask him, and how to wait for the true answer.

Grace and peace,
James

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

We pastors built this together. We celebrated the man who never took a day
off. We made tired a synonym for faithful. We told the young guy entering
ministry that the work would cost him everything, and we said it like a
recruiting pitch instead of a warning.

"I have praised exhaustion in another man because praising it was easier
than asking whether he was alright." [link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

He shook two hundred hands this morning. He remembered the surgery, the
layoff, the wayward son. Then he drove home and no one asked him anything.

His calendar is full of people. "And he is starving in the middle of the
crowd, because attention is not the same as care, and being needed is not
the same as being known." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

To the pastor reading this on a Sunday night, after the most crowded hour
of your week, in a quiet nobody suspects:

Elijah sat under the broom tree right after the greatest public victory of
his career, and God's first response was not a rebuke and not a plan. It
was sleep and bread. "The angel did not ask Elijah to be stronger. The
angel asked him to eat."

If tonight is heavier than anyone around you knows, do not carry it alone
into another week. Call the one pastor who will not talk shop, or sit down
with a counselor, and say the true sentence first. [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — no hand-off this week. The essay is about us, so this note is me
checking on you, not giving you something for your people. Let me ask the
question the essay says nobody asks, and let me wait for the true answer.
How are you? Not attendance, not giving, not the building. You. The essay
says that when we are depleted enough, our reading of our own situation
cannot be trusted — Elijah was certain he was the only one left, and he was
wrong by seven thousand. If you have been doing that math lately, the
numbers are off. They are always off in that chair.

If the honest answer is heavier than the one you give on reflex, do not
perform for me. Reply to this note, or call the man in this network you
trust, or sit down with a counselor and say the true sentence out loud —
and if the weight has turned into something darker than tired, tell someone
today, not after one more Sunday. This network exists so that no man in it
sits under the broom tree alone. Somebody fed Elijah. Let somebody feed
you. [link]
