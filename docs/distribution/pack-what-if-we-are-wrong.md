## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** What if Christianity is wrong — I have asked it too

**Body:**

Every believer I know has met the question, usually at two in the morning,
usually without warning. What if the whole thing is a story frightened people
told themselves so they could bear the dark. Most pastors will not admit from
a stage that they have asked it. I have. I came to faith from atheism, and
the question is not a visitor in my house; it is the house I grew up in.

So this week's essay asks it all the way down, without flinching. It refuses
the assumption that doubt is the neutral ground — the skeptic is wagering
too, on claims as large as any creed, and he cannot prove one of them. It
plants its feet at a tomb outside Jerusalem, because that is where the whole
structure stands or falls. And it turns to the morality the unbeliever
cannot stop believing anyway — the worth of the weak, the evil of cruelty in
the dark — and asks where that came from, and what it costs to keep it once
the foundation is gone.

No checkmate at the end. A wager nobody escapes, and an honest accounting of
both sides of the bill.

Grace and peace,
James

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

What if Christianity is wrong.

I want to say something most pastors will not say from a stage: I have asked
it. Not once, at a safe distance, years ago. There are still mornings when,
for a few seconds, the whole thing looks like wishful thinking, and I have
learned not to panic at those seconds.

"I came to faith from atheism, so the question is not a foreign army to me;
it is my own first language, and it never fully leaves."

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

The question assumes that doubt is the neutral ground — that the skeptic
stands on bedrock and the believer floats out over the void.

But to say the universe is finally meaningless, that love is a trick of
chemistry, that justice is a preference with no foundation outside the skull
— these are claims as large as any creed, and no one can prove them either.
The skeptic wagers his one life on the tomb staying shut. The believer wagers
his on it opening.

"Both of you are betting your whole existence on something you cannot prove.
Only one of you is honest about it." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

If you are awake tonight with the question — what if we are wrong — I am not
going to tell you to sing louder until it stops.

A suppressed doubt only goes underground. Hold it out loud instead, the way
the psalmists held theirs, addressed to a God you are not yet sure is
listening.

Fifteen years of pastoring has shown me this much: "The people whose faith
finally held were never the ones who never doubted." They were the ones who
took the question to what they thought was the bottom and found it was not
the bottom.

You are afraid the question will end your faith. It might end a version of
it — the inherited one, the sheltered one that was always going to break
anyway. [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — our people type this question into their phones when the house is
asleep, and then sit in our pews on Sunday performing a certainty they do not
have. Most of what we offer them treats the doubt as an attack to be
repelled, and the result is predictable: it goes underground and rots into
the thing that walks them out the door at fifty. I know the question from
the inside; it was my first language before it was theirs. The essay I
published this week asks it at full strength — Taylor's cross-pressure, the
tomb, the morality nobody can stop believing — and refuses to kill it with a
quick apologetic, because "sheltered faith breaks the first time the weather
turns — usually at a funeral, usually too late to find out it was never
load-bearing."

Hand it to the member who confessed a doubt in your office and flinched
while doing it, the elder's daughter home from college, the man who has
stopped singing but not yet stopped coming. It will not shame them for
asking, and it will not pretend the question is small. It takes them to the
floor and lets them find out whether the floor holds. [link]
