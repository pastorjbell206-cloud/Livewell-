## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** The case against the Bible is mostly a story about the Bible

**Body:**

The objections I hear most are not objections to the Bible. They are
objections to a story about the Bible — the telephone game, the council
that voted Jesus into godhood, the contradictions that unravel everything.
I know the story well. I lived inside it for years, and it was load-bearing
for my unbelief.

This week's essay checks the story against the scholarship, much of it
produced by non-Christians. The telephone game turns out to be the wrong
picture: thousands of branching manuscripts are how errors get caught, not
how they hide. The canon was not decided by decree; it emerged from below,
from scattered churches recognizing the books they were already reading and
dying for. And the contradictions at the edges of the gospels turn out to
be what independent testimony always looks like — witnesses who differ on
the number of angels and converge on the tomb.

None of this proves the Bible true, and the essay says so more than once.
It does something smaller and less comfortable: it takes away the excuses.
What is left is a book that has to be actually read — and most of the people
most confident about it never have.

Grace and peace,

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

For years I rejected the Bible without reading it. I did not need to read
it — I had the story about it. Garbled by centuries of copyists. Assembled
by a council with political motives. Contradicting itself on every page.

Then I checked the story, and the story collapsed. That did not make me a
Christian. But it left me facing what my unbelief had actually been made of:

"I had simply transferred my faith from the Bible to the secular folklore
about the Bible, and never noticed I had believed something on worse
evidence than the thing I was rejecting."

This week's essay checks the whole story, piece by piece. [link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

The gospels do not agree on every detail, and pretending otherwise is a
kind of lying that does the Bible no favors.

But historians know something about testimony: witnesses who agree on
everything have colluded. Independent witnesses differ at the edges and
converge at the center — and that is exactly the pattern the gospels show.
They differ on the number of angels. They do not differ on the crucifixion,
the burial, the empty tomb.

"The variation at the periphery is evidence of independence, not evidence
of fraud." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

Maybe you walked away from the Bible years ago, and the walking felt
clear-eyed — the refusal to be fooled. I remember the feeling. Mine turned
out to be built on stories about the book that I had never once checked.

I am not asking you to trust it tonight. Trust is not the first step.

"The book is not asking to be trusted blindly. It is asking to be opened."

Read it the way you would read a witness you suspected of telling the
truth. That is all. What happens after that is between you and what you
find. [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — when someone in your congregation tells you their brother or
their daughter no longer believes the Bible, it is almost never the Bible
they disbelieve. It is the telephone game, the council of bishops, the
folklore — a story about the book, absorbed from the culture and never
checked. I disbelieved that way for years, and I can tell you the folklore
does not survive contact with the manuscript evidence, the actual history
of the canon, or the scholarship on eyewitness testimony. The essay I
published this week walks through all of it and keeps saying the thing
careless apologetics never says: none of this proves the Bible true. It
clears the ground so the real question can finally be asked.

That restraint is what lets it cross a dinner table without a fight. Give
it to the parent whose son quotes internet skepticism, the small-group
member who whispered that the contradictions trouble her, the coworker your
deacon has been afraid to answer. It ends where the honest conversation
starts, with the question the confident despiser has usually never faced:
"Have you actually read it, with the attention you would give a hostile
witness you suspected of telling the truth?" [link]
