## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** Most marriages die from a draft, not a wound

**Body:**

Nobody circles the day on a calendar, because there was no day. There was an
evening you were tired and looked at your phone instead of the sentence
being said to you, and nothing happened. No fight. No consequence. And then
there were ten thousand evenings like it.

This week's essay is about marriage drift — the quiet distance that ends
more marriages than affairs do, and the reason it is so hard to fight: it
does not feel like anything while it happens. Nobody is cruel. Nobody
betrays anyone. Two competent people raise the kids and pay the mortgage
and become roommates who share a name, and when you ask them what happened,
they cannot tell you, because nothing happened. That was the problem.

But the essay is a map back, not a diagnosis left on the table. A thing
built out of small turnings away can only be rebuilt the same way — by
small turnings toward, none of which will feel significant on the day you
make them. You put down the phone for the sentence. You ask the question
and stay for the whole answer. It will not feel like much. That is how the
real repair works, and why the grand gesture, which is what we all reach
for first, was never going to.

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

There is no moment I can point to where I chose distance from the people I
love. There were only the moments I chose the urgent over the present — the
email over the eyes across the room, the productive evening over the
unproductive conversation that was actually the marriage.

I did not betray anyone. I was simply not there, repeatedly, with a clear
conscience. And absence with a clear conscience is the most dangerous kind.

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

"Most marriages do not die from a wound. They die from a draft."

Not the affair. Not the discovered text. Ten thousand ordinary Tuesdays,
each one a small turn away, and not one of them felt like a decision at the
time.

"The betrayer at least knows he has done something. The drifter feels like
a hard worker." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

If the quiet in your house has a shape you recognize, hear this first:
nothing is wrong with you that has not been wrong with most of us, and the
distance that arrived by small turnings can leave by small turnings. You
turn toward the sentence instead of the phone. You stay for the whole
answer. It will not feel like much on the day, and that is not a sign it
failed. The drift was invisible while it happened. So is its undoing.

"The vineyard does not need a hero. It needs a keeper." [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — the couples who worry me most are not the ones fighting. The
ones fighting are still turned toward each other, still convinced the other
person's answer matters enough to raise a voice over. The ones who worry me
are calm — competent, pleasant, exhausted people who have made a quiet truce
with distance and named it maturity, and who will never appear in a
counseling queue because nothing looks wrong. This week's essay names their
condition. It puts Gottman's turning-toward research and the old Hebrew word
*shamar* — to keep, to guard, to stay awake over — in the same room, and it
refuses the false comfort that drift is anyone's villainy. It is neglect
with a clear conscience, which is why it wins, and I write about it as a
man who knows the pull of the urgent over the present in his own house.

Hand it to the couple whose answer, every time you ask, is that they are
busy and fine. It works in premarital counseling as prevention, and it
works at an anniversary milestone as recovery, because it asks for nothing
dramatic — only the small daily turning, resumed. And it ends in hope
rather than alarm, which is the only weight a tired couple can actually
carry home from church. [link]
