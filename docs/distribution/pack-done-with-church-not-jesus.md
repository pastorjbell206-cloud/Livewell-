## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** You are not a problem the church needs to solve

**Body:**

There is a sentence people say to me in parking lots and coffee shops,
lowered, almost under their breath. I still believe. I just can't do the
building anymore. They brace for the argument, because the last time they
said it, somebody handed them a verse and changed the subject.

This week's essay does not make the argument. It starts with how ordinary
you are: the largest study ever done on this found around forty million
American adults gone from church in a quarter-century, and most of them did
not stop believing. They still pray. They still believe Jesus rose. They
drifted, or they moved, or they were burned, and Sunday kept not happening
until the not-going hardened into a life.

The essay is honest about what I have watched happen to a faith carried
alone — no crisis, no argument that knocks anyone over, just the slow
cooling nobody warns you about. And it is just as honest that I am not
asking you to walk back into the room that hurt you. Nobody gets to rush a
wounded person across a threshold. But the ache that makes you lower your
voice when you say you still believe is older than your leaving, and it
deserves a truer name than guilt.

Grace and peace,
James

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

Some of you have said it to me in a parking lot, lowered like a confession.
I still believe. I just can't do the building anymore. And you braced for
the argument, because last time somebody handed you a verse and changed the
subject.

I am not going to argue with you. "You are done with church. I understand.
I have been done with it too, and I am a pastor." [link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

Around forty million American adults have stopped attending church in a
single generation, and most of them did not stop believing. They drifted,
or they were burned, and Sunday kept not happening.

"The faith you are carrying by yourself right now is a coal you pulled out
of the fire. It is still glowing. I am not denying the glow." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

To the one who still prays, still believes Jesus rose, and cannot walk back
through the door:

"I think a lot of you are running toward the God you found, and away from
the people who handled him carelessly. That is not a contradiction. That is
grief."

Nobody gets to rush you across a threshold, and this essay does not try.
But the ache you keep lowering your voice to admit is not the residue of an
old habit. David wrote it down in Psalm 27, in a cave, surrounded by the
men who had refused to leave him. It has a name older than your leaving.

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — the person this essay is for is mostly not in your pews. They
are the ones who told you, almost under their breath, that they still
believe but cannot do the building anymore, and then braced for the
argument. The essay does not make the argument. It tells them how ordinary
they are — forty million gone in a generation, most still believing — it
takes the wound and the relief both seriously, and it says the one thing a
solitary faith needs said without shame: a coal pulled out of the fire
cools by physics, not by verdict. It never once asks them to come back.

Hand it to the person who said that sentence to you. No invitation
attached, no follow-up planned. It asks them to walk through no door, which
is exactly why they may read it to the end, and it leaves them with Psalm
27 instead of a guilt trip. If it does its work, the next conversation will
not be one you have to start. [link]
