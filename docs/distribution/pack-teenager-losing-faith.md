## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** You cannot argue your child back to faith

**Body:**

It usually comes out flat, across the kitchen, with about the same weight as
a comment about dinner: *I don't think I believe this anymore.* And
everything in you wants to do one of two things in the next three seconds —
argue them back, or grieve them gone.

This week's essay is for those three seconds. Both instincts come from
love, and both will fail, because the sentence your teenager just said is
not a thesis and it is not a death. It is a door. What is being tested in
that kitchen is not the case for the resurrection. It is whether your love
was ever unconditional, or only the reward for agreement.

I wrote it as a pastor who has sat with these parents, and as a man who
spent years as the one not believing. Nobody argued me home. The essay
walks through what to actually say at the table, which of two fears is
driving you — they feel identical and produce opposite parents — and why
most returns, when they come, come not because someone finally won the
argument but because there was still a home worth returning to. Not on your
schedule. But not never.

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

Every parent who hears *I don't believe anymore* wants to do one of two
things in the next three seconds: argue the child back, or grieve the child
gone. Both instincts come from love. Both of them will fail.

I say that as a pastor who has sat with these parents, and as a man who
spent years as the one not believing. Nobody argued me home. Somebody
stayed reachable. [link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

Your instinct is to reach for evidence — the historical case, the
fine-tuning, the documentary you saw. You may be right on every point. You
will still lose, because your teenager did not present a thesis. They ran a
test: is my honesty safe here.

"You won the point and lost the room." And the room was the only thing that
was ever going to bring them back. [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

To the parent lying awake tonight replaying the kitchen: you are allowed to
grieve, fully. You are not allowed to hand the grief to your child to carry.
A teenager who learns that their honesty devastates you does the only kind
thing left — they hide, and perform belief at the dinner table, and take the
real self underground. Their honesty, whatever else it is, is a door still
open.

"Keep the table set. Keep the porch light on. Keep loving them past the
point where it makes you feel like you are accomplishing anything, because
the point where it stops feeling effective is exactly the point where it
starts being love." [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — in every one of our congregations there are parents carrying
this in silence, ashamed to raise a hand in the one room where they most
need to, because a child's unbelief feels like a public grade on their
parenting. This week's essay is for them. It separates the two fears — fear
for the child's soul and fear of what the unbelief says about us, which
feel identical from the inside and produce opposite parents. It takes the
teenager's honesty seriously instead of treating it as an attack, sorts the
kind of deconstruction that is demolition from the kind that is a young
adult pulling on the beams to find out what holds, and puts Monica's long
patience with Augustine beside the father in Luke 15 who ran before the
speech was finished.

Hand it quietly. This is less a piece for the youth-parent meeting than for
the couple who lingered after the service with something on their faces. It
will not give them a formula, because there is none, and it refuses to
promise a timeline, because nobody honest can. What it gives them is a way
to stay — unanxious, reachable, undefeated — which is the one thing
entirely within their power, and the thing their child will remember when
the running slows. [link]
