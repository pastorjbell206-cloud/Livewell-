## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** The stores used to close on Sunday

**Body:**

A man I know turned seventy this year, and he can remember when every store
in his town closed on Sunday. The hardware store, the bank, the diner.
Nobody voted on it. Nobody had to. It was simply the air.

That air is gone. And this week's essay argues that its disappearance is the
most important fact about the church in the West — more important than any
election, any scandal, any worship style we keep fighting about.

But here is the claim that will bother both the anxious and the triumphant:
what is ending is not the Christian faith. What is ending is Christendom —
the seventeen-century arrangement in which you did not become a Christian,
you simply were one. Those two things were never the same, and the essay
traces how they got confused: Constantine, Theodosius, the long middle, and
the strange fact that the church grew fastest in the centuries when it had
no power at all.

I wrote it for the man grieving the closed stores, and for his grandchildren
who never saw them. A world is ending. The faith is not.

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

"What is ending is not the Christian faith. What is ending is Christendom,
and the two were never the same thing, though we spent seventeen centuries
confusing them."

The question is no longer whether the West will be Christian by default. It
will not be. The question is whether the church will remember how to be the
church when nothing in the air agrees with it anymore. [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

For its first three centuries the church had no power at all. It was
illegal, hunted, socially suspect — slaves and women and tradesmen meeting
in houses. And it grew anyway. Not through strategy. Through the sheer
strange attractiveness of transformed lives.

"We are not the first Christians to live without the empire's help. We are
only the first in a very long time to have to remember how." [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — half our congregations are anxious about decline and the other
half are pretending it is not happening, and both halves are working from
the same mistake: they think the faith and the culture's approval of it were
the same thing. This week's essay separates them. It gives the whole arc —
the Milvian Bridge, the Edict of Thessalonica, the long middle, the
emptying — and lands somewhere neither nostalgia nor panic can reach: the
church before Constantine and the church after Christendom are closer kin to
each other than either is to the centuries in between.

This one preaches. It would seed a series on exile without much effort, and
it is the piece I would put in front of an elder board that keeps asking how
to get the culture back. The honest answer is that we are not getting it
back, and that this is survivable — the early church would recognize our
situation better than our grandfathers would. [link]
