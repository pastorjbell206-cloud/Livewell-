## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** Your congregation is listening for your jersey

**Body:**

There is a moment, early in almost every sermon now, when the room decides
what team you are on. You have not taken a side. You said a word — justice,
order, the poor, the unborn — and one section of the congregation leaned in
while another braced.

This week's essay is for the ones who stand in that room, and it starts with
a confession, because I have given in to both of the temptations it names. I
have preached the sermon that flattered my own instincts and called it
courage, because the warmth coming back felt like fruit. And I have ducked a
text I should have preached and called it prudence. One temptation makes you
a chaplain to a tribe. The other makes you a chaplain to no one.

The way through is older than the fight. It runs from Paul's refusal to
shrink the whole counsel of God, through Brueggemann's poets, to Nathan
standing in front of David with a story about a lamb — the most disciplined
sermon to a hostile room in Scripture. And it ends with a single editing
question I now ask of any manuscript that touches something contested. The
question costs me the easy applause every time I ask it. It is worth more
than the applause.

Grace and peace,

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

Two temptations meet every preacher in a politically divided room, and I have
given in to both. I have preached the sermon that flattered my own instincts
and called it courage. And I have ducked a text I should have preached and
called it prudence.

One makes you a chaplain to a tribe. The other makes you a chaplain to no
one. Both feel like wisdom from the inside. This week's essay is about the
third way — the one Nathan took into the throne room. [link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

"The applause of the half that agrees with you is the most dangerous sound
in ministry, because it feels exactly like fruitfulness and is usually its
opposite."

The test of whole-counsel preaching runs over a year, not a Sunday. Does
every section of the divided room get wounded, and does every section get
comforted, and can anyone predict which Sunday will be which from your
politics.

"If they can predict it, you have a tribe. If they cannot, you may have a
pulpit." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

The man who has gone rigid about the border is often a father terrified for
his children's future. The woman who has gone rigid the other way is often a
daughter who has seen real cruelty and cannot bear to see it ignored. They
hardened in opposite directions for the same reason.

"Underneath nearly every hardened political conviction in your congregation
is a fear, and the fear is where the gospel actually does its work."

Preach to the position and you get a fight. Preach to the fear and you get a
hearing — because only the gospel is large enough to hold it. [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — the room sorts itself by the third sentence now, and most of us
have answered it with one of two derelictions. We picked a side and called
the applause fruitfulness, or we went quiet and walled off whole books of
the Bible because they would be heard as a vote. I have done both. This
week's essay is the work I am still learning to do without flinching toward
either ditch: Paul's whole counsel set against the itching ear, Brueggemann's
poet set against the flat two-team map the broadcasters handed our people,
and Nathan's lamb — four verses that show how a captured king was brought to
sentence himself before he knew he was the defendant. The essay holds both
tribes to the same standard and grieves for both, which is the only way any
of this earns a hearing.

The test inside it will cost you what it costs me: over a year, every
section of your room wounded, every section comforted, and no one able to
predict which Sunday will be which from your politics. It would serve an
elder board or a preaching cohort well, and it is the piece I would put in
front of a young preacher before an election year — not to make him bolder
or safer, but to make him harder to scout. Hand it to anyone who stands up
on Sunday in front of a sorted room. [link]
