## Substack issue — Tuesday

**Subject:** The church grew fastest when it had no power

**Body:**

By the year 300 the Christian movement had no army, no offices, no schools,
no press — and it had so saturated the Roman world that an emperor found it
easier to join than to crush. This week's essay sits with that fact,
because the Western church is being handed back the exact conditions under
which the faith first spread.

Rodney Stark's numbers say the growth was steady, relational, unhurried.
And they say something harder about why people stayed: when plague emptied
the cities, the pagans fled and the Christians remained at the bedsides of
strangers, often dying there, and the watching world drew its own
conclusion about what these people had. Alan Kreider adds that the early
church's defining trait was patience — not a tactic but a character, formed
in people who believed the work was God's and did not have to force the
outcome.

The essay is written toward the instinct I know best because I share it:
the reflex that reads the loss of our standing as the loss of the faith's
future, and reaches for the nearest lever. Paul asked three times for his
weakness to be removed. The answer was no, and the answer was better than
the request. I think the Western church is praying Paul's prayer right now.
The essay asks what it would mean to hear Paul's answer.

## Facebook post 1 — Wednesday (the confession)

Every time the church senses its power slipping, the loudest voices call
for more power — capture the institutions, win the elections, reclaim the
culture. It is the most natural instinct in the world.

"We keep failing this test, and I include myself and my own tribe in the
failure." The early church never had this instinct, because it had no power
to be tempted by. Ours is going. Now we find out what we actually trust.
[link]

## Facebook post 2 — Friday (the verdict)

The math of the empire said the Christians should have died out. They
multiplied.

"The early church conquered Rome by refusing to fight it on Rome's terms.
It out-loved, out-suffered, and out-died an empire that had every
advantage." [link]

## Facebook post 3 — Sunday evening (the pastoral turn)

To the pastor who can feel the standing going — the deference, the
microphone, the seat at the table — and who reads the loss, some nights, as
a verdict on his calling:

"A pastor after Christendom is not a public figure managing the church's
influence. He is a person forming a people whose shared life is the
argument. That is a smaller role and a truer one, and I am still, most
days, learning to want it." [link]

## PCN note — Thursday

Brothers — this one is for the anxiety underneath our sermons. Most of us
were formed for a church with standing, and we are pastoring the decades in
which the standing is being taken away, while the loudest counsel around us
says to get it back by any lever available. The essay takes the first three
centuries at full strength — Stark's plague-era numbers, Kreider's patient
ferment, Paul's thorn — and asks whether the power we are grieving was ever
the thing carrying the witness, or the thing that nearly cost us the
gospel.

Hand it to your elders before the next anxious meeting about decline, or to
the member who is certain the faith is one election away from extinction.
It will not tell them the losses are painless. It will tell them the
conditions we are being handed are the ones under which the church did its
best work, and that patience is not passivity — it is confidence about
whose work this is and whose timing governs it. [link]
