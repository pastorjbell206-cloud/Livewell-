# The Pattern Report

> Deliverable 5 of the Corpus Audit. After reading the corpus: what does this
> writer — and this platform's publishing process — do when tired? Three
> patterns, named so the next hundred essays avoid them at the desk instead of
> the audit. Ordered by how much damage each does.

## Pattern 1 — The stub published as an essay (the process failure, and the big one)

Half the non-lead corpus (154 of 299 essays) is a catalog abstract standing in
for the piece it advertises: a title, a live URL, and 20–60 words of "This
article explores…" where the essay should be. This is not a writing failure; it
is a **publishing** failure — placeholders shipped to production and indexed.
The damage is concentrated exactly where it hurts most: a skeptic follows a
shared link to `ai-authenticity-pastor-artificial-intelligence` or
`when-god-bless-america-replaces-thy-kingdom-come` and finds nothing to read.
One such landing undoes the trust ten real essays earned.

**The fix at the desk:** nothing is "published" until it has a body that passes
the Formation Test. A length guard (a body under ~600 characters cannot enter
the sitemap, the listings, or the distribution triage) makes the failure
impossible to ship silently. Draft stubs are fine; *indexed* stubs are the
problem.

## Pattern 2 — The borrowed line worn as your own (the trust failure)

When the writing reaches for borrowed eloquence, attribution is the first thing
that slips. Three shapes of it recur: a **famous line absorbed as the writer's
own** (`the-long-arc` opens with Parker-via-King's "arc of the moral universe"
with no attribution); a **Scripture allusion mis-sourced** (the marriage lead
put Song of Solomon's little foxes in Jesus' mouth — now fixed); and a
**quotation mark around words that match no translation and cite no reference**
(`stranger-at-gate`). Each is rare, but each is the one unforgivable move in
front of the Bible-literate skeptic the platform writes toward — the reader who
will catch it and never forgive it. Across 313 essays this surfaced only a
handful of times, but the cost per instance is total.

**The fix at the desk:** every quotation gets its reference before it gets its
polish; every famous cadence gets its author named or gets cut; ESV is the
default and the translation is named only when a choice is doing work. When a
citation cannot be verified, it comes out — the hole is safer than the guess.

## Pattern 3 — The tic and the soft landing (the rhythm failure)

In the real essays — the ones that are actually written — the single most common
flaw is the **"Not X. Y." move worn past the point of force**. It is a genuine
Bell signature, and used once per movement it lands like a verdict; repeated
every few paragraphs it becomes wallpaper and stops meaning anything. Ten of the
twelve distribution leads carry this as their *only* rubric failure (check 8).
Its cousin, on the shorter and more tired pieces, is the **soft PCN-style
closing** — a real essay that has done the hard work and then lands on "you
don't have to carry it alone," trading a verdict for a comfort. Both are
fatigue tells: the ground-clearing move on autopilot, and the ending reached for
when the true verdict was harder to find.

**The fix at the desk:** one "Not X. Y." per movement, not per paragraph — on
the read-aloud pass, if you hear the cadence twice in a page, cut one. And test
every final paragraph against the Verdict check: does it name what remains, or
does it resolve the weight to send the reader out comfortable? If it comforts,
the real ending is usually one paragraph up, unsaid.

## The through-line

The tired writer here is still a strong writer — the failures cluster at the
*edges* of the work (attribution, the reflexive move, the easy landing), never
at its center (the argument, the honesty, the refusal to flatter). The center is
sound; 29 flagships prove it. Guard the edges with three habits — no indexed
stubs, no un-sourced line, no reflex verdict — and the audit mostly stops
finding things.
