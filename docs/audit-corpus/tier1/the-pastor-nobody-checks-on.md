# Tier-One Audit — The Pastor Nobody Checks On

- **Slug:** `the-pastor-nobody-checks-on`
- **Pillar:** pastoral-ministry (~2,262 words, long-form register)
- **Audience:** pastors (the PCN channel, addressed directly), with a second door for the layperson who knows one ("There is a man in your network, your town, your phone")
- **Verdict tier:** **SOLID** (11/12; the single failure is check 8; three citation flags to clear before this piece is handed to the PCN network, none rising to a check-12 failure)

## The twelve checks

1. **MISSION — PASS.** Squarely the pastors audience — the strategic channel —
   served rather than flattered: the piece indicts pastors ("We did this")
   while caring for them. The PCN mention is earned by the argument that
   precedes it (care must come from outside the congregation's structure),
   not bolted on as promotion.

2. **ARC — PASS.** The piece's central intellectual move is the arc: pastoral
   loneliness is not timeless but a modern invention. Desert fathers, the
   Celtic *anam cara*, the medieval confessor, the Puritan brethren — then
   "We kept the giving and dropped the receiving... within a single century."
   The proximate cause (burnout headlines) never even gets the microphone.

3. **SELF-IMPLICATION — PASS.** The strongest of the three tier-one essays.
   "We did this. I have done this. I have praised exhaustion in another man
   because praising it was easier than asking whether he was alright." And
   the harder turn — implicating the lonely pastor himself: "Some of it we
   maintain, daily, with our own hands... I have made that preference. I know
   its taste."

4. **STEELMAN — PASS.** The party that could have been the villain — the
   congregation that will not care for its pastor — is given its honest due:
   "the congregation needs him to be the strong one, and that need is not
   wicked. It is just real, and it will not feed him." The physician-patient
   structural argument treats the elder's failed kindness generously ("The
   pastor feels the evaluation even when it is not intended"). Nobody is met
   in caricature.

5. **VERDICT — PASS.** "Somebody fed Elijah. Nobody is feeding him." Four
   words and four words; the reader carries it out of the room. No recap, no
   application box.

6. **FORMS, NOT INFORMS — PASS.** The grammar/arrow frame is a way of seeing,
   not a tip: a reader who absorbs "he is the subject of the verb, rarely its
   object" will see it next Sunday at their own church's back door, in a
   situation the essay never named. The pastor-reader is humbled, not armed —
   the door-held-from-the-inside movement blocks the piece from becoming a
   grievance the reader wields against his congregation.

7. **RHYTHM — PASS.** "Then he drives home and no one asks him anything." /
   "The body before the assignment. The care before the rebuke." / "The angel
   did not ask Elijah to be stronger. The angel asked him to eat." Short-long-
   short is the piece's native gait; verdicts land at three to eight words.

8. **NO TICS — FAIL.** The "Not X. Y." move runs three times in the opening
   movement alone, twice in consecutive sentences: "This is not an anomaly.
   It is the design. Pastoral loneliness is not a personality flaw in a few
   unlucky men. It is the predictable output of a vocation built so that the
   care runs one direction and never comes back." Then, two paragraphs later:
   "Not how the building campaign was going. Not whether attendance had
   recovered. How they were." It recurs in the history movement ("It is not
   the historic shape of the office. It is a modern invention") and the
   Elijah movement ("God does not rebuke him. God does not hand him a
   productivity plan."). Each instance is well made; at this density the
   signature move becomes the tic CLAUDE.md names. Mild signposting rides
   along ("This is worth saying plainly to ourselves"; "and I will say it
   plainly because I have watched it work").

9. **FORBIDDEN LANGUAGE — PASS.** Machine-checked. The only hits are two
   occurrences of "journey" — both inside the quoted angel's sentence from
   1 Kings 19:7, where the journey is literal and scriptural, not the
   forbidden metaphor. Zero other hits.

10. **EXCLAMATION — PASS.** Machine-checked: none.

11. **HEDGE — PASS.** No listed hedges. "In some quiet sense" (the elder
    "evaluating") is a precision qualifier, not a dodge; the piece's verdicts
    are all committed.

12. **CITATIONS — PASS, with three flags.** No unsourceable quotation (the
    Brigid saying is hedged as tradition, correctly), so nothing triggers the
    automatic CUT. But three items below must be cleared or repaired before
    this ships to pastors who will check: the Peterson ordination date, the
    unnamed "research," and the near-verbatim italicized Scripture.

## The skeptic's paragraph

Met. The skeptic is not this piece's primary reader, but the piece never
handles anyone: it makes no supernatural demand of the reader beyond the
Elijah narrative it exegetes, and its analytical spine — role asymmetry, the
physician who cannot be treated by his own patients, admiration as a
substitute for care — stands on ground a secular reader grants entirely. The
church is indicted by its own writer, not defended; a skeptical reader
finishes trusting the platform more, not less, because it turned the
instrument on its own profession first.

## The care standard (crisis-adjacent)

**Met, with one strengthening recommendation.**

- **Never glamorizes endurance:** the piece does the opposite — it names the
  glamor as the disease. "We made *tired* a synonym for *faithful*... we said
  it like a recruiting pitch instead of a warning." Praise-of-exhaustion is
  diagnosed as a way of not caring.
- **Handles the death-wish material without specifics:** Elijah "asking God
  to let him die" is presented in the biblical frame, no method, no
  elaboration, and is immediately answered by God's care (sleep, bread,
  touch) rather than dwelt in.
- **Validates the feeling, not the despair:** "The isolation was partly real
  and partly a distortion of exhaustion... The numbers are off. They are
  always off in that chair." That is the exact move the standard asks for —
  the loneliness is honored, the hopeless conclusion is contradicted.
- **Path to help visible:** yes — fellow pastor, an elder who pastors rather
  than evaluates, a spiritual director, a confessor, and PCN by name, with
  the mechanism explained (peers outside the evaluating structure).
- **Recommendation:** for a piece that touches "asking God to let him die"
  and "a man at the end of his rope," one sentence keeping a *professional*
  path visible — a counselor or physician alongside the confessor and the
  peer — would complete the standard. The historic structures the essay
  commends (director, confessor) point that direction already; naming the
  clinical door costs one clause and closes the one gap.
