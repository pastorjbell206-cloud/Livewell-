- **Audience:** the doubting Christian first (grief over decline reframed), the skeptic second (the "merely cultural" reader is named and met), pastors third

## The twelve checks

1. **MISSION — PASS.** This is the platform's central territory: the faith
   after its privilege. The opening image (the seventy-year-old man and the
   closed stores) plants it in a reader's actual grief, and the thesis —
   "What is ending is not the Christian faith. What is ending is Christendom"
   — is the Constitution's final impression in essay form: Christianity older
   than my culture.

2. **ARC — PASS.** Exemplary. It refuses the proximate cause (elections,
   scandals, worship wars — explicitly waved off in paragraph one) and pushes
   the origin back to 312 and 380, then forward through Taylor's two
   centuries of scaffolding removal. The full historical arc is the essay's
   skeleton, not its decoration.

3. **SELF-IMPLICATION — PASS.** "I have done this. I have stood in my own
   pulpit and addressed an audience of the imagination — a town that nodded
   along to the basics — when the actual town outside my doors no longer knew
   the story at all." The writer is inside the indictment at the exact point
   where the indictment turns toward pastors.

4. **STEELMAN — PASS.** The contested position — that Christendom was a good
   — gets its strongest available witness before the verdict: Tom Holland,
   "no apologist when he began," arguing the West's moral assumptions are
   Christendom's sediment. "Christendom built hospitals, universities, the
   very concept of human rights. It was not nothing." Only then the cost.
   The Constantine question itself is honestly bracketed ("a question no one
   living can usefully relitigate") rather than cheaply settled.

5. **VERDICT — PASS.** "The stone was never the statue. It was always the
   stone. And the stone outlasts the gold." A verdict, not a recap; it names
   what remains and hands it to the reader.

6. **FORMS, NOT INFORMS — PASS.** Symptom (empty pews, unease) → cause
   (the Constantinian trade, the scaffolding) → wisdom (the coffin question:
   habit, not faith). It refuses both certainty trades on offer — it will not
   comfort the nostalgist ("it deserves to fail") and will not flatter the
   secularist (Holland). A reader finishes able to re-read every decline
   statistic through the habit/faith distinction — a way of seeing that
   travels.

7. **RHYTHM — PASS.** "That arrangement had a beginning. It will have an end.
   We are living in the end." / "Take away the scaffolding and you find out
   what was load-bearing." Short verdicts land after long accumulating
   sentences throughout. No flatline stretches.

8. **NO TICS — FAIL.** Signposting has become a gait. Five meta-announcements
   in one essay: "It is worth saying at the outset why this matters beyond
   the walls of the church"; "The word *christendom* is worth slowing down
   on"; "This is the hinge of the whole story, and I want to be fair to it
   before I name its cost"; "But here is what the gift cost."; "There is a
   pattern worth naming here"; plus "Here is what I have come to believe the
   end requires." Each is defensible alone; together they are the "let me
   explain" reflex CLAUDE.md forbids. The "Not X. Y." move is also stacked as
   a run in the mustard-seed paragraph ("Not an army. Not an edict. Not an
   emperor's vision before a battle.") — one extended move, acceptable, but
   the density across the piece is near the ceiling.

9. **FORBIDDEN LANGUAGE — PASS.** Machine-checked against the full word,
   phrase, and therapy-speak lists: zero hits.

10. **EXCLAMATION — PASS.** Machine-checked: no exclamation points anywhere.

11. **HEDGE — FAIL.** One hit, and it sits in a verdict seat: "It was not
    nothing. It was, in many ways, a gift." — "in many ways" is on the
    explicit hedge list, and it is doing the verdict's job at the close of
    the steelman movement. Delete or commit: "It was a gift." (The concession
    is *stronger* undiluted; the next line, "But here is what the gift cost,"
    already carries the turn.) The other machine hit, "I think often of what
    the early church did not know it had," is not a hedge — it is reportage
    of the writer's habit, not a dodge on a claim.

12. **CITATIONS — PASS.** Full inventory below; everything checks. One
    in-text attribution gap noted (Tertullian), no fabrications, no
    unsourceable quotations.

## The skeptic's paragraph

Met, not handled — and met early. The fourth paragraph turns to face the
skeptic directly: "The people who feel the loss most acutely are often not
the devout but the merely cultural — the ones who never thought much about
faith and are now discovering, with a vague unease, that the floor they stood
on was held up by something they had stopped believing in." That is the
skeptical reader's own experience named without a sales pitch attached. The
Holland paragraph then hands the skeptic a secular historian's case rather
than an apologist's, and the essay's harshest verdicts fall on the church's
own power-grab, not on the unbeliever. No altar call; the ending leaves
weight ("whether, stripped of the empire, we can still be the seed") without
pressing for a decision. A thoughtful unbeliever finishes thinking the
Christians have finally admitted something true about themselves — which is
the essay's whole credibility.

   concession; the next sentence already takes it back with interest.
2. Thin the signposts. "But here is what the gift cost." can become "The gift
   had a cost." — the same hinge without the announcer. Cut "It is worth
   saying at the outset why this matters" and let the paragraph prove the
   point it promises.
