# Tier-One Audit — The Church Grew Fastest When It Had No Power

- **Slug:** `witness-without-power`
- **Pillar:** after-christendom (~2,498 words, long-form register)
- **Audience:** pastors first (the closing movement addresses them by name), the doubting Christian second, the skeptic third (served by the Stark material)
- **Verdict tier:** **REVISE** (fails checks 4, 8, 11 — three failures; one pass fixes all three)

## The twelve checks

1. **MISSION — PASS.** The companion piece to `christendom-is-ending`: it takes
   the same diagnosis and does the reconstruction — what the church actually
   does from the margins. The closing pastoral turn ("A pastor after
   Christendom is not a public figure managing the church's influence. He is
   a person forming a people whose shared life is the argument") serves the
   PCN channel without flattering it.

2. **ARC — PASS.** The whole piece lives in the long arc — Stark's three
   centuries of compounding growth, the plagues, the catechumenate — and
   reads the present decline through it rather than through this week's
   religion-and-politics news.

3. **SELF-IMPLICATION — PASS.** "We keep failing this test, and I include
   myself and my own tribe in the failure." And, better, the ending: "That
   is a smaller role and a truer one, and I am still, most days, learning to
   want it." The writer indicts his own appetite for standing, not just the
   loud voices.

4. **STEELMAN — FAIL.** The contested position — that Christians should seek
   cultural and political power — is met as an *instinct*, never as an
   *argument*: "the loudest voices call for more power — capture the
   institutions, win the elections, reclaim the culture by force of numbers
   and law. It is the most natural instinct in the world." No defender would
   sign "force of numbers and law." The strongest form of the case is never
   stated: that law teaches, that abandoning institutions abandons the
   neighbors those institutions crush, and — the defender's exhibit A —
   abolition and the civil rights movement, where the church's public power
   was spent for the weak and history vindicated it. The essay never faces
   that exhibit, and a thoughtful opponent will notice. (The standard
   objection to Hauerwas — that "just be the church" collapses into
   sectarian withdrawal — is likewise never allowed into the room.) This is
   fixable, and fixing it would strengthen the thesis: the civil rights
   movement won *as witness* — suffering endured in public, fire hoses
   answered with hymns — which is the essay's own argument wearing its best
   suit.

5. **VERDICT — PASS.** "A dying man held the hand of a stranger in a
   plague-emptied city, and an empire was changed. That is the method. It was
   always the method. We just got rich enough, for a while, to forget it."
   Weight, not recap; the last clause turns the indictment back on us.

6. **FORMS, NOT INFORMS — PASS.** It refuses the consolation reading it could
   have slid into (decline as painless providence): "There is a cost to this
   recovery that I do not want to soften." The Kreider movement converts the
   history into a way of seeing — depth as durability, levers vs. lives —
   that a reader can apply to their own congregation's next anxious decision.
   Watch one hazard in revision: a passive reader could still take "the
   answer is No" as permission to do nothing; the steelman fix in check 4
   guards this flank too.

7. **RHYTHM — PASS.** "It can only be patient. It can only ferment." /
   "The method is in the message." Long evidentiary sentences consistently
   resolve into short verdicts. No flatline.

8. **NO TICS — FAIL.** The "Not X. Y." move runs at more than once per
   movement. In the Stark movement alone: "Not an argument won in the forum.
   A body, fevered and dying, refusing to leave the bedside of a stranger."
   and "This was not a marketing strategy. It was the simple outworking of
   believing that every person bore the image of God." In the Paul movement,
   three of the shape in sequence: "He is not apologizing for this. He is
   claiming it is the design." / "Not tolerated in weakness. Not despite
   weakness. Made perfect in it." / "The thorn was not Paul's failure. It was
   the thing that kept the power from being mistaken for his." Each is good;
   stacked, the move becomes the tic CLAUDE.md warns it becomes. Keep the
   best in each movement (the 2 Corinthians one is the best in the essay),
   recast the rest.

9. **FORBIDDEN LANGUAGE — PASS.** Machine-checked: zero hits. ("Levers" is
   used throughout; "leverage" never is.)

10. **EXCLAMATION — PASS.** Machine-checked: none.

11. **HEDGE — FAIL.** "And it is possible — I think it is likely — that the
    answer is the same answer Paul received. No. My grace is enough." The
    double hedge ("it is possible — I think it is likely") stutters at the
    exact moment the movement lands its verdict-weight claim — what God is
    saying to the Western church. "I think" is on the explicit list, and here
    it is doing a verdict's job. The theological caution is right (no one
    should flatly assert God's providential answer); the first-person hedge
    is the wrong instrument for it. Frame it as a conditional and commit:
    "If the pattern of the thorn holds, the answer is the one Paul received.
    No."

12. **CITATIONS — PASS.** Full inventory below. Everything named checks out;
    one attribution is general rather than specific (Hauerwas) and noted, not
    flagged.

## The skeptic's paragraph

Met — and met by the essay's best structural decision: the case is prosecuted
through a sociologist's numbers before a single verse appears. The skeptic
gets Stark's mechanism (plague nursing, the status of women, compounding
relational growth) as history that stands on its own feet, and the essay's
contempt is reserved for the church's power-hunger, not for unbelief. The
resurrection is asserted once, flatly ("He overcame it by being crucified by
it and rising anyway") — as confession, unargued, which is the platform's
stated register for the creedal floor; a skeptic can decline it and keep
reading. The one place the skeptic will feel a thumb on the scale is the
missing counterexample named under check 4: a reader who knows the civil
rights movement was churches wielding public moral power will wonder why an
essay about church power never mentions it. That is the revision.

## The three most important fixes

1. **Steelman the case for Christian public power, then answer it** (check
   4). One paragraph before "We keep failing this test": state the position
   its defenders would sign — law teaches; retreat abandons the vulnerable to
   whoever does seize the levers; abolition and the civil rights movement are
   the church's public power spent for the weak. Then answer on the essay's
   own ground: the civil rights movement won by suffering witness, not by
   holding office — it is the plague-bedside method at national scale. The
   thesis comes out stronger.
2. **Commit at the thorn** (check 11). Replace "And it is possible — I think
   it is likely — that the answer is the same answer Paul received" with a
   committed conditional ("If the pattern of the thorn holds, the answer is
   the one Paul received. No."). Keep the theological modesty; lose the
   first-person stutter.
3. **Thin the "Not X. Y." tic to once per movement** (check 8). Keep "Not
   tolerated in weakness. Not despite weakness. Made perfect in it." and the
   plague-bedside instance; recast "This was not a marketing strategy...",
   "He is not apologizing for this...", and "The thorn was not Paul's
   failure..." as ordinary sentences. While in the Hauerwas movement, add the
   *Resident Aliens* citation from inventory note 8.
