# The Study Bible: advisory board audit and the plan that came from it

September 2026. Three board seats audited samples across the Study Bible:
- an Old Testament professor with a Hebrew grammarian and an ancient Near Eastern historian;
- a New Testament professor with a Greek grammarian and a systematic theologian;
- a church historian with a professor of preaching and a teacher of newcomers and skeptics.

They measured it against the ESV, NIV Zondervan, CSB, Jewish, and New Oxford Annotated study Bibles, the NET notes, and the major commentary series.

## What they found

**Strong.** The prose is accurate, fair, and often learned. The seats checked scores of scholars, finds, texts, and dates and found no fabrication. The Genesis verse notes were judged the best thing on the site and seminary-grade. Nothing sampled falls below the confessional floor.

**Not yet world class.** The problem is scale and structure, not competence. There are six gaps:

1. **Verse notes.** The verse-by-verse layer exists only for Genesis. The verses where Hebrew and Greek decide the argument have no exegesis below the chapter level: Isaiah 7:14 and 52:13-53:12, Psalm 22 and 110, Daniel 7:13, Exodus 3:14, John 1, Romans 3, Philippians 2, and Hebrews 6.
2. **Reception history.** How the church and the synagogue have read each text is almost absent. Too many notes lean on unnamed "many interpreters" where the names are knowable.
3. **Teaching help.** Pastors, the platform's distribution channel, get no teaching layer: no big idea, no pitfalls, no further reading.
4. **Orientation for newcomers.** Beginners get no help on what the Bible is, the canon, the text and translations, or how to read a reference.
5. **Apparatus.** There are no maps, timelines, dictionary, glossary, genre guides, textual commentary, Old-Testament-in-the-New index, or synoptic parallels.
6. **Doctrine tags.** Chapter notes tag coarse doctrines, so the fine doctrine pages were nearly empty for the New Testament. For example, Justification listed only Genesis.

About forty specific accuracy items were raised. Examples:

- **Genre.** Genesis 49:10 is a formal oracle with an ancient Jewish messianic reading, not a mere "pattern".
- **Wrong reference.** Psalm 107, not 108, opens Book V of the Psalms.
- **Misreported text.** Revelation 20 reported Satan as "destroyed".
- **Missed variants.** James 2:20 and Revelation 13:10 have manuscript differences the notes blended or missed.
- **Contested questions presented as settled.** Hell, kenosis, and the list of baptism views.
- **Story path chronology.** The path read every prophet after the fall of Jerusalem, and Job before Exodus.
- **Date.** Josephus wrote around AD 93, not "the next century".

## The plan

**1. Corrections (under way).** Every specific item from the three audits is being checked and fixed in the files named. The story path is being rebuilt so the prophets and datable psalms sit where they happened, and Job moves to the wisdom books.

**2. Six more doctrines (done).** Scripture and canon, the image of God, worship, government and the state, the intermediate state, and hell and final destinies. That makes 62 in all.

**3. Deeper chapter notes (spec version 2).** Every chapter note grows, from roughly 1,500 to roughly 3,500 words:
- **How it has been read:** Jewish, patristic, medieval, Reformation, and modern readers, plus liturgy, art, and music, with interpreters named with their works (described, not quoted).
- **The text:** manuscript and translation differences a reader will meet between English Bibles, and versification differences from the Hebrew.
- **For teaching and preaching:** the big idea, a teaching outline, the common pitfalls, and pastoral sensitivities.
- **Key words:** four to eight, with frequency and range.
- **Hard questions:** up to two, with the strongest objection (including non-Christian ones) stated by name before the replies.
- **Questions:** six in three kinds (observe, interpret, apply).
- **Doctrine tags:** the fine doctrine ids, so the doctrine pages fill with the chapters that teach them.
- **Primary sources:** cited by reference (Mishnah tractate, Qumran scroll, Josephus book and section) instead of "ancient texts".

**4. Verse notes, reprioritized.** The contested messianic and covenant chapters and the most-preached New Testament chapters come first, then the rest of the Bible. New Testament verse notes gain Old Testament quotation notes (Masoretic Text, Septuagint, and New Testament wording compared) and synoptic parallels.

**5. Book introductions gain three sections:** the history of the text, how the book has been read through the centuries, and graded further reading (first reading, the pastor's shelf, the scholar's shelf). Genesis gets its own ten "this is the account of" headings as its outline.

**6. Site-wide resources:**
- a "Start here" orientation (what the Bible is, the canon, how we got the text, translations, chapters and verses, how to read a reference);
- genre guides;
- primers on the Old and New Testament text;
- a versification table and a grammar glossary;
- tables of weights, measures, money, the calendar, and the offerings;
- a glossary;
- an Old-Testament-in-the-New index;
- a synoptic parallels view with an honest comparison of the resurrection accounts;
- an ancient Near Eastern source library;
- a chronology of the kings and empires;
- maps;
- a dictionary of people and places;
- a shorter first-pass reading path for beginners.

Every layer passes the same gates: `validate-bible-notes.mjs` for shape, verbatim Scripture, references, and register, and an independent fact-check with web verification before it is committed.
