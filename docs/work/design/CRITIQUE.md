## 1. The verdict

James, here it is plainly.

LiveWell today is a serious library with an honest voice, laid out like a careful blog. The material underneath it is better than almost anything in its niche: notes on all 1,189 chapters with the Greek and Hebrew under every word, 62 doctrine studies, 22 church-history essays, phrase-by-phrase notes on the creeds, 41 context guides, 72 family devotions tied to a 76-question catechism, 29 eight-week plans, 36 tools, and a few instruments (Test the Case, Which Lens Has You?, Tonight at the table, Find Help) that are as good as anything a pastor's site has built. The palette holds. Cream is the room, black carries weight, Cormorant at display size has real gravity, and the small print sounds like you ("A short shelf, kept short on purpose"; "I will not chase you"). There is no pop-up, no countdown, no stock photograph. Most ministry sites cannot say any of that.

It is not world-class yet, and the distance is not small. Measured against the places a serious reader already goes, here is where it stands.

**As a place to read, it is a well-kept template, not yet a publication.** Your essays and the Gospel of John are set in the same typeface as the Subscribe button, and every italic in them is one the browser fakes. The essay page offers to text itself to the reader before it lets him read the first sentence, and it follows your last line, the one meant to leave weight, with a book promotion, four more essays and two newsletter forms.

**As a place to study, it is behind the tools your readers already have.** A reader gets further, faster, on ESV.org, YouVersion, STEP or Sefaria. On John 1 the note on verse 14 sits inside one of thirteen collapsed sections below verse 51. The reader cannot mark a verse or write a note. Nothing on the site asks an adult to remember anything, and nothing tells him what to study this week. The children at the family table are the only readers the site actually teaches.

**As an organized site, it is weakest of all.** The menu sorts by what you made (Study, Essays, Books, Resources, About), not by what a reader came to do. The same job is built two to five times: two doctrine systems, two glossaries, two creed hubs, four ways to study a passage, five ways to ask what the Bible says, four Bible translations. The site search cannot find a doctrine: "grace" returns a page on aging and a pornography-recovery plan before anything on grace. And the subject in the home page's own headline, post-Christian America, is never defined and has no page.

**To the skeptic you write toward, it gives mixed signals.** The front door speaks to insiders ("the creeds you confess"). His two best instruments, Test the Case and Honest Answers, are filed in a drawer and a footer. The seams read to him as evasion: twenty-one books on one page and three on the next, 43 of 58 essay links answering "James is revising this essay," two church-history series counted as one list of 22.

The good news is the shape of the gap. Seven critics with seven different lenses arrived at the same place: the site does not need more content or more tools. It needs fewer, truer things wired together. One study surface, the Study Bible chapter, that everything else plugs into. One search that knows everything. One long-read template set in a real text face, with notes in the margin where a pastor's pencil would put them. Doors named for the reader's question. A teacher's order laid over the library, so a reader knows what to do this week and is asked, quietly, what he remembers from last week. A visual language drawn from what the site is about (the shape of the canon, two thousand years, the places, the table), so that attention comes from things worth looking at. That is how the tension in your brief resolves. "Draw people's attention" and "Unhurried. Weighted. Grown-up." are compatible if the attention is earned by depth and real interactivity. Nothing on the panel's list asks for a streak, a badge, a pop-up or a louder color.

Most of the distance is arrangement, not material. That makes it a smaller job than a rebuild and a larger one than a polish. The palette stays. The writing stays. What changes is how it is set, how it is found, how it connects, and whether it teaches.

## 2. The ten problems that matter most

Merged across the seven reports and ranked by what they cost a reader. Each names the pages, why it matters, and the world-class version. Severity is the panel's: **critical** breaks the brief on a flagship surface, **major** is felt on most visits.

### 1. The Study Bible chapter keeps the notes away from the verses, and does not set Scripture as Scripture

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** editorial, product, mobile, learning, brand, visitor.
**Pages:** `/study/bible/john/1` and every one of the 1,189 chapters; `/study/bible`, the book index.

**What is wrong.** John 1:1-51 is one undivided block of Inter, the interface face, with no paragraphs, no section headings, the prologue not set as the hymn it is, and verse numbers as mustard superscripts. The chapter's own outline ("1-5 The Word in the beginning," eight sections) exists, but only in a desktop side card; a phone never shows it. On a phone, verse 1 starts on the second screen, after an eleven-line gray summary. The notes come after verse 51 as fourteen accordions, thirteen closed, with the excellent Observe, Interpret and Apply questions last. The only way to a verse's note in place is a 24px verse number; the sheet it opens covers 72 percent of the phone screen, is not a dialog (focus stays behind it), and has no previous or next verse. The chapter arrows sit at the top and scroll away; "Next in the story" is on screen 12 of 13. On desktop the right column is empty for about 5,000px. The index above all this is 66 identical white cards, 15.4 phone screens long, with no chapter picker.

**Why it matters.** This is the flagship, and studying is reading with a question in your hand. Every time the answer is three screens away, the reader loses the sentence he was holding. A reader who reads a chapter a day pays that cost every day, and he already has an app on the same phone that does not charge it. The layout teaches people to read either the text or the notes, never both.

**The world-class version.** The text in a text serif at 20 to 21px, paragraphed, with the outline's headings in the text at every width (as ESV.org and printed Bibles do), the prologue in sense-lines, verse numbers small and muted with a 40px hit area. The whole verse selects, not just its number, and a quiet dot marks verses that have a note. On desktop, a sticky margin shows the note for the section in view until a verse is chosen, then that verse's note, words and cross-references (the ESV app's study notes, the NET Bible, Sefaria's Connections panel). On a phone, a real sheet with detents opens at about 40 percent with the verse still visible, moves focus in and back, steps verse by verse, and swipes away. An auto-hiding bottom bar carries previous chapter, a "John 1" book-and-chapter picker, next chapter, Aa and Listen, with a full-width "John 2" at the last verse before the notes. Above the text: a two-line summary, "John 1 in eight movements," and three questions to read with. The index becomes a compact canon list in the seven divisions that opens a chapter grid in place, shows progress per book, and offers "Pick up where you stopped."

### 2. The same job is built two to five times

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** product, IA, learning.
**Pages:** `/study/bible/doctrines` and `/theology/doctrine/:slug`; `/theology/glossary` (114 terms) and `/tools/glossary` (104); `/resources/creeds` and `/theology/creeds`; the Study Bible chapter, `/theology/passage` (230 passage notes, World English Bible), `/tools/deep-bible` and `/tools/bible-study`; `/wisdom`, `/tools/bible-on`, `/tools/bible-says`, `/tools/verse-finder` and `/tools/wisdom-finder`; My Library and `/tools/saved`; seven overlapping self-checks.

**What is wrong.** Every one of these is live and none redirects. A passage can be studied four ways, and John 1:1-18 has two separate deep treatments. The tool labelled "Flagship" (Deep Bible) covers 20 books in no canonical order and 37 passages, fourteen of them excerpts ending in "...", and closes by sending the reader to the Study Bible. "What the Bible Says," "What the Bible Says About" and "What the Bible Actually Says" are three different things. The self-checks go by four names (Self-Check, Assessment, Audit, Health Check). And one verse reads four ways depending on the door: the Berean Standard Bible in the Study Bible and the care pages, the World English Bible in the Passage Context Tool, the King James in the Devotion Builder and the tools glossary, the ESV in the essays.

**Why it matters.** You asked for the most organized site in its niche. A reader who finds three answers to "study a book of the Bible" cannot tell which is the real one, assumes the others are thinner, and stops trusting the shelf. A reader who meets the thin one first takes it for the site's full depth. Four wordings of one verse break memorization, quoting in a small group, and the sense that this is one coherent work. Every duplicate is also a second set of notes and definitions to keep correct, against the repo's own one-source-of-truth rule.

**The world-class version.** Sefaria's principle: one library, many ways into it. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's: one canonical entry per idea, with related entries. The Study Bible chapter becomes the single passage page, and the others fold into it: Deep Bible's worked passages and the 230 passage notes become notes on those chapters, the Bible Study Guide's themes become book introductions. One doctrine page per doctrine, one glossary linked from the verse panel, one creeds library, one topical-Scripture index searchable by need. One reading text on every study surface, named on every quotation, with other translations compared in place. The retired pages go through the one-hop redirect pattern the site already uses well. `/tools` shrinks to the genuine instruments, about fifteen.

### 3. The site cannot find its own best material

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** IA, product, learning, editorial, mobile, brand.
**Pages:** `/search` and the header search, `/theology/search`, the Study Bible's Go to, the boxes on `/writing`, `/tools` and `/help`; the references in essays, era essays and care pages; the related shelves on `/theology/history` and `/family`; the Nicene Creed.

**What is wrong.** `/search?q=grace` returns 16 cards: Grace and Works, a guide on patronage, "How Do You Discipline Children With Grace?", "How to Age With Grace," the pornography-recovery plan, blended families, willpower. Not one comes from the Study Bible. No doctrine of salvation, no glossary entry, no Ephesians 2:8-9, no charis, no Augustine and Pelagius. The index never includes the doctrines, the reading guides, the book introductions, the chapter notes, either glossary, the people, councils, heresies or the lexicon; those live in a second engine at `/theology/search`, linked from one row on `/study`. Around it sit six search boxes with six different reaches, filter chips in database words (ALL / ARTICLES / RESOURCES), and result labels in a mustard that fails contrast. Past search, the site does not link to itself at the hinges. Scripture references open in place only on context guides, creeds, family pages and plans; in the essay "(1 Samuel 8:5)" is plain text, as are the references on the anxiety page. The strongman essay's 26 links include none to 1 Samuel 8 in the Study Bible and none to the era essay's section on Barmen and Bonhoeffer. The Nicene Creed does not link to the council, to Arius, to Athanasius, to the Trinity study or to John 1. And keyword-matched shelves misfile: dating advice under "Church history," "Is 2 Chronicles 7:14 a Promise to America?" under home and family.

**Why it matters.** On a study site, search is the main door for anyone who arrives holding a word: justification, Nicaea, Romans 8. A reader who types "grace" and gets aging tips concludes the doctrine is not here, when it is, in depth. A reference that does not open cannot be checked, and being checkable is the site's trust contract with the skeptic. A misfiled shelf tells a careful reader that the filing is done by machine.

**The world-class version.** One index and one box everywhere, opened from the header or a keyboard shortcut (the Logos Go box, Stripe's docs search), answering in typed groups with counts and the canonical answer first: Passage (with the verse), Word (charis, with its occurrences), Doctrine, Glossary (defined in one line), History, then Essays, Help and Tools. Typing "John 3:16" still goes straight to the verse. The local boxes become scoped views of the one search that keep the query when widened; Find Help keeps its care-first ranking over the same index. Every reference on every page opens the verse in a sheet, with "Open in the Study Bible" one tap further, and every chapter shows where it is "Discussed on LiveWell," built from the same references. Underneath, a typed relation model (passage, doctrine, creed, council, heresy, person, era, essay), hand-filed and checked by a validator, renders one "Connected" block on every kind of page and replaces the keyword shelves.

### 4. The doors are named for what you made, not for what a reader came to do

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** IA, learning, brand, product, mobile.
**Pages:** the header, the phone menu and the footer on every page; the hubs `/study`, `/resources`, `/tools`, `/theology/history`, `/help`, `/family`.

**What is wrong.** Of the five things your brief names (study the Bible, understand doctrine, understand post-Christian America, learn how to live it, use the tools), only the Bible has a door. Doctrine is not in the menu; the 62 studies are row five of six on `/study`. "How to live it" sits in a drawer called Resources, fourth section; the 69-guide Integrated Life library appears in no header, footer or hub. Tools are Resources, then six of 36, then "Every tool." The "you are here" signal is wrong: the creed and context pages light Resources though the menu files them under Study, Study itself never lights, and tools, family and help light nothing. The paths contradict the menu (`/resources/creeds` lives under Study). The hubs have become dumps: `/theology/history` runs 18.4 phone screens, `/study/bible` 15.4, `/help` 14.4, `/family` 14, `/tools` 12.5; `/study` sends three of its links to one address; `/tools` presents 36 identical cards under the page title "Free Christian Tools for Families." Creeds go by three names, context guides by four, help by four.

**Why it matters.** A first-time visitor reads the menu as the table of contents, and this one says "a pastor's blog with a Bible attached." The parent, the doubter and the skeptic never see their question named, and have to guess that a marriage in trouble lives under Resources. Orientation fails on the deep pages, which is where search sends most people.

**The world-class version.** GOV.UK's founding rule: organize by what the user needs to do, not by who made the thing. BibleProject sorts by book, theme and topic, never by format. Formats (essay, guide, tool, plan, printable) become filters inside subject doors. Every page carries a breadcrumb that matches its URL and its door (MDN, Apple's developer documentation), and the door lights for every page beneath it. A hub is one screen of distinct choices and then "see all" pages (NYT Cooking). One controlled vocabulary for every label. The footer becomes a real index of the site.

**Already moving.** Your doors of 2026-10-26 (Study, Topics, Family, Essays, About) are built on the work branch (U19). They retire Books and Resources as doors and give the household and the subjects a front door. They do not yet give doctrine a door, fix the active state or the paths, or shorten the hubs. The IA critic would go further; see question 3.

### 5. The site's subject and its first reader both lack a front door

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** visitor, IA, learning.
**Pages:** `/` (the headline, hero, "Start with these," the books band); `/writing`'s "After Christendom"; `/post-christian` and `/exile` (both 301 to that section); `/writing/the-rise-of-the-nones` and its five siblings; `/nation`; `/theology/history/after-christendom-era`; `/tools/test-the-case`; `/answers`; `/help`'s "The church hurt me" and "I'm deconstructing my faith."

**What is wrong.** "Post-Christian America" is the home headline and is never defined. The clearest definition on the site is a paragraph two and a half phone screens into `/about` ("Nobody owes Christianity a hearing anymore"). The subject is scattered in seven pieces across five doors and an orphan: an essay section holding one essay, five pending essays that answer with the revising notice, a fourteen-page `/nation` library no menu reaches, the era essay "After Christendom" as card 22 of 22, two tools, a book, a care page. Every sentence above the fold addresses a believer. Your atheist-to-faith sentence, the platform's strongest credential with a skeptic, sits at the bottom of the first phone screen. Test the Case and Honest Answers are not linked from the home page or the header; the two pages for a reader hurt by church are cards 21 and 34 of 34 on a crisis page. Two of the three starting essays and the loudest object on the home page (the red, white and blue cover) narrow the subject to Christian nationalism. The history hub says "The story you were born into" and "the creeds you confess."

**Why it matters.** The skeptic is the reader the contract ranks first, and he decides on the first screen whether he is the audience. He came with a question that has an answer; the site's answer is "read around," which he takes as the evasion he expects from church. Opening on nationalism hands the left a flattery loop and the right a reason to leave, when the Constitution's final impression is "deeper than my politics."

**The world-class version.** One page for the subject, built the way the Stanford Encyclopedia leads an entry: the definition in the first paragraph (your two sentences from `/about`), then the long arc in dated turns linking era essays already written (the Constantinian turn, Westphalia in 1648, the American experiment, the attractional era, after Christendom), the secular reading at its strongest (Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age*), exile as the biblical frame (Jeremiah 29, Daniel 1 to 6, 1 Peter), and three doors at the end: read, test, ask. Like an Our World in Data topic page, it gathers every format on the subject, and `/nation` folds into it. The home page names its readers on the first screen ("I was an atheist for years. I know the objections from the inside.") and gives the skeptic a door in his own words: "Not sure you believe any of this? Start here," opening Test the Case, Honest Answers and the two church-hurt pages. Starting reads are chosen by the reader's question, not by the current book.

**Already moving.** U25 builds `/topics` with a page on what "post-Christian" means. Whether that page is this page is question 4.

### 6. A library with no teacher

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** learning, product, visitor.
**Pages:** `/`, `/study`, `/study/bible`, `/study/bible/story`, every chapter, the era essays, the creed, `/tools/theology-quiz`, `/resources` ("Help in a hard season"), `/plans`, `/my-library`.

**What is wrong.** Every learning page is a shelf and none is a syllabus. A reader passes three different "start here"s before he reaches a verse, and the first lesson is Genesis 1 of 1,189, unpaced (three and a quarter years at a chapter a day), while your own beginner plans start with Mark. The two best curricula on the site, "Eight Weeks of Reading Like an Adult" for the skeptic and "Eight Weeks of Beginnings" for the new believer, are filed under "Help in a hard season" between grief and a marriage under strain. No adult page asks a recall question or returns to a passage a week later. Beginner and pastor get one undifferentiated page. The only quiz tests disposition, not knowledge, scores into the retired three-pillar taxonomy, and uses a word on the forbidden list three times. The reader cannot highlight, note, bookmark, copy a verse with its reference, or compare translations in place. The progress the site does keep (chapters read, plan weeks walked, Continue the story) shows only on the page where it was recorded, so a returning adult sees nothing on the home page about where he is.

**Why it matters.** A novice cannot sequence a field he does not yet know. Run the panel's twelve-week test: a curious adult who visits weekly ends better informed and not formed, which fails the Formation Standard in your own contract. Retrieval and spacing are the two most robust findings in the science of learning (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006, on testing; Cepeda and colleagues, 2006, on spacing). A tool that keeps nothing of the reader's thinking gives him no reason to come back.

**The world-class version.** One spine over the library: a twelve-week course of thirty-to-forty-minute sittings, each holding a passage in the Study Bible, the era essay it belongs to, the creed line it grounds, one essay that carries it into 2026, one question to write an answer to, and one recall question from last week. Offered at three depths (Reading, Studying, Teaching, the last with a printable leader's sheet), as the ESV Reader's Bible and Study Bible set one text for two jobs. A router at the study door, in the words `/help` already uses: "I'm not sure I believe this," "I'm new to the faith," "I've read it for years." The story offered as three paced plans (the Gospel first in sixteen days, the story in twelve weeks, every chapter). A quiet recall question in the margin, a memorize mode on the creed that reuses Scripture Memory's first-letter drill. Highlights, notes and bookmarks kept on the device through `lib/storage.ts`, shown in My Library by book, with an export so a year of notes is never trapped. For a returning reader, the home page opens on "This week." The register is a ruled notebook: no scores, no streak flames.

**Already moving.** U28's 2027 plan is a paced story plan, which is one of the three.

### 7. The reading surface is not yet a book

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** editorial, brand, mobile, visitor.
**Pages:** `/writing/strongman-theology` and every essay; the 22 era essays; the context guides; the creeds; `/family/tonight`; the Bible chapters.

**What is wrong.** Every long text is set in Inter, the face the buttons use. Only Inter's roman is self-hosted, so every italic in body prose (the 1 Samuel 8 quotation, *The Prophetic Imagination*) is a slant the browser invents. Scripture appears in three typographic voices. The essay opens with a back link, kicker, title, dek, rule, byline, a black "Send to my phone" button that outweighs the title (and on a phone tells you to send the page to the phone you are holding), Save, Print, Listen, A-, A+, Focus and "In this essay"; the first sentence lands about 770px down an 844px phone screen and is not on the desktop's first screen at all. After your last line come a repeated toolbar, a black book band with a mustard button, a band holding one link, four essays, a newsletter card, an author card, five "elsewhere" links, "All essays," and the footer's second newsletter form: six or more asks in two screens. Whole sentences are bolded mid-paragraph; six lines of 1 Samuel 8 run inline in fake italic. The essay has no Sources block, though the era essays have one. Four long-read templates disagree about alignment, background, byline and back links; era essays and guides hug the left edge with 40 percent of a desktop screen empty, change background color section by section, and park their sources in a black slab at the end. Gray meant for captions sets primary text. Headline sizes run from 44 to 80px with no scale.

**Why it matters.** Your brief is "opening a serious book in a quiet room where the light is good." Serious books set long reading in a text serif, and fake italics are the clearest sign a page was not typeset. The reader came to read, and the page offers him tools first. Your own rule is that endings leave weight; the sales sequence makes a skeptic who has been marketed to by churches read the essay backwards, as a funnel.

**The world-class version.** Three faces with three jobs: Cormorant Garamond for display; a self-hosted, open-licence text serif with a true italic, built for screens (Source Serif 4 or Literata), for essays, Scripture, notes, creeds and devotions; Inter for interface and metadata. One long-read template with variants for essay, era essay, guide and creed: the same masthead, a centered column of about 62 characters, one paper color, a desktop margin for sidenotes, sources, key figures and Scripture references (Tufte CSS, Gwern.net), long Scripture set as block Scripture with its reference beneath, at most one pull quote, no bold in prose. The reading tools collapse into one Aa control (size, theme, Listen), as Apple Books and Kindle do, and Save, Print, Send and Cite go into one share menu at the end mark. The ending: an end mark (the Economist's small square), real silence, a one-line byline, one next read chosen by an editor, the sources, and the book only as a quiet line when it is the natural sequel. The newsletter once, in the footer. A declared type scale held as `:root` tokens. Body in ink; gray for metadata only.

**Contract note.** A text serif changes the typography table in `CLAUDE.md`. That is your call (question 1).

### 8. Two thousand years of church history have no shape

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** IA, editorial, product, learning, brand, visitor.
**Pages:** `/theology/history`, its timeline tab, and all 22 era essays (`/theology/history/the-apostolic-age` was photographed).

**What is wrong.** "22 era essays, from Pentecost to the present" are two overlapping series, fourteen and eight, both starting at Pentecost, so "The Reformation" sits beside "The Reformation and Its Wars." Their kickers follow two periodizations, and the timeline adds a third. The timeline is seven equal accordions of dated bullets, so thirty years and three hundred take the same height. The 36 people, 11 councils and 10 heresies are tabs of text. Each era essay ends with two links back to the hub and none forward; its key figures are unlinked though the hub holds their entries; it narrates Acts without linking it; it has no byline, date, reader tools, primary-source excerpt, map or image. "Everything on this: Church history" opens with sickness, dating and child discipline. "After Christendom," the era the reader is standing in, is the last card. The timeline sets "Jesus had been crucified, then raised" in the same voice as "70: the fall of Jerusalem."

**Why it matters.** History is learned as shape before detail, and as a sequence. A 22-part series with no next link ends every reader after part one. Two series and three sets of dates on the page meant to get the story right tell a careful reader the site does not know its own story. The brand promises the full historical arc, and the arc is not visible.

**The world-class version.** One spine of eras (the timeline's seven is the cleanest), one essay per era, and the other series kept as "the short version." A proportional time river across the hub, in the tradition of John B. Sparks's 1931 *Histomap* and the Met's Heilbrunn Timeline: eras as bands, councils as marks, people as lifelines, a "you are here" on After Christendom, each band opening its essay. Every era essay carries a series rail (previous, era 2 of 7, next), links its figures, councils and Scripture, opens with a line map, keeps its sources in the margin, gives one short "In their own words" excerpt from a verified public-domain text, ends with three "Can you say it back?" prompts, and has a byline. The hub speaks to every reader ("Whether or not you believe it, this is the story that made the country you live in"). Confession stays, marked as confession in one clause, as the era essays already do.

**Already moving.** U07 has built and verified a corpus of public-domain primary texts (Pliny, the Martyrdom of Polycarp, Eusebius, Perpetua, the Creed of Nicaea, Athanasius) that the excerpts can draw on.

### 9. On a phone, and at night, the site works against the reader

**Severity:** critical. **Raised by:** mobile, editorial.
**Pages:** site-wide; felt most on `/help/anxiety`, `/family/tonight`, `/study/bible/john/1`, `/search`, `/tools/test-the-case`, `/resources/creeds/nicene-creed`.

**What is wrong.** There is no way to read at night. Dark mode is switched off for every visitor, though its palette is written, because some heroes mix tokens that flip with tokens that do not; dark-mode phones paint a charcoal status bar over a cream header; full-width black and cream bands strobe as you scroll. The phone is treated as a narrow desktop: nothing sits within reach of the thumb, the 64px header never hides, the menu drops from the top with no dimming, and first screens are spent on preamble (Test the Case's first move is on screen three, the Nicene Creed's text 2,100px down, no tool on the first screen of `/tools`).
