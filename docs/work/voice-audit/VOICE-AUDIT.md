# Voice audit of the writing on LiveWell

For James. Written 17 November 2026.

You asked for two things: check that the writing fits your saved writing profile, and make sure nothing on the site sounds like a machine wrote it. This is what was found. The 25 rewritten essays were checked and corrected. Everything else was checked and listed, but not changed.

Each piece got two scores out of ten. **Human** means: would a careful reader suspect a machine? **Voice** means: does it sound like you, and only you?

---

## 1. The verdict

The site is two different things today. The 25 rewritten essays read like a person. Before this pass they scored between 8 and 9 for human (8.6 on average) and between 7.5 and 8.5 for voice (7.9 on average), and 64 small corrections have now gone into them. No reader found a forbidden word or a stray long dash in any of the 25. What holds them below a 10 can't be fixed by an editor: you are almost never in them. They sound like a careful, fair pastor. They don't yet sound like the one man who came to faith from atheism, grew up without a father and is raising five sons. Nobody wrote those sentences for you, because they would have had to be invented. The rest of the site is weaker, and it is most of the words. It scored between 4 and 7.5. The teaching underneath is sound, fair and kind. But the study guides, the family devotions, the Honest Answers, the church history essays and the older Bible notes were built from moulds, and the moulds show. A reader who opens one page hears a pastor. A reader who opens three hears the same sentences come back. Nine parts of the site were audited and just over a thousand exact fixes are written down and waiting, but none of them has been made. So the true answer to "does it sound extremely human" is: the essays, yes, nearly. The rest, not yet.

## 3. The machine tells found most often

These are the habits that make a careful reader think "a machine wrote this." Each one has two real examples. Examples from the 25 essays have been fixed. Examples from the rest of the site are still there.

### "It is not this. It is that."

The most common habit on the whole site. A sentence denies something nobody said, then states the point. Once, at a real turning point, it is your voice. Used in every paragraph it becomes a drumbeat. The study guides do it about 1,858 times. The family devotions do it 115 times.

- Family devotion on fear (not yet fixed). Before: "Trust is not the absence of fear. Trust is what you do with your hands while your heart is still pounding." After: "You can trust God while your heart is still pounding."
- Study Bible note on Psalm 23 (not yet fixed). Before: "The shepherd's care is not sentiment but faithfulness to his own name." After: "The shepherd cares for the sheep because he is faithful to his own name."

### The slogan at the end of the paragraph

A short, balanced line that could be stitched on a pillow. It says again what the paragraph already said. About thirty of the thirty-six Honest Answers end on one.

- Honest Answer on money (not yet fixed). Before: "The number was never the point. It was only ever a mirror." After: "The number only shows you what you already trust."
- Church history essay (not yet fixed). Before: "They built for weight. We build for weekends. The difference is the whole assignment." After: "They built churches that could carry weight, and we have mostly built for the weekend."

### The writing describes its own rules

The editorial rulebook leaks into the prose. Words like "steelman," "verdict," "indictment," "at full strength" and "this platform" are instructions to a writer. A pastor does the fair thing without announcing it.

- Study guide on womanhood (not yet fixed). Before: "Now let me do the thing this platform requires, which is to state each position in its strongest form, the form its own best defenders would sign, so that you cannot tell from the steelman alone where I land." After: "Let me state each position the way its best defenders would."
- Church history essay (not yet fixed). Before: "Steelman the crusaders, because honesty requires it: most believed..." After: "Most crusaders believed..."

### Your life story used as a stamp

Three true facts (atheism, no father, five sons) and one confession ("I'm a pastor, and I'm inside this") are repeated in the same words, page after page, as a reason to trust the page. The study guides mention your biography 297 times. The confession sentence sits on 36 of the 40 Find Help pages.

- Find Help page on doubt (not yet fixed). Before: "I'm a pastor, and this indictment includes me. We have too often met..." After: "We have too often met..."
- Study guide on manhood (not yet fixed). Before: "Bell writes this session from the inside, and he says so plainly, because he was raised without a father, and a man should tell you where he is standing before he talks about a thing like this." After: "I was raised without a father, and you should know that before I say anything about this."

### "Honest" calling itself honest

Writing that keeps vouching for its own honesty is the thing a skeptic trusts least. The word appears about 3,353 times in the study guides, 164 times on the Find Help pages, 131 times in the plans and 57 times in the history essays.

- Tools page (not yet fixed). Before: "Eight honest questions across four areas". After: "Eight questions across four areas".
- Study guide for skeptics (not yet fixed). Before: "This is the most honest objection of all and it deserves the most honest answer, which is to grant it almost entirely." After: "This objection is mostly right, and I'll say so."

### Long dashes

Your site already treats the long dash as a machine tell and the 25 essays have none. But there are 56 in the first fifteen Honest Answers, 201 in four church history essays, and about 30 on the main pages, including two in the home page headline.

- Home page headline (not yet fixed, and you chose this line, so it needs your nod). Before: "Learning to follow Jesus — and live well — in post‑Christian America." After: "Learning to follow Jesus and live well in post‑Christian America."
- Honest Answer on the Trinity (not yet fixed). Before: "Every analogy — water, clover, sun — fails, and the failure is a clue." After: "Every analogy fails, whether water, clover, or sun, and the failure is a clue."

### Stage directions to the reader

Telling the reader that a point is coming, or to stop and feel it. The history essays do this 48 times. The study guides say "Then the turn," "Then the reversal" or "This is the hinge" 118 times between them.

- Church history essay (not yet fixed). Before: "Let the weight of that land. The most studied church..." After: "The most studied church..."
- Essay on the Bible's first readers (fixed). Before: "What would it look like to read this way? Mostly it means slowing down." After: "Reading this way mostly means slowing down."

### No contractions in words meant to be spoken

Nobody says "you do not have to" to a child at the table. The family devotions have about 35,000 words written to be read aloud and not one contraction. Sixty of the 68 study guides have none either.

- Family devotion on identity (not yet fixed). Before: "But you do not have to earn the right to exist." After: "But you don't have to earn the right to exist."
- Study guide leader script (not yet fixed). Before: "Let me pray for us before we go, and then I will see you next time." After: "Let me pray for us before we go, and then I'll see you next time."

### Small filler in the essays

These were the main things left in the 25 essays, and they are fixed.

- Before: "A sixteenth-century friar, in other words, refused to spiritualize everything." After: "A sixteenth-century friar refused to spiritualize everything."
- Before: "which a great many people have had". After: "which plenty of people have had". (That essay used "a great many" or "a good deal" five times.)

## 6. Where your saved profile and your voice statement disagree

You have two descriptions of your writing. One is your saved Substack profile. The other is the voice statement kept with the site, which says it wins where they differ. Every reader in this audit followed the voice statement and did not push anything toward the profile. Here is where the two part ways. Neither is treated as wrong here. You decide.

| Question | Saved Substack profile | Voice statement kept with the site |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraphs | Two to four sentences, one idea each | Full, developed paragraphs. No stacks of one-line paragraphs |
| Length of a long essay | 1,500 to 2,500 words | As long as the argument needs. The rewritten essays run well past 2,500 |
| The memorable line | Every piece needs one line the reader screenshots. Also "contrast lines" and "stacked landing lines" | One verdict earned by the argument, not several slogans. Perfect balance and repeated "not this but that" are on the avoid list |
| Greek and Hebrew | Every post should have a Greek or Hebrew word that changes the argument | Only where it sharpens the point. "Decorative" Greek and Hebrew is on the avoid list |
| Monday morning | Every post ends with three questions and one practice | Asks for no such block. Says to show what a faithful person would do differently on an ordinary Tuesday. The site's own rules call the takeaway box a hazard |
| A real person, your own story | Every post needs a real person in a real situation, and your story when it belongs | First-person sparingly and concretely, and never invented |
| Both sides | Cultural pieces must present side A, side B, then what both miss | No even-handed criticism of right and left when the evidence isn't even |
| Exclamation marks | Never | The site allows them inside Scripture quoted word for word |
| Long dashes | Banned in social writing | Use sparingly. The essay rules on the site go further and ban them |
| Paid content | Paid posts should "justify the subscription" | Not addressed. But you have now said no membership fee |
| Web address | Ends in .com | The site is at .co |

Three things to weigh.

First, most of the machine tells in section 3 are things the profile asks for. Slogan endings, stacked short lines and contrast lines are exactly what readers flagged in the Honest Answers, the devotions and the older history essays. If you want those back, say so, and the "slogan" fixes should be skipped.

Second, the one thing the profile asks for that the essays most lack is the "real person" rule. The profile says a piece must be something only you could have written. By that test, the readers found that most of the 25 essays could have come from any careful pastor.

Third, the two agree on the things that matter most: state the other side fairly first, never make anything up, weave Scripture into the argument, and earn the ending.
