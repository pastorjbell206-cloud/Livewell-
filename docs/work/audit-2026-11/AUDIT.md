# LiveWell: the full audit, November 2026

For James Bell. Written 2026-11-17.

You asked for a full review of the site against what you want it to be: one place where families, ordinary readers and serious readers can study the Bible, learn church history, find answers to hard questions, lead family devotions, read your books, and reach you, your Facebook and your Substack. You asked whether the titles are in everyday language, whether the design is among the best, and whether the site can be more interactive.

Nine auditors each looked at the site through one lens. A second reviewer then re-checked every report and corrected it. This document gathers all nine into one. Where two auditors disagreed, it says so.

**Words used in this report.**

- **Live** means the site as visitors have it today.
- **Pending** means work that is finished but not yet merged into the site. There are three batches: PR #540 (the new menu, the Topics pages, the family improvements, a one-year Bible reading plan), PR #541 (ten essays on doubt) and a branch called land-next (ten more essays). A "PR" is a batch of changes waiting for approval.
- **Search title** means the line Google and Facebook show for a page. **Description** means the sentence under it.
- **Redirect** means an old address that sends the visitor on to another page.
- **Serif** means the kind of typeface books are printed in, with small strokes at the ends of the letters.
- **Size:** S is under an hour of work, M is under a day, L is more.

**What this audit could and could not see.** The auditors built the live site and the pending site and used both in a real browser, at phone size and at laptop size. They did not have your database, and they could not open outside websites. So nobody watched a contact message arrive, a Stripe sale complete, your Substack or Facebook pages, or the printable PDFs. Anything that rests on reading the code and not on watching it happen says so. The Substack comparison in the design section rests on the auditor's general knowledge of how Substack looks, not on your page.

## 1. The verdict

The site already holds nearly everything you described, and most of it is good. There are notes on all 1,189 chapters of the Bible, 62 doctrine studies, 22 church history essays, 72 weekly family devotions with 40 seasonal ones, a 76-question family catechism, 68 group study guides, 35 honest answers, 36 tools and 619 free printables. Nothing hides behind a sign-up. What is not yet true is the sentence you gave: "one place for families to go to read, to study." On a phone, the live home page gives a door to the Bible, the essays and the books, and no door to family devotions, hard questions, church history or discipleship. Your Facebook is linked in only two places: near the bottom of the About page, and in one small gray line at the end of each essay. The two kinds of page that matter most, a Bible chapter and an essay, load badly on a phone. The Bible reader holds better notes than most free Bible sites, but a student cannot search the Bible for a word, see a psalm set as a poem, or keep a highlight. Church history reads well and is not yet a course. The design is careful and quiet, and it is not yet among the ten best. On an honest scale the site is about a 6 out of 10 against a world-class bar today, and about a 7 once the pending work merges. The pending work fixes the menu, the family door and the thin essay shelf. It does not fix the loading, the search, the Bible reader, church history, the look of the site, or the one-stop links to you.

## 3. What is already strong (keep these)

1. **The headline says the mission in one line, in your words.** "Learning to follow Jesus — and live well — in post‑Christian America."
2. **The reach of the Study Bible.** An introduction to all 66 books. Notes on all 1,189 chapters, about 2.2 million words. A "hard question" on 1,033 chapters, with the objection stated in its strongest form first. Hebrew and Greek under every word, with the grammar in English words. The Study page tells the truth about what is still being written.
3. **Find Help speaks the way people speak.** "What are you facing?" "I can't stop worrying." "The church hurt me." All 40 pages are titled as a sentence a person would say. The crisis lines come first, and Find Help is one tap from every page.
4. **The Honest Answers and Test the Case treat a skeptic as intelligent.** "Did the resurrection actually happen?" "You can walk away unconvinced at any point and I will not chase you." Each step ends with "What this move does not settle."
5. **The family side is complete and usable tonight.** Tonight's devotion opens in Table mode: six screens (Read, Talk, Ask, Do, Remember, Pray) in large type. A devotion is about 260 words, so "About ten minutes" is true. The catechism gives an answer "for the little ones" of 3 to 16 words ("God made me."). Tonight follows the calendar for Thanksgiving week, Advent and Holy Week.
6. **Everything is free and says so.** "619 files, free, nothing gated." No pop-up, no cookie wall, no account. The site says plainly that what you save "lives in this browser on this device."
7. **The study guides.** Sixty-eight guides, each with a leader's guide, a handout and a script, plus a block for "When someone in the group is carrying this."
8. **The Go to box understands people.** "jn 3 16," "Revelations 21," "Canticles 2" and "Mt 5-7" all open the right place.
9. **The palette and its restraint.** Cream as the room, near-black for weight, thin rules, square corners, no stock photographs.
10. **The essay title block.** Section label, large serif title, italic summary, a short mustard rule, then name, reading time and date. It is the most publication-like moment on the site.
11. **The About page reads like a person.** "You may still be deciding whether to trust the voice. That is the right instinct, and you should keep it."
12. **The technical foundation.** The home page scores 100 out of 100 for speed. No page scrolls sideways on a phone. Not one dead link was found in 3,430 checked. Every one of 2,948 built pages has its own title and description.
13. **The pending work aims at the worst gaps.** A Family door and a Topics door, a search field and tonight's devotion on the home page, a one-year reading plan, and twenty finished essays with plain titles.

## 4. The twelve things that most hold the site back

In order. Each gives what a visitor meets, the fix, the size, and whether pending work fixes it. File names for each are in the appendix.

### 1. The front door shows one third of the house

**What a visitor meets.** On a phone, the live home page is a headline and two paragraphs. The first thing to tap sits past the first screen. The doors are "Study the Bible," "Read the essays" and "The books." The word "family" is not on the page. A mother who wants tonight's devotion has to guess "Resources," scroll about three screens, and tap twice more. Church history is promised in the paragraph ("learn the church's long history") and not linked. The title Google and Facebook show for the home page is an old tagline, "LiveWell by James Bell — Theology That Carries the Weight of Everyday Life," which names nothing the site offers. All three featured essays, and the first book, are about nationalism and power, so a stranger concludes the site is mainly about politics.

**The fix.** Merge PR #540. Then cut the dark top section to the headline and one sentence, and bring the search field and tonight's devotion onto the first screen. Add a sixth door to the home page's list, "Church history" (the header keeps your five). Link "post-Christian" in the headline to the page that explains it. Give the home page one plain title. Choose three featured essays that span the mission.

**Size.** S to merge, S for the door and the title, M for the first screen.

**Pending.** Partly. PR #540 adds the Family and Topics doors, "Hard questions" and "Family devotions" on the home page, a field labelled "Look up a passage or ask a question," and the line for tonight's devotion. It places them below the same two paragraphs, so the first door sits lower than it does today. The church history door, the link from the headline, the first screen, the title and the featured essays stay open.

### 2. Bible chapters and essays load badly on a phone

**What a visitor meets.** Open John 3 on an ordinary phone. First the chapter appears in plain, unstyled type. Then it is wiped and replaced by "One moment. Loading the page..." for about two seconds. Then the real page arrives and the bottom of the page jumps. Google's speed test scores the chapter 43 out of 100 and an essay 45. The home page scores 100. An essay also waits for the database before it shows, even when its whole text is already in a file on the site.

**Why it matters.** These are the pages a person lands on from Google or from a link you post on Facebook. The first three seconds look broken.

**The fix.** First, give the loading screen the full height of the phone so the bottom of the page cannot jump (S). Second, style the first plain version of the page and leave it on screen until the real one is ready (M). Third, show an essay as soon as its file loads (S).

**Pending.** No.

### 3. The site says things about you and your books that are not settled, or not true

**What a visitor meets.**

- The Books page says "Three books. Every word mine." The About page says "Author of 21 books." A page called the Roadmap says "Forty-eight books is the plan. Twenty-one are already in print." The About page says you have pastored "for twelve years" and, four sections later, "a pastor with fifteen years of mistakes behind him." These lines are also what Google, Facebook and the AI assistants quote about you.
- The Books page says "I took the rest of the shelf down to rewrite it in my own hand." Five of those books still open from Google as full sales pages: "NEW BOOK · EBOOK AVAILABLE NOW ... Get the ebook — $8.99."
- The Books page and the home page say "Read the opening free." The button leads to a sales page. No opening of any book can be read.
- A membership page that nothing links to, but Google can find, offers "The deeper room" for "$9 / month or $89 / year" with "Join the waitlist."
- The paid ebook files can be downloaded by anyone who has the address, without paying. That includes the books taken down.

**Why it matters.** The site's whole appeal to a careful reader is carefulness. A pastor deciding whether to recommend it reads About first.

**The fix.** You give one set of facts, or the numbers come off everywhere. The five book pages either go back on the shelf or come down. The "opening free" line either becomes true or is reworded. The membership page comes down until there is a membership. The ebook files move behind the paid download.

**Size.** S each once you decide; M for the ebook files.

**Pending.** No. Every item waits on you.

### 4. It is not yet the one stop for reaching you

## 5. Titles: are they in plain, everyday language?

### The rule

**Write the title the way the reader would ask it out loud. Put the line you love underneath.**

Five checks, in order:

1. **The out-loud test.** Would a tired parent say these words to a friend, or type them into a search box?
2. **The stranger test.** Shown the title alone, could someone who has never heard of you say what the piece is about?
3. **No word the piece has to teach.** If the reader learns the word by reading (Christendom, Constantinian, mishpat, attractional, rule of life), it does not go in the title. It can follow the plain words.
4. **Subject first for anything in a list.** "Money: A Bible Study on Where Your Treasure Is."
5. **Say it once on the site.** If the same words already name something else, change one.

Three habits go with it. The web address matches the title. The good line is kept, under the title. If a title says eight weeks or forty days, the piece holds that many.

One exception: devotions read aloud to children, and published books. A word-picture title is right there, as long as a plain label is always printed beside it.

### The count

- **Content titles:** 1,253 scored, 1,123 pass (90 percent), 130 to fix.
- That number flatters the front of the house. Of the 20 essays public today, 12 pass. Of 22 church history titles, 7 pass. Of 36 tool names, 23 pass.
- Already at or near full marks: Find Help (40 of 40), everyday-life questions (69 of 69), "What the Bible says" topics (208 of 208), how-to guides (128 of 132), Honest Answers (33 of 35), context guides (38 of 41), the 20 pending essays (19 of 20).
- **Search titles:** 1,984 of 2,274 (87 percent) run past the 60 characters Google usually shows. Shortening the ending from "| LiveWell by James Bell" to "| LiveWell" brings 639 under the limit. Of those still over, 1,109 are Bible chapters where the reference comes first, so the cut does no harm.
- **Descriptions:** 506 run past 160 characters.
- **Headlines:** 22 main pages have a slogan where the name should be.
- **Doubles:** 18 titles each name two or three different pages.

The 60 and 160 character limits are the auditors' working rule, not a measurement. Every "what people search" judgment below is a judgment; no search data was available.

## 6. Design: what separates it from a top-ten site

**In one sentence:** the best sites have a typeface for reading, a hand that draws, one set of parts used everywhere, and one thing you remember touching. LiveWell has a headline typeface, a palette and good words.

The comparisons with other sites below come from the design auditor's own knowledge of them. They could not be opened during the audit.

### What already looks world-class

The palette and its restraint. The essay title block. The table-of-contents layout on the Study, Resources and Essays pages, which PR #540 uses for its Topics page (the best-designed new page). Tonight's devotion, one narrow column with six plain headings. The first screen of Find Help. Test the Case.

### The gaps

1. **No typeface for reading.** Essays, devotions and Bible chapters are set in Inter, the face used for "Subscribe." Italics are faked by the browser; no italic file exists. Quotation marks are the straight typewriter kind. Serious reading sites set long text in a book face, and so does Substack.
2. **Nothing drawn.** On eighteen audited pages the only images are your portrait and three book covers. Church history is a list. The Bible index is 66 identical gray boxes. The tools page is 36 identical boxes.
3. **No single set of parts.** Page titles appear at ten sizes on a laptop. The text column on a phone is five different widths, so the left edge jumps from page to page. Nine pages open on black and nine on cream with no rule a visitor could guess. Buttons are square here and round there. Mustard is used as fill on the "not found" page and the footer button; the code holds 116 mustard backgrounds, against your own rule that mustard is punctuation.
4. **The first screen has nothing to touch.** The dark top of the home page is 912 pixels tall on a phone, taller than the screen. One line of the headline begins with a dash at each screen size.
5. **The essay does not end.** Eight controls stand before the first sentence. After the last line come a second toolbar, a Find Help band, four more essays, a newsletter card, an author card, a line of links, a back link, and the footer with a second newsletter form.
6. **The index pages are long columns of look-alike boxes.** The Study Bible front page is 15 phone screens, church history 18, Find Help 14, Family 14 (growing in PR #540), Tools 12.
7. **Small unfinished things.** The "not found" page has no header or footer. The footer email box cuts off its own hint text. The Subscribe button on the subscribe page looks dead until a label is chosen. No list of essays shows a date. Nothing has a pressed state: a tap on a phone gives no response. There is no night setting. The phone footer is 1.6 screens tall, 2.3 in PR #540. On a phone the header has no Subscribe.

### "Match my Substack"

Where the essay page already matches: one narrow column, a large serif title, an italic line under it, your name with reading time and date.

Where it does not: the body is not a serif; controls stand between your name and the first sentence; the ending carries many asks where Substack carries one; the essay list is grouped by theme with no dates, where a Substack home is a dated list, newest first; and the word "Substack" appears once per page, as a small footer link.
