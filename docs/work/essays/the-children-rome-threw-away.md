# What the First Christians Did About the Children Rome Threw Away

*In a world where leaving an unwanted newborn outside was an ordinary family decision, the first Christians refused to do it, and the history of that refusal still bears on how the church treats unwanted children and their mothers.*

## A Letter from Alexandria

In 1 BC a man named Hilarion, who was working in Alexandria and waiting on his wages, wrote a letter home to a woman named Alis. He calls her his sister, a word that in Roman Egypt could mean a wife, and most readers take her to be his wife. She was expecting a child.

The letter is short and mostly kind. He tells her not to worry if the others go home and he stays on in Alexandria. He asks her to take care of the small child they already have, and he promises to send money as soon as he is paid. Then comes the sentence that made the letter famous. In one standard translation it reads: "if it is a male, let it live; if it is a female, expose it." A few lines later he answers a message she had sent him through someone named Aphrodisias, asking him not to forget her. How could he forget her? he asks. He begs her once more not to worry.

The letter was found at Oxyrhynchus, a town in Middle Egypt, among the papyri dug out of its ancient rubbish mounds, and scholars know it as P.Oxy. 744. Hilarion isn't a monster, and that is what makes the letter hard to read. He is a working man far from home, worried about money, tender toward his wife, fond of the child he already has. The instruction about the baby sits in the letter as one more household arrangement, between the wages and the reassurance.

To expose a child was to leave a newborn outside, often somewhere public where it might be found, and walk away. Some of those babies died of cold or hunger or animals. Some were picked up by strangers, and many of those were raised as slaves. Hilarion did not have to defend the practice to his wife. Nobody around him would have asked him to.

## Why Roman Families Left Children Outside

Christians who tell this story usually hurry to the contrast. It's worth slowing down. Most Romans were neither stupid nor cruel, and Alis's position was a hard one. She had no reliable way to prevent a pregnancy. Childbirth itself was dangerous, and a great many babies died in their first year whatever anyone did. A laborer's household lived close to the edge, and one more mouth could mean less food for the child already at the table. A son might work and support his parents when they were old. A daughter would need a dowry if she was to marry, and she would leave for another household. There were no orphanages and no adoption agencies. A family that raised every child it conceived could go under, and take the children it already had down with it.

Many Romans also didn't think of exposure as killing. The historian John Boswell argued that the English word carries a sense of harm the ancient words lacked, and he preferred to call the practice abandonment. Augustine, writing in North Africa four centuries after Hilarion, described parents who left their infants "in order to their being cared for by any passer-by." A parent could tell himself that he had handed the child to fortune or the gods, and that some childless couple, or some household that needed hands, would come by. Sometimes one did. It's easy to call that self-deception, and much of it was. But anyone who has made a terrible choice under pressure knows how such a story gets told, and how badly a person needs to believe it.

Put most of us in Hilarion's place, with no wages yet and a wife at home with a small child, and we might well have written the same sentence. That's why the Christian difference is worth studying. It was not a difference between monsters and saints. It was a difference between people who carried the same pressures and lived by a different account of what a child is.

## What the First Christians Taught

The earliest Christian teaching on the subject is blunt. The Didache, a short manual of Christian teaching and one of the oldest Christian writings outside the New Testament, lists what a believer must not do. The list begins with murder and adultery, and partway through it says, in Kirsopp Lake's translation, "thou shalt not procure abortion, nor commit infanticide" (Didache 2.2). The child in the womb and the child just born appear in the same line, in the same list as murder.

An anonymous letter to a man named Diognetus, probably written in the second century, explains to an outsider how Christians live. They live in Greek and foreign cities alike, the writer says, and they follow the local customs in clothing and food and the rest of ordinary life. Then he names what sets them apart, and the first thing he names concerns their children: "They marry as all men, they bear children, but they do not expose their offspring" (Diognetus 5.6, again in Lake's translation). He treats it as one of the few things that would let a neighbor tell a Christian household from any other.

Justin Martyr made the same point to the emperor. His First Apology, addressed to Antoninus Pius around 150, tells him that "we have been taught that to expose newly-born children is the part of wicked men." His first reasons aren't the ones a modern reader expects. Christians refuse, he says, because "almost all so exposed (not only the girls, but also the males) are brought up to prostitution," and a man who visits prostitutes may be lying with his own abandoned child without knowing it. A little later he gives the plainest reason of all. Christians will not expose a child "lest some of them be not picked up, but die, and we become murderers" (First Apology 27 and 29). Justin knew where the foundlings of his city ended up, and he wrote it down for the emperor to read.

Tertullian, writing in Carthage around 197, went further. Christians in his day were accused of killing and eating babies at their secret meetings, and he turned the charge back on the people who made it. How many of the crowd shouting for Christian blood, he asked, and how many even of the rulers who had been severe with Christians, could he charge in their own consciences "with the sin of putting their offspring to death"? He named the methods: "by drowning, or by exposure to cold and hunger and dogs." For Christians, he wrote, "murder being once for all forbidden, we may not destroy even the foetus in the womb" (Apology 9). The community accused of eating children was the one place in town where a child could not be put out.

The Christians did not invent this conviction. They inherited it. Jews had long refused to kill their newborns, and even Tacitus, who wrote about the Jews with open contempt early in the second century, recorded that "they count it a crime to kill any of their later-born children" (Histories 5.5). What the church added was a story that made the old conviction very hard to set aside.

## Why Christians Saw Children Differently

Part of that story was creation. Genesis says that God created man in his own image, "male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). Hilarion's sentence sorted children along exactly that line, and the Christians would not. Every child, girl or boy, sickly or strong, bore God's image, and no household could decide that one of them was surplus.

Part of it was Jesus. When people brought children to him and the disciples tried to send them away, Mark says Jesus "was indignant" and told them, "Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God." Then "he took them in his arms and blessed them, laying his hands on them" (Mark 10:14, 16). In Matthew he sets a child in the middle of his disciples and says, "Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me" (Matthew 18:5).

The deepest part of it was older. Israel had long told its own story as the story of an unwanted child. In Ezekiel 16 the Lord describes Jerusalem as a baby left to die on the day of its birth: "No eye pitied you, to do any of these things to you out of compassion for you, but you were cast out on the open field, for you were abhorred, on the day that you were born." Then God comes by: "And when I passed by you and saw you wallowing in your blood, I said to you in your blood, 'Live!'" (Ezekiel 16:5–6). The rest of the chapter is a hard charge against the city's unfaithfulness, but it begins there. The people of God started out as a foundling that God picked up.

Moses is the same story told another way. Pharaoh ordered, "Every son that is born to the Hebrews you shall cast into the Nile" (Exodus 1:22). The boy who would lead Israel out of Egypt survived because his mother set him in a basket among the reeds and Pharaoh's own daughter found him. "She took pity on him," Exodus says (Exodus 2:6). The early Christians read those stories, and they read Paul telling them that God sent his Son "so that we might receive adoption as sons" (Galatians 4:5). They understood themselves as children who had been taken in. People who believe that about themselves find it very hard to put a baby out.

## Did the Early Christians Rescue Abandoned Babies?

Here the story usually told in churches runs ahead of the evidence. You may have heard that the early Christians went out to the rubbish heaps at night and gathered up the babies left there. It's a moving picture, but it isn't what the early writers quoted here describe. What they describe is refusal. Christians would not expose their own children, and they said so publicly, to emperors and to the crowds that accused them.

Evidence that they took in other people's foundlings comes later. In a letter written in 408, Augustine mentions in passing that foundlings were sometimes picked up by women vowed to virginity, who had no children of their own, and brought to be baptized. In 442 a council of bishops at Vaison, in Gaul, made provision for infants left at churches. Homes for foundlings came later still; a document dated 787 credits an archpriest of Milan named Datheus with founding one, though historians have questioned the date.

That same letter of Augustine's shows something else. Nearly a century after Constantine's conversion, in a Christian province, parents were still leaving children for passers-by. The Christian emperors changed the law slowly. A law of Constantine in 331 protected people who took in children their fathers had cast out. In 374 a law issued under Valentinian I made the killing of an infant a capital crime, and historians think exposure itself was probably outlawed the same year. It went on anyway.

Historians disagree about almost every number in this story. Boswell, in *The Kindness of Strangers*, estimated that urban Romans of the first three centuries abandoned somewhere between 20 and 40 percent of their children, but argued that most of those children were taken in by others and did not die. He also argued that abandonment stayed common in Christian Europe until the Renaissance, and that it changed form more than it disappeared: parents gave children to monasteries, and later left them at foundling hospitals.

W. V. Harris, in a study of child exposure in the Roman Empire, agreed that exposure was widespread but read the evidence darker. He described it as "very often but by no means always resulting in death," and as a way of moving children who were born free into slavery. Earlier, Donald Engels had argued that a high rate of female infanticide was demographically impossible, and Harris had replied that the argument didn't hold. The sociologist Rodney Stark built part of his account of the church's growth on the difference: pagan families exposed more girls than boys, Christians refused, and so the churches came to have more women of childbearing age and grew faster. His argument rests on the very estimates the historians are still contesting.

What can be said with confidence is narrower than the sermon illustration and still remarkable. From its earliest documents, the church taught that a newborn could not be put out. It said so to emperors who could have its members killed, and it treated the refusal as one of the marks of a Christian household. Rescue came later and unevenly. Abandonment did not end when the empire became Christian, and Christians practiced it too.

## What the Church Did with Foundlings and Their Mothers

If the early chapters of this history are less heroic than we like to tell them, some of the later chapters are much worse. Over the centuries the church's care for abandoned children grew into institutions, and some of those institutions became places where children died in great numbers and their mothers were punished for having them.

In Italy, as the historian David Kertzer showed in *Sacrificed for Honor*, church policies meant to prevent abortion and infanticide and to protect the honor of unmarried mothers eventually hardened into laws that required single women to give up their babies at birth. Hundreds of thousands of those children died in foundling homes.

In Ireland, a state commission reported in January 2021 on eighteen institutions, fourteen mother and baby homes and four county homes. About 56,000 unmarried mothers and 57,000 children had passed through them, and about 9,000 of the children died, roughly 15 percent. At the home in Tuam run by the Bon Secours Sisters, the local historian Catherine Corless found death records for 796 children who died there between 1925 and 1961.

It would be easy for a Protestant to read that and feel safely distant. Bethany Home in Dublin was run by evangelical Protestants, most of them from the Church of Ireland, the Presbyterians and the Plymouth Brethren. Research published in 2010 found that more than two hundred of its children, who died between 1922 and 1949, lay in unmarked graves in Dublin's Mount Jerome Cemetery.

In Canada, most of the maternity homes for unmarried women in the decades after the Second World War were tied to the Catholic Church and the Salvation Army, and others to the Anglican and United churches. In the United States, Ann Fessler's oral history *The Girls Who Went Away* gathered the stories of women who surrendered babies between 1945 and 1973, years in which, by the estimate her book uses, more than a million and a half babies were given up for adoption. The women she interviewed were not throwing their children away. Many of them were told they had no choice.

I don't know how to read these histories without seeing Hilarion's letter in them. Hilarion decided for Alis what would happen if the baby was a girl. The homes decided for the mothers. The church that had once refused to put children out learned, in too many places, to put out the women who bore them outside marriage, and in some of those places the children did not survive either.

Jesus said that whoever receives a child in his name receives him. In the next breath he warned that it would be better to be drowned with a great millstone around your neck than to cause "one of these little ones who believe in me to sin" (Matthew 18:6). A girl taught by her church that her baby was a disgrace, and that she was one too, was being taught a lie about God. Whatever else Jesus' warning covers, it covers that.

## The Unwanted Child in Post-Christian America

Nobody in this country leaves newborns on a rubbish heap as an accepted custom. Every state now has a safe haven law that lets a parent leave a newborn safely at a hospital, a fire station or another designated place. Texas passed the first in 1999, and it's still called the Baby Moses law. But unwanted children are still with us. On the last day of September 2024, about 329,000 children were in foster care in the United States, by the federal government's preliminary count. More than fifteen thousand young people aged out of foster care in 2023 without a permanent family, by the National Council for Adoption's count. Every one of those children is in the state's care because a family could not, for a time or for good, keep them safe.

And there is abortion. The Didache and Tertullian drew no line between the child in the womb and the child on the doorstep, and the church's historic opposition to abortion begins with them, among people who had no power to make any law at all. Americans are deeply divided over what the law should say, and Christians are divided too. This essay won't settle that argument. It is after something both sides of it are tempted to leave undone.

I have written about how a church's politics can [blind it](/writing/the-conservative-blind-spot) to the parts of Scripture its side would rather not hear, and how that works [on the progressive side as well](/writing/the-progressive-blind-spot). Here the blind spot has a face. Some Christians will speak up for the unborn child and never ask what a pregnant seventeen-year-old in their own youth group would need in order to keep her baby. Others will speak up for the women and look away from the child. Each is loving half of what the early church loved. Plenty of Christians do better than either picture. They staff pregnancy centers, become foster parents, adopt, sit with frightened mothers in hospital rooms, and push for prenatal care and paid leave. But the pull is real, and it tends to run along the same line our voting does.

The church before Constantine had no political power to use for anyone. I've written elsewhere about [how it lived under Rome without it](/writing/the-early-church-under-rome). What it had was households, and a teaching it was willing to be seen keeping. What would that look like in an American congregation now? It would look like a church where an unmarried, pregnant teenager could tell her youth leader before she told anyone else and be met with help before she heard a lecture. It would look like more families becoming foster parents, and many more carrying meals, babysitting and giving a night off to the foster parents already worn thin. It would look like adoption that honors the birth mother instead of erasing her, and like churches that keep helping a young mother long after the baby shower is over.

Psalm 68 calls God the "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows" and says that he "settles the solitary in a home" (Psalm 68:5–6). The homes he settles them in are meant to include ours. James wrote that the religion that is pure before God the Father is this: "to visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (James 1:27). He did not say to hold the right opinion about them.

## If You Have Lost a Child

Some of you won't read this as history. You may have lost a child, to miscarriage or stillbirth or later. You may have given a child up, willingly or because you were pressed to, and you may still count the birthdays. You may have been the child who was given up. Or you may have ended a pregnancy, and the lines from the Didache and Tertullian landed hard.

I don't want to hurry past any of that. If you are grieving a child, Scripture doesn't ask you to be comforted on a schedule. Matthew remembers the mothers of Bethlehem after Herod's soldiers came, and he gives their grief the words of Jeremiah: "Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted, because they are no more" (Matthew 2:18). God put that grief in his book, and he did not rebuke it.

If you ended a pregnancy, you are not reading about a monster, and you are not beyond the reach of the God this essay has been describing. Grief over what happened is not a sign that God is finished with you. Often it is where he begins. The gospel doesn't ask you to pretend nothing happened. It asks you to bring it to Christ, who carried sin like this to the cross, and to take the forgiveness he died to give.

Tell him in whatever words you have, and tell someone you trust, a pastor or a friend who can keep a confidence. Many churches and ministries quietly help women after an abortion, and you won't be the first to ask. Paul wrote, "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1), and he wrote it to people with real sins behind them. The psalmist says, "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (Psalm 34:18).

If any of this has brought you close to despair, please tell someone today. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Our [Find Help](/help) page lists other places to start. This essay can't give medical or counseling advice, and it isn't meant to take the place of the people who can.

The first Christians didn't end the abandonment of children in Rome, and the Christians who came after them didn't either. What they did was refuse it in their own houses, slowly and imperfectly, because they believed they had been taken in themselves. The church has forgotten that more than once since.

So the question this history leaves is not really about Rome.

Who in your town is the child nobody planned for, and the mother nobody has asked about?

Would either of them be safe with us?

Every one of us came to God the way Jerusalem did in Ezekiel's story, cast out on the open field with no eye to pity us. In Christ he passed by and said, "Live!"

He still says it, to the child and to the mother, and to those of us who failed them both.
