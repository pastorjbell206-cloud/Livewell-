# Did the Resurrection Actually Happen?

*Christianity has staked everything on the claim that Jesus of Nazareth rose bodily from the dead, and the evidence for that claim can be weighed by anyone willing to read it carefully.*

Sometime in the 50s, writing from Ephesus, Paul answered a church he had planted in Corinth. Among the troubles in that congregation was a group that had decided the dead are not raised. Paul did not treat it as a small difference of opinion. "Now if Christ is proclaimed as raised from the dead, how can some of you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?" (1 Corinthians 15:12). Then he followed the thought all the way to the bottom. If Christ has not been raised, he wrote, "your faith is futile and you are still in your sins," and "if in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:17, 19).

That is a remarkable thing for an apostle to put in writing. Paul doesn't say the faith would keep its beauty or its ethics if the body stayed in the grave. He says the apostles would be "found to be misrepresenting God" (1 Corinthians 15:15). Plenty of religions can survive having their founding stories read as poetry. Christianity has never been able to do that with Easter, and Paul closed that door himself. The question in the title is the one everything else in the faith hangs on.

And the people Paul was answering sat inside the church. They were believers in Corinth who had decided there is no resurrection of the dead, and that ought to make the rest of us slower to treat the skeptic as a stranger. A great many Christians have carried the skeptic's question into church with them, and some of us still carry it.

## What Paul Handed On

Paul began his answer by reminding the Corinthians of what he had already handed on to them.

> "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep. Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me." (1 Corinthians 15:3–8)

"Delivered" and "received" are the words Paul uses when he hands on something he did not compose. He uses the same pair a few chapters earlier for the Lord's Supper: "For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you" (1 Corinthians 11:23). Most of this list came to Paul already formed, and he passed it on as he got it. Where did he get it? He tells the Galatians that about three years after his conversion he went up to Jerusalem "to visit Cephas and remained with him fifteen days," and that the only other apostle he saw there was "James the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:18–19). Two of the names on the list are the two men he saw.

Jesus was crucified while Pilate governed Judea, between the years 26 and 36. Paul's visit to Jerusalem came a few years later, and the letter itself two decades or more after the crucifixion. Scholars across the field, including many with no stake in Christianity, think Paul received this summary within a few years of the events it describes. That matters because a common skeptical account treats the resurrection as a story that grew over generations, the way a dead teacher becomes a hero and then a god. The list in 1 Corinthians doesn't have that shape. It is early, and it names names. It even adds a detail no legend needs, that most of the five hundred were still alive, which is how you write when you expect people to check.

That doesn't prove anyone saw anything. It does mean the claim was not a late growth. It was being repeated, in fixed form, while the people it named were still alive to be asked.

## What Almost Every Historian Grants

Before weighing explanations, it helps to be clear about what needs explaining, and here believers and skeptics who study the period agree more than their popular writers let on. Jesus of Nazareth was crucified under Pontius Pilate. Early in the second century the Roman historian Tacitus, who despised Christians, recorded that Pilate had put him to death. (Whether Jesus existed at all is a separate question, which historians answer more firmly than the internet does. See [Was Jesus Real?](/writing/the-historical-jesus).)

Soon after his death, a number of his followers became convinced they had seen him alive. Paul, who by his own account "persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13), became convinced of the same thing. So did James, a brother who had not believed in him during his ministry: "For not even his brothers believed in him" (John 7:5). And the preaching began in Jerusalem, in the city where he had been executed and buried.

Bart Ehrman, one of the most widely read skeptical New Testament scholars in America, grants that some of Jesus' followers came to believe soon after the crucifixion that God had raised him. What he denies is that the belief was true. That's a reasonable line to draw, and Christians do themselves no favors when they blur it. A historian can establish that people were convinced. Whether the thing that convinced them was real is a further question.

So the question is narrower than it first looks. What happened to make these particular people, within weeks, stake their lives on the claim that a crucified man had been raised? Why would they say it in Jerusalem, where it was easiest to refute? And why did they reach for the word resurrection?

## Was the Tomb Empty?

All four Gospels say that Mary Magdalene, alone or with other women, found the tomb empty early on the first day of the week. The strongest objection to that story comes from Ehrman. In *How Jesus Became God* he argued that Roman practice was to deny the crucified a decent burial, and that it is hard to see why Pilate would have made an exception for Jesus. If Jesus was never laid in a known tomb, the empty-tomb stories are later legend.

Other scholars, Craig Evans among them, answered that in Judea the Romans allowed the Jews to bury their dead. The law of Moses required that a hanged man's body "shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day" (Deuteronomy 21:23), and Josephus, the Jewish historian of the first century, said his people took down even the crucified and buried them before sunset. In the 1960s a construction crew in a Jerusalem neighborhood called Giv'at ha-Mivtar broke into a family tomb from around the time of Jesus. In one bone box lay the remains of a young man named Yehohanan, with an iron nail still driven through his heel. At least one crucified man in Jerusalem was buried by his family. Ehrman replies that one case proves very little, and the argument goes on.

Some details in the Gospels push hard against invention. Mark says the man who buried Jesus, Joseph of Arimathea, was "a respected member of the council" (Mark 15:43), the same council the Gospels hold responsible for handing Jesus over. A Christian making up a burial would hardly give the honor to one of them. Then there are the women. In that world a woman's testimony carried little weight, and Josephus, summarizing the law, said it was not to be admitted in court. When the pagan philosopher Celsus attacked Christianity late in the second century, he went straight for the weak point. Who saw the risen Jesus? "A half-frantic woman, as you state." Anyone inventing evidence to persuade the ancient world would have chosen other witnesses.

The first reaction to the empty tomb wasn't faith, either. In the oldest manuscripts we have, Mark's Gospel ends with the women running from the tomb: "they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid" (Mark 16:8). Luke says the women's words "seemed to them an idle tale, and they did not believe them" (Luke 24:11). John has Mary Magdalene assume what anyone would assume. Seeing a man she takes for the gardener, she says, "Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away" (John 20:15).

Mary was the first person in the story to suggest the body had been taken. The authorities were the second. Matthew reports that the guards were paid to say, "His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep," and adds that "this story has been spread among the Jews to this day" (Matthew 28:13, 15). In the middle of the second century Justin Martyr complained, in his dialogue with a Jew named Trypho, that men had been sent throughout the world to say the disciples stole the body from the tomb by night. Many scholars doubt Matthew's guard story. Still, the charge it answers points the same way as Justin's complaint. The church's earliest opponents didn't say the body was still in the tomb. They argued about how it got out.

An empty tomb, by itself, proves nothing. It made Mary think of grave robbers and made the women afraid. If an empty grave were the only evidence, nobody would have preached a resurrection, so the preaching needs more than a missing body to explain it.

## Could They Have Seen Visions?

The most serious alternative is also the oldest. In the same passage where Celsus mocked Mary Magdalene, he offered his own account of the appearances: the witness "had either dreamed so, owing to a peculiar state of mind, or under the influence of a wandering imagination had formed to himself an appearance according to his own wishes, which has been the case with numberless individuals." Celsus's own book is lost. His words survive in the point-by-point answer Origen of Alexandria wrote around the middle of the third century. The modern hallucination theory adds clinical vocabulary, but the idea is his.

He had a point, and modern research strengthens it. In 1971 a doctor in Wales named W. Dewi Rees published a study in the British Medical Journal of 293 widowed men and women, and almost half of them reported experiences of their dead spouse, some for years afterward. Rees thought these experiences normal and often helpful. The disciples were bereaved and frightened, and Peter had denied knowing Jesus on the night of his arrest. Why wouldn't they see him? Ehrman thinks some of them did, and that belief in the resurrection grew out of those visions. Dale Allison, a New Testament scholar who is himself a Christian, has conceded more here than most apologists like. He has collected reports of the dead appearing to the living, some of them to more than one person at once, and he warns believers against claiming that such visions are always private.

He's right to warn them. Still, the theory strains at the edges of the evidence. Bereavement experiences come to people who are mourning the dead. Paul wasn't mourning Jesus. He was trying to destroy his followers. James, as far as the Gospels tell us, didn't believe in his brother while he lived. The list in 1 Corinthians includes groups, and the Gospels describe the risen Jesus eating, being touched, and talking at length. A vision theory can account for some of that. To account for all of it, it has to keep adding a new explanation for each witness, and it still has to explain the word the witnesses chose.

## What "Resurrection" Meant

Grieving people in the first century saw their dead too, and they had words for it. When Peter, freed from prison, knocked at the door of a house where the church was praying for him, a servant girl named Rhoda ran to tell them he was outside. They said to her, "You are out of your mind," and then, "It is his angel!" (Acts 12:15). When the risen Jesus stood among the disciples in Luke's Gospel, "they were startled and frightened and thought they saw a spirit" (Luke 24:37). Ghost, angel, spirit, vision: the ancient world had a full vocabulary for seeing the dead, and resurrection was not part of it.

Resurrection meant something specific. N.T. Wright, a historian who also served as an Anglican bishop, devoted much of a long book, *The Resurrection of the Son of God*, to tracing what the word meant to Jews and pagans of the period. For most pagans, it named the thing everyone knew does not happen. When Paul spoke of the resurrection of the dead in Athens, "some mocked" (Acts 17:32). Celsus understood the Christian claim exactly and rejected it for that reason. The question, he wrote, "is whether any one who was really dead ever rose with a veritable body."

Many Jews did hope for resurrection, though the Sadducees denied it (Mark 12:18), and for those who hoped, it meant the bodily raising of God's people together at the end of history. "And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2). Martha says it to Jesus when her brother has been four days in the tomb: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day" (John 11:24).

So if the disciples had only seen visions, Jews of their day had natural things to say. Jesus had been vindicated, his spirit was with God, and he would rise with the righteous on the last day. What they actually said had no precedent: one man had been raised bodily, ahead of everyone else, while the world went on. Paul reached for a farmer's word: "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). Firstfruits are the first sheaf of the harvest, brought in before the rest. Wright's argument is that it took the empty tomb and the appearances together to produce that belief. On its own, a missing body looks like a robbery, which is how Mary first read it. And if his body had still been lying in a known grave, the sightings would have been taken for visions, the kind grieving people already had words for.

Other Jewish messianic movements show the same thing from the outside. In 132 a Jewish leader named Simon bar Kosiba led a revolt against Rome, and Rabbi Akiva hailed him as the messiah and applied to him the prophecy of a star out of Jacob (Numbers 24:17). He became known as Bar Kokhba, the son of the star. Rome killed him at Betar in 135. A thousand years later Maimonides explained that the sages had believed he was the messiah until he was killed, and that his death showed them he was not. That was the normal logic. A messiah executed by the pagans had failed. Jesus' followers shared that logic, and they had a natural successor in his brother James, who led the church in Jerusalem until he was stoned there around 62. Nobody called James the messiah. They said God had raised Jesus.

## The Other Explanations

The theory that the disciples hid the body and lied has few defenders among scholars now, for a plain reason. They paid for the claim. Acts records that Herod Agrippa "killed James the brother of John with the sword" (Acts 12:2). Josephus describes the stoning of James the brother of Jesus. A letter from the church in Rome to the church in Corinth late in the first century, the one we call 1 Clement, says that Peter and Paul suffered martyrdom. The evidence for how most of the other apostles died is later and less certain, and Christians should say so instead of repeating legends as fact. (There's more on that in [Why Were the Early Christian Martyrs Willing to Die?](/writing/the-first-christians-died-for-this).)

People die for false beliefs all the time. What these deaths show is that the men believed what they said, and they were in a position to know whether they had taken the body themselves. That is as far as the deaths take us, though. They make fraud very hard to believe and leave a sincere mistake possible, which is why the vision theory is the serious rival.

The other alternatives have fared worse. The idea that Jesus didn't really die but revived in the cool of the tomb was popular among some rationalist critics two centuries ago. Another skeptic, the German critic David Friedrich Strauss, answered it best: a half-dead man in need of bandages and nursing could never have persuaded anyone he had conquered death. The idea that the women went to the wrong tomb runs into Joseph of Arimathea, who knew where he had laid the body, and into the authorities, who could have ended the preaching by pointing to the right one. Celsus also compared the Christian story to Greek and Egyptian legends of figures said to have come back from the underworld, a comparison skeptics still make (see [Did Christianity Copy Pagan Myths?](/writing/did-christianity-copy-pagan-myths)).

## Can a Historian Say a Miracle Happened?

For many skeptics, the reason to reject the resurrection comes before any of this evidence is weighed. David Hume gave that reason its classic form in his essay "Of Miracles," published in 1748. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence," he wrote. Our experience that the dead stay dead is as uniform as experience gets. No testimony to a miracle, he argued, has ever been strong enough to outweigh that experience, and none could ever prove a miracle as the foundation of a religion. The reasonable course is to conclude that the witnesses were deceived or deceiving.

Some of that is simply true. Hume's principle is sound, and the dead do stay dead. The disciples knew that as well as Hume did, which is why they didn't believe the women. If the claim were that Jesus revived through some natural process nobody understands, Hume would be right to wave it off. But the first Christian sermon didn't claim that. Peter told the crowd in Jerusalem, "God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it" (Acts 2:24). As a natural event, the resurrection is about as improbable as anything can be, and it was never offered as one. How likely it is depends on whether there is a God who acts, and on whether this man was who he said he was.

Hume's own example shows where his argument finally rests. He imagined every historian of England agreeing that Queen Elizabeth died on the first of January 1600, was seen by her physicians and the whole court before and after her death, and after a month in the grave reappeared, took back the throne, and governed for three years. Even then, Hume said, he "should not have the least inclination to believe so miraculous an event." That is candid of him, and it shows that in a case like this no testimony could have moved him. His conclusion rested on a judgment, made in advance, about what kind of world this is. If nothing beyond nature ever acts within it, then the resurrection didn't happen and some natural explanation must be true, however strained. But whether the world is closed in that way is the very question the resurrection raises. You can't settle it by assuming the answer and calling the result skepticism.

I was an atheist before I was a Christian, so I don't assume that a skeptic reading this is simply being stubborn. I also don't think history can prove the resurrection. Its tools are built to tell us what usually happens, and this is by definition what doesn't.

What history can do is more modest. It can show that the facts are strange, that each natural explanation covers part of them and strains at the rest, and that the explanation the first witnesses gave covers more of them than most readers expect. That won't carry anyone to faith, but it can move a person from contempt to consideration, which is a real distance. Matthew admits that when the eleven saw the risen Jesus on a mountain in Galilee, "they worshiped him, but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17). The Gospel writers didn't hide that doubt, and the church has no reason to be more frightened of it than they were.

## What the Church Has Done With Easter

The resurrection has never been threatened only from outside. Like the Corinthians, the church keeps finding ways to say there is no resurrection of the dead, usually without saying it aloud.

In much of mainline Protestantism the change came gently. Marcus Borg, a scholar who wrote a book with Wright setting their two views side by side, treated the empty-tomb stories as parables whose truth didn't depend on what happened to the body. Many pulpits went the same way, and Easter became a feast of renewal and new beginnings. That is easier to preach, and it is very close to the position Paul was answering in Corinth. Celsus, who rejected the claim outright, at least understood that it was about a body.

Evangelical churches, and Baptist churches like the one I pastor, tend to defend the empty tomb fiercely and then preach a hope that has little to do with it. The working hope of a great deal of American Christianity is that the soul goes to heaven when you die and the body is left behind for good. The creeds don't confess that. The Apostles' Creed confesses "the resurrection of the body," and the Nicene Creed says, "we look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come." A church that insists on a bodily resurrection for Jesus while expecting something disembodied for itself has kept the argument and misplaced the hope. It leaves Easter with Jesus, as something that happened to him once and changes little for the rest of us.

Paul brought the whole chapter down to ordinary life. If the dead are not raised, he admitted, then the sensible way to live is the motto of the revelers Isaiah rebuked in Jerusalem (Isaiah 22:13): "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" (1 Corinthians 15:32). But if they are, "be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58). That covers the care given to a dying parent, the forgiveness that cost you something, the faithfulness in a marriage nobody applauds, and the years of raising children who may never thank you.

Because God raises bodies, what we do in them and for them matters. That's why the church buries its dead with hope and doesn't pretend they aren't dead. Paul wrote to believers in Thessalonica "that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). He took it for granted that they would grieve, and he wanted their grief to rest on hope. If you are reading this in the middle of a fresh loss, the argument can wait, and you shouldn't carry the grief alone. [Find Help](/help) is a place to start.

Paul didn't soften what follows if Jesus is still in the grave: Christians are the people most to be pitied.

If he isn't, the question was never only whether the evidence is good enough for a historian. It's what you will do in a world where death has already been undone once.

None of us, believer or skeptic, should settle that by what we assumed before we looked.
