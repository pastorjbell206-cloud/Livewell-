# What Does Romans 13 Say About Obeying the Government?

*Romans 13 tells Christians to be subject to whoever governs, and read in its place in Paul's letter it also tells them what government is for.*

On June 14, 2018, Attorney General Jeff Sessions spoke to a law enforcement audience in Fort Wayne, Indiana, and defended the administration's policy at the southern border, under which children were being separated from their parents. He cited "the Apostle Paul and his clear and wise command in Romans 13" and summarized it as a command to obey the laws of the government because God has ordained them. Later that day the White House press secretary told reporters it was "very biblical to enforce the law."

The answers came quickly. The bishops of the African Methodist Episcopal Church rejected his reading, and within days more than six hundred United Methodist clergy and laypeople filed a complaint under their church's law against Sessions, himself a United Methodist. Franklin Graham, one of the president's most loyal evangelical supporters, had already called the separations "disgraceful" in an interview two days before the speech. For a lot of Americans that week was the first time they noticed Romans 13 being used as a political weapon. It had been one for a long time.

A skeptic watching from outside could fairly conclude that Christians will make the Bible say whatever their side needs that year. Sometimes we have. But the opposite habit, treating Romans 13 as an embarrassment to be explained away whenever it is quoted against us, is no better. The passage is in the Bible, and it says something. In the week of an election, when some of us are about to be governed by people we voted for and some by people we voted against, it's worth asking what.

## Who Paul Was Writing To

Paul wrote Romans in the middle or late 50s, probably from Corinth, to Christians in a city he had never visited. They met in households; he greets "the church in their house" (Romans 16:5). Some of them had already been on the wrong end of an emperor's order. Acts tells of a Jewish couple, Aquila and Priscilla, who had come to Corinth "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to leave Rome" (Acts 18:2). When Paul wrote, they were evidently back in Rome, and theirs is the first greeting in his list (Romans 16:3).

The emperor now was Nero, who had come to the throne in 54 at the age of sixteen. Tacitus records that in 58 the people complained so persistently about the greed of the tax collectors that Nero considered abolishing the indirect taxes altogether, until his advisers talked him out of it. The Christians of Rome paid those taxes. They had no vote and no say in who ruled them. And a few years after the letter, when fire swept through Rome in 64, Nero blamed the Christians and had many of them put to death so cruelly that Tacitus, who despised the sect, says people began to pity them. Early Christian tradition holds that Paul himself was executed in Rome under the same emperor.

So when Paul wrote "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities" (Romans 13:1), the authority he had in mind was not a Christian prince or an elected government. It was Nero's Rome, a few years before it turned on the church. That fact cuts in both directions, and the rest of this essay is about why.

## Romans 13 in Its Place in the Letter

The chapter break between Romans 12 and 13 was added in the Middle Ages. Paul wrote one continuous section, and it begins this way: "I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship" (Romans 12:1). Everything that follows describes a life that has been given to God as worship. Love has to be genuine. Paul writes, "Bless those who persecute you; bless and do not curse them" (12:14), and "Repay no one evil for evil" (12:17). Then comes the hardest line in the section: "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (12:19). And "if your enemy is hungry, feed him" (12:20).

Only then does Paul turn to the governing authorities, and his words tie the two passages together. The ruler, he says, "is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (13:4). In Paul's Greek, "avenge" in 12:19 and "avenger" in 13:4 come from the same root, and "wrath" is the same word in both verses. He has just taken vengeance out of the Christian's hands, and now he tells them where some of it has gone. A church that has given up revenge has not handed the world over to chaos. God restrains evil, in part, through the ordinary authority of rulers who punish wrongdoing.

Then Paul names what is owed: "Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed, revenue to whom revenue is owed, respect to whom respect is owed, honor to whom honor is owed" (13:7). In the next breath he picks up the same word and turns it: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (13:8). The debt to Caesar can be paid in full. The debt of love never can. "Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the law" (13:10).

And then he sets the whole thing in time. "The night is far gone; the day is at hand" (13:12). The authorities of verse 1 are real, and they belong to the night that is ending. Read this way, the section begins with Christians offering their bodies to God as worship and ends with them putting on Christ (13:14). The governing authorities receive taxes, respect and honor in the middle. They are not offered the worship of the opening verse.

## The Case for Obedience

The Christians who read Romans 13 as a strong command to obey have the plainest words on their side. Paul says "every person." He says the authorities that exist "have been instituted by God," not the good ones or the elected ones. He says, "Therefore whoever resists the authorities resists what God has appointed, and those who resist will incur judgment" (13:2). He wrote that under Nero, to people who had no part in choosing him. Peter told the churches of Asia Minor the same thing: "Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution, whether it be to the emperor as supreme" (1 Peter 2:13).

The early church took it that way. Polycarp of Smyrna, facing the proconsul around 155, refused to swear by the fortune of Caesar. Yet he told the proconsul why he was willing to give him an account of his faith: "we are taught to give all due honour (which entails no injury upon ourselves) to the powers and authorities which are ordained of God." He died rather than swear. But he died honoring the office of the governor who let him be burned.

John Calvin spent much of the last chapter of his *Institutes* teaching that private Christians owe obedience even to wicked rulers. A bad king, he argued, may be God's judgment on a people's sins, and the private person's part is to repent and pray, not to take up arms.

There is practical wisdom here too. Order is a gift, and people who have lived where it collapsed know what a gift it is. A Christian who decides for himself which laws he'll keep has taken back into his own hands what Paul just told him to leave with God. And the urge to resist is rarely as pure as it feels. Most of us discover the limits of Romans 13 when a law costs us something, and we're slower to find them when it costs someone else.

## The Case for Resistance

The other reading has its own history, and in America it has a date. In 1750, on the Sunday after the anniversary of King Charles I's execution, Jonathan Mayhew, pastor of the West Church in Boston, preached the sermon that became *A Discourse Concerning Unlimited Submission and Non-Resistance to the Higher Powers*. The Church of England kept January 30 as a solemn day in memory of the king, and Mayhew complained that on that day "the slavish doctrine of passive obedience and non-resistance, is often warmly asserted."

He went through Romans 13 verse by verse. Paul's case for submission, he argued, rests on what rulers are for. In Paul's description they serve God for the people's good, a terror to bad conduct and not to good. Paul was not describing Nero's actual character, since "the apostle was no sycophant, or parasite of power." He was describing the office. A ruler who turns it upside down and makes himself a terror to the good is not the ruler Paul commands Christians to obey. "Rulers have no authority from God to do mischief," Mayhew wrote, and "It is blasphemy to call tyrants and oppressors, God's ministers."

He was careful about where that led. Every ruler is fallible, he said, and if a government's being badly managed in some instances were enough to justify disobedience, no government could stand. What forfeits the obedience Paul commands is a ruler who acts against the common good habitually, as a settled course. Calvin had left a door open too. At the end of the same chapter that counsels private Christians to suffer, he allowed that lesser magistrates appointed to check kings, like the ephors of ancient Sparta, would betray the people if they did not resist a king's tyranny, and he closed the *Institutes* with Peter's words to the council in Jerusalem.

A generation after Mayhew, the argument came to war. In Maryland the Anglican rector Jonathan Boucher taught that obedience to government is commanded by God, and he was preaching with loaded pistols on the pulpit before he sailed for England in September 1775. The next summer Benjamin Franklin proposed a seal for the new nation that showed Pharaoh being overwhelmed by the Red Sea, with the motto "Rebellion to tyrants is obedience to God." Whether the Revolution was a just war is a question faithful Christians still answer differently, and [whether the nation it made was a Christian one](/writing/was-america-founded-as-a-christian-nation) is another. But the resistance reading asks a question the obedience reading has to answer. If it makes no difference whether a ruler does good, why does Paul say the ruler is "God's servant for your good" (Romans 13:4)?

## How the Passage Has Been Used

The history after Mayhew shows how easily either reading can be bent. On November 24, 1850, about two months after Congress passed the Fugitive Slave Act, Ichabod Spencer, a Presbyterian pastor in Brooklyn, preached on Paul's charge to be "submissive to rulers and authorities" (Titus 3:1) and printed the sermon as *The Religious Duty of Obedience to Law*. The law he urged Christians to obey required the return of people who had escaped slavery.

Germany in the 1930s is the hardest case. The German Christians, a movement that wanted a Protestant church shaped to the Nazi state, won the church elections of 1933 with the regime's support, and the new order's friends leaned on Romans 13 in a Protestant culture long taught that a Christian's duty to the state was obedience. At the end of May 1934, a synod of 139 delegates from Lutheran, Reformed and United churches met at Barmen and adopted a declaration written mostly by Karl Barth.

Its fifth article took up the state, and it took its text from Peter's command to fear God and honor the emperor (1 Peter 2:17). The state, it said, has by God's appointment the task of providing for justice and peace in a world not yet redeemed, and does it by the threat and use of force. Then it rejected the idea that the state could become "the single and totalitarian order of human life" and take over the church's calling as well. Barmen did not deny what Romans 13 says about the state. It described the state the way Paul does, as a servant with a task, and it denied that the servant's authority reached past the task.

Not everyone agreed. Soon afterward two respected Lutheran professors at Erlangen, Paul Althaus and Werner Elert, signed a statement with a circle of pastors at Ansbach that thanked God for giving the German people the Führer as a pious and faithful sovereign. The phrase is close to the one Luther's Small Catechism uses when it lists good rulers among the things a Christian asks for in praying for daily bread. Before that summer was over, Hitler had rivals inside and outside his party killed without trial in the Night of the Long Knives.

Barmen had a silence of its own. It said nothing about the Jews, who were already being pushed out of German public life, and in 1967 Barth wrote that he had long felt guilty for not making that question central at Barmen. If the best confession of that year could miss the people suffering most, we shouldn't assume our own reading of Romans 13 sees everyone it ought to.

The American uses since then have moved with the party in office. In 1982, under a Republican administration, Southside Presbyterian Church in Tucson, led by its pastor, John Fife, declared itself a public sanctuary for refugees from the wars in Central America, in open defiance of federal immigration law. Hundreds of congregations followed, most of them from the religious left. In 1985 a federal grand jury indicted sixteen sanctuary workers, and eight of them, Fife among them, were convicted the next year.

In 2018, under another Republican administration, an attorney general quoted Romans 13 to defend enforcement at the same border. In 2020, when California's Democratic governor restricted indoor worship during the pandemic, John MacArthur's Grace Community Church in Los Angeles, which had complied with the first shutdown that spring, published a statement titled "Christ, not Caesar, Is Head of the Church" and kept meeting, arguing from Romans 13 that the state had stepped outside the sphere God gave it. Defenders of the orders quoted Romans 13 back at the churches that stayed open.

Which of those uses looks faithful to you probably depends on how you vote. That is the trouble. A reading of Romans 13 that changes when the party in office changes isn't really a reading of Romans 13.

## What the Rest of Scripture Adds

Romans 13 is not the Bible's only word about rulers, and the other witnesses do not cancel it. They show what being subject looks like when obedience becomes impossible.

When the council in Jerusalem ordered the apostles to stop teaching in Jesus' name, Peter answered, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). The council had them beaten and let them go, and they left "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (5:41). They refused the order. They did not resist the beating.

Three young men in Babylon refused to bow to Nebuchadnezzar's golden image: "But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up" (Daniel 3:18). The king himself later described what they had done. They "set aside the king's command, and yielded up their bodies rather than serve and worship any god except their own God" (3:28). Daniel, under a decree forbidding prayer to anyone but the king, went home and prayed toward Jerusalem three times a day, "as he had done previously" (Daniel 6:10). He did not hide and he did not make a show. When the king came to the lions' den at dawn, Daniel's first words were "O king, live forever!" (6:21), and then, "before you, O king, I have done no harm" (6:22). He had disobeyed the decree, and he still honored the king.

The words matter here. Paul's verb in Romans 13:1 is "be subject," the word for taking your place under an order. Peter's word in Acts 5:29 is the ordinary word for obeying a ruler. The two overlap, and Paul can ask for both at once, telling Titus to remind Christians "to be submissive to rulers and authorities, to be obedient" (Titus 3:1). But submission is the larger word. A Christian can refuse a command and still remain under the authority that gave it, accepting its penalty instead of fleeing or fighting. Martin Luther King Jr. pointed to the three men in Babylon in his letter from the Birmingham jail in 1963 and wrote that "one who breaks an unjust law must do so openly, lovingly, and with a willingness to accept the penalty."

The New Testament also has another chapter 13. In Revelation 13 John sees a beast rise from the sea. "And to it the dragon gave his power and his throne and great authority" (Revelation 13:2). The world worships it and asks, "Who is like the beast, and who can fight against it?" (13:4). It makes war on the saints, and a second beast demands worship for its image and decides who may buy and sell. Later John sees a woman seated on seven mountains, "drunk with the blood of the saints" (17:6, 9), and most readers ancient and modern have seen Rome in her. Romans 13 and Revelation 13 describe the same empire. Paul shows what government is when it does what God gave it to do. John shows what it becomes when it claims worship and makes war on the church. Even then John does not call the churches to revolt: "Here is a call for the endurance and faith of the saints" (Revelation 13:10).

The task in every generation is to tell which of those two chapters is in front of us. Christians who can only see Romans 13 will bless the beast. Christians who see Revelation 13 everywhere will call every government they dislike a beast and lose the ability to give honor where it is owed.

## Romans 13 for Citizens Who Vote

Paul's readers had no vote. American Christians do, and that changes how Romans 13 applies without changing what it says. In the United States the governing authority is not finally a person. The Constitution begins with "We the People of the United States," every federal and state officer is bound by oath to support it, and the president swears to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution of the United States." Being subject to the governing authorities here means being subject to that order of law, and it binds the president as surely as the citizen. A Christian who cheers an official for ignoring the law because the official is on the right side has not obeyed Romans 13. He has taken a side against the governing authority.

It also means the citizen holds a small share of what Paul calls the sword. A vote helps decide whom the government will treat as a wrongdoer and whose conduct it will protect. Romans 13 tells us what that sword is for: to be God's servant "for your good," a terror to bad conduct and not to good, under a law of love that "does no wrong to a neighbor." That standard now falls on the voter. Every Christian who casts a ballot is answerable for whether the authority he helps to choose looks more like Romans 13 or Revelation 13.

So what does the passage ask of us this week? It asks for honor, and honor is owed to officials we did not vote for. Paul told the Christians of Rome to give "honor to whom honor is owed" with Nero on the throne, and contempt for the people who govern us, the kind that fills so much of our talk about them, is disobedience to Scripture even when our policy disagreements are right. It asks us to pay our taxes, including the ones collected by an administration we opposed. It asks us to pray for them. Romans 13 doesn't say so in as many words, but the chapter before it tells Christians to bless even those who persecute them, and Paul told Timothy to pray "for kings and all who are in high positions" (1 Timothy 2:2).

And it asks for conscience. Paul says we are subject "not only to avoid God's wrath but also for the sake of conscience" (Romans 13:5). The conscience that makes a Christian submit is the same conscience that will sometimes make him refuse, and I can't tell you in advance where yours will draw that line. Daniel and the apostles can show you what it looks like when you get there.

What the passage does not give is a blank check. It gives none to any party, and none to whichever side of the church is winning this year. The authority Paul describes is a servant. When Christians on the right quote Romans 13 only when their candidate holds office, and Christians on the left discover it only when a governor of theirs is giving the orders, both have stopped reading the passage and started using it. Few of us can say we've never done it. [The love of country Scripture assumes](/writing/what-christian-nationalism-is-and-is-not) knows the difference between honoring a government and belonging to it.

The Christians in Rome who first heard this letter had no candidate to vote for. They were told to pay their taxes, honor a young emperor, and leave vengeance to God in a city that would soon kill many of them. [The church lived that way for most of three centuries](/writing/the-early-church-under-rome), at times under emperors who wanted it gone.

They were also told that the night was nearly over.

Nero is long gone, and the Rome he ruled with him, and the Lord those Christians confessed is still Lord.

Whoever takes office in January will be owed our taxes, our respect and our prayers. Not our worship, and not our conscience.

Those belong to the One who stood before a Roman governor and told him, "You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above" (John 19:11). He submitted to that authority all the way to the cross, and on the third day God raised Him. Every authority Paul told the Romans to honor now answers to Him.

*"Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law."* (Romans 13:8)
