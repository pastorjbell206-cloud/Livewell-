# Deconstructing Your Faith: What to Tear Down and What to Keep

*Much of what people tear down when they deconstruct was never the Christian faith, and the work is learning to tell the difference.*

In late July 2019 Joshua Harris told his followers on Instagram that he no longer considered himself a Christian. Harris had written *I Kissed Dating Goodbye* in 1997, in his early twenties, and the book shaped how a generation of evangelical teenagers thought about dating, sex, and marriage. In 2018 he had asked his publisher to stop printing it and apologized to readers it had hurt. Now, days after announcing that he and his wife were separating, he wrote: "I have undergone a massive shift in regard to my faith in Jesus. The popular phrase for this is 'deconstruction,' the biblical phrase is 'falling away.'"

He gave two names to one experience, and most of the argument about deconstruction since then has been over which name is right. To many in the church, the word is a gentler label for apostasy, a way of leaving that sounds like thinking. To many outside the church, it means growing up and finally telling the truth about what you were taught. I don't know how Harris's story ends. Nobody does, and it isn't ours to finish for him. But both readings assume that what is being taken apart is Christianity. Very often it is something else.

The word itself came from the French philosopher Jacques Derrida, who used it in the late 1960s for a way of reading texts against their own assumptions. In American churches it now means something plainer. People take apart the faith they were raised in, piece by piece, to see what holds. Some leave. Some come back to a deeper faith than the one they left. Most are somewhere in between. Where does deconstruction usually start? What deserves to come down? And what is underneath when it does?

## Where It Usually Starts

Deconstruction rarely begins with philosophy. It usually begins with something that happened. In January 2002 the Boston Globe began reporting how the Archdiocese of Boston had moved priests accused of abusing children from parish to parish. In May 2022 an independent investigation by Guidepost Solutions found that staff of the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee had kept a private list of ministers accused of sexual abuse for more than a decade, with no sign that anyone had acted to keep those men out of positions of power in the convention's churches.

In February 2021 an investigation commissioned by the ministry of Ravi Zacharias, one of the best-known Christian apologists of his generation, found that he had sexually abused women over many years under the cover of that ministry. Most people who deconstruct never read those reports. They remember a youth pastor, a deacon, or an elder board that protected the wrong person. For them the question about God arrived after the question about the people who claimed to speak for him.

The second wound is watching the faith put to work for something else. Over the last half century much of American evangelicalism tied itself so tightly to one political party that many young people raised inside it can't tell where the gospel ends and the platform begins. When they pull at the platform, the gospel comes loose with it, because no one ever showed them the seam. The left has its own version, in churches that make one set of political convictions the measure of real faith. The fusion is the same sin wherever it appears. But in the churches most people mean when they talk about deconstruction, it has run mostly to the right, and pretending otherwise would be a dodge.

The third wound is the answers. Many people were handed promises the Bible never makes. Purity culture left a lot of teenagers believing that if they waited, God would reward them with a happy marriage and a good sex life. Prosperity preaching promised health and money for enough faith. When the promises failed, people concluded that God had failed. And when they brought real questions about suffering, about hell, or about the hard chapters of the Old Testament, they were often given a verse and a look that meant the conversation was over.

Indifferent people rarely bother to take a faith apart. They drift. Jim Davis and Michael Graham, working with surveys run by the political scientists Ryan Burge and Paul Djupe, estimated in *The Great Dechurching* (2023) that about 40 million American adults who once attended church at least monthly now go less than once a year. Most of them, the authors found, left for ordinary reasons. They moved, their family changed, the habit lapsed. The person who deconstructs is usually doing something else.

She is the teenager who actually read the book of Joshua and noticed the conquest. She is the woman who believed the church was the body of Christ and so could not shrug when it protected an abuser. People like that are not usually rebels. They are grieving something they took seriously. When a church hands people a faith that can't carry the weight of their questions, it shouldn't be surprised when the faith breaks. We built it that way.

## What Deserves to Come Down

Much of what falls deserves to fall. In 2005 the sociologists Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton published *Soul Searching*, built on a national survey of more than three thousand American teenagers and long interviews with more than two hundred of them. They gave the faith most of those teenagers actually held a name: moralistic therapeutic deism. Its creed, as they summarized it, runs roughly like this. A God exists who made the world and watches over it. He wants people to be good and nice to each other. The point of life is to be happy and feel good about yourself. God doesn't need to be involved unless you have a problem. Good people go to heaven.

Look at what the list leaves out. There is no sin that needs forgiving, no cross, no resurrection, no judgment, and nothing that costs anything. Smith and Denton found this faith among Catholic, mainline Protestant, and evangelical teenagers alike, and they argued that the teenagers had largely learned it from the adults around them. That is the god much deconstruction is taking apart. Beside him stand the national god who blesses whatever the country does and the prosperity god who pays out for faith. When someone tears those down, they are not attacking Christianity. They are agreeing with its first commandment.

God has torn down false religion before, and he used a prophet to do it. In the thirteenth year of King Josiah, the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah, a young man from a family of priests in the town of Anathoth. Most of his commission was demolition:

> "See, I have set you this day over nations and over kingdoms, to pluck up and to break down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant." (Jeremiah 1:10)

Four of the six verbs are about tearing down. God later sent Jeremiah to the gate of the temple to tell the worshipers going in, "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (Jeremiah 7:4). Judah believed the temple guaranteed its safety, whatever the people did on the way to it. Jeremiah named their theft, murder, adultery, and offerings to Baal. When he preached in the temple court that the house itself could fall, the priests and the prophets seized him and said he deserved to die (Jeremiah 26:8–11). The man tearing down Judah's religion was the one God had sent.

But the commission did not end with overthrow. Its last two verbs were *build* and *plant*, the slow ones. Late in Jeremiah's ministry, with the Babylonian army besieging Jerusalem and the prophet confined in the court of the guard, God told him to buy his cousin Hanamel's field at Anathoth. Jeremiah paid for it, signed the deed before witnesses, and had the documents stored in a clay jar so they would last. The reason was a promise: "Houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (Jeremiah 32:15). A man who had spent decades tearing things down bought land in a war zone. The demolition was always for the sake of something that would grow.

## The Worry on the Other Side

The Christians who worry about deconstruction are not only protecting an institution. Scripture speaks of people who fall away, and it treats the possibility seriously: "Take care, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart, leading you to fall away from the living God" (Hebrews 3:12). In the parable of the sower, Jesus describes people with no root, who "believe for a while, and in time of testing fall away" (Luke 8:13). Some deconstruction is a decision already made, out looking for respectable reasons. Pastors can tell you about people who found serious objections to the faith in the same season they found a reason to want out of it.

Alisa Childers knows the worry from the inside. A former singer with the Christian pop group ZOEgirl, she describes in *Another Gospel?* (2020) how a class at her own church left her doubting the faith she grew up with, and how she worked her way back. Her argument is that much of what is called progressive Christianity keeps the vocabulary of the faith while changing what the words mean. A cross that only teaches us to love, or a resurrection that is only a way of talking about hope, keeps the word and empties it of what it meant. A faith rebuilt to agree with you about everything is a new idol standing where the old one stood.

The same thing happens in the other direction, though people rarely call it deconstruction. Some leave a soft, therapeutic church and rebuild around a harder political Christianity that offers certainty and a clear set of enemies. They have traded one tribe for another, and the new one also agrees with them about everything they care about. The test of any rebuilt faith, left or right, is whether anything in it can still tell you no.

I came to faith as an adult, from atheism, and I don't think the questions are the danger. They aren't new. In the late second century the philosopher Celsus wrote that belief in the resurrection rested on the word of a half-crazed woman and a few others caught up in the same delusion. In the middle of the third century Origen answered him point by point. The church has heard the hardest objections before and kept confessing its faith. What matters is the direction of the taking apart. Is it moving toward something, or only away?

## What Lies Underneath

What is down there when you dig through the additions? It isn't empty dirt. One of the oldest Christian confessions we have was written down before any of the Gospels. Writing to Corinth in the mid-50s, Paul reminded the church of what he had handed on when he first came:

> "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve." (1 Corinthians 15:3–5)

Paul says he received this, so it was already a fixed tradition before he wrote it down. And he stakes everything on it: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). That is not a man offering a helpful way of seeing the world. It is a claim about something that happened, and it can be weighed the way other claims about the past are weighed.

N. T. Wright devoted a very long book, *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003), to arguing that the empty tomb and the appearances are the best explanation for how the earliest church began. You don't have to accept every step of his case to see where the weight of the faith sits. The evidence gets a fuller hearing in [Did the Resurrection Actually Happen?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen)

When the church later had to say what that confession meant, it did so under pressure. Around 318 an Alexandrian presbyter named Arius began teaching that the Son of God, however exalted, was a created being. The teaching came to be summed up in a slogan: there was a time when he was not. It was a reverent position, meant to protect the oneness of God, and some form of it returns in every century. In 325 the Council of Nicaea rejected it. Its creed confessed the Son "begotten, not made," of one being (*homoousios*) with the Father, and condemned the slogan by name.

The creed was expanded at Constantinople in 381 into the form most churches still say. In 451 the Council of Chalcedon confessed Christ as one person in two natures, truly God and truly man, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation. [What Was Decided at the Council of Nicaea?](/writing/the-council-of-nicaea-what-was-decided-in-325) tells the story of the first council.

The floor is smaller than many people expect. The creeds say nothing about which party to vote for. They say nothing about the age of the earth, the order of events at the end of the world, how to school your children, or whether teenagers should date. Faithful churches divide over baptism, over the millennium, over whether women may serve as pastors, and over how to read the first chapter of Genesis, and they remain Christian churches while they argue. Many of the things falling off a deconstructing person's faith were never part of the creed. They were the rules of one congregation, one movement, or one decade.

But a small creed is not an easy one. The creed that leaves your politics alone says that God became flesh, was crucified under Pontius Pilate, rose on the third day, and will come again to judge the living and the dead. Those claims are harder for a modern person to accept than anything purity culture taught. A rebuilt faith that keeps the comfortable parts and quietly drops these has stopped short of the floor.

## Augustine Took His Faith Apart Too

One of the church's greatest teachers went through all of this. Augustine was born in 354 in Thagaste, in what is now Algeria, to a Christian mother named Monica. At nineteen he read a book of Cicero's that set him on fire for philosophy. When he turned to the Bible, he found it crude beside Cicero's polished Latin. He spent the next nine years among the Manichees, a sect that promised a religion of pure reason and mocked the Old Testament. When their most famous teacher, Faustus, came to Carthage and could not answer his questions, Augustine drifted toward the skeptics, who held that nothing could be known for certain.

In 384 he moved to Milan to teach rhetoric and went to hear the bishop, Ambrose, mostly to judge his eloquence. What he heard was a man who showed that passages which seemed crude on the surface carried a deeper sense, and who often repeated Paul's line that "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (2 Corinthians 3:6). Ambrose received the young skeptic kindly, Augustine says, like a father. It still took two more years. In the summer of 386, in a garden in Milan, he heard a child's voice chanting "take up and read," opened Paul's letter to the Romans, and read the passage that ended his resistance (Romans 13:13–14). Ambrose baptized him at Easter in 387.

Augustine didn't return to the faith as he had first heard it at nineteen, and he didn't invent a new one. He came to the same faith his mother held and found it far deeper than the version he had rejected. The Bible he had thought crude became the book he preached for the rest of his life. It took more than a decade, and the help came from a pastor who read Scripture better than its critics did. Parents praying for a grown child might remember Monica. A bishop she begged to argue her son out of his errors told her to leave him alone for a while and pray, and assured her that the son of so many tears could not be lost.

More than a thousand years later the whole Western church went through something like it. Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses of 1517 took aim at a specific thing, the sale of indulgences, which had made release from the punishment for sin something a person could pay for. Rome excommunicated him in 1521. The Reformation that followed split Western Christianity, and wars came after it. Yet Luther kept the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds, and his *Small Catechism* of 1529 taught children the Apostles' Creed article by article. He understood himself to be digging back toward Scripture and the early church, not walking away from them.

## How People Rebuild

Paul gave a young church an instruction that fits this work. Writing to the Thessalonians about the prophetic words spoken in their gatherings, he told them, "Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21). The setting is narrower than ours, but the shape carries over. Testing is commanded, not merely tolerated. And the command assumes the testing will turn up something worth holding on to.

Rebuilding usually starts with sorting. Most people who deconstruct have torn down two different things at once and treated them as one. The first is the party, the formula, the cover-up, and the answer that ended the conversation. The second is the faith the creeds confess. Was the second ever examined on its own terms? Or did it simply come down with the first because it was standing nearby? A woman can be right to walk away from the church that hurt her and still owe the resurrection a hearing of its own.

The way back also tends to run through practice before it reaches certainty. James K. A. Smith argued in *You Are What You Love* (2016) that we are formed less by the ideas we hold than by what we love, and that habit trains our loves. If habits shaped your loves on the way out, they can reshape them on the way back. That might mean praying when it feels like talking to the ceiling, or reading a Gospel while arguing with it, or sitting in a back row with your arms folded. The desert monks of Egypt kept a saying, attributed to Abba Moses, that told a restless brother to go and sit in his cell, because his cell would teach him everything.

Jesus told a story about a son who came home before he had everything worked out. The younger son in Luke 15 rehearsed a speech on the road, a confession that ended with a request to be taken on as a hired servant. "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). The son got partway through his speech before his father called for a robe, a ring, and shoes. He never reached the part about being a servant. The father wasn't waiting at the end of a finished argument. He was watching the road.

The church you rebuild in will almost certainly be older and wider than the one you left. Most of the world's Christians now live in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, and what they confess is the creed of Nicaea, not the subculture of one American decade. People formed in a shallow church often find what they were missing in older practices, like the church year and the Lord's Supper taken seriously. People formed in a harsh one often find that the tradition has always had room for lament. Either way, don't do this alone. Augustine had Ambrose. Find someone who won't panic at your questions or flatter them, and give the work the years it takes.

## If the Doubt Has Turned Dark

For some readers this isn't an intellectual project at all. Taking apart the faith you were raised in can cost you friendships and the whole way you made sense of your life, and that loneliness is real. If the darkness has grown to the point where you wonder whether there is any reason to keep going, set the theology aside for tonight and reach for a person. In the US, you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. An essay is not counseling, and you deserve a real person beside you. [Find Help](/help) lists places to start.

If your doubt began with abuse by a pastor or another church leader, that abuse should be reported to the police or other civil authorities, not handled quietly inside the church. [Doubt After Being Abused by a Church Leader](/writing/doubt-after-church-abuse) was written for you. Matthew applies to Jesus an old promise from Isaiah about the servant of the Lord: "a bruised reed he will not break, and a faintly burning wick he will not quench" (Isaiah 42:3). A faith bruised by the people who should have protected it is exactly the kind he does not throw away.

## What Are You Digging Toward?

The church usually asks how to keep people from deconstructing. We would do better to ask which of our additions they are tearing down, and why we put them there. Every congregation has taught some things with more confidence than Scripture does. Which of our sermons would survive the questions our children will eventually ask? Which of mine would?

For the person doing the digging, the question is different. You can take apart everything piled on top of the gospel and keep going until nothing is left. Or you can dig through it to the floor the church has stood on since Paul wrote to Corinth. For a while the two look the same.

The father in Mark 9 had watched his son suffer since childhood, and Jesus' disciples had already failed to help. He came with half a faith and said so: "Immediately the father of the child cried out and said, 'I believe; help my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:24). Jesus did not make him finish believing first. He healed the boy.

Bring him what you have, the belief and the unbelief together.

That prayer is in the Bible because he answered it.
