# Why Didn't the Bible Just Ban Slavery?

*The sentence the skeptic is looking for isn't in the Bible. The reason it isn't is harder on the church than on the book.*

In 1845 Frederick Douglass, seven years out of slavery in Maryland, published his *Narrative* in Boston. He attached an appendix explaining that his attacks on religion were aimed at "the slaveholding religion of this land" and had "no possible reference to Christianity proper." That same year, Baptists from the slaveholding states met in Augusta, Georgia, and organized the Southern Baptist Convention, after the denomination's mission boards had made clear they would not appoint slaveholders as missionaries. Both sides had Bibles. Both believed they were reading them faithfully.

The skeptic looks at a year like that and asks the obvious question. If the Bible is the word of a good God, why didn't it just say that owning people is wrong and spare everyone the argument? Of all the objections the church hears, this one does as much damage as any, because it doesn't need any philosophy. Most objections ask the believer to defend something abstract. This one needs only the text and the record, and the church supplied both. The Bible regulates slavery without forbidding it, and when the institution was finally challenged in the English-speaking world, some of its most confident defenders were preachers with open Bibles. The skeptic doesn't have to invent the accusation. He only has to read.

The objection goes like this. The law of Moses rules on what a garment may be woven from (Leviticus 19:19) and whether a young goat may be boiled in its mother's milk (Deuteronomy 14:21). On whether one human being may own another, the same law gives instructions for doing it, and Leviticus 25 doesn't leave much room for a comfortable reading:

> "As for your male and female slaves whom you may have: you may buy male and female slaves from among the nations that are around you. You may also buy from among the strangers who sojourn with you and their clans that are with you, who have been born in your land, and they may be your property. You may bequeath them to your sons after you to inherit as a possession forever." (Leviticus 25:44–46)

That is permanent, inheritable ownership, passed down like land. The New Testament doesn't reverse it with a decree. Paul writes, "Bondservants, obey your earthly masters with fear and trembling" (Ephesians 6:5), and he sends Onesimus — whom most readers have taken to be a runaway — back to the man who owned him. If God had wanted the institution ended, the objection says, the book had every opportunity. Every time, it chose to manage slavery instead.

Most of that is true, and the church doesn't help itself by pretending otherwise. The history is worse than the texts. George Whitefield, the best-known preacher of the First Great Awakening, campaigned to make slavery legal in the colony of Georgia and owned enslaved people whose labor supported his orphanage there. Jonathan Edwards bought and owned slaves. John Newton was converted in 1748 and kept sailing on slave ships for years afterward — first as a mate, then as a captain — and he didn't publicly oppose the trade until 1788.

Before the Civil War, Southern pulpits produced a whole literature defending slavery from Scripture. Some of the most widely read of it came from a Virginia Baptist minister named Thornton Stringfellow, who built his case on Abraham's household, on Leviticus 25, and on Paul. The historian Mark Noll, in *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis*, makes the uncomfortable point that if you read the Bible the way most American Protestants had been taught to read it — verse by verse, at face value — the defenders of slavery often had the easier argument. The abolitionists had to appeal to the spirit and direction of Scripture, and to a great many devout people that sounded like an evasion.

I pastor a Baptist church, and Baptists don't get to tell this story from a safe distance. A hundred and fifty years after Augusta, in 1995, the Southern Baptist Convention adopted a resolution acknowledging the part slavery had played in its founding and repenting of the racism it had condoned. Whatever answer a Christian gives to this question has to be given by someone who knows the skeptic is describing his own family.

## The Usual Answer

The usual Christian answer starts with a distinction, and the distinction is real. When an American hears the word *slavery*, our own history fixes the picture: people kidnapped in Africa, shipped across an ocean, and held as property from birth to death, with their children born into the same condition on the basis of race. Much of what the Hebrew law regulates under the same English word was something else. It was debt servitude in an economy with no bankruptcy court and no public relief, where a man who had lost everything could sell his labor to stay alive. The law limited it. "When you buy a Hebrew slave, he shall serve six years, and in the seventh he shall go out free, for nothing" (Exodus 21:2), and Deuteronomy adds that he wasn't to be sent away empty-handed (Deuteronomy 15:13–14). A slave who lost an eye or a tooth to his master's hand went free because of it (Exodus 21:26–27). Every fiftieth year Israel was to "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants" (Leviticus 25:10). The King James wording of that line is cast into the Liberty Bell, and it was abolitionists in the 1830s who gave the bell its name.

I have to be careful here, because this is exactly where a great deal of Christian apologetics likes to stop. The distinction is true, and it doesn't cover Leviticus 25:44–46. The release laws in that chapter protect the Israelite who sold himself into debt. The permanent ownership applies to foreigners. The same chapter that gave the abolitionists their slogan gave the slaveholders their warrant, and a Christian who quotes the first half to a skeptic and leaves out the second has done what the slaveholder did in the other direction. He has picked the verses that help him. Exodus 21 has its own hard sentence. A master who beat a slave to death on the spot was to be punished, but "if the slave survives a day or two, he is not to be avenged, for the slave is his money" (Exodus 21:21). No reading of that verse makes it pleasant, and a believer doesn't get to skip it for that reason.

## The Line Leviticus Draws

Read Leviticus 25 closely and the permission turns out to rest on a single distinction. "If your brother becomes poor beside you and sells himself to you, you shall not make him serve as a slave" (Leviticus 25:39). The reason comes three verses later: "For they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt; they shall not be sold as slaves" (Leviticus 25:42). An Israelite couldn't be owned by another Israelite because he already belonged to the God who had bought him out of Egypt. The foreigner could be held, and the passage ends by drawing the line in plain words: "You may make slaves of them, but over your brothers the people of Israel you shall not rule, one over another ruthlessly" (Leviticus 25:46).

Everything depends on who counts as a brother. That is the question the New Testament reopens. Paul tells Gentile believers, "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). He tells the Corinthians, slave and free together, "You were bought with a price; do not become bondservants of men" (1 Corinthians 7:23), which is the logic of Leviticus 25:42 carried through the cross to everyone Christ has redeemed. And when he sends Onesimus back, he asks Philemon to receive him "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother—especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord" (Philemon 16). He closes by saying he is confident Philemon "will do even more than I say" (Philemon 21).

Paul doesn't issue an order there. In the categories of Leviticus 25 he does something that reaches further. He moves Onesimus across the exact line the permission depended on. By the law's own logic a brother can't be held as property, and in the household of God there are no longer any strangers to hold. This isn't a modern sensibility pressed back onto an ancient text. It's the canon interpreting itself: "Here there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all" (Colossians 3:11). The American system drew its line between brother and stranger by color, and it drew that line inside churches where enslaved and free Christians had been baptized into the same body. The gospel had already erased it.

## The Law That Was Already There

There's a second problem with the skeptic's question, and it lands on the church at least as hard as it lands on the book. The question assumes that one plain sentence would have settled the matter. But the Bible does contain a plain sentence that condemns what the Atlantic slave trade actually was. It made very little difference.

"Whoever steals a man and sells him, and anyone found in possession of him, shall be put to death" (Exodus 21:16). In the Torah, kidnapping a human being to sell or to keep is a capital crime, and in the ESV's rendering the law reaches past the kidnapper to whoever ends up holding the stolen person. The Atlantic trade, from the raid on the village to the auction in Charleston, was a chain of exactly that. The New Testament repeats the charge. When Paul lists the people the law exists to restrain, he names "enslavers" (1 Timothy 1:10), using a Greek word, *andrapodistēs*, that means a slave-dealer, a man who trades in captured human beings. The King James Version rendered it "menstealers," and American abolitionists took the word straight out of their Bibles. Douglass used it in that same 1845 appendix: "We have men-stealers for ministers, women-whippers for missionaries, and cradle-plunderers for church members."

Defenders of slavery had an answer ready. Many of them conceded that the African trade had been wicked, and the United States had outlawed the importation of slaves in 1808. What they would not concede was that a man who inherited slaves, or bought them in Richmond from another churchgoer, was holding stolen people. But on the ESV's reading the verse had already answered them when it named "anyone found in possession of him." They read past it.

In *The Monster in the Mirror* I've written at length about the slaveholder who kept a Bible on his desk — a sincere man inside a community that had wrapped its conclusions in Scripture until he couldn't see what he was doing. The point for this question is narrower. He didn't lack a prohibition. He had one, carrying the death penalty, in the same chapter of Exodus he cited for a master's rights. And Paul's word for menstealers sat in the same letter he cited for a slave's duty to "regard their own masters as worthy of all honor" (1 Timothy 6:1). The missing sentence was never the problem.

Here the skeptic and the slaveholder turn out to share more than either would like. This is not a moral comparison. It's a comparison of method. Both read the Bible as a legal code in which silence settles things. The slaveholder said that because the book never forbids slavery in so many words, slavery must be lawful. The skeptic says that because the book never forbids it in so many words, the book must be guilty. Both are looking for a clause, and neither is reading the book the way it asks to be read — as one story with a center, in which laws given to a particular people at a particular moment are measured against what God intended "from the beginning" and against where he was taking his people. The enslaved Christians of the American South read it that way, and so did Douglass, with the same Bible their masters had.

## Hardness of Heart

That still leaves the hardest form of the question standing. Granting all of this, why give Israel a law that regulated slavery at all? Why not forbid it at Sinai, the way the law forbade carved images?

Jesus gives the closest thing Scripture has to a direct answer, though he gives it about divorce. When the Pharisees pressed him on why Moses had made provision for a man to send his wife away, he said, "Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8). Christians tend to forget what that sentence establishes. Not everything the law permits, God approves. Some of the law meets a hard-hearted people where they are and restrains the damage they'll do, while creation stands behind it as the measure. And creation isn't silent about human beings. "So God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27) is said of every one of them, and Job draws the conclusion out loud. If he had ever brushed aside the complaint of his own servants, he says, he would have had no answer when God rose up and made inquiry: "Did not he who made me in the womb make him? And did not one fashion us in the womb?" (Job 31:15).

Read against its own world, the law looks more like a restraint than an endorsement. Every society in the ancient Near East held slaves, and the Code of Hammurabi, from Babylon some eighteen centuries before Christ, made it a capital crime to help a slave out through the city gate or to hide a runaway in your house. Israel's law turned that around: "You shall not give up to his master a slave who has escaped from his master to you. He shall dwell with you, in your midst, in the place that he shall choose within one of your towns, wherever it suits him" (Deuteronomy 23:15–16). The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which required Americans to help return escaped slaves, points the opposite way. Its defenders liked to cite Paul sending Onesimus home. They spent less time on what Paul asked Philemon to do once he arrived.

The Sabbath command in Deuteronomy gives rest to the male and female servant "as well as you" and grounds it in memory: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:14–15). In a world where Aristotle could argue that some human beings are slaves by nature, a law that freed the injured slave, sheltered the runaway, and made the master remember he had been a slave himself was pushing against the grain.

None of this makes the question easy. "Hardness of heart" explains how a law can permit what God doesn't love. It doesn't make the permission painless, and it doesn't give back a single year to anyone who lived and died under it. Those people weren't a stage in someone's theory of moral progress, and a Christian who offers this answer shouldn't sound pleased with himself for having found it.

But the pattern is consistent. Through the whole of Scripture, God's way is to form a people whose central story will eventually make certain things unbearable to them. Israel's founding memory is a God who says, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings" (Exodus 3:7). A people commanded to retell that story every year at Passover can carry bad readings for a long time — but not forever. A decree can be repealed, ignored, or explained away, as Exodus 21:16 was. A people who come to believe that the slave is a brother for whom Christ died will eventually find that they can't own him. That way is slower than legislation, and the cost of the delay fell on people who never saw the end of it.

## How It Actually Ended

The history bears that out, slowly, and not in a way that flatters the church. The first Christian writer we know of who condemned slavery itself, and not just its cruelties, was Gregory of Nyssa in the late fourth century. Preaching on the verse in Ecclesiastes where the Preacher boasts, "I bought male and female slaves" (Ecclesiastes 2:7), Gregory asked his congregation what price they thought they had paid for a creature made in the image of God. The church didn't follow him for well over a thousand years, and any account of this history has to say so.

When the conscience finally moved, though, it moved from those texts. Quakers in Germantown, Pennsylvania, protested slavery in 1688 on the strength of the Golden Rule. Newton's 1788 pamphlet was the confession of a man who had been a Christian for forty years and had taken most of them to say in public what the trade was. William Wilberforce, whose evangelical conversion came in 1785, wrote in his diary two years later, "God Almighty has set before me two great objects, the suppression of the Slave Trade and the Reformation of Manners." He brought the cause back to Parliament again and again for nearly twenty years before the trade was abolished in 1807. In July 1833 he lived just long enough to hear that the bill ending slavery across most of the British Empire was assured of passage. He died three days later.

In America the deepest conviction was among the enslaved themselves, who heard Exodus read — often by preachers hired to teach them obedience — and knew whose story it was. When they sang "Go Down, Moses," nobody had to explain to them who Pharaoh was. Douglass drew the line that the whole controversy finally came down to: "I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ; I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land."

The skeptic is right to resist any version of this story in which the church simply takes a bow. Abolition had other engines. Enlightenment arguments about natural rights mattered, and so did the revolt of the enslaved in Saint-Domingue that began in 1791 and ended in an independent Haiti, and so did economics, and finally war. The church was on both sides, and it was on the wrong side first and for longest. But the Christian engine of abolition was real. It ran on Exodus, on the image of God, on the law against manstealing, and on the brother in Philemon — the same texts the slaveholders had to read around.

## What We Owe the Person Asking

A Christian who answers this question by explaining that biblical slavery was really just employment with an unfortunate name tells the skeptic that the church still cares more about the book's reputation than about the people the book was used against. The answer the record supports is harder to give. The sentence the skeptic wants isn't in the Bible, and Leviticus 25 permits what it permits. But the same book contains a capital law against stealing and holding human beings, and a letter that turns an owned man into a brother, and the church had both and read around them for a very long time. That is the charge. It falls on us before it falls on the book.

It also puts a question to the church that doesn't belong only to the nineteenth century. Every congregation, mine included, has passages it quotes and passages it skips, and it's rarely an accident which is which. The slaveholders' preachers skipped Exodus 21:16. Which verses does our preaching skip? And whose comfort does the skipping protect?

The faith outlived the people who held it captive, and the church has the least right of anyone to claim that as its own doing. In the slave quarters and brush arbors of the South, men and women who had been taught the Bible so that they would obey heard in it the God who had seen the affliction of his people and heard their cry. The churches that grew out of those gatherings have carried that reading ever since.

A skeptic doesn't have to share their faith to notice that they read the book more truly than the men who owned them did.

If the church wants to answer him, it will have to learn to read the way they did — starting with the passages that indict us.
