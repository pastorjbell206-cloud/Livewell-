# What the First Readers of the Bible Actually Heard

*Every book of the Bible was first heard by particular people in a particular place, and learning what they heard keeps us from making Scripture say what we already wanted.*

In 2018 the most-read verse on the Bible Gateway website was Jeremiah 29:11: "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope." You have probably seen it on a graduation card. Most people who love it hear God speaking about their own lives, about the next job or the next school or the diagnosis they fear. That isn't a foolish way to hear it. But it isn't how the people who first heard it took it, and one of them wrote to Jerusalem to ask why Jeremiah hadn't been put in the stocks.

The verse sits in a letter "that Jeremiah the prophet sent from Jerusalem to the surviving elders of the exiles, and to the priests, the prophets, and all the people, whom Nebuchadnezzar had taken into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon" (Jeremiah 29:1). Babylon's army had taken Jerusalem in 597 BC and carried off King Jehoiachin, with his mother, his officials, the craftsmen and the metal workers (2 Kings 24:10–16). The letter was addressed to the whole community of exiles. It was meant to be read aloud to them.

They had been hearing other voices. In the temple a prophet named Hananiah had announced that within two years God would bring back the king, the exiles, and the temple vessels Nebuchadnezzar had carried off (Jeremiah 28:3–4). In Babylon, the letter says, there were prophets telling the exiles lies in God's name (Jeremiah 29:8–9, 15). Jeremiah told them to stop listening. "Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce" (Jeremiah 29:5). Marry, he said, and see your children married. Pray for the city you've been sent to, "for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7). Then came the timetable: "When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you" (Jeremiah 29:10). The verse on the graduation card comes next.

One of the exiles, Shemaiah of Nehelam, understood the letter perfectly. He wrote to the priests in Jerusalem, reminded them that their charge was to put any madman who prophesied "in the stocks and neck irons," and asked why they had not rebuked Jeremiah. He summed up the prophet's offense in a single line: "Your exile will be long" (Jeremiah 29:26–28). That is what the first audience heard. The promise of a future and a hope came to people who had just been told they would grow old in a foreign city. Seventy years is longer than most adult lives. Many who heard that letter read would be buried in Babylon.

An English reader also can't see that the "you" in that verse is plural in Hebrew. God was speaking to a people about a future many of its members wouldn't live to see. This doesn't take the verse away from Christians. Paul wrote that "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Corinthians 1:20), and the church has always read Israel's promises as belonging, through Christ, to everyone joined to him. But the promise comes to us the way it came to them. It says that God's purpose for his people outlasts any one of us. It was given to people told to settle where they didn't want to be and to work for the good of a city that wasn't theirs. Read that way, it has more to say to Christians learning to live in a [post-Christian](/writing/what-post-christian-means) country than to a senior choosing a college.

## Who the Bible Was First Written To

John Walton, an Old Testament scholar, has said one thing about Scripture so often that when a book of essays was published in his honor in 2020, it took the saying as its title. The Bible was written for us, he says, but it was not written to us. Every book had a first audience with its own language, its own enemies, its own memories and fears, and its writers assumed things those listeners already knew. When we skip over that audience, we don't read the text neutrally. We quietly take their seats and assume our questions were theirs.

We also forget how they received it. For most of history God's people didn't read the Bible so much as hear it. In the fifth century before Christ, after Jerusalem's walls had been rebuilt, "all the people gathered as one man into the square before the Water Gate" and asked Ezra the scribe to bring out the Book of the Law (Nehemiah 8:1). He read it to the men and women and all who could understand, from early morning until midday, while the people stood (Nehemiah 8:2–5). Levites helped them follow it: "They read from the book, from the Law of God, clearly, and they gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). When the people understood it, they wept.

One of the church's favorite lines comes out of that morning. Nehemiah, Ezra and the Levites told the weeping crowd that the day was holy and they should stop mourning. "Go your way. Eat the fat and drink sweet wine and send portions to anyone who has nothing ready, for this day is holy to our Lord. And do not be grieved, for the joy of the LORD is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:10). We usually hear the last clause as encouragement for a hard week. Its first hearers heard a command to stop weeping over a law they had only now understood and to hold a feast. The portions were for neighbors with nothing prepared.

The pattern held for centuries. At the council in Jerusalem, James reminded the church that Moses "is read every Sabbath in the synagogues" (Acts 15:21). Paul expected his letters to be heard the same way. "I put you under oath before the Lord to have this letter read to all the brothers," he told the Thessalonians (1 Thessalonians 5:27), and he told the Colossians to trade letters with the church at Laodicea (Colossians 4:16). The last book of the Bible opens with a blessing on "the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy" and on "those who hear" (Revelation 1:3).

Historians argue about how many people in the Roman world could read. William Harris, whose 1989 study set the terms of that argument, put the figure at not much more than one in ten. Some scholars think he set it too low. Either way, most people in those first congregations knew Paul's letters by ear.

Hearing changes what a text is. A letter read aloud to a room is heard whole, from greeting to farewell, and the "you" lands on everyone at once. When Paul asks, "Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16), every "you" is plural. He means the church in Corinth, a congregation splitting into camps around its favorite teachers, and he warns that God will destroy whoever destroys that temple (1 Corinthians 3:4, 17). Read alone, the verse easily becomes advice about taking care of yourself.

The numbers in our Bibles make that easier still. The chapter divisions most Bibles use go back to Stephen Langton, an English churchman who became archbishop of Canterbury, in the early thirteenth century. The New Testament's verse numbers come from a Greek New Testament that the French printer Robert Estienne published in 1551. The Geneva Bible of 1560 was the first English Bible to print chapters and verses together. Those numbers are a real help, since they let a whole congregation find the same line. But they make every sentence look like a separate saying, ready to be lifted out and carried off. Paul wrote letters. He never wrote a verse.

## When the Text Confronted Its First Hearers

Once you ask who first heard a passage, you notice how often it was aimed at them. Luke tells of Jesus in the synagogue at Nazareth, "where he had been brought up," reading from the scroll of Isaiah about good news to the poor and liberty to the captives (Luke 4:16–19). He sat down and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). The room loved it. "All spoke well of him and marveled at the gracious words that were coming from his mouth" (Luke 4:22).

Then he reminded them that in Elijah's day God sent the prophet to a widow in Sidon and to none of the widows in Israel, and that in Elisha's day the only leper cleansed was "Naaman the Syrian" (Luke 4:25–27). "When they heard these things, all in the synagogue were filled with wrath" (Luke 4:28). They drove him to the brow of the hill their town was built on, meaning to throw him off (Luke 4:29). The congregation welcomed the good news until it reached a widow in Sidon and a Syrian general.

Luke sets the parable of the Good Samaritan in the same kind of room. A chapter earlier, a Samaritan village refuses to receive Jesus because he is headed for Jerusalem, and James and John ask, "Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?" (Luke 9:52–54). Jesus rebukes them. Shortly afterward in Luke's account, a lawyer stands up to test him.

The hostility was old. Samaritans worshiped on Mount Gerizim, which is why the woman at the well told Jesus, "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship" (John 4:20). "For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans," John explains (John 4:9). Late in the second century before Christ, the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus captured Shechem and destroyed the Samaritan temple on Gerizim. Within Jesus' own lifetime, while Coponius governed Judea for Rome, Josephus reports that some Samaritans slipped into the Jerusalem temple at Passover and threw human remains about its colonnades. After that, Samaritans were kept out of the temple.

So when the lawyer, "desiring to justify himself," asked, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29), Jesus told him about a man left half dead on the road to Jericho. A priest passed by on the other side, and so did a Levite. A Samaritan stopped, bound up his wounds, took him to an inn and paid for his care (Luke 10:30–35). Then Jesus turned the question around. He asked which of the three "proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers" (Luke 10:36). The lawyer answered, "The one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:37). Readers have long noticed that he answered without saying the word Samaritan.

We name hospitals after the Good Samaritan, and American states have Good Samaritan laws to protect people who stop to help. The phrase now means a kind stranger. The lawyer heard a hero drawn from a people his own people despised and blamed for defiling the temple, and Luke's readers had just watched a Samaritan village turn Jesus away. Jesus made that man the one who kept the commandment the lawyer had recited so well. Then he said, "You go, and do likewise" (Luke 10:37). The lawyer was told to go and do what a Samaritan had done.

The book of Jonah works the same way. A prophet named Jonah the son of Amittai appears in 2 Kings, announcing that King Jeroboam II would restore Israel's border (2 Kings 14:25). He was a prophet of good news for his own nation. In the book that carries his name, God sends him to "Nineveh, that great city" (Jonah 1:2). For the people of Israel and Judah, Nineveh came to stand for Assyria, the empire that captured Samaria in 722 BC and carried the northern tribes away (2 Kings 17:6), and whose king Sennacherib "came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them" (2 Kings 18:13). Jonah boarded a ship going the other way.

He tells us why only after Nineveh has repented and God has spared it. "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster" (Jonah 4:2). He was echoing the words the LORD proclaimed to Moses on the mountain (Exodus 34:6), and he was angry enough to ask to die. The book ends with God's question: "And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city, in which there are more than 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left, and also much cattle?" (Jonah 4:11). Jonah never answers. The question is left with readers who knew what Assyria had done.

Israel's Bible did not ask them to pretend Assyria was innocent. The book of Nahum is one long cry against "the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder" (Nahum 3:1), and it also ends with a question: "For upon whom has not come your unceasing evil?" (Nahum 3:19). The canon kept both. It let the people who had suffered under Nineveh say so in God's presence. It also asked whether they could bear a God who had mercy on Nineveh anyway. The first readers of Jonah weren't being invited to admire God's kindness to someone else. They were being asked whether they wanted it for their enemies.

In each of these passages the first audience sat in the uncomfortable seat: the hometown congregation, the lawyer, the prophet who loved his country. We usually hear the stories from somewhere safer. We take the Samaritan's side and God's side, and we wonder how the people of Nazareth could have been so blind. Distance makes that easy. Once we know what the first hearers knew, it gets harder, because we start to recognize ourselves in them.

## Verses Taken Out of Their Chapters

Every church has a short list of verses its people know by heart, and passages it rarely sits through. Some of that can't be helped, since nobody memorizes the genealogies. But the short list isn't random. The verses we carry around tend to ask least of us, and a verse lifted out of its chapter will carry almost any meaning we need.

Many American Christians know 2 Chronicles 7:14 as a promise about their country: "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land." The words were first spoken to Solomon after he finished the temple (2 Chronicles 7:11–12). "My people" meant Israel. "Their land" was the land God had given them, and the same speech warned that if they served other gods, "I will pluck you up from my land that I have given you" (2 Chronicles 7:20). The book ends with the Persian king Cyrus sending the exiles home (2 Chronicles 36:22–23). Its first readers lived after the return from Babylon. They knew the warning had come true.

There's real truth in the American reading. God does hear people who humble themselves, and Christians who pray for their country are obeying Scripture. But read with its first audience, the verse puts the weight somewhere unexpected. The ones told to turn from their wicked ways are God's own people, not the nation around them. It doesn't promise that the country will be healed if the church prays about other people's sins. If it speaks to American Christians at all, it calls the church to repent of its own. Reading America into Israel's place is an old habit in this country, and it sits close to the heart of [Christian nationalism](/writing/what-christian-nationalism-is-and-is-not). This verse, read in its chapter, cuts against it.

Other readers have their own lists. "Judge not, that you be not judged" (Matthew 7:1) is quoted by plenty of people who otherwise never quote the Bible, and church people reach for it too, usually against whoever is correcting them. Jesus keeps going, though: "You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye" (Matthew 7:5). He expects his hearers to help with the speck. A few lines later he warns them about false prophets and says, "You will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16). What he forbids is the judgment of a hypocrite who never looks at his own eye.

The pattern runs in every direction. Conservative churches know Romans 13:1, "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities," better than they know what Paul says a few lines later: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (Romans 13:8). Few have memorized Leviticus 19:34: "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt."

Progressive churches quote Amos, "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24), and the first two thirds of Micah 6:8. They pass more quickly over the end of Micah's sentence, "to walk humbly with your God," and over the New Testament's teaching on sexual holiness. On both sides, the verses that get memorized are the ones that support what the church already believes. I've written elsewhere about how [the church you belong to translates the Bible for you](/writing/how-the-bible-gets-translated-by) before you open it.

Preachers build those lists one sermon at a time, and I have built one too. It is easier to preach the verse a congregation already loves than the chapter around it. Paul's farewell to the elders of Ephesus sets the standard. He called them to Miletus, knowing he wouldn't see them again, and told them, "I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God" (Acts 20:17, 25, 27). They were the first to hear that sentence, and they wept as they walked him to the ship (Acts 20:37–38). Paul measured his faithfulness by what he hadn't left out.

## Can Ordinary Christians Still Read the Bible?

All of this raises a real worry. If reading the Bible well means knowing about Samaritan temples and Babylonian deportations and ancient literacy, it sounds as if the Bible belongs to scholars again. The Reformers fought to put Scripture into ordinary hands in ordinary languages. Did they fight for nothing, if a farmer with an English Bible still can't understand it without a library?

The worry is right about the most important thing. The heart of Scripture is plain. English Baptists said so in the confession they published in 1677, and again in 1689 with the names of their churches attached, keeping the Westminster Confession's words almost exactly: the things "necessary to be known, believed, and observed for Salvation" are so clearly set out "that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of ordinary means, may attain to a sufficient understanding of them." A child can understand that God made us, that we have turned from him, and that Christ died and rose for sinners.

But the same paragraph begins, "All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all." The confession holds both convictions at once. It names "ordinary means" as the way the unlearned come to understand, and those means turn out to be mostly the Bible itself. Nearly everything this essay has said about the first audience comes from the pages around the verses we know. The exiles' situation is in Jeremiah 28 and 29. The village that refused Jesus is in Luke 9. Jonah's history is in 2 Kings. Josephus filled in the story of the Samaritan temple, but John's Gospel had already said what mattered.

A reader with a whole Bible, the patience to read whole books, and a church that teaches will hear most of what the first audience heard. Notes in a [study Bible](/study/bible) can supply some of the rest, as long as they serve the text and don't replace it. The Levites at the Water Gate show what teachers are for. They didn't stand between the people and the book. They stood among the people, and the people went home to feast and send portions "because they had understood the words that were declared to them" (Nehemiah 8:12).

## Reading With the First Hearers

What would it look like to read this way? Mostly it means slowing down. Read whole books, aloud when you can and with other people, the way they were first received. Before you decide what a passage says to you, ask who first heard it, what they feared, and what it asked of them. When a verse you love turns up, read the chapter around it. And pay attention when you find yourself sitting comfortably in the hero's seat. The first hearers seldom did.

The Bible itself shows the way. On the day of the resurrection, two disciples walked toward Emmaus, and a stranger joined them. "We had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel," they told him (Luke 24:21). They knew their Scriptures, and they had read them for the redemption they wanted. The risen Jesus didn't hand them a better verse. "Beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27). Afterward they asked each other, "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?" (Luke 24:32).

We come to the Bible late. Exiles in Babylon, a crowd in a square in Jerusalem, a synagogue in Nazareth and a handful of small churches around the Mediterranean heard it before we did. Most of them heard something harder than we usually let ourselves hear.

That should make us slower readers, and humbler ones.

It should also give us hope. The exiles who first heard Jeremiah's letter waited a lifetime, and many died in Babylon before anyone went home. The future and the hope God promised them were larger than the road back to Jerusalem.

They find their Yes in Christ, who opened the Scriptures on the road to Emmaus and still opens them to anyone willing to hear them whole.
