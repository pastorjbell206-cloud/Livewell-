# What the First Readers of the Bible Actually Heard

*Every book of the Bible was first heard by particular people in a particular place, and learning what they heard keeps us from making Scripture say what we already wanted.*

Soon after Jerusalem's rebuilt walls were finished, in the fifth century before Christ, "all the people gathered as one man into the square before the Water Gate" and asked Ezra the scribe to bring out the Book of the Law of Moses (Nehemiah 8:1). It was the first day of the seventh month. Ezra stood on a wooden platform built for the occasion and read from early morning until midday, to the men and the women and all who could understand, and when he opened the book the people stood (Nehemiah 8:2–5). Levites helped them follow it. "They read from the book, from the Law of God, clearly, and they gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). When the people understood it, they wept.

One of the best-loved lines in the Bible comes out of that morning. Christians who have never read Nehemiah know it by heart: "the joy of the LORD is your strength." It was first spoken by Nehemiah, Ezra and the Levites to a crowd in tears. "This day is holy to the LORD your God; do not mourn or weep," they said, and then, "Go your way. Eat the fat and drink sweet wine and send portions to anyone who has nothing ready, for this day is holy to our Lord. And do not be grieved, for the joy of the LORD is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:9–10). Its first hearers had just been undone by what they heard. They were told to stop weeping, to keep the day as a feast, and to see that neighbors with nothing prepared had something to eat.

We usually hear the line alone, as encouragement for a hard week, without the square or the tears or the portions sent to the poor. It still comforts. But it has lost the thing that made it strong for the people who first heard it, which was a word from God that had brought them low before it lifted them up. That loss is the subject of this essay. Every book of the Bible was first heard by real people in a real place, and a verse heard without them tends to drift toward whatever we already wanted it to mean.

## Who the Bible Was First Written To

John Walton, an Old Testament scholar, has said one thing about Scripture so often that when a book of essays was published in his honor in 2020, it took the saying as its title. The Bible was written for us, he says, but it was not written to us. Every book had a first audience with its own language, its own enemies, its own memories and fears, and its writers assumed things those listeners already knew. When we skip over that audience, we don't read the text neutrally. We quietly take their seats and assume our questions were theirs.

We also forget how they received it. For most of history God's people didn't read the Bible so much as hear it. At the council in Jerusalem, James reminded the church that Moses "is read every Sabbath in the synagogues" (Acts 15:21). Paul expected his letters to be heard the same way. "I put you under oath before the Lord to have this letter read to all the brothers," he told the Thessalonians (1 Thessalonians 5:27), and he told the Colossians to trade letters with the church at Laodicea (Colossians 4:16). The last book of the Bible opens with a blessing on "the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy" and on "those who hear" (Revelation 1:3).

Historians argue about how many people in the Roman world could read. William Harris, whose 1989 study set the terms of that argument, put the figure at not much more than one in ten. Some scholars think he set it too low. Either way, most people in those first congregations knew Paul's letters by ear.

Hearing changes what a text is. A letter read aloud to a room is heard whole, from greeting to farewell, and the "you" lands on everyone at once. When Paul asks, "Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16), every "you" is plural. He means the church in Corinth, a congregation splitting into camps around its favorite teachers, and he warns that God will destroy whoever destroys that temple (1 Corinthians 3:4, 17). Read alone, the verse easily becomes advice about taking care of yourself.

The numbers in our Bibles make that easier still. The chapter divisions most Bibles use go back to Stephen Langton, an English churchman who became archbishop of Canterbury, in the early thirteenth century. The New Testament's verse numbers come from a Greek New Testament that the French printer Robert Estienne published in 1551. The Geneva Bible of 1560 was the first English Bible to print chapters and verses together. Those numbers are useful, since they let a whole congregation find the same line. But they make every sentence look like a separate saying, ready to be lifted out and carried off. Paul wrote letters. He never wrote a verse.

## When the Text Confronted Its First Hearers

Once you ask who first heard a passage, you notice how often it was aimed at them. Luke tells of Jesus in the synagogue at Nazareth, "where he had been brought up," reading from the scroll of Isaiah about good news to the poor and liberty to the captives (Luke 4:16–19). He sat down and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). The room loved it. "All spoke well of him and marveled at the gracious words that were coming from his mouth" (Luke 4:22).

Then he reminded them that in Elijah's day God sent the prophet to a widow in Sidon and to none of the widows in Israel, and that in Elisha's day the only leper cleansed was "Naaman the Syrian" (Luke 4:25–27). "When they heard these things, all in the synagogue were filled with wrath" (Luke 4:28). They drove him to the brow of the hill their town was built on, meaning to throw him off (Luke 4:29). The congregation welcomed the good news until it reached a widow in Sidon and a Syrian general.

Luke sets the parable of the Good Samaritan in the same kind of room. A chapter earlier, a Samaritan village refuses to receive Jesus because he is headed for Jerusalem, and James and John ask, "Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?" (Luke 9:52–54). Jesus rebukes them. Shortly afterward in Luke's account, a lawyer stands up to test him.

The hostility was old. Samaritans worshiped on Mount Gerizim, which is why the woman at the well told Jesus, "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship" (John 4:20). "For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans," John explains (John 4:9). Late in the second century before Christ, the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus captured Shechem and destroyed the Samaritan temple on Gerizim. Within Jesus' own lifetime, while Coponius governed Judea for Rome, Josephus reports that some Samaritans slipped into the Jerusalem temple at Passover and threw human remains about its colonnades. After that, Samaritans were kept out of the temple.

So when the lawyer, "desiring to justify himself," asked, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29), Jesus told him about a man left half dead on the road to Jericho. A priest passed by on the other side, and so did a Levite. A Samaritan stopped, bound up his wounds, took him to an inn and paid for his care (Luke 10:30–35). Then Jesus turned the question around. He asked which of the three "proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers" (Luke 10:36). The lawyer answered, "The one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:37). Readers have long noticed that he answered without saying the word Samaritan.

We name hospitals after the Good Samaritan, and American states have Good Samaritan laws to protect people who stop to help. The phrase now means a kind stranger. The lawyer heard a hero drawn from a people his own people despised and blamed for defiling the temple, and Luke's readers had just watched a Samaritan village turn Jesus away. Jesus made that man the one who kept the commandment the lawyer had recited so well. Then he said, "You go, and do likewise" (Luke 10:37). The lawyer was told to go and do what a Samaritan had done.

The prophet Amos did the same thing to a whole nation. He spoke to the northern kingdom of Israel "in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel" (Amos 1:1), in the eighth century before Christ, when Israel's border had been pushed back out to its old limits (2 Kings 14:25) and the wealthy lay on "beds of ivory" (Amos 6:4). His book opens with judgments on Israel's neighbors, one after another. Damascus had "threshed Gilead with threshing sledges of iron" (Amos 1:3). The Ammonites had "ripped open pregnant women in Gilead, that they might enlarge their border" (Amos 1:13). Gaza, Tyre, Edom and Moab each take their turn, and then Judah, Israel's rival to the south (Amos 2:4). An Israelite listening would have agreed with every word.

Then the eighth judgment fell on the listeners. "For three transgressions of Israel, and for four, I will not revoke the punishment, because they sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6). The charges that follow aren't war crimes. They are the sins of a comfortable people at home and at worship: trampling the poor, a man and his father going in to the same girl so that God's holy name is profaned, lying down beside every altar "on garments taken in pledge" (Amos 2:7–8). Our churches tend to split that list, each side preaching the half that troubles the other. Amos preached all of it to the same people. Then he told them why they would be judged more strictly than their neighbors: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2). [The Bible expects God's own people to go wrong](/writing/the-bible-assumes-you-will-be-wrong), and it sends prophets like Amos to say so to their faces.

In each of these passages the first audience sat in the uncomfortable seat: the hometown congregation, the lawyer, the towns of Israel in Jeroboam's good years. We usually hear the stories from somewhere safer. We take the Samaritan's side and the prophet's side, and we wonder how the people of Nazareth could have been so blind. Distance makes that easy. Once we know what the first hearers knew, it gets harder, because we start to recognize ourselves in them.

## Verses Taken Out of Their Chapters

Every church has a short list of verses its people know by heart, and passages it rarely sits through. Some of that can't be helped, since nobody memorizes the genealogies. But the short list isn't random. The verses we carry around tend to ask least of us, and a verse lifted out of its chapter will carry almost any meaning we need.

"I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13) is the verse many Christians reach for before a big game or a hard exam. Paul wrote it in prison, to a church that knew "my imprisonment is for Christ" (Philippians 1:13). The Philippians had sent him a gift by their messenger Epaphroditus (Philippians 4:18), and the famous verse comes in his thank-you note. "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content," he tells them. "I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need" (Philippians 4:11–12). The "all things" include going hungry. His first hearers knew he might not leave that prison alive, since he had told them so (Philippians 1:20–21). They heard a friend in chains saying that Christ was enough for whatever came.

Other readers have their own lists. "Judge not, that you be not judged" (Matthew 7:1) is quoted by plenty of people who otherwise never quote the Bible, and church people reach for it too, usually against whoever is correcting them. Jesus keeps going, though: "You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye" (Matthew 7:5). He expects his hearers to help with the speck. A few lines later he warns them about false prophets and says, "You will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16). What he forbids is the judgment of a hypocrite who never looks at his own eye.

The same thing happens along political lines. Conservative churches know Romans 13:1, "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities," better than they know what Paul says a few lines later: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (Romans 13:8). Few have memorized Leviticus 19:34: "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt."

Progressive churches quote Amos, "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24), and the first two thirds of Micah 6:8. They pass more quickly over the end of Micah's sentence, "to walk humbly with your God," and over the New Testament's teaching on sexual holiness. On both sides, the verses that get memorized are the ones that support what the church already believes. I've written elsewhere about how [the church you belong to translates the Bible for you](/writing/how-the-bible-gets-translated-by) before you open it.

Preachers build those lists one sermon at a time, and I have built one too. It is easier to preach the verse a congregation already loves than the chapter around it. The preachers who defended American slavery knew their Bibles well, and they [explained away the passages that condemned them](/writing/why-didnt-the-bible-ban-slavery). Paul's farewell to the elders of Ephesus sets the standard against that. He called them to Miletus, knowing he wouldn't see them again, and told them, "I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God" (Acts 20:17, 25, 27). They were the first to hear that sentence, and they wept as they walked him to the ship (Acts 20:37–38). Paul measured his faithfulness by what he hadn't left out.

## Can Ordinary Christians Still Read the Bible?

All of this raises an obvious worry. If reading the Bible well means knowing about Samaritan temples and the reign of Jeroboam and ancient literacy, it sounds as if the Bible belongs to scholars again. The Reformers fought to put Scripture into ordinary hands in ordinary languages. Did they fight for nothing, if a farmer with an English Bible still can't understand it without a library?

The worry is right about the most important thing. The heart of Scripture is plain. English Baptists said so in the confession they published in 1677, and again in 1689 with the names of their churches attached, keeping the Westminster Confession's words almost exactly: the things "necessary to be known, believed, and observed for Salvation" are so clearly set out "that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of ordinary means, may attain to a sufficient understanding of them." A child can understand that God made us, that we have turned from him, and that Christ died and rose for sinners.

But the same paragraph begins, "All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all." The confession holds both convictions at once. It names "ordinary means" as the way the unlearned come to understand, and those means turn out to be mostly the Bible itself. Nearly everything this essay has said about the first audience comes from the pages around the verses we know. The tears before "the joy of the LORD" are in the same chapter. The village that refused Jesus is in Luke 9. Amos names his king in his first verse. Paul's chains are in the first chapter of Philippians. Josephus filled in the story of the Samaritan temple, but John's Gospel had already said what mattered.

A reader with a whole Bible, the patience to read whole books, and a church that teaches will hear most of what the first audience heard. Notes in a [study Bible](/study/bible) can supply some of the rest, as long as they serve the text and don't replace it. The Levites at the Water Gate show what teachers are for. They didn't stand between the people and the book. They stood among the people, and the people went home to feast and send portions "because they had understood the words that were declared to them" (Nehemiah 8:12).

## Reading With the First Hearers

What would it look like to read this way? Mostly it means slowing down. Read whole books, aloud when you can and with other people, the way they were first received. Before you decide what a passage says to you, ask who first heard it, what they feared, and what it asked of them. When a verse you love turns up, read the chapter around it. And pay attention when you find yourself sitting comfortably in the hero's seat. The first hearers seldom did.

The letter to the Hebrews opens by naming the distance between us and them. "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1–2). The fathers were the first hearers, and God spoke to them in their own places and languages and troubles. Hebrews was written to a church in danger of drifting, and it drew the conclusion for them plainly: "Therefore we must pay much closer attention to what we have heard, lest we drift away from it" (Hebrews 2:1).

We come to the Bible late. A crowd in a square in Jerusalem, the towns of Israel in Jeroboam's day, a synagogue in Nazareth and a handful of house churches around the Mediterranean heard it before we did. Most of them heard something harder than we usually let ourselves hear.

That should make us slower readers, and humbler ones.

It should also give us hope. The Son through whom God has spoken is the same Jesus who told the synagogue at Nazareth that the Scripture had been fulfilled in their hearing. It is fulfilled in ours as well.

The people at the Water Gate wept before they feasted. If we listen to the whole of what God has said, we may weep over the parts we have been skipping. The joy of the LORD is strength for people who have been brought that low.
