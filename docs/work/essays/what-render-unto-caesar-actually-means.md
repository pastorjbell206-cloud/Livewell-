# What Did Jesus Mean by "Render Unto Caesar"?

*Jesus said "Render to Caesar" in the temple during the last week of his life, when men who wanted him dead asked him whether to pay a Roman tax.*

People who never otherwise quote Jesus know this line. "Render unto Caesar" comes up whenever a church says something about an election, usually from someone who wishes it hadn't. It comes up when a Christian grumbles about taxes, from a friend who thinks he should pay them and keep quiet. Most of us know it in the King James wording: "Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's" (Matthew 22:21, KJV). And most of us were taught, without anyone quite saying so, that it divides life into two territories. Government has one and God has the other, and a sensible person keeps them apart.

That reading isn't foolish, and part of it is true. But it's a reading of one sentence lifted out of a scene, and the scene was a trap. The question Jesus answered came from men who had already decided he had to die. It was about a tax that had already gotten people killed, and he was asked it in a city where Roman soldiers kept the peace. Put back where it was spoken, the sentence asks a good deal more of the people who quote it.

## The Question in the Temple

Matthew, Mark and Luke all tell the story, and Mark tells it in five verses (Mark 12:13–17). It happens in Jerusalem a few days before the crucifixion. Jesus has ridden into the city to a crowd's welcome, overturned the tables of the money-changers in the temple, and told a parable about tenants who kill the owner's son. The chief priests, scribes and elders "perceived that he had told the parable against them" (Mark 12:12). They wanted him arrested, but they were afraid of the crowd. So they tried something else. "And they sent to him some of the Pharisees and some of the Herodians, to trap him in his talk" (Mark 12:13).

The two groups had little in common. The Pharisees were devoted to keeping the law of Moses in every corner of life, and ordinary people respected them. The Herodians, as far as we can tell, were partisans of the family of Herod, whose hold on power had always depended on Rome. Mark has shown them together once before, back in Galilee, where the Pharisees "held counsel with the Herodians against him, how to destroy him" (Mark 3:6). Whatever divided them, they had found something to agree on.

They began with flattery, and every word of it was true. "Teacher, we know that you are true and do not care about anyone's opinion. For you are not swayed by appearances, but truly teach the way of God." Then came the question. "Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not? Should we pay them, or should we not?" (Mark 12:14). Luke doesn't leave the motive to guesswork. They sent spies, he says, "who pretended to be sincere, that they might catch him in something he said, so as to deliver him up to the authority and jurisdiction of the governor" (Luke 20:20). If Jesus said no, they could take him to Pilate as a rebel. If he said yes, he'd be siding with the occupier in front of the crowd that had cheered him into the city that week.

## The Roman Tax and Judas the Galilean

This wasn't a toll or a sales tax. Mark's word for it is a Latin one, *census*, spelled out in Greek letters, and it meant the tribute the people of Judea paid straight to Rome. It was about a generation old. In AD 6 Augustus removed Herod's son Archelaus and put Judea under a Roman governor, and a Roman senator named Quirinius came to take an account of everyone's property so it could be taxed. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing near the end of the first century, says the high priest talked most people into accepting the assessment. Not everyone accepted it. A man named Judas, joined by a Pharisee named Sadduc, called the nation to revolt and said "this taxation was no better than an introduction to slavery."

Josephus says Judas started a fourth school of Jewish thought, alongside the Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes, whose followers held "that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord" and would not call any man lord even if it cost them their lives. The book of Acts remembers him too. Years later, when the apostles were on trial in Jerusalem, the teacher Gamaliel warned the council that "Judas the Galilean rose up in the days of the census and drew away some of the people after him. He too perished, and all who followed him were scattered" (Acts 5:37).

Josephus said Judas and Sadduc "laid the foundations of our future miseries," the long slide that ended with the temple burned in AD 70. He himself had fought against Rome in that war and then gone over to the Romans, so he had his own reasons to blame the rebels. But the question Judas asked didn't go away, and serious, devout people had answered it no.

Pilate gave them reasons to keep asking. Josephus tells how Pilate had his troops carry standards bearing images of Caesar into Jerusalem at night, something earlier governors had avoided because, in Josephus's words, "our law forbids us the very making of images." Crowds went down to Caesarea and pleaded with him for days. When he surrounded them with soldiers and threatened to kill them, they threw themselves on the ground and bared their necks, and Pilate had the images taken back. Caesar's image in the holy city was something people would die over. So was Caesar's tax. And Pilate was the governor the spies meant to hand Jesus to.

## The Coin in Their Hands

Jesus didn't answer right away. "Why put me to the test? Bring me a denarius and let me look at it" (Mark 12:15). A denarius was a small silver coin, a day's pay for the laborers in one of his parables (Matthew 20:2). Someone produced one. We can't know which denarius it was, but the one Tiberius struck through most of his reign showed the emperor's head crowned with laurel and the Latin words TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS: Tiberius Caesar Augustus, son of the divine Augustus. On the back, around a seated female figure, ran another of his titles, PONTIF MAXIM: chief priest of Rome's religion. "Whose likeness and inscription is this?" Jesus asked. They said to him, "Caesar's" (Mark 12:16).

The men asking whether it was lawful to pay Caesar could produce Caesar's money in the temple courts, with his face on it and the claim that he was the son of a god. Whatever they thought about the tribute, they were already using his coinage and living inside the order his soldiers kept. So was everyone listening. The question pretended there was a clean place to stand outside Caesar's world. There wasn't one.

Then the answer: "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Mark 12:17). Jesus changed the verb. They had asked whether to pay, using the ordinary Greek word for giving. He answered with *apodote*, the word for paying back what is owed, handing over what already belongs to someone else. Paul uses the same verb when he tells the Christians in Rome, "Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed" (Romans 13:7). The coin carried Caesar's name and face, so Jesus told them to give it back to him.

"And they marveled at him," Mark says. Luke adds that "they were not able in the presence of the people to catch him in what he said, but marveling at his answer they became silent" (Luke 20:26).

## What Caesar Is Owed

The first thing the answer does is tell people to pay the tax. Some readers would rather it didn't. One line of interpretation hears a coded refusal: if everything belongs to God, nothing is left over for Caesar, so the crowd would have heard Jesus siding with Judas the Galilean while the spies heard nothing they could use. It's a clever reading, and it takes seriously how much the answer keeps back from Caesar. But Luke rules it out. When the temple leaders bring Jesus to Pilate, they tell him, "We found this man misleading our nation and forbidding us to give tribute to Caesar, and saying that he himself is Christ, a king" (Luke 23:2). Luke's readers have just read chapter 20. They know the charge is false, because Jesus had told the crowd, in public, to pay.

The first generations of Christians took him that way. Justin Martyr wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius in the 150s and told him that "everywhere we, more readily than all men, endeavour to pay to those appointed by you the taxes both ordinary and extraordinary, as we have been taught by Him." Then he retold the story of the coin and drew his conclusion: "Whence to God alone we render worship, but in other things we gladly serve you, acknowledging you as kings and rulers of men, and praying that with your kingly power you be found to possess also sound judgment" (First Apology 17).

This sits hard on anyone who wants a more defiant Jesus. The tribute he told them to pay went to the empire that had occupied their land, stationed its soldiers in their holy city, and would crucify him before the week was out. If he could tell them to hand Tiberius his coin, a Christian in a free country can pay taxes to a government he didn't vote for and wouldn't have chosen, without wondering whether he has compromised his faith. Taxes paid honestly, laws kept and officials given their due are part of obeying Christ, not exceptions to it.

## What Belongs to God

The weight of the sentence falls on its second half. Jesus asked whose "likeness" was on the coin, and the Greek word is *eikōn*, image. Jews who read their Scriptures in Greek knew that word from the first page of the Bible, where God says, "Let us make man in our image," and the narrator adds, "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:26–27). The Greek translation uses *eikōn* there. The coin bears Caesar's image, so it goes back to Caesar. What bears God's image?

Jesus doesn't say, and it's possible to hang too much on one word. But the early church heard the question. Around the year 200, Tertullian of Carthage wrote a treatise against Christians who were drifting into idolatry, and in it he explained Jesus' answer as "the image of Caesar, which is on the coin, to Caesar, and the image of God, which is on man, to God; so as to render to Caesar indeed money, to God yourself." Then he asked the question that follows from it: "Otherwise, what will be God's, if all things are Caesar's?" (On Idolatry 15).

Even if you set the image aside, the two halves of the sentence aren't the same size. A few verses later in the same chapter, a scribe asks Jesus which commandment matters most, and Jesus answers with the Shema, Israel's great confession: "And you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength" (Mark 12:30). Caesar is owed a coin with his name on it, and Paul would later add revenue, respect and honor. God is owed all of it. "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof, the world and those who dwell therein" (Psalm 24:1), and those who dwell therein include Tiberius. Caesar's things sit inside God's things, not in a second territory beside them.

The coin itself made claims. It called Tiberius the son of a god and Rome's chief priest. Jesus sent the silver back to Caesar and gave him nothing else the coin claimed for him.

## Where the Church Drew the Line

For the Christians who came after, the line was drawn in courtrooms. On July 17, 180, a group of Christians from Scili, a small town in Roman North Africa, stood before the proconsul Saturninus at Carthage. He told them that Romans were religious too: "our religion is simple; and we swear by the genius of our lord the Emperor, and pray for his welfare, which also ye, too, ought to do." Their spokesman, Speratus, answered, "I have committed no theft; but if I have bought anything I pay the tax; because I know my Lord, the King of kings and Emperor of all nations." A woman named Donata said, "Honor to Cæsar as Cæsar, but fear to God." They were sentenced to the sword. When the sentence was read, Speratus said, "We give thanks to God."

They paid the tax and refused the oath. The emperor could have their coins and their good conduct, but not an oath that treated him as more than a man. That is where [the church before Constantine](/writing/the-early-church-under-rome) drew the line again and again: at worship.

After Constantine the problem changed shape. The emperors were Christians now, and Christian emperors wanted things from the church. In 385 and 386 the imperial court at Milan, acting for the young emperor Valentinian II, demanded a church building for the Arian party's worship. Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, refused to hand it over. He wrote to his sister that when the court insisted "everything is lawful for the Emperor, that all things are his," he quoted the saying about the coin and added, "The palaces belong to the Emperor, the churches to the Bishop." With soldiers around the church, he told his congregation, "We pay to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's. Tribute is due to Caesar, we do not deny it. The Church belongs to God, therefore it ought not to be assigned to Caesar." The emperor, he said, "is within the Church, not above it." The court backed down.

Two years later, in 388, Christians at Callinicum, a town on the Euphrates, burned a synagogue, and the report to the emperor said their bishop had put them up to it. Theodosius ordered the bishop to rebuild it at his own expense. Ambrose wrote to the emperor in protest, and when that didn't settle the matter he confronted Theodosius in church and would not go to the altar until the emperor promised to change the order. Theodosius gave way. The bishop who had told one court that God's house was not Caesar's used his standing with another emperor to spare Christians from repairing what they had destroyed.

We see the line between Caesar's things and God's things clearly when Caesar reaches across it, and lose sight of it when [the church has its own hand on Caesar's power](/writing/when-the-church-married-empire).

## Is This the Separation of Church and State?

Americans usually hear the saying as an early version of the separation of church and state, and there's a family resemblance. The saying does put some things beyond Caesar's reach. Ambrose reached for it when an emperor's court demanded one of his churches, and Christians who have argued since then that no government may compel belief have reached for it too. They were right to.

But the passage is less about institutions than about what is owed, and it doesn't say that faith is private and government is public. The coin changed hands in the temple courts, in front of a crowd. And every person standing in a courtroom, a hospital, a legislature or a border station bears the image of God. So what a government does to people is never outside God's claim, and no Christian can use this sentence to excuse himself from caring what happens to them. Nor does it license the opposite move, the one Ambrose made at Callinicum, where the church borrows Caesar's power to get its way. Giving Caesar his coin was never a request for his sword.

## When a Party Asks for What Belongs to God

No American official asks a Christian to swear by the emperor's genius. The government asks for taxes and obedience to the law, and those we owe. The pull to give away the things that are God's mostly comes from somewhere else now. It's harder to notice because nobody in uniform is doing the asking.

The Gospels describe that pull before they describe the coin. At the start of his ministry, Matthew says, "the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. And he said to him, 'All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me'" (Matthew 4:8–9). The devil didn't ask him to do anything cruel with the kingdoms. He offered them whole, for the price of worship. Jesus answered, "Be gone, Satan! For it is written, 'You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve'" (Matthew 4:10). Later, when a crowd he had fed was "about to come and take him by force to make him king," he "withdrew again to the mountain by himself" (John 6:15). He wouldn't take the kingdoms on those terms, from the devil or from a crowd.

Dostoevsky imagined a church that took the offer. In *The Brothers Karamazov*, Ivan tells his brother a story about Christ returning to Seville in the worst days of the Inquisition and being arrested by the Grand Inquisitor, an old cardinal who explains to his silent prisoner that the church has corrected his work. "We have taken the sword of Cæsar," the cardinal says, "and in taking it, of course, have rejected Thee and followed *him*." Dostoevsky aimed the story at Rome. It reads no more comfortably in any church that has decided it could do more good with the kingdoms than without them.

In America the offer seldom comes as a state church. It comes through a political party, and a party asks for loyalty, for our fear of what happens if we lose and our hope of what happens if we win. Those belong to God. A party also supplies a way of reading the news that turns neighbors into enemies. The political scientist Michele Margolis has found that for many Americans the influence runs the opposite way from what we assume: party loyalty, settled early in life, helps decide whether people go back to church as adults and how they describe their faith once they do. When that happens, a congregation can become the religious wing of a political identity its members already had, without anyone intending it.

On the right, that looks like a church where the party's agenda and the gospel's agenda line up item for item, and criticizing the candidate sounds like unbelief. On the left, it looks like a church whose moral vocabulary arrives each season from the movement, where Scripture carries authority mainly when it agrees, and where dissent from the platform is treated as a failure of love. Each side can describe the other's blind spot, [conservative](/writing/the-conservative-blind-spot) or [progressive](/writing/the-progressive-blind-spot), better than its own. The test is the same for both. When your side does what you condemned in the other side, do you say so? And does saying so cost you anything?

Amos met an older form of this at Bethel, the royal shrine of the northern kingdom. The priest there, Amaziah, told him to go home to Judah and prophesy there, "but never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom" (Amos 7:13). Amaziah wasn't an unbeliever. He kept a house of worship whose real business had become blessing the throne. A congregation can drift into that arrangement without ever deciding to, and it is always easier to spot in someone else's church. I notice it faster in churches across the political aisle than in the church I serve, which usually tells me where I ought to look.

## The Inscription Over the Cross

The trap in the temple failed, and the temple leaders found another way. The charge they took to Pilate was the one Jesus had already answered. In John's account, when Pilate asked, "Shall I crucify your King?" it was the chief priests, the same temple leadership whose circle in Mark had sent the questioners with the coin, who answered, "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15). The men who had tried to make Jesus choose between God and Caesar ended by choosing Caesar out loud.

It's easy to read that line and judge them. Most of us say it more quietly, and we seldom hear ourselves.

Then Rome added an inscription of its own. Mark uses the Greek word for "inscription" only twice: once for the writing on Caesar's coin, and once for the charge fastened above Jesus on the cross. "And the inscription of the charge against him read, 'The King of the Jews'" (Mark 15:26). Rome meant it as a sneer. The church has read it ever since as the plain truth.

The man under that inscription is the one Paul calls "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15). He was the one person who ever gave God everything that was God's. In Gethsemane he prayed, "Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36), and on the cross he gave his life into his Father's hands. God raised him on the third day. And Paul says that those who belong to him "are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Corinthians 3:18). The image we have defaced in ourselves, and in the neighbors we have treated as enemies, is being restored by the One whose image it always was.

So pay the tax. Give the officials their due, including the ones you voted against.

Then ask what else you've been handing over: your fear, your hope, your loyalty, your sense of who your people are.

Whose image is on you?

Give God the things that are God's, which means all of you. Then look at the neighbor beside you, who bears his likeness too, and treat him as God's.

The King of the Jews has already given himself for you both.
