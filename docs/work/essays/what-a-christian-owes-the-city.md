# What Does It Mean to Seek the Welfare of the City?

*Jeremiah's command to seek the welfare of Babylon is the plainest answer Scripture gives to what Christians owe the places where they live, and the church has had to learn it again in every age.*

## A Bishop in a Plague Year

Around the year 252 a plague reached Carthage, the great Roman city on the coast of North Africa. It had been moving through the empire for a few years already. Two years earlier, under the emperor Decius, every inhabitant of the empire had been ordered to sacrifice to the gods, and in Carthage many Christians had given way while others refused. Now the city had a new grievance against them. A man named Demetrianus, who had come more than once to argue with the bishop, put into words what many of his neighbors were saying. The wars, the famine, the drought and now the plague were the Christians' fault. They would not worship the gods.

Cyprian, the bishop, answered him in writing. He didn't grovel, and he didn't flatter the city either. He told Demetrianus that the famine was being made worse by men who shut up their barns and waited for prices to rise, "as if drought made a greater famine than rapacity." In the plague, he said, the sick were being abandoned while "avarice and rapine are waiting open-mouthed for the dead." Then he described what the Christians were doing for the city that blamed them: "we entreat constantly and urgently, day and night, for your peace and salvation." Near the end he put it in one line. "We repay kindness for your hatred."

His deacon Pontius, who later wrote Cyprian's life, says the bishop's answer went beyond words. When the plague came, Cyprian gathered the church and told them there was nothing remarkable about caring for their own people. Christ had told his disciples to love their enemies and pray for those who persecuted them. Their care, in other words, could not stop at the church door. Pontius admired the man he wrote about, and we should read him as we would any admiring biographer. But Cyprian's own treatise went to a hostile reader in the same city, and it makes the same kind of claim.

Cyprian is one of the oldest Christian witnesses to a question many American Christians are asking again. What do we owe the place where we live, now that it no longer shares our faith, and in some quarters no longer likes us much? Some of us want to take the country back. Some want to pull our children out and build something sturdier on the edge of town. Others want the church to be useful and agreeable and to keep its doctrine to itself. Nearly all of us reach for the same verse to explain it.

## The Letter Jeremiah Sent

The verse is Jeremiah 29:7. After Nebuchadnezzar carried Judah's king, its officials and its craftsmen to Babylon in 597 BC, Jeremiah sent the exiles a letter from Jerusalem, and its center was this: "But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare." The letter also told them to build houses, plant gardens and marry off their children in Babylon, and it warned them against prophets who promised a quick return. [The setting of that letter and its seventy years](/writing/what-post-christian-means) deserve their own telling. Here I want to stay with the command itself.

Start with a word the exiles already knew. Israel's psalter taught the pilgrims going up to Jerusalem to "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem!" (Psalm 122:6). The Hebrew word for peace there is shalom, the same word the ESV translates "welfare" three times in Jeremiah's sentence. The prayer God's people had prayed for their own holy city, Jeremiah now told them to pray for Babylon. This was the city whose army had carried off the treasures of the temple and taken their king away as a prisoner. They were to ask God for its peace.

Then look at the reply the letter got. A prophet among the exiles named Shemaiah of Nehelam wrote back to the priests in Jerusalem. He reminded the priest Zephaniah that his office gave him charge over "every madman who prophesies," and asked why Jeremiah had not been rebuked. His evidence was the letter: "For he has sent to us in Babylon, saying, 'Your exile will be long; build houses and live in them, and plant gardens and eat their produce'" (Jeremiah 29:28). To a religious leader in Babylon, settling down and praying for the city sounded like madness, and probably like a betrayal of Zion. Zephaniah read the complaint to Jeremiah. The Lord's answer was judgment on Shemaiah, who had "made you trust in a lie" (29:31).

We tend to treat Jeremiah 29:7 as a gentle verse. Its first readers didn't.

## The Same Prophet Wrote Babylon's Doom

The prophet who told the exiles to seek Babylon's welfare also wrote two long chapters of judgment against Babylon, and the last scene in them is dated. In the fourth year of King Zedekiah, the same year Jeremiah confronted the false prophet Hananiah in the temple, Jeremiah gave a book to Seraiah, the quartermaster, who was traveling to Babylon with the king. In it he had written "all the disaster that should come upon Babylon" (Jeremiah 51:60). When Seraiah arrived he was to read it, tie a stone to it, throw it into the Euphrates, and say, "Thus shall Babylon sink, to rise no more" (51:64).

So in those same years Jeremiah told the exiles to pray for Babylon's peace, and he told Babylon it would fall. He saw no contradiction. The exiles were to work and pray for the good of the streets around them, for the bread on their neighbors' tables and the safety of their neighbors' children, knowing all the while that the empire itself stood under God's judgment and would not last. Seeking the welfare of a city was never the same as believing in it. It didn't require them to call Babylon's gods good or its conquests just. It also gave them no permission to sit in their houses hoping for the worst.

Cyprian gave Carthage that same double word. He named the city's greed and prayed for its peace in one treatise. Most of us manage only half of it. When the city is run by people we trust, we seek its welfare and forget that it answers to God. When it's run by people we don't trust, we remember the judgment. Somewhere along the way we stop praying for its peace.

## What Makes a City Well

What did Jeremiah mean by a city's welfare? He had already told the kings of Judah. At the king's house in Jerusalem he delivered the Lord's word: "Do justice and righteousness, and deliver from the hand of the oppressor him who has been robbed. And do no wrong or violence to the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow, nor shed innocent blood in this place" (Jeremiah 22:3). Then he turned on King Jehoiakim, who was building himself a palace without paying the men who built it, and compared him with his father Josiah: "Did not your father eat and drink and do justice and righteousness? Then it was well with him. He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (22:15–16).

In Jeremiah's vocabulary, then, a city is well when the weakest people in it are safe. Its welfare isn't measured first by its skyline or its property values. It's measured by whether the widow gets a fair hearing and the man who did the work gets paid. Knowing God, the prophet says, looks like that. So when the exiles were told to seek Babylon's shalom, they were not being told to make Babylon rich, or to make themselves comfortable there. They were told to want for Babylon what God had always wanted for Jerusalem.

Daniel shows what that looked like for an exile who had the king's ear. He was taken to Babylon as a youth, given a Babylonian name and trained in "the literature and language of the Chaldeans" (Daniel 1:4), and the book notes that he was still there "until the first year of King Cyrus" (1:21). He was good at the work. He also drew a line at the king's table, where he resolved "that he would not defile himself with the king's food" (1:8).

When Nebuchadnezzar told him a dream that meant disaster for the king, Daniel's first words were a wish for his good: "My lord, may the dream be for those who hate you and its interpretation for your enemies!" (4:19). Then he told him the truth anyway: "break off your sins by practicing righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed, that there may perhaps be a lengthening of your prosperity" (4:27).

Daniel asked nothing for himself. He told the king of Babylon to his face that his prosperity depended on mercy to the oppressed. Proverbs puts the conviction plainly: "By the blessing of the upright a city is exalted, but by the mouth of the wicked it is overthrown" (Proverbs 11:11).

## Augustine and the Peace of Babylon

In 410 the Goths under Alaric sacked Rome, and many in the empire blamed the disaster on Christianity. Rome had fallen, they said, because it had abandoned its gods. It was the charge Demetrianus had made in Carthage more than a century and a half before. Augustine, the bishop of Hippo in North Africa, began his answer in 413, and it grew into *The City of God*, which he finished in 426.

Most of that enormous book isn't about politics. But in its nineteenth book Augustine took up Jeremiah's letter directly. As long as the two cities are mixed together in this life, he wrote, the people of God share the earthly peace of the place where they live: "we also enjoy the peace of Babylon. For from Babylon the people of God is so freed that it meanwhile sojourns in its company" (*City of God* 19.26). He set Paul's charge to pray for kings beside Jeremiah's command to pray for Babylon, and he read the two together.

Earlier in the same book he spelled out what that sharing means. The heavenly city, living "like a captive and a stranger in the earthly city," makes no scruple about obeying the earthly city's laws, because both cities need the same things to get through this mortal life. It wants what its neighbors want for ordinary life. It keeps the customs and institutions it finds, so long as they don't hinder the worship of the one true God (19.17). Worship is the one thing the two cities cannot share. On that point, Augustine said, the heavenly city has had to dissent and to bear the anger and hatred of those who think otherwise.

That is the freedom the letter gives. A Christian can obey the city's laws, pay its taxes and work for its peace without needing the city to last forever. He can grieve its fall without losing his hope. The peace of Babylon is temporary. It is still worth having, and God's people are still told to pray for it.

## Withdrawal and Takeover

Since the late nineteenth century, American Protestants have dropped one half of that balance and then the other.

In 1886 a young Baptist minister named Walter Rauschenbusch became pastor of the Second German Baptist Church near Hell's Kitchen in New York. He spent eleven years among families worn down by unemployment, disease and poverty, and he came away convinced that the church had shrunk Jesus' message of the kingdom of God into a private hope. In 1907 he published *Christianity and the Social Crisis*, which became one of the founding books of the Social Gospel. He saw something many comfortable churches had missed. But in the hands of many who came after him, the kingdom looked more and more like social progress. The cross and the call to conversion moved to the edges.

Conservative Protestants saw that drift and pulled back, and in pulling back they left a good deal behind. Historians call what followed the Great Reversal, a phrase Timothy Smith used and the sociologist David Moberg made the title of a book. Evangelicals whose nineteenth-century forebears had poured revival energy into reform came to treat social concern itself as a mark of liberal theology, and many turned almost entirely to personal piety. In 1947 Carl F. H. Henry rebuked his own movement in *The Uneasy Conscience of Modern Fundamentalism* for letting go of that heritage and having little to say about war, racism and economic injustice. He had no use for the Social Gospel's liberalism either. He wanted a church that preached the cross and cared about the city.

From the late 1970s many white evangelicals came back into public life, mostly through elections. Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority in 1979, and for a great many churches the country became something to [win back](/writing/the-conservative-blind-spot). The left has its own version of the mistake, in which a platform becomes the measure of faithfulness and the church's public voice sounds like [one side's talking points](/writing/the-progressive-blind-spot).

In 2010 the sociologist James Davison Hunter took on the political theologies of the Christian Right, the Christian Left and the neo-Anabaptists in *To Change the World*. All too often, he argued, they made worse the very problems they meant to solve. In their place he proposed what he called faithful presence, a way of living the faith in work, institutions and every ordinary sphere of life. Asking whether it would change the world was the wrong question, he thought, because the question assumes history can be controlled. Jeremiah's exiles would have understood him.

How far the church should try to shape the culture around it is still a live question. Anabaptists, Lutherans and the Reformed have argued about it for five centuries, among people who love the same Lord. Jeremiah's letter doesn't settle it. It lays a floor beneath all of them. Whatever your tradition says about changing the world, you are still told to pray for the city and work for its good, and you are still forbidden its gods.

## Order or Justice in Birmingham

One of the clearest American arguments over what a city's welfare requires took place in Birmingham, Alabama, in the spring of 1963. It was an argument among clergymen. On April 12 eight white clergymen in the city, Protestant, Catholic and Jewish, issued a public statement, printed in the local paper, that is known as "A Call for Unity." They called the demonstrations Martin Luther King Jr. was leading "unwise and untimely" and urged Black citizens to withdraw their support and press their grievances in the courts instead. These men were not segregationist firebrands. They had earlier issued an appeal for law and order of their own, and they believed the welfare of Birmingham required calm.

King answered from his jail cell in a letter dated April 16. He called them "men of genuine good will" and treated their criticism as sincere. Then he told them he had nearly concluded that the greatest obstacle to Black freedom was not the open segregationist but the white moderate, "who is more devoted to 'order' than to justice; who prefers a negative peace which is the absence of tension to a positive peace which is the presence of justice." That is an argument about shalom. A city can be quiet and still not be well. Jeremiah had charged the prophets of his own day with exactly this: "They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14).

Birmingham also shows where the comfortable American reading of Jeremiah 29 breaks down. We like to picture ourselves as the exiles. But segregation in Birmingham was enforced in a city full of white churches, and the people in the exiles' position, seeking the good of a city that did not want them, were meeting in Black churches. For most of American history the majority church in this country has not been in the exiles' position. Often it has been closer to Babylon. A reading of the letter that never asks which way the power runs will get the letter wrong.

One of the eight signers was Earl Stallings, pastor of the First Baptist Church of Birmingham. He was the only one of them King named in the letter, and he named him to commend him. On the Sunday before, which was Easter, Stallings had welcomed Black worshipers into his church. He was harassed for it, and by 1965 pressure from his own congregation led him to resign. The man who signed a call for patience also opened his doors and paid for it. He wanted his city calm and his church faithful, and he signed a statement that asked his Black neighbors to wait. I pastor a Baptist church too, and I know how much a pastor wants his town calm and his people together. I'd like to think I would have seen what King saw. I know myself well enough to doubt it. Calm is easy to mistake for peace when someone else is paying for it.

## What a Christian Owes the City

So what does a Christian owe the city? Not everything. The city can't have our worship, and it can't be where our hope finally rests. But it is owed far more than most of us give it.

It is owed our prayers, to begin with, and not the vague kind. Jeremiah told the exiles to pray "to the LORD on its behalf," and prayer for a place is only as real as our knowledge of it. Do you know what your town's schools are struggling with, or which neighborhood has been asking for help and not getting it? Do you pray by name for the mayor, including the one you voted against? Cyprian prayed day and night for people who blamed him for a plague. Most of us have never been asked anything that hard.

Jeremiah's houses and gardens ask for something slower, which is staying. Faithfulness to a place looks like the family that keeps living in a neighborhood when it changes, and the church that keeps its doors open on a Tuesday night. Most of us have far more say over our county than over our country, and far less interest in it. The national argument is the one we can do least about, and it's built to take most of our attention.

Daniel adds the work itself. Most of the welfare of any city is carried by people who never think of their jobs as ministry, like the nurse on the night shift or the clerk who treats the poor woman at the counter the way she'd treat the mayor.

Then there are the people Jeremiah named. Every county has its resident aliens, its fatherless children and its widows, and its own quiet ways of letting them be cheated. A church that wants to know what seeking the city's welfare means could start by finding out who those people are in its own town, and what is being done to them, and by whom. That will lead some Christians onto school boards and city councils and into the courts. It should. The prophets were never shy about the conduct of kings.

Daniel also shows how to say the hard word: as a man who wants the king to prosper. Contempt is the easiest sin in American public life, and both parties reward it. A Christian who despises half his neighbors has already stopped seeking their welfare, whatever he says about the city.

The hardest part of the debt is good done to a city that doesn't want us. Jesus said, "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven. For he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matthew 5:44–45). That is the command Pontius says Cyprian set before his church in the plague. God sends rain on cities that never thank him for it. When the church does good to a city that resents it, it is only doing what its Father has always done.

## The God Who Pities Great Cities

The book of Jonah ends with a question. God sent Jonah to Nineveh, "that great city" (Jonah 1:2), in the Assyrian empire that would one day carry the northern kingdom of Israel into exile. Jonah ran the other way. When he finally went and preached, the city repented and God spared it, and Jonah was furious. He had known all along, he told God, that "you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster" (4:2). He sat outside the city to see what would become of it. God's last word to him is a question about the city's people, and even its cattle, and it begins, "And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city" (4:11). The book never records Jonah's answer.

When some of the scribes and Pharisees demanded a sign, Jesus told them that "something greater than Jonah is here" (Matthew 12:41). Jonah ran from the enemy city and sulked when it was spared. Jesus went up to the city that would reject him, and he died outside its gate for the people who put him there. Paul says that is how God dealt with every one of us: "while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son" (Romans 5:10).

That is the deepest reason a Christian owes the city anything. We were Nineveh once, and God sought our welfare at the cost of his Son.

On the evening of the resurrection, with the doors locked for fear, Jesus stood among his disciples and said, "Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you" (John 20:21). He sent them out from the city that had crucified him, and they began in that same city.

Most of us know what it is to sit with Jonah outside the city, waiting to see it get what it deserves.

Jesus went outside the city too. He went to bear what it deserved, and what we deserved with it.

Then he sent his people back in, carrying his peace.

*"He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD."* (Jeremiah 22:16)
