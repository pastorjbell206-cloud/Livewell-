# What Was Decided at the Council of Nicaea?

*In 325 the bishops at Nicaea decided that Jesus Christ is not the first and greatest of God's creatures but God in the same full sense as the Father, and most of the world's churches still confess what they wrote.*

Late in 324, the emperor Constantine sent a letter to Alexandria. He had just defeated Licinius, his last rival, and for the first time in decades a single emperor ruled the whole Roman world. Now he learned that the Christians of the East were tearing at one another. The letter was addressed to two men, Alexander, the bishop of Alexandria, and Arius, one of his presbyters. Constantine had looked into their dispute, he wrote, and he found its cause "to be of a truly insignificant character, and quite unworthy of such fierce contention."

He urged them to behave like philosophers, who argue over fine points and still keep the peace. He called the matter a "truly idle question" and asked them to stop contending over "these small and very insignificant questions" in front of ordinary believers. A trusted bishop, Hosius of Cordova in Spain, carried the letter to Egypt. It settled nothing. By the next summer Constantine had summoned bishops from across the empire to the town of Nicaea, and the question he had called small became the business of the largest gathering of bishops the church had ever seen.

Much of what people repeat about Nicaea isn't true. In one version the emperor had the bishops vote Jesus into godhood. In another the council chose the books of the Bible, which it did not do ([the canon came together another way](/writing/who-decided-what-books-are-in-the-bible)). What the council actually decided was narrower than either story, and it reaches deeper into ordinary Christian faith than both. It decided whether the Son of God is a creature. And Constantine's first instinct, that this was a quarrel over words and not worth dividing over, sounds a good deal more like most American Christians than anything the bishops said.

## How the Argument Began in Alexandria

Historians usually date the quarrel to around 318, though some place it a few years later. Arius was a presbyter in Alexandria, a man "possessed of no inconsiderable logical acumen," as the historian Socrates described him a century later. Socrates tells it this way. Alexander was teaching his clergy about the unity of the Trinity when Arius objected: "If the Father begat the Son, he that was begotten had a beginning of existence: and from this it is evident, that there was a time when the Son was not." That last phrase became the slogan of the whole dispute. There was a time when the Son was not.

Alexander gathered the bishops of Egypt and Libya, "nearly a hundred in number," and they condemned Arius and those who followed him. Arius appealed to friends abroad. He wrote to Eusebius, the bishop of Nicomedia, where the emperors of the East kept their court, and greeted him as "my fellow-Lucianist," a fellow student of the teacher Lucian of Antioch. "We are persecuted," he told Eusebius, "because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning." Eusebius took up his cause and wrote to bishops across the East, and Alexander wrote to them too. Philostorgius, a later historian who took Arius's side, says Arius even set his teaching to songs for sailors, millers and travelers on the road. By the time Constantine heard of it, the argument had left the clergy's meetings and gone down to the docks.

## What Arius Was Trying to Protect

It would be easy to make Arius the villain of the story and move on. The church did that for centuries, until "Arian" was simply a curse. But Arius did not believe he was attacking Christ. He believed he was guarding the one God. Christians had just come through [the worst persecution in their history](/writing/the-early-church-under-rome) for refusing to honor any god but one, and Arius's statement of faith to Alexander starts exactly there: "We acknowledge One God, alone Ingenerate, alone Everlasting, alone Unbegun, alone True, alone having Immortality, alone Wise, alone Good, alone Sovereign." If God alone is without beginning, then a Son who is begotten must have had one. To say anything else, as Arius saw it, was to set a second God beside the first.

He honored the Son above everything that exists. In the same statement he called him a "perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures." Writing to Eusebius, he went further and called the Son "perfect God, only begotten and unchangeable." He meant that the Son was divine by the Father's gift, the first and highest thing the Father made and the one through whom he made everything else. And Arius had texts. Athanasius reports that his allies kept quoting Proverbs 8 in the Greek most of these bishops read, where Wisdom says, "The Lord created me a beginning of His ways unto His works." The church had long heard Wisdom's voice there as the voice of the Son. The ESV, translating the Hebrew, says "possessed" (Proverbs 8:22), but the Greek said created. Jesus himself said, "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28). Paul called him "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15).

It is a tidy answer, and it still sounds reasonable to a great many people. If Jehovah's Witnesses have come to your door, you have met a descendant of it, since they teach that the Son was the first being God created. Far more Americans hold a looser version without ever thinking about doctrine. Jesus was the best man who ever lived, closer to God than anyone, and that is as far as they will go. To them Arius would sound sensible and Alexander would sound fussy. In the 320s plenty of bishops felt the same way.

## What Christians Already Believed About Jesus

Nicaea did not start the worship of Jesus. Christians had worshiped him from the beginning, and the evidence starts inside the New Testament. Every devout Jew confessed the Shema: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Writing to Corinth in the 50s, Paul, who never stopped confessing it, restated it this way: "yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist" (1 Corinthians 8:6). He kept the one God and placed Jesus inside the confession as its "one Lord." In another letter he took a line in which Israel's God swears by himself, "To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance" (Isaiah 45:23), and applied it to Jesus: "so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Philippians 2:10–11).

John's Gospel opens by saying "the Word was God" (John 1:1), and near its end it brings Thomas before the risen Jesus: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28). Early in the second century Ignatius, the bishop of Antioch, writing letters on his way to execution in Rome, spoke without hesitation of "our God, Jesus Christ." So the question in Alexandria was never whether to worship Jesus. Arius's party worshiped him too. The question was how to say what Jesus is without giving up the one God, and it was a real question. The church had answered it in prayers and hymns for generations before it was forced to answer it in a sentence.

Arius's answer was that the Son deserved worship as the highest of creatures. To Alexander and his young deacon Athanasius, that answer undid itself. Israel's God had said, "my glory I give to no other" (Isaiah 42:8). Paul traced idolatry to people who "worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator" (Romans 1:25). If the Son is a creature, then every Christian who prays to him is doing what Paul described. Athanasius put it plainly years later: "He had not been thus worshipped, nor been thus spoken of, were He a creature merely." Either the church had been wrong to worship Jesus all along, or Jesus is not a creature.

## The Bishops Gather at Nicaea

Nicaea sat in Bithynia, not far from the court at Nicomedia, in what is now İznik in Turkey. Eusebius of Caesarea, who attended and later wrote a life of the emperor, noted that the town was named for victory. Constantine paid the bishops' way, giving some the use of the public post and others "an ample supply of horses." Socrates dates the opening to May 20. Eusebius counted more than two hundred and fifty bishops, with priests, deacons and attendants "altogether beyond computation." Later writers settled on 318, and Ambrose of Milan saw in that number the 318 men of Abraham's household who went out to rescue Lot (Genesis 14:14).

Almost all of them came from the Greek-speaking East. Eusebius mentions a Persian bishop and one from Scythia, and Hosius came from Spain. The bishop of Rome, Silvester, was kept home by "extreme old age" and sent presbyters in his place. Many in the hall had been tortured for Christ. Theodoret, writing more than a century later, says Paul, the bishop of Neocaesarea on the Euphrates, had lost the use of both hands to a red-hot iron under Licinius, who had turned on the Christians of the East only a few years before. Others had lost the right eye or the right arm, among them Paphnutius of Egypt. "In short," Theodoret wrote, "the Council looked like an assembled army of martyrs."

Constantine entered last, in a purple robe glittering with gold and jewels, Eusebius says, and he would not sit on his low golden chair until the bishops signaled that he should. He spoke in Latin, with an interpreter, and his speech had one theme. Strife inside the church of God, he told them, was more dangerous than any war. Then he let the bishops argue, and they did. Some had brought him written accusations against fellow bishops. Theodoret says Constantine sealed the papers with his ring, and once agreement was reached he burned them in front of everyone, swearing he had not read a word.

## How the Bishops Chose Their Words

Athanasius was in the room as Alexander's deacon, and years later he described how the creed was written. The bishops wanted to use the words of Scripture. They proposed to say that the Son is "from God," that he is God's Word and Wisdom, that he is the "True Power and Image of the Father," like the Father in all things and always with him. Each time, the party around Eusebius of Nicomedia agreed, because Scripture says some of these things about us too. Creatures are from God. Man is the image of God. Athanasius says they were "caught whispering to each other and winking with their eyes." So the bishops reached for words that could not be read two ways. The Son is "of the substance of the Father" and "of one substance with the Father," *homoousios* in Greek.

Eusebius of Caesarea, who also signed, wrote home to his church to explain himself. He had hesitated before signing, and his letter says it was the emperor himself who urged the creed "with the insertion, however, of that single word 'homoousios.'" Much of the letter explains what Eusebius took the word to mean. Here is the creed the council published, in a translation printed in 1900:

> We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible; and in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the only-begotten of his Father, of the substance of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father. By whom all things were made, both which be in heaven and in earth. Who for us men and for our salvation came down [from heaven] and was incarnate and was made man. He suffered and the third day he rose again, and ascended into heaven. And he shall come again to judge both the quick and the dead. And [we believe] in the Holy Ghost.

It closed by condemning anyone who said "that there was a time when the Son of God was not, or that before he was begotten he was not, or that he was made of things that were not," or who called him a creature, or of another substance than the Father, or able to change.

Each phrase answered something Arius had said. "Begotten, not made" separates two things he had run together. A carpenter makes a table, and the table is not what the carpenter is. A father begets a son, and the son shares his father's nature. "Light of Light" borrows the picture in Hebrews, where the Son is "the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature" (Hebrews 1:3). There was never a sun without its light. And "of one substance" said plainly what the scriptural phrases had been bent to avoid. Whatever the Father is, the Son is.

In the end only two bishops refused to sign, Secundus of Ptolemais and Theonas of Marmarica, both from Libya. Philostorgius, the historian who sided with Arius, agrees on that. They went into exile with Arius. Eusebius of Nicomedia signed, and within three months, Philostorgius says, he was exiled too. The creed most churches recite today under Nicaea's name is longer than this one. Its fuller words about the Holy Spirit, "the Lord and Giver-of-Life," and its confession of "one, holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church" belong to the form linked with the Council of Constantinople in 381. The creed of 325 says only that we believe in the Holy Ghost, and then it stops.

The council handled other business too. It agreed that all the churches would keep Easter on the same day, so that Christians in the East who had followed the Jewish reckoning would now celebrate "at the same time with the Romans" and the Alexandrians. It settled a schism in Egypt led by a bishop named Melitius. It issued twenty canons on church order, among them a rule that Christians should pray standing, not kneeling, on Sundays and in the season between Easter and Pentecost. The emperor's letter announcing the Easter decision to the churches called the Jews "that nation of parricides who slew their Lord." Christians would repeat that kind of language for centuries, and Jewish communities would pay for it many times over.

The council ended in the summer, as Constantine was celebrating his twentieth year on the throne, and he invited every bishop to a banquet. Soldiers of the guard lined the palace entrance with drawn swords, and the bishops walked between them into the emperor's own rooms. Theodoret says that when Constantine saw bishops whose right eyes had been torn out for the faith, he pressed his lips to their wounds. Eusebius could hardly believe what he was seeing. "One might have thought," he wrote, "that a picture of Christ's kingdom was thus shadowed forth, and a dream rather than reality."

It isn't hard to understand him. A few years earlier some of those men had been in an emperor's prisons. Now soldiers stood guard while they ate. But the picture should trouble us more than it troubled Eusebius. The emperor kissing their wounds was the same man who had called their question idle, and what he wanted from the council was what he had wanted from the start, a united church for a united empire. The church had gained a patron. It had not yet learned [what a patron asks in return](/writing/when-the-church-married-empire).

## Why the Argument Went On for Fifty Years

The emperor's favor did not stay put. Within about three years Eusebius of Nicomedia was back from exile and close to the throne again. Athanasius became bishop of Alexandria in 328, and in 335 Constantine sent him into exile in Trier, in Gaul. Arius was received back at court, and in 336 he died in Constantinople on the eve of his readmission to communion. When Constantine lay dying the next year, the bishop who baptized him was Eusebius of Nicomedia. Jerome's chronicle says the emperor, "baptized by Eusebius of Nicomedia at the very end of his life, falls into the dogma of Arius."

His son Constantius, who ruled the whole empire through most of the 350s, favored bishops who wanted *homoousios* dropped altogether. In 359 bishops gathered at Ariminum, on the coast of Italy, were pressed into accepting a creed without it. Jerome, writing some twenty years later, put the result in one sentence: "The whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." Athanasius was driven from his city five times in all. In Constantinople the Nicene believers were reduced to a small house converted into a chapel, where Gregory of Nazianzus preached to them, after the city's churches had been, in Sozomen's words, "during forty years in the hands of the Arians."

The tide turned with Theodosius. In 380 he issued a law requiring his subjects to hold the faith professed by the bishops of Rome and Alexandria, and Sozomen says it ordered that those who held otherwise "should be treated as heretics, regarded with contempt, and delivered over to punishment." The next year a council at Constantinople confirmed the faith of Nicaea. The people who had kept that faith through decades of exile now had imperial law on their side, and they did not refuse it. We shouldn't tell this part of the story as a simple victory. The doctrine Athanasius held through five exiles was true. The law that compelled it was a different thing, and Christians on every side of later quarrels would reach for laws like it.

The historian R. P. C. Hanson called his account of these years *The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God*, and the title carries his argument. Between 318 and 381, he contended, the church was not simply defending a settled answer against one rebel. It was searching for words it did not yet have. Many of the bishops Athanasius lumped together as "Arians" had never followed Arius, and some who resisted *homoousios* did so because they feared it erased the difference between the Father and the Son. The real history is messier than the version many of us learned in Sunday school.

## The Strongest Case Against Nicaea

A skeptic can take that history and build a serious case from it. An emperor called the council, paid the bishops' way, and by Eusebius's account pressed the decisive word himself. That word appears nowhere in Scripture, and Arius had already rejected "one in essence" by name, listing it with the teaching of Mani. Arius's own writings survive mostly in fragments quoted by his enemies. The side that won wrote the histories, and when it finally prevailed it did so with imperial law behind it. Bart Ehrman, a New Testament scholar who left the faith of his youth, argues in *How Jesus Became God* (2014) that Christians came to call Jesus divine in stages and in more than one sense, and that ways of speaking about Christ that once sounded faithful were later ruled heretical. On that reading Nicaea did not preserve what Christians had always believed. It picked one belief out of several and called it orthodoxy.

Much of that is true. The emperor's hand was on the council, and the bishops in it were men who brought him complaints against one another. We know Arius mostly through people who wanted him condemned. A Christian who tells the story as though politics played no part in it hasn't read the sources.

But the evidence also sets limits on the skeptic's case. The worship of Jesus came long before any emperor took an interest in it. Paul placed Jesus inside the Shema within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion, and the question at Nicaea was how to account for a worship the church already practiced, not whether to begin it. Arius's answer and Alexander's were both attempts to explain that worship. Only one of them could do it without making the church guilty of idolatry.

Nor do the emperors explain the outcome. Constantine accepted the creed and then spent his last years favoring its opponents. His son spent much of his long reign working to replace it. For much of the half century after 325, the bishops who held to Nicaea were the ones being sent into exile. Whatever carried their creed through those decades, it wasn't the palace. The palace was usually against them.

As for the word that isn't in the Bible, the bishops did not begin with it. They began with Scripture's own phrases and turned to *homoousios* only when they found those phrases could be signed by men who meant something else by them. Athanasius admitted the council's terms were not in Scripture word for word and insisted they "contain the sense of the Scriptures." Every church that has had to say what a verse means, against someone using the same verse to say the opposite, has done the same thing on a smaller scale.

And if anyone in the argument bent the gospel to fit Greek philosophy, Arius is at least as easy to accuse as Athanasius. His God is so alone and so far above the world that he cannot share his own being with anyone, and he has to make a lesser being to deal with creation. That looks less like the God of Israel, who comes down to walk among his people, than like the remote God of the philosophers.

## Why the Word Still Matters

Athanasius did not think he was defending a word. He thought he was defending salvation. In a short book called *On the Incarnation* he wrote a sentence the church has quoted ever since: "He was made man that we might be made God." He did not mean that we stop being creatures. He meant what Peter meant when he wrote that through God's promises we "may become partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). God means to bring us into his own life, and a creature, however high, can bring us only as far as itself. In his argument against the Arians he said it directly: "man had not been deified if joined to a creature, or unless the Son were very God."

The stakes are close at hand. Jesus told Philip, "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). If the Son is a creature, that sentence is a generous way of speaking, and what we see in Jesus is a likeness of God at a distance. The cross changes too. If the Son is a creature, then at Golgotha God sent someone else to suffer in his place. The creed says that the one who came down "for us men and for our salvation" and "suffered" is God of God, so the love shown at the cross is God's own. Paul could say of Jesus, "For in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (Colossians 2:9). Nicaea said the same thing in the language of its own century.

## The Question We Treat as Small

Of all the documents in this story, Constantine's letter sounds the most like us. Many American Christians share his instinct without knowing they have it. Doctrine divides and love unites, we say, so why should anyone fight over a word? Sometimes that instinct is right. Christians have divided over plenty of things that deserved the emperor's word "idle." But Constantine was wrong about this one, and it is worth asking why we so easily side with him.

Part of the answer is that many of us want from Jesus what Constantine wanted from the church. On the right, a church that values Christianity mainly as the glue of a nation's morals and public order has little reason to care whether Jesus is of one substance with the Father, so long as he stands behind the flag. On the left, and in therapeutic churches of every politics, Jesus is preached as the great teacher of compassion or the source of strength for a hard week. The sociologists Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, studying American teenagers in the early 2000s, found that the faith many of them had absorbed from their parents and churches was what Smith called moralistic therapeutic deism. God wants people to be good and nice, wants them to be happy, and stays in the background until he is needed. In none of these versions would anything change if Jesus were only the greatest of creatures.

We fail that test more often than we notice, and preachers fail it first. It is easier to preach Jesus as the example to follow than as God come down to save us, because an example asks only for effort, and effort is something we can measure. I pastor a Baptist church, and Baptists have a long suspicion of creeds, learned from state churches that once enforced them with fines and prison. Many in free churches like mine have said they have no creed but the Bible. Yet the English Baptists who published their confession in 1677 wrote Nicaea's language into it, confessing Father, Son and Spirit "of one substance, power, and Eternity." They called that doctrine "the foundation of all our Communion with God, and comfortable dependance on him." And Nicaea shows what "no creed but the Bible" leaves out. Arius's party could say every biblical phrase the bishops proposed and wink while they said it. A church that only repeats the Bible's words has not yet said what it believes they mean.

Constantine thought the bishops were quarreling over a word, and he put it in writing. Some of the men who answered him had lost an eye or the use of their hands for confessing Christ, and they did not think the question was idle.

Most of us will never pay what they paid. Our test is quieter. Would our prayers or our preaching sound any different if Jesus were only the best of God's creatures?

Jesus put the question to his disciples long before any council did: "But who do you say that I am?" (Matthew 16:15).

The bishops answered with John's Gospel open in front of them. The Word was God, and the Word became flesh.

He is God of God. He did not send someone else to save us. He came himself.

*"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth."* (John 1:14)

*"For in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily."* (Colossians 2:9)

*"Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'"* (John 20:28)
