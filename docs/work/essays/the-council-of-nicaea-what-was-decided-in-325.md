# What Was Decided at the Council of Nicaea?

*In 325 the bishops at Nicaea decided that Jesus Christ is not the first and greatest of God's creatures but God in the same full sense as the Father, and most of the world's churches still confess what they wrote.*

Late in 324, the emperor Constantine sent a letter to Alexandria. He had just defeated Licinius, his last rival, and now ruled the whole Roman world alone. He had also learned that the Christians of the East were tearing at one another. The letter went to Alexander, the bishop of Alexandria, and to Arius, one of his presbyters. Constantine had looked into their dispute, he wrote, and found its cause "to be of a truly insignificant character, and quite unworthy of such fierce contention." He called it a "truly idle question."

The letter settled nothing. By the next summer Constantine had summoned bishops from across the empire to the town of Nicaea, and the question he had called idle became the business of the largest council the church had yet held.

Much of what people repeat about Nicaea isn't true. The emperor did not have the bishops vote Jesus into godhood, and the council did not choose the books of the Bible ([the canon came together another way](/writing/who-decided-what-books-are-in-the-bible)). What it decided was narrower than either story, and it reaches deeper into ordinary faith. It decided whether the Son of God is a creature. And Constantine's first instinct, that this was a quarrel over words, sounds a good deal more like most American Christians than anything the bishops said.

## How the Argument Began in Alexandria

Historians usually date the quarrel to around 318. Socrates, writing about a century later, tells it this way. Alexander was teaching his clergy about the unity of the Trinity when Arius, a man "possessed of no inconsiderable logical acumen," objected: "If the Father begat the Son, he that was begotten had a beginning of existence: and from this it is evident, that there was a time when the Son was not." That last phrase became the slogan of the whole dispute. There was a time when the Son was not.

Alexander gathered the bishops of Egypt and Libya, "nearly a hundred in number," and they condemned Arius. Arius appealed abroad. He wrote to Eusebius, the bishop of Nicomedia, where the emperors of the East kept their court, and called him "my fellow-Lucianist," since both had studied under Lucian of Antioch. "We are persecuted," he told him, "because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning." Eusebius took up his cause with bishops across the East. Philostorgius, a later historian on Arius's side, says Arius even set his teaching to songs for sailors, millers and travelers. The argument was no longer a matter for the clergy alone.

## What Arius Was Trying to Protect

It would be easy to make Arius the villain and move on. The church did that for centuries. But Arius did not believe he was attacking Christ. He believed he was guarding the one God. Christians had just come through [the worst persecution in their history](/writing/the-early-church-under-rome) for refusing to honor any god but one, and his statement of faith to Alexander begins there: "We acknowledge One God, alone Ingenerate, alone Everlasting, alone Unbegun, alone True, alone having Immortality, alone Wise, alone Good, alone Sovereign." If God alone is without beginning, a Son who is begotten must have had one. Anything else, as Arius saw it, set a second God beside the first.

He honored the Son above everything else that exists. He called him a "perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures," and in his letter to Eusebius he even called him "perfect God." He meant that the Son was the first and highest thing the Father made, the one through whom he made everything else. And he had texts. Athanasius reports that Arius's allies kept quoting Proverbs 8 in the Greek most of these bishops read: "The Lord created me a beginning of His ways unto His works." The church had long heard Wisdom's voice there as the voice of the Son. (The ESV, translating the Hebrew, says "possessed" in Proverbs 8:22.) Jesus said, "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28). Paul called him "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15).

It is a tidy answer, and it still sounds reasonable. Jehovah's Witnesses teach a version of it at the door: the Son was the first being God created. Many more Americans hold a looser one without thinking about doctrine at all. Jesus was the best man who ever lived, and that is as far as they will go. To them Arius sounds sensible. In the 320s plenty of bishops thought so too.

## What Christians Already Believed About Jesus

Nicaea did not begin the worship of Jesus, and the evidence for that is inside the New Testament. Every devout Jew confessed the Shema: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Writing to Corinth in the 50s, Paul restated it: "yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist" (1 Corinthians 8:6). He kept the one God and placed Jesus inside the confession. Writing to the Philippians, he took a line in which Israel's God swears, "To me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance" (Isaiah 45:23), and applied it to Jesus (Philippians 2:10–11).

John's Gospel opens with "the Word was God" (John 1:1), and near its end it brings Thomas before the risen Jesus: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28). Early in the second century Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to execution in Rome, wrote of "our God, Jesus Christ." So the question was never whether to worship Jesus. Arius's party worshiped him too. The question was how to say what he is without giving up the one God. The church had been answering it in prayer for generations, and now it had to answer in words a council could sign.

Arius's answer was that the Son deserved worship as the highest of creatures. To Alexander and his young deacon Athanasius, that answer undid itself. Israel's God had said, "my glory I give to no other" (Isaiah 42:8). Paul traced idolatry to people who "worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator" (Romans 1:25). If the Son is a creature, then every Christian who prays to him is doing what Paul described. Athanasius later put it plainly: "He had not been thus worshipped, nor been thus spoken of, were He a creature merely."

## The Bishops Gather at Nicaea

Nicaea sat in Bithynia, near the court at Nicomedia, in what is now İznik in Turkey. Constantine paid the bishops' travel, and the council opened, Socrates says, on May 20. Eusebius of Caesarea, who attended and later wrote the emperor's life, counted more than two hundred and fifty bishops, with attendants "altogether beyond computation." Later writers settled on 318, and Ambrose of Milan saw in that number the 318 men Abraham led out to rescue Lot (Genesis 14:14). Nearly all came from the Greek-speaking East. The bishop of Rome, kept home by "extreme old age," sent presbyters.

Many in the hall had been tortured for Christ. Theodoret, writing more than a century later, says that Paul, the bishop of Neocaesarea on the Euphrates, had lost the use of both hands to a red-hot iron under Licinius, who had turned on the Christians of the East only a few years before. Others had lost the right eye or the right arm, among them Paphnutius of Egypt. "In short," Theodoret wrote, "the Council looked like an assembled army of martyrs."

Constantine entered last, in purple and gold, Eusebius says, and he would not sit until the bishops signaled that he should. He spoke in Latin through an interpreter. Strife inside the church, he told them, was more dangerous than any war. Then the bishops argued. Some had brought him written accusations against one another. Theodoret says he sealed them with his ring and later burned them in front of everyone, swearing he had not read a word.

## How the Bishops Chose Their Words

Athanasius, there as Alexander's deacon, later described how the creed was written. The bishops first tried the words of Scripture. They proposed that the Son is "from God," that he is God's Word and Wisdom, the "True Power and Image of the Father." Each time, the party around Eusebius of Nicomedia agreed, because Scripture says such things about creatures too. We are from God. Man is the image of God. Athanasius says they were "caught whispering to each other and winking with their eyes." So the bishops reached for words that could not be read two ways. The Son is "of the substance of the Father" and "of one substance with the Father," *homoousios* in Greek.

Eusebius of Caesarea hesitated before he signed. In a letter home he said it was the emperor himself who urged the creed "with the insertion, however, of that single word 'homoousios.'" Here is the creed, in a translation printed in 1900:

> We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible; and in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the only-begotten of his Father, of the substance of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father. By whom all things were made, both which be in heaven and in earth. Who for us men and for our salvation came down [from heaven] and was incarnate and was made man. He suffered and the third day he rose again, and ascended into heaven. And he shall come again to judge both the quick and the dead. And [we believe] in the Holy Ghost.

It closed by condemning anyone who said "that there was a time when the Son of God was not, or that before he was begotten he was not, or that he was made of things that were not," or who called him a creature. The creed most churches recite under Nicaea's name is a longer form linked with the Council of Constantinople in 381, which confesses the Holy Spirit as "the Lord and Giver-of-Life." The creed of 325 names the Holy Spirit and stops.

Each phrase answered Arius. "Begotten, not made" separates what he had run together. A carpenter makes a table, and the table is not what the carpenter is. A father begets a son, and the son shares his nature. "Light of Light" borrows the picture in Hebrews, where the Son is "the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature" (Hebrews 1:3). There was never a sun without its light. And "of one substance" left no room for a second reading. Whatever the Father is, the Son is.

Only two bishops refused to sign, Secundus of Ptolemais and Theonas of Marmarica, both from Libya, and they went into exile with Arius. Even Philostorgius, who sided with Arius, agrees. Eusebius of Nicomedia signed, and within three months he was exiled too. The council also set one date for Easter, settled a schism in Egypt, and issued twenty canons on church order, including one that Christians should pray standing, not kneeling, on Sundays and between Easter and Pentecost. The emperor's letter announcing the Easter decision called the Jews "that nation of parricides who slew their Lord." Christians would repeat that kind of language for centuries, and Jewish communities would pay for it.

The council ended in the summer, as Constantine celebrated twenty years on the throne, and he gave the bishops a banquet. Soldiers lined the palace entrance with drawn swords, and the bishops walked between them into the emperor's own rooms. Theodoret says Constantine pressed his lips to the wounds of bishops whose eyes had been torn out for the faith. Eusebius wrote, "One might have thought that a picture of Christ's kingdom was thus shadowed forth, and a dream rather than reality."

It isn't hard to understand him. A few years earlier some of those men had been in an emperor's prisons. Now soldiers guarded their dinner. But the scene should trouble us more than it troubled Eusebius. The emperor kissing their wounds had called their question idle, and he wanted from the council what he had wanted from the start, one church for one empire. The church had gained a patron. It had not yet learned [what a patron asks in return](/writing/when-the-church-married-empire).

## Why the Argument Went On for Fifty Years

The emperor's favor did not stay put. Within about three years Eusebius of Nicomedia was back. Athanasius became bishop of Alexandria in 328, and in 335 Constantine exiled him to Trier, in Gaul. Arius was received at court and died in Constantinople in 336, the day before he was to be readmitted to communion. The next year the dying Constantine was baptized by Eusebius of Nicomedia. Jerome's chronicle says the emperor then "falls into the dogma of Arius."

His son Constantius favored bishops who wanted *homoousios* dropped. In 359 the bishops at Ariminum, in Italy, were pressed into a creed without it. Jerome, writing some twenty years later, summed it up: "The whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." Athanasius was driven from his city five times in all. In Constantinople the Nicene believers met in a small house turned into a chapel, where Gregory of Nazianzus preached to them, after the churches had been, in Sozomen's words, "during forty years in the hands of the Arians."

The tide turned with Theodosius. In 380 he ordered his subjects to hold the faith of the bishops of Rome and Alexandria, and Sozomen says the law directed that dissenters "should be treated as heretics, regarded with contempt, and delivered over to punishment." In 381 a council at Constantinople confirmed the faith of Nicaea. Its defenders had survived decades of exile. Now they had imperial law on their side, and they did not refuse it. We shouldn't tell that part as a simple victory. The doctrine Athanasius kept through five exiles was true. The law that enforced it was something else, and Christians of every party would reach for laws like it.

The historian R. P. C. Hanson titled his account of these years *The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God*, and the title is his argument: between 318 and 381 the church was searching for words it did not yet have. Many bishops Athanasius called "Arians" had never followed Arius. Some resisted *homoousios* because they feared it erased the difference between the Father and the Son. The history is messier than the Sunday school version.

## The Strongest Case Against Nicaea

A skeptic can build a serious case from all this. An emperor called the council, paid for it, and by Eusebius's account pressed the decisive word. The word is not in Scripture, and Arius had rejected "one in essence" by name, listing it with the teaching of Mani. His own writings survive mostly in fragments his enemies quoted. The winners wrote the histories and finally won with imperial law behind them. Bart Ehrman, a New Testament scholar who left the faith of his youth, argues in *How Jesus Became God* (2014) that Christians came to call Jesus divine in stages and in more than one sense, and that ways of speaking about Christ that once sounded faithful were later ruled heretical. On that reading Nicaea picked one belief out of several and called it orthodoxy.

Much of that is true. The emperor's hand was on the council, and its bishops brought him complaints against one another. We know Arius mostly through people who wanted him condemned. Anyone who tells the story as though politics played no part hasn't read the sources.

But the evidence limits the case too. The worship of Jesus came long before any emperor cared about it. Paul placed Jesus inside the Shema within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion. Nicaea's question was how to account for a worship the church already practiced, and only one of the two answers could do it without making the church guilty of idolatry. Nor do the emperors explain the result. For much of the half century after 325 the palace favored Nicaea's opponents. Its defenders were the ones sent into exile.

As for the word that isn't in the Bible, the bishops did not start with it. They turned to it when Scripture's own phrases could be signed by men who meant something else by them. Athanasius granted that the terms were not in Scripture word for word and insisted they "contain the sense of the Scriptures." Every church that has had to say what a verse means, against someone using the same verse for the opposite, has done the same thing on a smaller scale.

And if anyone bent the gospel to fit Greek philosophy, Arius is at least as easy to accuse. His God is so alone and so far above the world that he must make a lesser being to deal with it. That looks less like the God of Israel, who comes down to walk among his people, than like the remote God of the philosophers.

## Why the Word Still Matters

Athanasius did not think he was defending a word. He thought he was defending salvation. In *On the Incarnation* he wrote, "He was made man that we might be made God." He did not mean that we stop being creatures. He meant what Peter meant when he wrote that we "may become partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). God means to bring us into his own life. A creature, however high, can bring us only as far as itself. Against the Arians Athanasius said it directly: "man had not been deified if joined to a creature, or unless the Son were very God."

The stakes are close at hand. Jesus told Philip, "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). If the Son is a creature, that is a generous way of speaking, and what we see in Jesus is a likeness of God at a distance. The cross changes too. At Golgotha God would have sent someone else to suffer. The creed says the one who "suffered" is God of God, so the love shown there is God's own. Paul said it first: "For in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (Colossians 2:9).

## The Question We Treat as Small

Of all the documents in this story, Constantine's letter sounds most like us. Doctrine divides and love unites, we say, so why fight over a word? Sometimes that instinct is right. Christians have divided over plenty of things that deserved the emperor's word "idle." But he was wrong about this one, and we side with him more easily than we should.

Part of the reason is that many of us want from Jesus what Constantine wanted from the church. On the right, a church that values Christianity mainly as the glue of a nation's morals has little reason to care whether Jesus is of one substance with the Father, so long as he stands behind the flag. On the left, and in therapeutic churches of every politics, Jesus is preached as the great teacher of compassion or the strength for a hard week. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, studying American teenagers in the early 2000s, called the faith many had absorbed from their parents and churches moralistic therapeutic deism. God wants people to be good and happy, and he stays in the background until he is needed. In none of these versions would anything change if Jesus were only the greatest of creatures.

We fail that test more often than we notice, and preachers fail it first. Jesus the example is easier to preach than God come down to save us, because an example asks only for effort. I pastor a Baptist church, and Baptists have a long suspicion of creeds, learned from state churches that once enforced them with fines and prison. Yet the English Baptists who published their confession in 1677 wrote Nicaea's language into it, confessing Father, Son and Spirit "of one substance, power, and Eternity." They called that doctrine "the foundation of all our Communion with God, and comfortable dependance on him." Nicaea also shows what "no creed but the Bible" leaves out. Arius's party could say every biblical phrase the bishops proposed and wink while they said it. Repeating the Bible's words is not yet saying what we believe they mean.

Constantine thought the bishops were quarreling over a word. Some of the men who answered him had lost an eye or the use of their hands for confessing Christ. They did not think the question was idle.

Most of us will never pay what they paid. Our test is quieter. Would our prayers or our preaching sound any different if Jesus were only the best of God's creatures?

The question underneath it is older than any council. Jesus asked it: "But who do you say that I am?" (Matthew 16:15).

The bishops gave John's answer in the language of their own century. The Word was God, and the Word became flesh.

He is God of God. He did not send someone else to save us. He came himself.

*"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth."* (John 1:14)

*"For in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily."* (Colossians 2:9)
