## The Case for Loving Your Country

- C. S. Lewis, *The Four Loves*, begins his discussion of patriotism with love of home (place grown up in, people, way of life). Search-checked (C.S. Lewis Institute "Reflections" excerpt; FEE; Independent Institute, "C.S. Lewis on Patriotism," March 5, 2025; Harding University). Book is 1960 (Wikipedia, "The Four Loves"); the year is not stated in the essay.
- Lewis: that love is not aggressive and asks only to be let alone. Paraphrased in the essay ("It mostly wants to be left alone"), no quotation marks. Search-checked (FEE and Independent Institute quote "not in the least aggressive. It asks only to be let alone"; cepreaching.org has a variant, "It only wants to be left alone").
- Lewis: a person who loves home with imagination sees that others rightly love theirs. Paraphrase. Search-checked (results matched the line "How can I love my home without coming to realise that other men, no less rightly, love theirs?"; exact wording not seen in a primary text, so not quoted).
- Lewis, adapting Denis de Rougemont, of the natural loves: love "begins to be a demon the moment he begins to be a god." Search-checked (Desiring God, "Dangerous Compassion"; supersummary; booksataglance; several summaries give Rougemont's form "love ceases to be a demon only when he ceases to be a god" and Lewis's restatement as quoted). The line is in Lewis's introduction, about all the natural loves, and the essay says so. Primary page not opened. unverified against print.
- "He never concluded that the cure was to stop loving." Interpretation; supported by search summaries that Lewis warned rejecting patriotism would mean rejecting "half the high poetry and half the heroic action our race has achieved" (not quoted in the essay).
- Chesterton opposed Britain's war in South Africa (pro-Boer). Search-checked (Britannica: *The Defendant* (1901) expressed "strongly pro-Boer views in the South African War"; Christian History Institute, "Issues & G.K.'s Answers").
- *The Defendant*, 1901; essays reprinted from *The Speaker*; the last essay is "A Defence of Patriotism." Gutenberg-checked (PG #12245 contents list ends with "A DEFENCE OF PATRIOTISM") and search-checked for the 1901 date (Britannica; PG edition dated October 1901). The essay says "gathered a set of essays," not "newspaper essays."
- Quotation: "'My country, right or wrong,' is a thing that no patriot would think of saying except in a desperate case. It is like saying, 'My mother, drunk or sober.'" Gutenberg-checked (PG #12245, "A Defence of Patriotism").
- Quotation: "would share her troubles to the last." Gutenberg-checked (same essay: "No doubt if a decent man's mother took to drink he would share her troubles to the last").
- Quotation: "the first of all the marks of love is seriousness" and "will always esteem the most candid counsellor the best." Gutenberg-checked (same essay: "For the first of all the marks of love is seriousness: love will not accept sham bulletins or the empty victory of words. It will always esteem the most candid counsellor the best."). The essay supplies "such love" for Chesterton's "It."
- Simone Weil wrote *The Need for Roots* in London in 1943 while working for the Free French; France under German occupation. Search-checked (The Conversation, "Guide to the classics: Simone Weil's The Need for Roots"; Church Times, 2019; attentionsw.org: she went to London in November 1942 to work for the French provisional government and wrote the reports that became the book).
- "later published as *The Need for Roots*." Search-checked (published after the war as *L'Enracinement* and in English as *The Need for Roots*; attentionsw.org, Church Times "posthumously published"). The 1949 and 1952 dates are not used in the essay.
- Quotation: "To be rooted is perhaps the most important and least recognized need of the human soul." Search-checked (technologyandsociety.org; The Conversation; Veritas Journal). British editions spell "recognised"; the essay uses the American spelling. Primary translation (Arthur Wills) not opened. unverified against print.
- Weil was drawn powerfully to Christ in her last years. Search-checked (attentionsw.org and EBSCO "Waiting for God": 1938 mystical experience; "a sincere attraction to Christianity" late in life). The essay deliberately says nothing about baptism: whether she was baptized at the end is disputed (attentionsw.org, Springsted; Vatican laity office paper "Simone Weil and baptism").
- Weil argued the right love of country is compassion for it, not pride in its greatness. Paraphrase. Search-checked (Christian Century, "Practicing compassionate patriotism," quoting her phrase "compassion for one's country" set against Hitler's cult of greatness; attentionsw.org: "patriotism is not national pride, but rather national compassion"). The wording "beautiful, precious, fragile and perishable" was NOT confirmed and is not used.
- "She never doubted that a soul cut off from every place and people is poorer for it, not freer." Interpretation of the book's thesis (rootedness as a need of the soul; uprootedness as the great modern disease), not a quotation.
- Weil died in August 1943. Search-checked (attentionsw.org: died August 24, 1943, buried in Ashford, Kent).
- Exodus 20:12, "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you." mdbible-checked. Called "the fifth commandment" in the Protestant and Reformed numbering; Catholic and Lutheran numbering (and Aquinas) count it as the fourth.
- Thomas Aquinas, thirteenth century: after God, we owe most to parents and country, who gave us being and upbringing; the virtue that repays this is piety. Paraphrase. Search-checked (Summa Theologiae II-II q.101 a.1, as given by CCEL, aquinas.cc and clerus.org: "Man becomes a debtor to other men in various ways ... after God, man is debtor chiefly to his parents and his country"; Catholic World Report, "The Forgotten Virtue of Patriotism"). The Dominican translation renders *cultus* as "worship"; the essay avoids that word for piety.
- Acts 17:26, "made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place." mdbible-checked.
- Paul spoke in the Areopagus at Athens. mdbible-checked (Acts 17:22, "So Paul, standing in the midst of the Areopagus").

## Frederick Douglass and Dietrich Bonhoeffer

- July 5, 1852; Corinthian Hall, Rochester, New York; a meeting of the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. Search-checked (Wikipedia "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?"; National Constitution Center; NMAAHC blog). Date also Gutenberg-checked (PG #202 heading: "Extract from an Oration, at Rochester, July 5, 1852").
- Douglass had escaped from slavery in Maryland. Not checked (textbook; he escaped in 1838, a date the essay does not use).
- Quotation: "The signers of the Declaration of Independence were brave men." Search-checked (ushistory.org; NC Historic Sites reading; NMAAHC; becauseofthemwecan.com: "The signers of the Declaration of Independence were brave men. They were great men too"). Not in the 1855 extract, so not Gutenberg-checked. It comes early in the full speech, before the turn; the essay's "He began with praise" depends on that order, which the search results support.
- Quotation: "This Fourth of July is *yours*, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn." Gutenberg-checked (PG #202; italics on "yours" are Douglass's in the 1855 text).
- Douglass recited Psalm 137, "How can we sing the Lord's song in a strange land?" Gutenberg-checked (PG #202). This is Douglass's own wording, close to but not identical with the KJV ("How shall we sing the LORD's song in a strange land?"), so the essay does not call it the King James text.
- He applied it to the millions still enslaved. Gutenberg-checked ("I hear the mournful wail of millions, whose chains ...").
- Quotation: "What to the American slave is your Fourth of July? ... I answer, a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim." Gutenberg-checked (PG #202; the 1852 pamphlet reads "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?").
- Quotation: "your prayers and hymns, your sermons and thanksgivings, with all your religious parade and solemnity," ... "mere bombast, fraud, deception, impiety, and hypocrisy." Gutenberg-checked (PG #202; the original continues "are to him mere bombast ..."; the essay supplies "were").
- Quotation: the Constitution, interpreted as it ought to be, is "a GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT" (capitals Douglass's). Search-checked (Daily Signal, July 3, 2020, reproducing the passage; National Constitution Center historic document library; Reason, October 1, 2006). - Quotation: "I do not despair of this country." Search-checked (Kenosha Civil War Museum transcript: "notwithstanding the dark picture I have this day presented of the state of the nation, I do not despair of this country"; drt.cmc.edu; Hebrew College discussion guide). Near the end of the speech. Not in the 1855 extract.
- Quotation: "false to the past, false to the present." Gutenberg-checked (PG #202: "America is false to the past, false to the present, and solemnly binds herself to be false to the future.").
- Bonhoeffer, a Lutheran pastor and theologian, came to New York in June 1939 at the invitation of American friends including Reinhold Niebuhr (Union Theological Seminary). Search-checked (Britannica; PBS Bonhoeffer timeline; New Republic; Samuel Marks Substack: Niebuhr and other American friends arranged the visit).

- Quotation, letter to Niebuhr: "I must live through this difficult period of our national history with the Christian people of Germany." Search-checked (Christianity Today; Robinson College, Cambridge, "Dietrich Bonhoeffer: Traitor or Martyr"; Samuel Marks Substack; consistent across sources).
