## A Bishop in a Plague Year

- A plague reached Carthage around 252. Search 2026-10-28: heurist.huma-num.fr (Judaism and Rome database) dates *Ad Demetrianum* to 252, "when the great plague was spreading through the empire, and had reached Carthage"; a translation's header "[Written a.d. 252.]".
- The plague had been moving through the empire for a few years already. Search 2026-10-28: result summary gives the plague's span as roughly 249 to 262. Generalized to "a few years."
- Two years earlier, under Decius, every inhabitant of the empire had been ordered to sacrifice. Sibling ledger (the-early-church-under-rome: edict of late 249 or early 250; certificates including a pagan priestess's).
- In Carthage many Christians had given way while others refused. Library: Cyprian, *On the Lapsed* 8 ("They ran to the market-place of their own accord"), and 2 (the "confessors illustrious with the heraldry of a good name"). Also sibling ledger (the-early-church-under-rome, *On the Lapsed* 8).
- Demetrianus had come more than once to argue with Cyprian. Library: *To Demetrianus* 1 ("when you used often to come to me with the desire of contradicting rather than with the wish to learn"). The ANF argument calls him "the Proconsul of Africa"; modern sources differ on his office (one result gives the name as Demetrius), so the essay calls him only "a man named Demetrianus."
- The charge: wars, famine, drought and plague were the Christians' fault because they would not worship the gods. Library: *To Demetrianus* 2 ("that to us it is ascribed that wars arise more frequently, that plague, that famines rage, and that long droughts are suspending the showers and rains") and 3 ("because your gods are not worshipped by us").
- Paraphrase: famine made worse by men who shut up their barns and waited for prices to rise. Library: *To Demetrianus* 10 ("grasping at the increase of the year's produce, and the accumulation of their price"; "the barns are shut up on earth").
- Quotation: "as if drought made a greater famine than rapacity." Library, *To Demetrianus* 10. Verbatim.
- Paraphrase: in the plague the sick were being abandoned. Library: *To Demetrianus* 10 ("mercy is not manifested to the weak"; "the wretched are probably forsaken in their sickness").
- Quotation: "avarice and rapine are waiting open-mouthed for the dead." Library, *To Demetrianus* 10. Verbatim.
- Quotation: "we entreat constantly and urgently, day and night, for your peace and salvation." Library, *To Demetrianus* 20. Verbatim (the sentence begins "And yet we always ask for the repulse of enemies ... and we pour forth our prayers, and, propitiating and appeasing God,").
- Quotation: "We repay kindness for your hatred." Library, *To Demetrianus* 25 (of 26 chapters; hence "near the end"). Verbatim.
- Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, later wrote his life. Search 2026-10-28 (Wikipedia, "Pontius of Carthage": Cyprian's deacon who accompanied him into exile; EWTN and New Advent list *The Life and Passion of Cyprian*).
- Paraphrase of Pontius (no quotation marks): when the plague came, Cyprian gathered the church and told them there was nothing remarkable about caring for their own; Christ had told his disciples to love their enemies and pray for those who persecuted them. Search 2026-10-28: result summary of bkv.unifr.ch, *Life and Passion of Cyprian* division 10 ("caring only for one's own community is unremarkable, and the higher standard is loving enemies and praying for persecutors, as Christ taught"; contrasts God's gifts to "aliens" with his gifts to his people); nftu.net summary ("Cyprian first urging the congregation toward mercy and then pressing them to show a clemency like God's, rather than merely loving their own people"). Partly verified: the primary text could not be opened, and the summary did not confirm the word "gathered" or any statement that the care reached pagans by name. Unverified: whether Pontius says the congregation was assembled.
- "Their care, in other words, could not stop at the church door." Interpretation of the Pontius passage above.
- Pontius admired Cyprian; read him as an admiring biographer. Interpretation.

## Augustine and the Peace of Babylon

- In 410 the Goths under Alaric sacked Rome, and many blamed Christianity. Sibling ledger (what-christian-nationalism-is-and-is-not: Gutenberg #45304, *Retractations* ii.43, "Rome having been stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric their king", editor's note A.D. 410; Book 1 answers pagans who blame Christ).
- Augustine began *The City of God* in 413 and finished it in 426. Sibling ledger (same: Dods's preface, "begun the very year of Marcellinus' death, A.D. 413," completed in 426).
- Demetrianus's charge was more than a century and a half before (252 to 410 is 158 years). Arithmetic from the dates above.
- Augustine was bishop of Hippo in North Africa. Unverified (textbook).
- Quotation, *City of God* 19.26, "we also enjoy the peace of Babylon. For from Babylon the people of God is so freed that it meanwhile sojourns in its company". Library (Dods): verbatim. The preceding clause, paraphrased in the essay, reads "for as long as the two cities are commingled".
- He set Paul's charge to pray for kings beside Jeremiah's command to pray for Babylon. Library, 19.26 ("the apostle also admonished the Church to pray for kings and those in authority ... And the prophet Jeremiah ... counselled them also to pray for Babylonia").
- Quotation, *City of God* 19.17, "like a captive and a stranger in the earthly city". Library (Dods): verbatim.
- Paraphrases of 19.17 (no quotation marks): makes no scruple about obeying the earthly city's laws because both need the same things for this mortal life; wants what its neighbors want for ordinary life; keeps the customs and institutions it finds so long as they do not hinder the worship of the one true God; the two cities cannot share worship; the heavenly city has had to dissent and bear anger and hatred. Library (Dods, 19.17: "it makes no scruple to obey the laws of the earthly city, whereby the things necessary for the maintenance of this mortal life are administered"; "desires and maintains a common agreement among men regarding the acquisition of the necessaries of life"; "preserves and adopts them, so long only as no hindrance to the worship of the one supreme and true God is thus introduced"; "the two cities could not have common laws of religion"; "compelled in this matter to dissent ... and to stand the brunt of their anger and hatred and persecutions").
- "That is the freedom the letter gives" paragraph (obey laws, pay taxes, work for peace without needing the city to last). Interpretation; "pay its taxes" is the writer's, not Augustine's.
