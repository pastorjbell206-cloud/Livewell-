## Pliny's Letter to Trajan

- Around 112, Pliny the Younger, a Roman governor, wrote to Trajan about the Christians (*Letters* 10.96). Checked against McGiffert's note to *Church History* 3.33 (library): the letter is "No. 96 (97)" of book ten, written from Bithynia "probably within a year after Pliny became governor there, which was in 110 or 111." Second pass, search: the results (bible-researcher.com's "Documents on the Persecution of the Early Church," World History Encyclopedia's "Pliny the Younger on Christianity" and others) date the letter 111 or 112, most often 112; "around the year 112" holds.
- Pliny had been sent to Bithynia and Pontus, on the southern shore of the Black Sea, now northern Turkey. Verified, closing pass: Melmoth's letters speak of "the cities of Bithynia and Pontus," one of its notes places "cities of Pontus near the Euxine or Black Sea," and another describes a neighboring province of Asia Minor as "bounded by the Black Sea on the north, Bithynia on the west, Pontus on the east." "Northern Turkey" is the modern name for that coast.
- He wrote to Trajan about nearly everything. Supported by the letter's opening (Perseus Latin; McGiffert: "It is my custom, my Lord, to refer to thee all questions concerning which I am in doubt"; Melmoth: "IT is my invariable rule, Sir, to refer to you in all matters where I feel doubtful"). Characterization.
- After a fire in Nicomedia he asked to form a company of 150 firemen; Trajan refused because such groups became political clubs. Checked against Perseus Latin, *Letters* 10.33-34, and, in the closing pass, Melmoth ("a company of fire-men, consisting only of one hundred and fifty members"; Trajan: "they will not fail to form themselves into factious assemblies").
- Rome distrusted any group that met on its own terms. Interpretation.
- Pliny had never attended a trial of Christians; he did not know whether the name or the attached crimes were punishable. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert and Melmoth.
- He asked the accused three times, threatening punishment, and had those who persisted led away to execution. Checked against Perseus Latin and McGiffert ("led away to punishment") and Melmoth ("repeated the question twice, and threatened them with punishment"). "To execution": verified, closing pass, by Tertullian, *Apology* 2 (ANF, library): "the younger Pliny ... having condemned some Christians to death."
- Stubbornness and unbending obstinacy deserved punishment, whatever they believed. Paraphrase. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert, and Melmoth ("a contumacious and inflexible obstinacy certainly deserved correction").
- Those who denied had to call on the gods, offer incense and wine before the emperor's statue, and curse Christ, things no real Christian could be forced to do. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert, and Melmoth ("offered religious rites with wine and incense before your statue ... and even reviled the name of Christ: whereas there is no forcing, it is said, those who are really Christians").
- Former Christians had left some as long as twenty years before. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert, and Melmoth ("a few above twenty years ago").
- They met on a fixed day before dawn and sang a hymn to Christ "as to a god." Martin, *Carmen Christi* and *Vox Evangelica* 3 (search, final read); Perseus Latin *carmenque Christo quasi deo dicere*; Melmoth: "met on a stated day before it was light, and addressed a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity."

- Two enslaved women tortured, women who were called *ministrae* (was "women the Christians called"; final read, correction 20: the Latin *quae ministrae dicebantur* is passive and names no speaker), often translated deaconesses.
