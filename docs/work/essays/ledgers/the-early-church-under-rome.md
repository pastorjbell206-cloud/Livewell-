## Pliny's Letter to Trajan

- Around 112, Pliny the Younger, a Roman governor, wrote to Trajan about the Christians (*Letters* 10.96). Checked against McGiffert's note to *Church History* 3.33 (library): the letter is "No. 96 (97)" of book ten, written from Bithynia "probably within a year after Pliny became governor there, which was in 110 or 111."
- Pliny had been sent to Bithynia and Pontus, on the southern shore of the Black Sea, now northern Turkey. Verified, closing pass: Melmoth's letters speak of "the cities of Bithynia and Pontus," one of its notes places "cities of Pontus near the Euxine or Black Sea," and another describes a neighboring province of Asia Minor as "bounded by the Black Sea on the north, Bithynia on the west, Pontus on the east." "Northern Turkey" is the modern name for that coast.
- He wrote to Trajan about nearly everything. Supported by the letter's opening (Perseus Latin; McGiffert: "It is my custom, my Lord, to refer to thee all questions concerning which I am in doubt"; Melmoth: "IT is my invariable rule, Sir, to refer to you in all matters where I feel doubtful"). Characterization.
- After a fire in Nicomedia he asked to form a company of 150 firemen; Trajan refused because such groups became political clubs. Checked against Perseus Latin, *Letters* 10.33-34, and, in the closing pass, Melmoth ("a company of fire-men, consisting only of one hundred and fifty members"; Trajan: "they will not fail to form themselves into factious assemblies").
- Rome distrusted any group that met on its own terms. Interpretation.
- Pliny had never attended a trial of Christians; he did not know whether the name or the attached crimes were punishable. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert and Melmoth.
- He asked the accused three times, threatening punishment, and had those who persisted led away to execution. Checked against Perseus Latin and McGiffert ("led away to punishment") and Melmoth ("repeated the question twice, and threatened them with punishment"). "To execution": verified, closing pass, by Tertullian, *Apology* 2 (ANF, library): "the younger Pliny ... having condemned some Christians to death."
- Stubbornness and unbending obstinacy deserved punishment, whatever they believed. Paraphrase. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert, and Melmoth ("a contumacious and inflexible obstinacy certainly deserved correction").
- Those who denied had to call on the gods, offer incense and wine before the emperor's statue, and curse Christ, things no real Christian could be forced to do. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert, and Melmoth ("offered religious rites with wine and incense before your statue ... and even reviled the name of Christ: whereas there is no forcing, it is said, those who are really Christians").
- Former Christians had left some as long as twenty years before. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert, and Melmoth ("a few above twenty years ago").
- They met on a fixed day before dawn and sang a hymn to Christ as to a god. Paraphrase (correction 1). Perseus Latin; Melmoth: "met on a stated day before it was light, and addressed a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity."
- The oath against theft, robbery and adultery, keeping their word, returning deposits. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert and Melmoth.
- The ordinary, harmless meal, stopped after Pliny's edict banning associations on Trajan's orders. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert and Melmoth ("to eat in common a harmless meal ... desisted after the publication of my edict, by which, according to your commands, I forbade the meeting of any assemblies").
- Two enslaved women tortured, called *ministrae*, often translated deaconesses. Checked (correction 2).
- He found nothing but a depraved and excessive superstition. Paraphrase. Perseus Latin, McGiffert ("a superstition depraved and immoderate"), Melmoth ("an absurd and extravagant superstition").
- Every age and rank, men and women, villages and farms as well as cities. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert and Melmoth.
- Temples filling again; the trade in sacrificial animals picking up. Checked against Perseus Latin, McGiffert and Melmoth ("a general demand for the victims, which till lately found very few purchasers").
- Trajan approved: not to be hunted, no anonymous accusations, punished unless they proved their denial by worshiping the gods. Checked against Perseus Latin, *Letters* 10.97, and Melmoth ("Do not go out of your way to look for them ... by invoking our gods ... Anonymous informations ought not to be received").
- Trajan's reply set the terms for most of the next century and a half. Verified, closing pass, from dated sources: the letter about 112 (above) and Decius's edicts "early in the year 250," which McGiffert's note to 6.39 calls the first to aim at "the complete extermination of the Christians," "universal" and "far more terrible than any that had preceded it." 112 to 250 is about 138 years.
- 1 Peter 1:1, "To those who are elect exiles of the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia." Corrected (correction 11). Search plus ESV copy.
- First Peter was addressed to that region a generation or more before Pliny. Verified, closing pass, under the traditional authorship: Eusebius counts the letter undisputed, written "to the Hebrews of the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (*Church History* 3.4.2), and has Peter "crucified under Nero" (2.25.5), so before 68 and more than forty years before 112. Later datings of 1 Peter were not looked up; the essay's "a generation or more" holds for any date before about 90.
- 1 Peter 2:12, "Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation." Verified, closing pass: search (esv.org and christianity.com ESV pages, as quoted) and ESV copy.

## How Rome Saw the Christians

- Rome absorbed conquered peoples' gods; the *pax deorum* was kept by public rites everyone shared. Verified, closing pass: Minucius Felix, *Octavius* 6 (ANF, library), where the pagan speaker says the Romans "venerate the conquered deities ... seek for the gods of the strangers, and make them their own," and owe their dominion to it; Livy (Perseus Livy) uses the phrase in its older form, *pax deum*, for what the city sought by public rites when plague and portents came ("omnia delubra pacem deum exposcentium virorum mulierumque turba inplebantur"). "Pax deorum" is the same phrase with the classical genitive.
- Romans called the Christians atheists. Checked against the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* 3 and 9 (ANF, library) and Julian's letter in Sozomen 5.16.
- Polycarp brought into the stadium at Smyrna around 155; told to say "Away with the atheists"; he waved his hand at the crowd and said it. Quotation checked against the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* 9 (ANF) and Eusebius's copy of it (*Church History* 4.15.18-19, NPNF: "Away with the Atheists"). Case difference only. "Around 155": verified, closing pass: McGiffert's note in book 4 puts Polycarp's martyrdom in "155 or 156 a.d."
- Tertullian, writing in Carthage around 197, on the Tiber, the Nile, famine and plague, and "the Christians to the lion." Paraphrase. Checked against *Apology* 40 (ANF). Date verified, closing pass: McGiffert's note to *Church History* 2.2 says the Apology's composition in "the latter part (summer or fall) of the year 197 ... may be accepted as practically established." Carthage: the same note has Tertullian born in Carthage and spending much of his life there, and the Apology itself speaks of Africa's proconsul (ch. 9). It does not say outright where the Apology was written.
- Celsus wrote around 170 to 180 the earliest full-length attack on Christianity that survives in any quantity. Verified, closing pass: McGiffert's note to 6.36 calls Celsus "the first great literary opponent of Christianity," writing "probably in the reign of Marcus Aurelius (Keim fixes the date of the work at 178 a.d.)." Marcus Aurelius reigned to 180.
- We have much of it only because Origen quoted it at length in his reply about seventy years later. Origen's eight books in the library quote Celsus throughout. Interval verified, closing pass: *Against Celsus* "was written between 246 and 250" (McGiffert, book 6) and Celsus about 178. That no independent copy of Celsus survives was not stated in a source seen; it follows from McGiffert's account and is not doubted anywhere seen.
- Celsus sneered at wool-workers, cobblers and laundry workers. Paraphrase. Checked against *Against Celsus* 3.55 (ANF).
- If everyone did as the Christians, the emperor deserted and the world to lawless barbarians. Paraphrase. Checked against *Against Celsus* 8.68 (ANF).
- Robert Louis Wilken, a historian of early Christianity, wrote a book on how the Christians looked to the Romans and takes those critics seriously. **Still open.** The search for it was refused (budget spent) and the publisher's site was unreachable. The book meant is *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them* (Yale, 1984); confirm with one search.
- Jerry Falwell, two days after September 11, 2001, on national television, blamed the pagans, abortionists, feminists, gays and lesbians, the ACLU and others; he apologized soon after. Paraphrase. Verified, closing pass: search (Beliefnet's partial transcript of the Thursday, September 13, 2001 *700 Club*: "the pagans, and the abortionists, and the feminists, and the gays and the lesbians ... the ACLU, People For the American Way ... I point the finger in their face and say 'you helped this happen'"; Windy City Times, "Falwell apologizes for blaming gays," and his September 20 retraction on ABC's *Good Morning America*, as quoted).

## Decius and the Certificates of Sacrifice

- Around 248, Origen wrote that the martyrs were few and easily counted. Paraphrase. Checked against *Against Celsus* 3.8 (ANF). Date: McGiffert, *Against Celsus* "written between 246 and 250."
- For two centuries persecution had been local and sporadic, set off by a mob or a governor. Verified as characterization, closing pass: Trajan's rescript (no searching out; accusers needed), the Polycarp and Lyon accounts (crowds and governors), Origen's "easily numbered," and McGiffert's note to 6.39 that Decius "was the first one that aimed at the complete extermination of the Christians," his persecution "universal."
- Rome had celebrated its thousandth birthday shortly before, under the emperor Philip, amid crisis, with Goths pressing on the Danube and armies making and unmaking emperors. Generalized (correction 12).
