## Pliny's Letter to Trajan

- Around 112, Pliny the Younger, a Roman governor, wrote to Trajan about the Christians (*Letters* 10.96). Checked against McGiffert's note to *Church History* 3.33 (library): the letter is "No. 96 (97)" of book ten, written from Bithynia "probably within a year after Pliny became governor there, which was in 110 or 111." "Around 112" fits.
- Pliny had been sent to Bithynia and Pontus, on the southern shore of the Black Sea, now northern Turkey. Not checked (textbook).
- He wrote to Trajan about nearly everything. Supported by the letter's opening, *Sollemne est mihi, domine, omnia de quibus dubito ad te referre* (Perseus Latin; McGiffert: "It is my custom, my Lord, to refer to thee all questions concerning which I am in doubt"). Characterization.
- After a fire in Nicomedia he asked to form a company of 150 firemen; Trajan refused because such groups became political clubs. Checked against Perseus Latin, *Letters* 10.33 (*Nicomediae vastissimum incendium*; *collegium fabrorum dumtaxat hominum CL*) and 10.34 (*hetaeriae eaeque brevi fient*).
- Rome distrusted any group that met on its own terms. Interpretation.
- Pliny had never attended a trial of Christians; he did not know whether the name itself or the crimes attached to it were punishable. Checked against Perseus Latin (*Cognitionibus de Christianis interfui numquam*; *nomen ipsum ... an flagitia cohaerentia nomini*) and McGiffert's English.
- He asked the accused three times, threatening punishment, and had those who persisted led away. Checked against Perseus Latin (*iterum ac tertio interrogavi supplicium minatus; perseverantes duci iussi*) and McGiffert ("led away to punishment"). "To execution" is the usual sense of *duci*; not separately checked.
- Stubbornness and unbending obstinacy deserved punishment, whatever they believed. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Checked against Perseus Latin (*pertinaciam certe et inflexibilem obstinationem debere puniri*) and McGiffert.
- Those who denied had to call on the gods, offer incense and wine before the emperor's image, and curse Christ, things no real Christian could be forced to do. Checked against Perseus Latin and McGiffert ("offering incense and wine to thine image ... cursed Christ-things which they say that those who are truly Christians cannot be compelled to do").
- Former Christians had left some as long as twenty years before. Checked against Perseus Latin (*non nemo etiam ante viginti*) and McGiffert ("one even twenty years before").
- They met on a fixed day before dawn and sang a hymn to Christ as to a god. Now paraphrase (correction 1). Checked against Perseus Latin (*stato die ante lucem convenire, carmenque Christo quasi deo dicere*).
- They bound themselves by an oath not to commit a crime but to keep from theft, robbery and adultery, keep their word, and return deposits. Checked against Perseus Latin (*ne furta ne latrocinia ne adulteria committerent, ne fidem fallerent, ne depositum appellati abnegarent*) and McGiffert.
- They later shared ordinary, harmless food, and stopped after Pliny's edict, on Trajan's orders, banning associations. Checked against Perseus Latin (*promiscuum tamen et innoxium ... post edictum meum, quo secundum mandata tua hetaerias esse vetueram*) and McGiffert ("prohibited fraternities").
- He had two enslaved women tortured, called *ministrae*, often translated deaconesses. Checked against Perseus Latin (*ex duabus ancillis, quae ministrae dicebantur*) and McGiffert ("two female slaves who were called deaconesses"). Wording softened (correction 2).
- He found nothing but a depraved and excessive superstition. Paraphrase. Checked against Perseus Latin (*superstitionem pravam et immodicam*) and McGiffert ("a superstition depraved and immoderate").
- It had spread to every age and rank, men and women, villages and farms as well as cities. Checked against Perseus Latin and McGiffert.
- Nearly deserted temples were filling again and the trade in sacrificial animals was picking up after a long stretch with few buyers. Checked against Perseus Latin (*prope iam desolata templa coepisse celebrari ... passimque venire carnem victimarum, cuius adhuc rarissimus emptor inveniebatur*) and McGiffert ("fodder for the victims to be sold"). The manuscripts' reading varies between meat and fodder for the victims; "the trade in sacrificial animals" covers both.
- Trajan approved: Christians not to be hunted, anonymous accusations not admitted, the accused and convicted punished unless they proved their denial by worshiping the gods. Checked against Perseus Latin, *Letters* 10.97 (*Actum quem debuisti ... secutus es. ... Conquirendi non sunt; si deferantur et arguantur, puniendi sunt ... supplicando dis nostris ... Sine auctore vero propositi libelli in nullo crimine locum habere debent*).
- Trajan's reply set the terms for most of the next century and a half. Not checked (textbook); it ran until Decius in 250.

Matched word for word against the ESV copy, except that the copy capitalizes "Dispersion." Unresolved: the copy's edition is not stated, so the essay's lowercase is left as it was (the sibling ledger for what-post-christian-means made the same call). Check with the ESV API.

## How Rome Saw the Christians

- Rome absorbed conquered peoples' gods; the *pax deorum* was kept by public rites. Not checked (textbook).
- Romans called the Christians atheists. Checked against the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* 3 and 9 (ANF, library), where the crowd cries "Away with the Atheists," and Julian's letter in Sozomen 5.16 ("the progress of Atheism").
- Polycarp brought into the stadium at Smyrna around 155; the governor urged him to say "Away with the atheists"; he waved his hand at the crowd and said it. Quotation checked against the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* 9 (ANF, library): the proconsul says "Swear by the fortune of Caesar; repent, and say, Away with the Atheists," and Polycarp, "waving his hand towards them," says "Away with the Atheists." The translation capitalizes "Atheists"; the essay's lowercase is a case difference only. "Around 155": not checked (textbook; the library's metadata for the work gives 155).
- Tertullian, writing in Carthage around 197, complained that when the Tiber flooded or the Nile failed to rise, or there was famine or plague, the crowd's answer was to throw the Christians to the lion. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Checked against Tertullian, *Apology* 40 (ANF, library): "If the Tiber rises as high as the city walls, if the Nile does not send its waters up over the fields ... if there is famine or pestilence, straightway the cry is, 'Away with the Christians to the lion!'" Carthage and "around 197": not checked (textbook).
- Celsus wrote around 170 to 180 the earliest full-length attack on Christianity that survives in any quantity. Not checked (textbook).
- We have much of it only because Origen quoted it at length in his reply about seventy years later. Origen's reply is in the library (*Against Celsus*, ANF, eight books, quoting Celsus throughout). Eusebius dates it to when Origen was "over sixty years old" (*Church History* 6.36.1-2, NPNF, library), that is, the late 240s. The interval follows from the dates; not separately checked.
- Celsus sneered that Christians recruited wool-workers, cobblers and laundry workers. Paraphrase. Checked against *Against Celsus* 3.55 (ANF, library): "workers in wool and leather, and fullers, and persons of the most uninstructed and rustic character."
- If everyone did as the Christians did, the emperor would be left deserted and the world would fall to the most lawless barbarians. Paraphrase. Checked against *Against Celsus* 8.68 (ANF, library): "there would be nothing to prevent his being left in utter solitude and desertion, and the affairs of the earth would fall into the hands of the wildest and most lawless barbarians."
- Robert Louis Wilken, a historian of early Christianity, wrote a book on how the Christians looked to the Romans (*The Christians as the Romans Saw Them*, 1984) and takes those critics seriously. Not checked (reference knowledge). The next sentence (a community with officers, a common fund and "a Lord who was not Caesar") is interpretation.
- Jerry Falwell, two days after September 11, 2001, on national television, blamed the pagans, the abortionists, the feminists, gays and lesbians, the ACLU and others for helping make the attacks happen, and apologized soon after. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Not checked (reference knowledge: September 13, 2001, on Pat Robertson's *The 700 Club*). No search was available. Kept because it is widely reported public record; confirm the date, the program and the apology when search returns.

## The Martyrs of Lyon

- The persecution at Lyon and Vienne in 177, under Marcus Aurelius. Checked against *Church History* 5.1.1 and 5 prologue (NPNF, library): "In the seventeenth year of the Emperor Antoninus Verus," with McGiffert's note giving "the seventeenth year of Marcus Aurelius (177 a.d.)."
- The account survives in a letter of the churches there to the Christians of Asia and Phrygia, copied by Eusebius. Checked (*Church History* 5.1.2-3: "to the brethren through out Asia and Phrygia").
- Christians were first barred from houses, baths and markets. Checked (5.1.5: "shutting us out from houses and baths and markets").
- Then came the crowd, with beatings, stoning and plunder, then arrest. Checked (5.1.7: "clamors and blows and draggings and robberies and stonings and imprisonments"; 5.1.8 brought to the forum and examined).
- Pagan slaves of Christian households, fearing the tortures they had seen, accused their masters of eating children and incest. Checked (5.1.14: "fearing for themselves the tortures which they beheld the saints endure ... accused us falsely of Thyestean banquets and Oedipodean intercourse"). "Eating children" is the meaning of "Thyestean banquets."
- Pothinus, the bishop, was past ninety, was beaten by the crowd, and died in prison two days later. Checked (5.1.29-31: "more than ninety years of age"; "cast into prison and died after two days").
- Sanctus, a deacon, would not give name, city, or whether slave or free, and answered in Latin, "I am a Christian." Checked (5.1.17 "Sanctus, the deacon from Vienne"; 5.1.20 "answered in the Roman tongue to all their questions, `I am a Christian.'").
- Blandina's mistress feared she was too frail; she was tortured from morning to evening until her torturers were exhausted; her answer was that she was a Christian and nothing wicked was done among them. Checked (5.1.18-19: "her earthly mistress ... feared that on account of the weakness of her body"; "from morning till evening"; "`I am a Christian, and there is nothing vile done by us.'"). Paraphrase.
- Hung on a post in the arena, she reminded the others of the crucified one. Checked (5.1.41: "suspended on a stake ... as if hanging on a cross").
- She was the last to die. Checked (5.1.55: "the blessed Blandina, last of all").
- The emperor ruled that confessors be put to death and deniers set free. Checked (5.1.47: "Caesar commanded that they should be put to death, but that any who might deny should be set free").
- Bodies left six days, burned, ashes swept into the Rhône so the Christians would have no hope of rising again. Checked (5.1.62-63: "exposed for six days ... burned and reduced to ashes, and swept into the Rhone"; "`that they may have no hope of a resurrection'").
- The survivors refused to be called martyrs; the title belonged to Christ and to those already dead; they were only confessors. Checked (5.2.2-3: "they conceded cheerfully the appellation of Witness to Christ `the faithful and true Witness' ... `but we are lowly and humble confessors.'").
- Revelation 3:14, "the faithful and true witness." Matched word for word, with case, against the ESV copy ("The words of the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's creation").
- They did not despise the ones who broke, and prayed for them as for their torturers. Checked (5.2.6-7: "they did not boast over the fallen ... shedding many tears on their account before the Father"; 5.2.5: "they prayed for those who had inflicted cruelties upon them").
- Many who had denied later confessed. Corrected from "several" (correction 3). Checked (5.1.45-46).
- The church received back alive the children it had counted dead. Checked (5.1.45: "the virgin mother had much joy in receiving alive those whom she had brought forth as dead").
- Irenaeus, a presbyter, succeeded Pothinus and wrote *Against Heresies*. Checked for presbyter and succession (*Church History* 5.4.1 "already at that time a presbyter of the parish of Lyons"; 5.5.8 "Irenaeus succeeded him in the episcopate"). *Against Heresies*: not checked (textbook).

## How the Church Grew

- Rodney Stark estimated in the 1990s that the church grew at a steady rate until by 300 Christians numbered in the millions, still a minority. Generalized (correction 6). Not checked.
- Historians argue about his figures but the shape is widely accepted. Not checked (reference knowledge).
- Dionysius of Alexandria, during a plague around 260, wrote that most of the brethren nursed the sick without sparing themselves, and many died; the heathen deserted the sick, fled those dearest to them, and left the dead unburied. Checked against *Church History* 7.22.7-10 (NPNF, library): "The most of our brethren were unsparing in their exceeding love ... many who cared for the sick and gave strength to others died themselves"; "They deserted those who began to be sick, and fled from their dearest friends ... and left the dead like refuse, unburied." Wording corrected (correction 7). "Around 260": approximate; Eusebius places the letter after the Alexandrian strife that followed Valerian's capture, so the early 260s. Not checked further.

## The Great Persecution

- After 260 came the longest peace the church had known. Checked for the start against Gallienus's rescript to Dionysius and the bishops (*Church History* 7.13, NPNF, library) and McGiffert's note that Gallienus succeeded Valerian in 260. "Longest peace": not checked (textbook).
- Eusebius described large new churches in every city and Christians given provincial governorships. Checked (*Church History* 8.1.2, 8.1.5: "committed to them the government of provinces"; "erected from the foundation large churches in all the cities").
- He described what freedom did to them: laxity and sloth, envy and attacks, leaders quarreling. Checked (8.1.7-8). Corrected from "lax and proud" (correction 8).
- Cyprian had said much the same half a century earlier; both read the following persecution in part as God's discipline. Checked: Cyprian, *On the Lapsed* 5 ("The Lord has desired His family to be proved; and because a long peace had corrupted the discipline ... the heavenly rebuke has aroused our faith"); Eusebius 8.1.7 ("the divine judgment ... gently and moderately harassed the episcopacy").
- On February 23, 303, soldiers tore down the church at Nicomedia within sight of the palace. Checked against Lactantius, *Persecutors* 12 (ANF, library): the festival of Terminus, "the sevens of the kalends of March" (February 23); the church "was within view of the palace"; "the Pretorian Guards ... levelled that very lofty edifice with the ground."
- The next day the first edict ordered churches destroyed, Scriptures burned, and Christians stripped of rank and of the courts' protection. Checked against Lactantius, *Persecutors* 13 ("Next day an edict was published, depriving the Christians of all honours and dignities ... every suit at law should be received against them; while ... they were debarred from being plaintiffs") and Eusebius, *Church History* 8.2.4 ("commanding that the churches be leveled to the ground and the Scriptures be destroyed by fire, and ordering that those who held places of honor be degraded"). Eusebius dates the edict's publication in Palestine to March; the essay follows Lactantius for Nicomedia.
- Later edicts jailed the clergy. Checked (*Church History* 8.2.5: "all the rulers of the churches in every place be first thrown into prison").
- In 304 everyone was again ordered to sacrifice. Checked against Eusebius, *Martyrs in Palestine* 3.1 (Cureton's translation, library): in "the second year of the persecution" came edicts that "compelled all persons equally" to sacrifice. The year 304 follows from the 303 start; not separately checked.
- In Gaul and Britain, Constantius, Constantine's father, did little. Checked for Constantius's conduct against Lactantius, *Persecutors* 15 ("permitted the demolition of churches ... but he preserved entire that true temple of God, which is the human body"). Region corrected (correction 9); his rule of Gaul and Britain not checked (textbook).
- In the East, under Galerius and then Maximinus Daia, it went on for most of a decade. Not checked (textbook, 303 to 313). Lactantius names both.
- On April 30, 311, Galerius, dying of a horrible illness, issued an edict letting Christians exist and meet, and asked them to pray to their God for his safety and the state's; he was dead within days. Checked against Lactantius, *Persecutors* 33-35 (ANF, library): the edict "was promulgated at Nicomedia on the day preceding the kalends of May" (April 30); "it will be the duty of the Christians ... to pray to their God for our welfare, and for that of the public"; "In a few days after he was consumed by the horrible disease."
- 1 Timothy 2:1-2, "First of all, then, I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified in every way." Matched word for word, with case, against the ESV copy.
- The next year an emperor in the West marched on Rome under a sign he believed the Christian God had given him. Not checked (textbook: Constantine, 312).
- Eusebius's teacher Pamphilus was executed in the persecution. Checked against McGiffert's note in *Church History* book 7 (library: "Pamphilus, a presbyter of Caesarea, was Eusebius' teacher and most intimate friend") and *Church History* 8.13.6 ("Pamphilus, a presbyter ... among the men of our time most admirable," listed among the martyrs).

Checked: the *Life of Constantine* is in the library; the *Oration in Praise of Constantine* (NPNF, library) says the emperor "frames his earthly government according to the pattern of that Divine original, feeling strength in its conformity to the monarchy of God."
