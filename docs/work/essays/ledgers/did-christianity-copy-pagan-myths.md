## Corrections made in the essay (verifier, 2026-10-27)

1. Standfirst: "copied from Horus, Mithras and other dying-and-rising gods" became "copied from Horus, Mithras and other pagan gods." Reason: the essay itself shows that no ancient source gives Mithras a death (see Mithras below), so calling him a dying-and-rising god in the essay's own voice contradicted its argument.
2. Celsus: "he runs through a list: Orpheus, Heracles, Theseus, Pythagoras, and an Egyptian king" became "a list that includes ...". Reason: Celsus's list in *Against Celsus* 2.55 (WDB) also names Zamolxis and Protesilaus; the colon presented a selection as the whole list.
3. Plutarch: "there is no star" became "there is no star in the east." Reason: Plutarch's treatise does mention a star, the Dog-star, which the Egyptians call the star of Isis (ch. 21 and 38, Babbitt, Perseus). No star announces a birth in his story, which is the film's claim; the new wording says only that.
4. Mithras: "he and Tertullian mention a sacred meal and a mark on the forehead" became "Both he and Tertullian mention bread in the cult's rites, and Tertullian adds a mark on the forehead." Reason: Justin mentions bread and a cup of water at Mithraic initiations (First Apology 66, WDB) but not a mark on the forehead; Tertullian mentions the mark and "the oblation of bread" (*Prescription* 40, WDB). Neither uses the words "sacred meal."
5. Smith: "Some of Frazer's gods die and don't come back, like Osiris" became "don't come back to the living, like Osiris." Reason: Plutarch, quoted earlier in the essay, has interpreters who say Osiris "comes to life and reappears" (ch. 65) and speaks of his "revivification and regenesis" (ch. 35, Babbitt, Perseus). What Plutarch's priests teach is that Osiris rules the dead (ch. 78), which is the point Smith is paraphrased as making.
6. Overstatements: "Tertullian knew that initiates of Isis and Mithras were washed in water, the followers of Mithras shared a sacred meal, and the people of Byblos said Adonis lived again" became "Tertullian knew that initiates of Isis and Mithras were washed in water and that the followers of Mithras offered bread. The people of Byblos said Adonis lived again." Reason: the old sentence made Tertullian the witness for the Adonis rite at Byblos, which comes from *The Syrian Goddess*; and Tertullian's word is "oblation of bread," not a shared meal.

## Celsus and Origen

- Celsus, a pagan philosopher, wrote against the Christians late in the second century. Verified. Ayer (Gutenberg, §18): Celsus's *True Word* was "written about 178" and "represents the best philosophical criticism of Christianity of the latter part of the second century." Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.8 (WDB), places him "in that of Adrian, and later."
- His Jewish spokesman asks why Jesus' rising should be treated differently and lists Zamolxis, Pythagoras, Rhampsinitus "in Egypt" (who "played at dice with Demeter in Hades"), Orpheus, Protesilaus, Hercules and Theseus. Verified, WDB, *Against Celsus* 2.55. The essay says the list "includes" Orpheus, Heracles, Theseus, Pythagoras and the Egyptian (correction 2).
- Rhampsinitus was an Egyptian king. Verified. Perseus, Herodotus 2.122 (Godley): "later this king went down alive to what the Greeks call Hades and there played dice with Demeter."
- Quotation: "the statements of others not only to be myths, but to have the appearance of such." Verified, WDB, *Against Celsus* 2.55: "Or do you imagine the statements of others not only to be myths, but to have the appearance of such, while you have discovered a becoming and credible termination to your drama ...". The essay's "while their own story was credible" is paraphrase of the rest of the sentence.
- We have Celsus's words because Origen quoted them to answer them. Verified. Ayer (§18): the work "is lost, but it has been so incorporated in the elaborate reply of Origen that it can be reconstructed without much difficulty." Origen quotes him throughout Book 2 (WDB).
- Celsus thought the Christian story a myth like the others, and his complaint shows the Christians insisted otherwise. Verified as inference, WDB, *Against Celsus* 2.55.

## Christmas, Gregory, Bede

- The New Testament gives no date for Jesus' birth. Verified. Frazer, third edition vol. 5 (Gutenberg 43605): "The Gospels say nothing as to the day of Christ's birth, and accordingly the early Church did not celebrate it." Miles (Gutenberg 19098): "No one now imagines that the date is supported by a reliable tradition."
- A Roman calendar compiled in 354 lists Christ's birth in Bethlehem on December 25 and marks the day as the birthday of the Unconquered, a title of the sun; it is the first clear evidence for the date. Verified. Miles: "The first mention of a Nativity feast on December 25 is found in a Roman document known as the Philocalian Calendar, dating from the year 354, but embodying an older document evidently belonging to the year 336." Frazer, vol. 5, notes 914 and 920: "In the calendar of Philocalus the twenty-fifth of December is marked _N. Invicti_, that is, _Natalis Solis Invicti_"; "The earliest mention of the festival of Christmas is in the calendar of Philocalus ... The words are _VIII. kal. jan._, _natus Christus in Betleem Judee_."
- Two explanations argued over: the church chose the date to answer a festival of the sun, or it followed a reckoning that Jesus was conceived on March 25. Verified. Miles, ch. II and its note 7: the sun-festival reason "is the explanation adopted by most scholars"; "Duchesne suggests as an explanation of the choice of December 25 the fact that a tradition fixed the Passion of Christ on March 25. The same date, he thinks, would have been assigned to His Conception ... and the Birth would come naturally nine months after the Conception." That historians "still" argue is the essay's characterization; Miles shows the question open in 1912.
- Augustine passes on that Jesus was conceived on March 25 and born on December 25. Verified, WDB, *On the Trinity* 4.5.9: "He is believed to have been conceived on the 25th of March, upon which day also He suffered ... But He was born, according to tradition, upon December the 25th." Paraphrase in the essay, no quotation marks.
- In 601 Pope Gregory the Great wrote to the abbot Mellitus, who was on his way to the mission in England. Verified, WDB, *Register of Epistles* 11.76, "Gregory to Mellitus, Abbot in France": dated "the 19th year of the empire of our most pious lord Mauricius Tiberius ... Indiction 4," and the NPNF editor's notes (63 and 45) say it was sent after Mellitus left Rome with the new missionaries, later than letters dated 22 June 601. Also in Bede's Latin copy, Latin Library, *Ecclesiastical History* 1.30 ("Dilectissimo filio Mellito abbati").
- Quotation: "should not be destroyed, but that the idols themselves that are in them should be." Verified, WDB, Register 11.76. Bede 1.30 agrees ("fana idolorum destrui ... minime debeant; sed ipsa, quae in eis sunt, idola destruantur").
- Temples sprinkled with holy water and made churches; the old feasts made into feasts for the martyrs. Verified (paraphrase), WDB, Register 11.76 ("Let blessed water be prepared, and sprinkled in these temples"; feasts "on the anniversaries of the holy martyrs"). Bede's Latin reads "aqua benedicta."
- Quotation: "It is undoubtedly impossible to cut away everything at once from hard hearts." Verified, WDB, Register 11.76. Bede 1.30: "duris mentibus simul omnia abscidere impossibile esse non dubium est."
- More than a century later Bede recorded that the month in which the English kept Easter was named for a goddess Eostre. Verified. Bede's Latin for this passage was not reachable, but Kemble, *The Saxons in England* vol. 1 (1849, Gutenberg 61161), translates it: "Eóstermónað, which is now interpreted by the 'Paschal month,' had its name of old from a goddess of theirs named Eostre, to whom in this month they offered celebrations." (Kemble's note cites *De Natura Rerum* 15; Miles cites the same chapter of month names as *De Temporum Ratione* 15, the usual reference.) Turner, *History of the Anglo-Saxons* (Gutenberg 45366): "April they named Easter-month, after their goddess, Eostre." The interval: in *Ecclesiastical History* 5.23-24 (Latin Library) Bede dates his account "anno DCCXXXI," says he wrote his books from his ordination as priest at thirty to his fifty-ninth year, and lists among them "de temporibus librum I maiorem," so all his writing falls after about 702, more than a century after 601.
- The Christmas tree and the yule log came out of the folk customs of Europe. Verified as corrected (correction 8). Miles, ch. XI: "as most people know, it is a German creation, and even in Germany it attained its present immense popularity only in the nineteenth century"; ch. ## Frazer and the History of Religions School

- James George Frazer published the first edition of *The Golden Bough* in 1890 and larger editions later. Verified. Gutenberg 41082: the two-volume first edition, with Frazer's preface signed "Trinity College, Cambridge, 8th March 1890"; the third edition runs to twelve volumes (Gutenberg 43605 is "Third Edition Vol. 5 of 12"). "Scottish": verified by Search (second closing run): Britannica, EBSCO and New World Encyclopedia summaries give his birth in Glasgow on 1 January 1854 and call him a Scottish social anthropologist.
- Frazer grouped Adonis, Attis and Osiris as gods who died and rose with the crops. Verified. First edition, ch. III §4 (Gutenberg 41082): "it is in Egypt and Western Asia that the death and resurrection of vegetation appear to have been most widely celebrated ... Under the names of Osiris, Adonis, Thammuz, Attis, and Dionysus, the Egyptians, Syrians, Babylonians, Phrygians, and Greeks represented the decay and revival of vegetation." Sections 4 to 6 of that chapter are "Adonis," "Attis," "Osiris."
- For a generation the idea had standing in the universities; German scholars of the History of Religions School argued Paul's baptism language drew on the mystery cults. Verified in substance by Search (second closing run), secondhand. Ehrman, summarized from an interview (toughquestionsanswered.org), says it was "widely thought" that dying and rising gods were a staple of pagan religion and traces the idea to Frazer; a BMCR review (2002.09.07) says the idea "was embraced by numerous scholars." On Romans 6, a Dutch study in the results summarizes the religionsgeschichtlich assumption that Paul took over a baptism tradition in which the initiate shares the fate of the dying and risen deity, an *Expositor* article notes that Reitzenstein favoured such a reading, and a *New Testament Studies* abstract calls the derivation from the sacramental theology of the mysteries "widely held." No names are given in the essay.
- Romans 6:4 "buried therefore with him by baptism into death." Verified. ESV copy (rerun) and ESV search (second closing run), matched; BSB confirms the sense.
- Specialists later went back to the texts god by god and Frazer's family came apart; the popular lists did not follow. Verified in substance by Search (second closing run), secondhand: the BMCR review says the evidence for the category "progressively eroded" and that J.Z. Smith and Mark S. Smith declared the dying and rising god "a fantasy, the product of uncritical comparison"; a vridar.org summary says even Mettinger grants that Frazer's original conception can hardly be defended. For the popular lists, Ehrman's blog says mythicists accept Graves's 1875 claims without checking them. Characterization, kept.

## Mithras

- The god Mithra is very old in Persia. Verified in substance. Xenophon, *Economics* 4.24 (Perseus, Greek and Marchant's English): the Persian prince Cyrus swears "ὄμνυμί σοι τὸν Μίθρην" (Marchant renders it "I swear by the Sun-god"). Herodotus 1.131 (Godley) gives "Mitra" as a Persian divine name, though he takes it for a goddess. That Xenophon wrote in the fourth century before Christ: verified by Search (third closing run): the summary dates his life from the 430s to the 350s BC and the *Oeconomicus* late in it, "perhaps after 362 BC"; it also notes that Herodotus names "Mitra" in the fifth century, and that the name of the god appears in a Hittite-Mitanni treaty of about 1400 BC (Wikipedia, "Mithras (name)" and "Mithraism"; Roger Pearse's collection of ancient passages on Mithras, mithras.tertullian.org).
- Cilician pirates whom Pompey fought in the 60s before Christ practiced "certain secret rites, among which those of Mithras continue to the present time." Verified, Perseus, Plutarch, *Pompey* 24 (Perrin): the pirates "celebrated there certain secret rites, among which those of Mithras continue to the present time, having been first instituted by them." The date is Perrin's note on the law that gave Pompey the command (ch. 25): "In 67 B.C., Pompey being then thirty-nine years old."
- The Roman mysteries are known mostly from shrines built from the late first century on, cave-like halls with the bull-killing scene; the followers left almost no writing. Verified by Search (second closing run), secondhand: the summary of Wikipedia's "Tauroctony" and "Mithraism" articles says the oldest bull-killing relief (CIMRM 593/594, dedicated by Alcimus, steward of T. Claudius Livianus) and the five other earliest Mithraic monuments date to around AD 100, that the cult "first became evident in Rome towards the late 1st century AD," and that "No written narratives or theology from the religion survive." A BMCR review (2019.03.05) dates the Caesarea mithraeum later than once thought, which does not touch the essay's claim. Ayer (§35, Gutenberg), from the closing run, mentions numerous sanctuaries at army posts.
- Statius, writing around AD 90, pictured Mithras twisting the horns beneath the rocks of a Persian cave. Verified for the text, Perseus Latin, *Thebaid* 1.719-720: "seu Persei sub rupibus antri / indignata sequi torquentem cornua Mithram." The date is supported by the poem itself (Latin Library): 1.18-20 alludes to Domitian's campaigns on the Rhine and the Danube and against the Dacians, 12.811 calls the *Thebaid* the work of twelve years, and 12.814 says Caesar already knows it. Search (second closing run): Wikipedia's summary dates the *Thebaid*'s publication to about 92, a Clarendon listing gives its composition as about AD 80 to 92, and a *Florentia* article dates Domitian's triumph over the Germans and Dacians, the last event the poem mentions, to 89.
- No surviving source tells a story of Mithras dying; in the monuments he is the one who kills. Supported by Search, secondhand: a result summary on the film's claims (results included aomin.org and effectualgrace.com) quotes the Mithras scholar Richard Gordon (the summary writes "Gordon Richard") that there "Is no death of Mithras." The ancient sources read for this ledger (Justin, Tertullian, Plutarch, Statius) give him no death. Kept. Search (second closing run): the Wikipedia summary above adds that no written narrative from the religion survives.
- The Roman mysteries grew up in the same centuries as the church; influence either way is hard to prove. Judgment, not a factual claim.

## Overstatements and Protestant polemic

- Martin Hengel, a German New Testament scholar, showed in the 1960s how far Greek language and culture had spread in the Jewish homeland.
