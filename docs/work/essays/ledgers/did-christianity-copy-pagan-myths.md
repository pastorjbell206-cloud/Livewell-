## Zeitgeist and Kersey Graves (opening)

- *Zeitgeist* (2007), a documentary by Peter Joseph, released online in 2007. Unverified (memory).
- The film's first section claimed that Horus was born of a virgin on December 25, was announced by a star in the east, was visited by three kings, taught at twelve, was baptized at thirty, had twelve disciples, worked miracles, was crucified, was dead three days and rose. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Unverified (memory of the film's Part I; the film says "Isis-Meri" and names "Anup" as the baptizer, which the essay leaves out).
- The film said much the same of Attis, Krishna, Dionysus and Mithras, and concluded that the Jesus story is a solar myth. Unverified (memory).
- Kersey Graves, an American freethinker, published *The World's Sixteen Crucified Saviors* in 1875. Unverified (memory; full title adds "or, Christianity Before Christ").
- Graves gave readers almost nothing they could check against an ancient text. Unverified (characterization; it is the standard criticism of the book).
- Richard Carrier, a historian who argues Jesus probably never existed, warned readers not to rely on Graves's book. Unverified (memory of Carrier's review "Kersey Graves and The World's Sixteen Crucified Saviors," Secular Web, about 2003). Paraphrase only.
- "a century and a half" from 1875 to 2026. Arithmetic.

## Celsus and Origen

- Celsus, a pagan philosopher, wrote against the Christians late in the second century. Unverified (standard date about 170 to 180).
- His Jewish spokesman lists Zamolxis, Pythagoras, Rhampsinitus (who "played at dice with Demeter in Hades"), Orpheus, Protesilaus, Hercules and Theseus. The essay names Orpheus, Heracles, Theseus, Pythagoras and "an Egyptian king said to have played dice with Demeter in the underworld." Verified locally: Origen, *Against Celsus* 2.55 (ANF, HCF "Origen of Alexandria/Against Celsus/Book 2").
- Quotation: "the statements of others not only to be myths, but to have the appearance of such." Verified locally, *Against Celsus* 2.55 (ANF): "Or do you imagine the statements of others not only to be myths, but to have the appearance of such, while you have discovered a becoming and credible termination to your drama...". (Did-the-resurrection-happen already quotes the next line of this passage, "whether any one who was really dead ever rose with a veritable body"; this essay avoids it.)
- We have Celsus's words because Origen quoted them to answer them. General history, unverified (consistent with the pilot the-early-church-under-rome).
- Celsus thought the Christian story a myth like the others. Inference from *Against Celsus* 2.55, verified locally.

## Justin Martyr

- Justin, a Christian philosopher teaching in Rome, wrote an appeal (the First Apology) to the emperor around the middle of the second century. Unverified (standard date about 150 to 155; addressed to Antoninus Pius).
- Quotation, First Apology 21: "was crucified and died, and rose again, and ascended into heaven" and "we propound nothing different from what you believe regarding those whom you esteem sons of Jupiter." Verified locally (ANF, HCF "Justin Martyr/The First Apology", ch. 21; one continuous sentence in the original, split by "Justin told him").
- Bacchus "after he had been torn limb from limb." Verified locally, First Apology 21 (ANF).
- In the mysteries of Mithras bread and a cup of water were set out at initiations, in imitation of the Supper. Verified locally, First Apology 66 (ANF): "Which the wicked devils have imitated in the mysteries of Mithras... bread and a cup of water are placed with certain incantations in the mystic rites of one who is being initiated." The essay says "in imitation of," which is Justin's claim (he blames the devils).
- Bacchus was torn to pieces and "ascended into heaven." Verified locally, First Apology 54 (ANF): "having been torn in pieces, he ascended into heaven."
- Justin compared the virgin birth with Perseus, son of Danae. Verified locally, First Apology 22 ("if we even affirm that He was born of a virgin, accept this in common with what you accept of Perseus") and 21 ("Perseus, son of Danae").
- Quotation: "those many women whom he had violated." Verified locally, First Apology 21 (ANF): "he came in to Ganymede and those many women whom he had violated."
- Demons heard the prophets and put forward sons of Jupiter so Christ's story would seem "mere marvellous tales, like the things which were said by the poets." Verified locally, First Apology 54 (ANF).
- Mithras "begotten of a rock," initiates in a cave. Verified locally, *Dialogue with Trypho* 70 (ANF): "those who record the mysteries of Mithras say that he was begotten of a rock, and call the place where those who believe in him are initiated a cave."
- Heracles on his own pyre. Justin, First Apology 21: "Hercules, when he had committed himself to the flames to escape his toils." Verified locally. That "the poets said he rose from it to the gods" is general Greek myth (the apotheosis of Heracles), unverified.

## Tertullian

- About half a century after Justin; Tertullian of Carthage. Unverified date (The Prescription Against Heretics is usually dated about 200).
- "if my memory still serves me," Mithras marks his soldiers on the forehead, celebrates the oblation of bread, and "introduces an image of a resurrection." Verified locally, *Prescription Against Heretics* 40 (ANF, HCF): "if my memory still serves me, Mithra there... sets his marks on the foreheads of his soldiers; celebrates also the oblation of bread, and introduces an image of a resurrection."
- The devil imitates the sacraments. Verified locally, *Prescription* 40 (paraphrase of "vies even with the essential portions of the sacraments of God"; chapter heading "Satan Imitated and Distorted the Divine Institutions").
- Initiates of Isis and Mithras were washed in water. Verified locally, *On Baptism* 5 (ANF): "washing is the channel through which they are initiated into some sacred rites-of some notorious Isis or Mithras."
- Tertullian dated the passion to March 25. Not stated in the essay; checked as background: *An Answer to the Jews* 8 (ANF) "on the eighth day before the calends of April."

## Christmas, Gregory, Bede

- The New Testament gives no date for Jesus' birth. General, unverified (uncontroversial).
- A Roman calendar compiled in 354 (the Chronograph or Philocalian calendar) lists Christ's birth in Bethlehem on December 25 (Depositio Martyrum: "VIII kal. Ian. natus Christus in Betleem Iudeae") and marks December 25 as "N. INVICTI," the birthday of the Unconquered. Unverified (memory). The essay calls it "the first clear evidence for December 25," which is the usual scholarly statement; unverified.
- "the Unconquered, a title of the sun." Unverified (Sol Invictus; memory).
- Two theories: the church chose the date to answer a sun festival; or it came from reckoning that Jesus was conceived on March 25. Both are standard, unverified as a summary of the debate.
- Augustine passes on that Jesus was conceived on March 25 (and born December 25). Verified locally, *On the Trinity* 4.5.9 (NPNF, HCF): "He is believed to have been conceived on the 25th of March, upon which day also He suffered... But He was born, according to tradition, upon December the 25th." Paraphrased in the essay, no quotation marks.
- In 601 Pope Gregory the Great wrote to the abbot Mellitus, on his way to the English mission. Verified locally: Gregory, *Register of Epistles* 11.76 "To Mellitus, Abbot" (NPNF, HCF "Gregory the Dialogist/Register of Epistles/Book 11"); the editor's notes date the batch to June 601 (Indiction 4). Bede copies it as *Ecclesiastical History* 1.30 (unverified locally).
- Quotation: "should not be destroyed, but that the idols themselves that are in them should be." Verified locally, Register 11.76 (NPNF).
- Temples to be sprinkled with holy water and made churches; sacrificial feasts turned into feasts for the martyrs. Verified locally, Register 11.76 (paraphrase).
- Quotation: "It is undoubtedly impossible to cut away everything at once from hard hearts." Verified locally, Register 11.76 (NPNF).
- Bede, more than a century later, recorded that the month the English kept Easter (Eosturmonath) had been named for a goddess Eostre, the source of our word Easter. Unverified (memory of Bede, *De temporum ratione* 15, AD 725). The derivation of "Easter" from Old English Eastre is standard; unverified.
- The evergreen and the yule log came from northern Europe's winter customs. General, unverified.

## Frazer and the History of Religions School

- James George Frazer, Scottish, published the first edition of *The Golden Bough* in 1890 and larger editions later (third edition 1906 to 1915, with the volumes *Adonis, Attis, Osiris*). Unverified (memory).
- Frazer grouped Adonis, Attis and Osiris as gods who died and rose with the crops. Unverified (memory).
- German scholars of the History of Religions School (Religionsgeschichtliche Schule; Reitzenstein, Bousset) argued Paul's baptism language drew on the mystery cults. Unverified (memory). No names given in the essay.
- Romans 6:4 "buried therefore with him by baptism into death." Unverified (memory, ESV).

## Plutarch, Isis and Osiris

All checked against Perseus tlg0007.tlg089: the Babbitt translation (Loeb, 1936, perseus-eng3) for every quotation, with the Goodwin/Baxter translation (1874, perseus-eng4) as a cross-check.

- Plutarch was a Greek writer and a priest at Delphi; he wrote *Isis and Osiris* around the beginning of the second century. Unverified (standard biography; the treatise is addressed to Clea, a priestess at Delphi).
- Typhon is the Greek name for Seth. Verified locally, ch. 41 and 49 ("the Egyptians regularly call Typhon Seth").
- Typhon is Osiris's brother. Verified locally, ch. 12 (children of Rhea: Osiris, Arueris, Typhon, Isis, Nephthys).
- Typhon tricks Osiris into lying down in a chest; the plotters slam down the lid; it is sent down the river to the sea. Verified locally, ch. 13.
- Isis is Osiris's sister and wife. Verified locally, ch. 12 and 27 ("the sister and wife of Osiris").
- Isis finds the chest at Byblos. Verified locally, ch. 15-16.
- Typhon, hunting by moonlight, finds the body and divides it into fourteen parts; Isis searches the swamps in a papyrus boat. Verified locally, ch. 18.
- Horus was already being reared at Buto. Verified locally, ch. 18 ("Isis proceeded to her son Horus, who was being reared in Buto").
- Quotation: "Osiris consorted with Isis after his death," and Harpocrates is "untimely born and weak in his lower limbs" (only the first phrase is quoted in the final essay). Verified locally, ch. 19 (Babbitt).
- No virgin, star, twelve, cross or third day in Plutarch's story. Verified locally by reading and by searching the Babbitt text: no "virgin," "twelve," "crucif" or "three days"; "third day" appears only for Typhon's birth among the epagomenal days (ch. 12); "cross" appears only as priests crossing to Philae.
- Quotation: "this god Osiris is the ruler and king of the dead." Verified locally, ch. 78 (Babbitt).
- Quotations: "is being buried at the time when the grain is sown," "comes to life and reappears when plants begin to sprout," Harpocrates born "about the time of the winter solstice." Verified locally, ch. 65 (Babbitt). Plutarch attributes these to the interpreters, not to himself.
- Quotation: "numerous and tiresome." Verified locally, ch. 65 (Babbitt): "the numerous and tiresome people... who say that Osiris is being buried..."
- Plutarch a devout pagan. Characterization; consistent with ch. 1-2 and 78. Unverified as stated.

## Mithras

- The god Mithra is very old in Persia. General, unverified (Avestan Mithra; memory).
- Cilician pirates whom Pompey fought in the 60s BC: "certain secret rites, among which those of Mithras continue to the present time." Verified locally, Plutarch, *Pompey* 24 (Perrin translation, Perseus tlg0007.tlg045 eng2). The date of Pompey's campaign (67 BC) is unverified (memory).
- The Roman mysteries of Mithras are known mostly from shrines built from the late first century onward, small cave-like halls with the bull-killing scene (tauroctony). Unverified (standard; Clauss, Beck).
- Followers left almost no writing behind. Unverified (standard).
- Statius, writing around AD 90, pictured Mithras twisting the horns beneath the rocks of a Persian cave. Verified locally for the text: *Thebaid* 1.719-720, "seu Persei sub rupibus antri / indignata sequi torquentem cornua Mithram" (Perseus phi1020.phi001, Latin only; translation is mine). The date (Thebaid published about AD 91-92) is unverified.
- No surviving source tells a story of Mithras dying; in the monuments he is the one who kills. Unverified (the consensus of Mithras scholarship as I know it). Verifier: please confirm or soften.
- The Roman mysteries grew up in the same centuries as the church; influence in either direction is hard to prove. Judgment, unverified.

## Dionysus

- Pausanias, a Greek traveler of the second century. Unverified date (about 150 to 180).
- At Elis, at the festival of the Thyia, priests set three empty pots in a building, the doors are sealed, and the next day the pots are found full of wine; Pausanias was not there for the festival but says the most respected citizens swore to it. Verified locally, Pausanias 6.26.1-2 (W.H.S. Jones translation, Perseus tlg0525.tlg001 eng2). The essay says "at his festival" (Dionysus) and "overnight," both in the text.
- Some scholars think John's Cana story was told with Dionysus in mind; others hear the prophets. Unverified (general state of the debate; no names given).
- Amos 9:13 "the mountains shall drip sweet wine." Unverified (memory, ESV).
- Euripides' *Bacchae*, staged at the end of the fifth century BC. Unverified date (produced posthumously about 405 BC).
- Dionysus opens the play naming himself son of Zeus and Semele, daughter of Kadmos; Zeus's lightning killed her as she gave birth; her sisters claimed she conceived by a mortal and blamed Zeus. Verified locally, *Bacchae* opening speech and first chorus (Buckley translation, Perseus tlg0006.tlg017 eng2): "whom once Semele, Kadmos' daughter, bore, delivered by a lightning-bearing flame"; "Semele had conceived a child from a mortal father and then ascribed the sin of her bed to Zeus"; "leaving life by the stroke of a thunderbolt." "Princess of Thebes" follows from Kadmos being king of Thebes (general myth, unverified).

## Adonis

- A second-century treatise on the Syrian goddess, traditionally credited to Lucian. Unverified (date and the doubt about authorship are standard).
- At Byblos each year people mourn Adonis, killed by a boar; make offerings to him as to the dead; next day declare he is alive and carry him into the air. Verified locally, *De Dea Syria* 6 (Harmon translation, Loeb 1925, Perseus tlg0062.tlg041 eng2, rendered in mock-Middle English: "thei maken offringes to Adoon, as though he were ded; and than, on the morwe, thei fablen that he is quick, and fecchen him forth in to the eyr"). Paraphrased.
- Written about a century after Paul's letters. Arithmetic on standard dates (Paul in the 50s; the treatise in the mid or late second century). Unverified.

## Jonathan Z. Smith

- Historian of religion at the University of Chicago. Unverified (memory).
- His work had nothing to do with defending Christianity. Characterization, unverified.
- 1987 encyclopedia article on dying and rising gods (*The Encyclopedia of Religion*, ed. Mircea Eliade, "Dying and Rising Gods"). Unverified (memory).
- He argued the category is largely a modern construction resting on imaginative reconstruction and very late or ambiguous texts; the gods sort into those who die and do not return and those who disappear and return without dying. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Unverified (memory; the well-known line is "a misnomer based on imaginative reconstructions and exceedingly late or highly ambiguous texts," which the essay does not quote).
- Much evidence for a celebrated rising comes from writers after Christianity began (Lucian's included). Unverified as Smith's point; the Lucian date is in the Adonis section.
- In a later book (*Drudgery Divine*, 1990) Smith argued that comparison of early Christianity with the pagan cults grew up largely inside Protestant anti-Catholic polemic. Unverified (memory). Title and year not given in the essay.

## Tryggve Mettinger

- Swedish Old Testament scholar; in 2001 published a study (*The Riddle of Resurrection: "Dying and Rising Gods" in the Ancient Near East*) arguing that Dumuzi and Baal (and Melqart) were understood to die and return, tied to the seasons; he thought Smith went too far. Unverified (memory).
- He found no evidence that the Christian proclamation of Jesus' death and resurrection was built from those myths. Unverified (memory of his conclusion; paraphrase, no quotation marks).

## Bart Ehrman

- Agnostic New Testament scholar; *Did Jesus Exist?* (2012) argued against the idea that Jesus was invented. Unverified here (the-historical-jesus ledger carries the same claim).
- In it he took up the pagan parallels; found many popular claims not locatable in ancient sources and the real parallels weaker than they seem; read the resurrection belief as arising from Jewish apocalyptic hope; does not believe Jesus rose. Paraphrase. Unverified (memory). Verifier: this characterization of the book's pagan-parallels chapter is the riskiest unverified paragraph; soften or cut if it does not match.

## Overstatements and Protestant polemic

- Martin Hengel, German historian of ancient Judaism, showed in the 1960s how far Greek language and culture had spread in the Jewish homeland. Unverified (memory: *Judentum und Hellenismus*, 1969; English 1974).
- Jews went to their deaths rather than sacrifice to a Greek god. General (the Maccabean martyrs, 2 Maccabees 6-7). Unverified.
- Alexander Hislop, a Scottish minister, first published *The Two Babylons* in 1853 (expanded 1858), arguing the Roman Catholic Church was Babylonian religion in Christian dress. Unverified (memory; Free Church of Scotland).
- Hislop claimed Easter was the goddess Astarte, called Ishtar by the Assyrians. Unverified (memory of *The Two Babylons*, ch. 3). Paraphrase only.
- The book is still in print. Unverified.
- Every spring its Easter claim circulates on social media. General observation, unverified.
- No connection between English "Easter" and "Ishtar." Unverified (standard etymology; the English word is Germanic, the name Akkadian).
- The link was popularized by a Protestant attack on Rome. Softened from "invented" because I cannot confirm Hislop was first. Unverified.
- Ralph Woodrow, an evangelical writer, popularized Hislop in the 1960s in *Babylon Mystery Religion* (1966); years later he re-examined Hislop's sources, withdrew the book and wrote another explaining why (*The Babylon Connection?*, 1997). Unverified (memory). Dates of the retraction left out of the essay.

## C.S. Lewis

- Lewis came to Christian faith partly through conversations with friends about myth (with J.R.R. Tolkien and Hugo Dyson, September 1931). Unverified (memory). Names and date not given in the essay.
- He thought the resemblances were what one would expect if human imagination had been reaching for something real, and called the story of Christ a true myth that works like the others except that it really happened. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Unverified (memory of his letter to Arthur Greeves, October 1931, and the essay "Myth Became Fact," 1944).
- Lewis took more of Frazer for granted than specialists now would. Judgment (Lewis wrote of "the Dying God"). Unverified.
