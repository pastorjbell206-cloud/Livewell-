## The Treaty of Tripoli

- In the 1780s and 1790s Barbary corsairs seized American ships and held crews for ransom, and the United States, without a navy, bought peace with treaties and tribute. Library-checked (Gutenberg #22169, Lane-Poole): from 1785 Algiers preyed on American commerce, which "carried not an ounce of shot to defend it"; the sufferings of the captives moved Congress to order a fleet; "immunity was purchased by the payment of a heavy tribute," and Tunis and Tripoli then demanded the same.
- The treaty was negotiated under Washington and signed at Tripoli on November 4, 1796, with an English translation credited to Joel Barlow, the American consul in Algiers. Library-checked (Gutenberg #10894, Richardson): Adams's message to the Senate of May 26, 1797 lays before it a treaty "concluded at Tripoli on the 4th day of November, 1796." Search-checked (2026-10-27): Barlow's translation is the English text submitted to the Senate, and he is often credited with the provision. PARTLY OPEN: Barlow's post as consul in Algiers was not seen.
- Senate approval June 7, 1797; Adams signed June 10. As above: June 10 search-checked; June 7 STILL OPEN (conflicting search results).
- Block quotation of Article 11, from "As the Government of the United States" to "the harmony existing between the two countries." Search-checked (2026-10-27): the opening clause and the closing clause ("it is declared by the parties that no pretext arising from religious opinions shall ever produce an interruption of the harmony existing between the two countries") in Mark Foster's transcription and au.org; "no character of enmity against the laws, religion, or tranquility, of Mussulmen" in the Jewish Currents and Prospect transcriptions; "never entered into any war or act of hostility against any Mahometan nation" reported by the search as the 1796 text (Religion Dispatches, Wikisource among the results), with Foster's transcription spelling "Mahometan." Printings vary in small ways ("Mahometan" or "Mehomitan," "Christian religion" or "Christian Religion," "tranquility" or "tranquillity"); the essay's wording matches the Foster and Jewish Currents transcriptions.
- A treaty is a diplomatic instrument; the article assured a Muslim ruler of no religious quarrel. Interpretation.
- Hunter Miller published the treaty in 1931 in the State Department's collection; a 1930 translation of the Arabic showed no Article 11, and in its place a letter from the Dey of Algiers to the Pasha of Tripoli. PARTLY OPEN. Search-checked (2026-10-27: Wikipedia; Miami University's Empire and American Religion document): the Arabic text has no Article 11 and in its place is a letter from the Dey of Algiers to the Pasha of Tripoli. Miller, 1931 and the 1930 translation were not seen (the verifier recalls *Treaties and Other International Acts of the United States of America*, vol. 2, with the C. Snouck Hurgronje translation).
- The 1805 treaty, after four years of war, kept the promise of no religious hostility and dropped the sentence about the Christian religion. Search-checked (2026-10-27: the 1805 treaty's Article 14 rewords the clause, keeping the promise and adding "except in the defence of their just rights to freely navigate the high seas"; the "not founded on the Christian religion" provision was not repeated in later treaties). Library-checked for the war: Johnson (Gutenberg #3004) reports that by Jefferson's first annual message (1801) Tripoli had begun depredations on American commerce and a squadron had been sent, and Lane-Poole (Gutenberg #22169) has the treaty signed by the Pasha in June 1805.
- The Constitution neither established Christianity nor required it of officeholders. Library-checked (Gutenberg #5, Article VI; Gutenberg #2, First Amendment).
