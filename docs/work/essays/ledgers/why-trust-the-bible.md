# Claims ledger: why-trust-the-bible

Essay: `docs/work/essays/why-trust-the-bible.md` ("Can You Trust the Bible?"). Writer's ledger, 2026-10-26. "Search-checked" means the claim matched WebSearch result summaries run on 2026-10-26 (sources named); no primary page was opened. "Unverified" means written from memory and not yet checked. Scripture marked search-checked matched search results quoting esv.org, biblia.com or biblestudytools.com ESV pages on 2026-10-26; the full text of longer passages was matched only in part where noted.

## Bart Ehrman and Mark 2:26 (opening)

- Ehrman studied at Moody Bible Institute (diploma 1976) and Wheaton College (BA 1978), then Princeton Theological Seminary (MDiv 1981, PhD 1985 under Bruce Metzger). Search-checked (Wikipedia, "Bart D. Ehrman"). The essay names the schools only, no years.
- As a seminary student at Princeton he wrote a paper on Mark 2:26 reconciling it with 1 Samuel 21 (Ahimelech vs Abiathar). Search-checked (Bible Archaeology Review "Ehrman on Abiathar"; UNC Endeavors spring 2006; Equip/Hanegraaff; summaries of *Misquoting Jesus*).
- He believed, as he had been taught, that the Bible was without error in every detail. Search-checked in substance (Bible Archaeology Review: the episode began the unraveling of his belief in inerrancy). Wording is a paraphrase.
- Professor Cullen Story wrote one line at the end of the paper: "Maybe Mark just made a mistake." Search-checked (UNC Endeavors; Bible Archaeology Review; "a one-line note"/"single comment"). Verbatim wording relies on secondary quotations of *Misquoting Jesus*; verify against the book.
- Quotation: "Once I made that admission, the floodgates opened." Search-checked as quoted in secondary sources (Monergism, SuperSummary, UNC Endeavors); verify against *Misquoting Jesus*.
- *Misquoting Jesus* published 2005 (HarperCollins) and a surprise bestseller (New York Times list). Search-checked (Wikipedia "Misquoting Jesus"; Challies review calls it a surprise bestseller).
- He became one of the best-known New Testament scholars in the country, and eventually an agnostic. Characterization; agnosticism search-checked (Christianity Today 2008 on *God's Problem*).
- What finally ended his faith was suffering, not the manuscripts. Search-checked (Christianity Today, Feb 2008, "Painful Subject": suffering "put me over the top"; America magazine review of *God's Problem*, 2008). The essay paraphrases; no quotation.
- Many of his readers closed the book convinced the Bible couldn't be trusted. General characterization; unverified.
- *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* argued scribes sometimes altered passages to make them say more plainly what they believed about Christ. Unverified (book is Oxford University Press, 1993; year not given in essay).

## Scripture in the opening

- Mark 2:26, quoted phrase "in the time of Abiathar the high priest"; paraphrase that David "entered the house of God and ate the bread of the Presence." Search-checked (esv.org and Bible Hub ESV pages for Mark 2:26).
- 1 Samuel 21 names Ahimelech as the priest who gave David the bread; Abiathar was Ahimelech's son (1 Samuel 22:20). Search-checked for 1 Samuel 21:1 / Ahimelech (search summary); 1 Samuel 22:20 relationship unverified.

## Manuscripts

- Quotation (Ehrman): "there are more variations among our manuscripts than there are words in the New Testament." Search-checked (stonethepreacher.com reproduction of the passage; STR; Christian Post). Verify verbatim against *Misquoting Jesus*.
- More than five thousand Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, whole or in part. Search-checked (Daniel Wallace 2013 citing INTF: 5,838; museum-digital INTF entry: about 5,800 in 2017).
- Thousands more in early translations such as Latin, Syriac and Coptic. Unverified (commonly cited about 10,000 Latin); stated generally.
- Most variants are spelling, word order, skipped lines, slips of the pen; many don't show in English. Search-checked in substance (Christian Post, STR summaries); general consensus statement.
- The first six books of Tacitus's *Annals* survive in a single manuscript copied in the ninth century (Codex Mediceus I, Florence, Laurentian Plut. 68.1). Search-checked (Wikipedia "Annals (Tacitus)"; tertullian.org Pearse "Tacitus and his manuscripts"; Sotheby's; one source dates it c. 1000, most to the ninth century).
- *Annals* 1–6 are a main source for the reign of Tiberius. Unverified (textbook).
- "some seven hundred years after Tacitus wrote." Unverified arithmetic (Annals written c. AD 115–120; manuscript c. 850).
- Oldest surviving piece of the New Testament: a fragment of John's Gospel (P52, John 18) in Manchester (John Rylands Library), about credit-card size, usually dated to the second century; some papyrologists argue later. Search-checked for date and dispute (Nongbri, HTR 2005 and NTS 2020; vridar summary). Size comparison unverified (about 8.9 x 6 cm).
- Larger papyri survive from the late second and third centuries. Unverified (P46, P66, P75; dates debated).
- Codex Sinaiticus is the oldest complete New Testament, copied in the fourth century. Search-checked (Leipzig University Library; Smithsonian; OrthodoxWiki: c. 330–350).
- The early papyri and the fourth-century codices carry substantially the same text. Unverified general statement.
- Modern Bibles note that some of the earliest manuscripts end Mark at 16:8. Unverified (ESV note reads "Some of the earliest manuscripts do not include 16:9–20"); paraphrased, not quoted.
- John 7:53–8:11 set in brackets with a note that the earliest manuscripts don't include it. Unverified (ESV bracket note); paraphrased, not quoted.
- KJV 1 John 5:7: "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." Unverified verbatim (KJV quoted from memory; search summary confirms the phrase "the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost"). Translation named because the KJV wording is the point.
- The Comma came into the text through Latin copies and appears in only a handful of late Greek manuscripts. Search-checked (Wikipedia "Johannine Comma"; bible-researcher.com summary of Metzger: eight late manuscripts; Latin origin).
- Erasmus left it out of his 1516 Greek New Testament and added it in his third edition of 1522; from there into the King James (via the Textus Receptus). Search-checked (H. J. de Jonge, "Erasmus and the Comma Johanneum"; Wikipedia). The essay does not repeat the disputed "promise" story.
- The fourth-century Greek theologians of the Trinitarian controversies never quoted it. Search-checked (Metzger, *Textual Commentary*, as quoted on bible-researcher.com: "quoted by none of the Greek Fathers, who, had they known it, would most certainly have employed it in the Trinitarian controversies"). Secondary quotation; verify in Metzger pp. 716–718.
- Most modern translations, made by scholars who confess the Trinity, omit it. Unverified general statement (ESV, NIV, CSB omit; NKJV retains).

## Dead Sea Scrolls

- The Leningrad Codex, the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, was copied in 1008. Search-checked (Wikipedia "List of Hebrew Bible manuscripts"; Wikiquote; 1008 CE; some give 1008/1009).
- Beginning in 1947, Bedouin shepherds and later archaeologists brought hundreds of scrolls out of caves near Qumran above the Dead Sea. Unverified (first cave usually dated 1946–47; "hundreds" deliberately general).
- A complete scroll of Isaiah (1QIsa-a) usually dated to the second century BC. Search-checked (Wikipedia "Isaiah Scroll": c. 125 BCE; palaeography 150–100 BCE).
- About a thousand years older than any Hebrew copy of Isaiah known before. Search-checked (Wikipedia: "approximately 1000 years older than the previous oldest Hebrew manuscripts known before the scrolls' discovery").
- Most of its differences from the later (Masoretic) text are spelling and small points of grammar. Search-checked in substance (search summary: "most differences are grammatical"; "many minor differences").
- Some books, Jeremiah among them, circulated in more than one form (Qumran Jeremiah fragments agreeing with the shorter Septuagint form). Unverified (4QJer-b, 4QJer-d; textbook).
- Isaiah 53 is in the scroll. Unverified (the scroll contains all 66 chapters).

## Ehrman and Metzger

- In a question-and-answer appendix to *Misquoting Jesus*, Ehrman said his position did not stand at odds with Metzger's that "essential Christian beliefs are not affected by textual variants in the manuscript tradition of the New Testament." Search-checked (Wikipedia "Misquoting Jesus"; Credo House; STR; summaries describe a dialogue with the editors in the book's appendix). Verify verbatim and which edition carries the appendix.
- Metzger was Ehrman's teacher (PhD supervisor). Search-checked (Wikipedia).
- Metzger was an ordained Presbyterian minister. Search-checked (Christianity Today obituary, Feb 2007; Christian Century: ordained 1939).

## Canon

- The Council of Nicaea (325) argued about whether the Son is fully God and did not draw up a list of books. Unverified (textbook).
- Around 180, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, argued there are four Gospels, no more and no fewer (*Against Heresies* 3.11.8, the four winds). Search-checked (michaeljkruger.com; jdavidstark.com; st-takla.org ANF text). Paraphrased, not quoted.
- Paul's letters were read as Scripture even earlier. Unverified general statement (supported in the essay by 2 Peter 3:16).
- 2 Peter 3:16, quoted phrases "the other Scriptures" and "there are some things in them that are hard to understand." Search-checked (biblestudytools ESV; esv.org).
- Athanasius's Easter (39th Festal) letter of 367 is the first known list naming exactly the 27 books. Search-checked (earlychurchtexts.com; EPFL Graphsearch; jdavidstark.com). "First" is the common scholarly framing.
- Hebrews, James and Revelation were questioned for a long time in some places; the church's own historians preserved the arguments (Eusebius, *Church History* 3.25). Unverified (textbook; Eusebius not named in the essay).
- Later councils mostly put in writing what churches already read. Unverified general statement (Hippo 393, Carthage 397 not named).

## History and archaeology

- Luke 1:1–4 quoted in full. Search-checked in part: opening ("Inasmuch as many have undertaken to compile a narrative") and close ("that you may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught") matched esv.org and biblia.com ESV; middle clauses from memory, verify verbatim.
- Luke 3:1–2: John's preaching dated to the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Pilate governor of Judea, during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas. Paraphrase; unverified against ESV text.
- 1961: the Pilate Stone found at Caesarea Maritima, a limestone block reused in the theater, naming Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judea. Search-checked (madainproject; 4enoch wiki; holylandphotos; June 1961, Italian team under Antonio Frova; reading "[PRAEF]ECTUS IUDA[EA]E"). Caesarea as the seat of the Roman governors: unverified (textbook).
- The Delphi (Gallio) inscription places Gallio's proconsulship of Achaia around AD 51–52, one of the firmest dates in Paul's life (Acts 18:12). Search-checked (Wikipedia "Acts 18"; holylandphotos; Bible Archaeology Report "Gallio"; jw.org Insight).
- Acts 18:12: Paul brought before Gallio in Corinth. Paraphrase; unverified against ESV text.
- 1993: Tel Dan stele fragment found in northern Israel; Old Aramaic, ninth century BC; a foreign (Aramean) king boasts of victory and names the "House of David"; most scholars read it as the earliest extra-biblical mention of David's dynasty. Search-checked (Wikipedia "Tel Dan stele"; New World Encyclopedia; reading disputed by some, hence "as most scholars read it").
- The archaeology of the exodus and the conquest is intensely argued, including among believing scholars. Unverified general statement.

## Paul and the resurrection

- 1 Corinthians written in the mid-50s. Unverified (common dating c. 53–55).
- Paul passes on a summary he says he received: Christ died for our sins, was buried, raised on the third day, appeared to Peter (Cephas) and the Twelve (1 Corinthians 15:3–5). Paraphrase; unverified against ESV text.
- 1 Corinthians 15:6: "Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep." Search-checked (esv.org; Bible.com ESV).
- 1 Corinthians 15:17: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins." Search-checked (esv.org).

## Gospel differences

- Easter accounts: Matthew 28:1–2 (Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary"; an angel sitting on the stone); Mark 16:1, 5 (three women; a young man in a white robe inside the tomb); Luke 24:4, 10 (two men in dazzling apparel; Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James and the other women); John 20:1, 11–12 (Mary Magdalene; two angels where the body had lain). Unverified against ESV (from memory). Quoted phrase "the other Mary" (Matthew 28:1) unverified.
- Tatian, a Syrian Christian, composed the *Diatessaron* in the second half of the second century; it was long the Gospel text of many Syriac-speaking churches. Search-checked (Wikipedia "Tatian"; Riddle's ANF introduction: between 153 and 170; GEDSH).
- Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, in the fifth century, found more than two hundred copies in the churches of his region, collected them, and replaced them with the Gospels of the four evangelists. Search-checked (Theodoret, *Compendium of Heretical Fables* 1.20, as quoted in McFall, WTJ 1994, and Drum, Bibliotheca Sacra 1918). Essay says "collected," not "destroyed," following Drum.
- Around 400, Augustine wrote a long work on the agreement of the Gospels (*De consensu evangelistarum*), answering critics who pointed out differences. Search-checked (Wikipedia "Harmony of the Gospels"; 2014 article abstract via relbib).
- Ancient biographers arranged material by theme, compressed events and summarized speeches as a matter of course. Unverified general statement (cf. Licona, *Why Are There Differences in the Gospels?*, 2017; not cited in essay).
- Mark 12:26 uses the same Greek construction (epi + genitive) to mean "in the passage about" the bush; some scholars read Mark 2:26 the same way, others don't. Quoted phrase search-checked (esv.org Mark 12:26); the proposed solution search-checked (BYU New Testament Commentary lists it; Themelios 47.2 "Revisiting 'the Time of Abiathar the High Priest'").

## Chicago Statement

- The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, 1978. Search-checked (Ligonier: October 28, 1978; ETS archive PDF).
- Quotation from Article XIII: inerrancy is not negated by "the topical arrangement of material, variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations." Search-checked (Carolina University divinity copy; Ligonier; ETS PDF wording agrees).
- Drafted by scholars who knew these passages well. Characterization; unverified.
- Many evangelical churches confess that Scripture is without error. General statement; unverified.

## Hard passages

- Laws that regulate slavery (Leviticus 25, Exodus 21); commands to destroy whole towns (Joshua 6, 8); psalms asking God to break enemies' teeth (Psalm 58:6; cf. Psalm 3:7). References not given in the essay; unverified.
- Job questions God's justice across the book; Jeremiah (12:1) and Habakkuk (1:2–4, 13) put the question to God directly. References not given in the essay; unverified.

## Jesus and Scripture

- John 10:35: "Scripture cannot be broken"; Jesus's argument rests on a phrase from Psalm 82 (John 10:34, "I said, you are gods"). Quoted phrase search-checked (esv.org John 10:35); Psalm 82 link search-checked (ESV Reformation Study Bible note via Bible Gateway: one word of a psalm of Asaph).
- John 5:39–40: "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me, yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life." First clause search-checked (esv.org, biblia ESV); "yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life" from memory, search summary confirms the sense; verify verbatim.
- Luke 24:27: "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself." Search-checked (esv.org, christianity.com ESV).
- The first Christians had no New Testament and had the Scriptures of Israel. Unverified (textbook).

## Ending

- Mark is the shortest Gospel; it includes the disciples' misunderstanding, Peter's denial, and women fleeing the tomb afraid (Mark 16:8). Unverified (textbook; Mark 16:8 paraphrased).
- Luke 24:32: "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?" Search-checked (esv.org, biblia ESV).
- Closing text, Isaiah 40:8: "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever." Search-checked (esv.org, biblia, Bible Hub ESV).
- Closing text, John 20:31: "These are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." Search-checked (biblestudytools ESV). ESV begins the verse "but these are written"; the opening "but" is dropped and "These" capitalized for the Closing Texts line.
