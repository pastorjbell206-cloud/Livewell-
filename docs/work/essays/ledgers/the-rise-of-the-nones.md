## How the Nones Grew

- Glenn M. Vernon, "The Religious 'Nones': A Neglected Category," *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*, 1968 (vol. 7, no. 2, pp. 219-229). Search-checked (Wordorigins "nones" entry citing JSTOR; Pew, "'Nones' on the Rise," 2012, preface).
- Vernon was a sociologist at the University of Utah. Search-checked (Pew 2012 preface, as summarized).
- Vernon objected that the label defines people by what they lack. Search-checked (Pew 2012 quotes him on this). The essay paraphrases ("defined these people by what they lacked and then mostly ignored them"); "mostly ignored" rests on his title, "A Neglected Category."
- Vernon proposed "religious independents"; the term never caught on. Search-checked (Religion Dispatches, "What's in a name? Religious 'nones' and the American religious landscape," 2015). Also note his 1968 *Review of Religious Research* article title, "Marital Characteristics of Religious Independents" (Wordorigins).
- The General Social Survey began asking religious preference in 1972. Search-checked (Berkeley news release, March 2013, Hout and Fischer: GSS "tracking trends in religious preference since 1972").
- About 5 percent said "no religion" in 1972. Search-checked (Berkeley/Duke 2013 release summary: "5% in 1972 and 8% in 1990"; Fortress Press blurb for Burge's *The Nones*: "about 5 percent in 1972").
- "Twenty years later the figure was still in the single digits." Search-checked (8% in 1990 per the 2013 release; Hout and Fischer 2002: 7 percent, "its level for about 20 years," at the start of the 1990s).
- Michael Hout and Claude Fischer, "Why More Americans Have No Religious Preference: Politics and Generations," *American Sociological Review* 67 (2002), pp. 165-190. Search-checked (krimdok record; Berkeley PDF listed but not opened; summaries).
- No religious preference doubled during the 1990s, from 7 percent to 14 percent. Search-checked (abstract as summarized: "doubled from 7 percent, its level for about 20 years, to an unprecedented 14 percent between the start and end of the 1990s").
- By 2018 the GSS put it "near a quarter of adults." Search-checked (Fortress Press blurb for *The Nones*: 23.7 percent in 2018).
- Pew's 2023-24 survey found "nearly three in ten" unaffiliated. Search-checked (NPR coverage, Feb. 26, 2025: "nearly three in 10"; pilot ledger what-post-christian-means has 29 percent verified). The essay avoids the forbidden word in Pew's study name.
- The climb "had leveled off, at least for now." Search-checked (Pew 2025 executive summary as quoted: the unaffiliated share "has leveled off – at least temporarily").
- Link /writing/what-post-christian-means: approved pilot, merged in PR #539; in link-targets.json shelf.
- Most of the new nones had not stopped believing in God; they held conventional beliefs. Search-checked (2002 abstract as summarized: "Most people with no preference hold conventional religious beliefs, despite their alienation from organized religion").
- "Later researchers had a name for them: unchurched believers." Search-checked (2009 press release on a follow-up study, ScienceDaily/EurekAlert summary: "unchurched believers," people who believe in God but report no religion). Attributed to later researchers, not to the 2002 paper.
- The increase was concentrated among political moderates and liberals; tied to the religious right of the 1990s. Search-checked (secondary source quoting the paper: "the significant increase in no religious preference was confined to liberals and moderates ... and the magnitude of the change increased with political distance from the right"). Not quoted in the essay. unverified against the published article text.
- 2014 follow-up concluded that political identity shapes religious identity, not the reverse. Search-checked (Hout and Fischer, "Explaining Why More Americans Have No Religious Preference: Political Backlash and Generational Succession, 1987-2012," *Sociological Science* 1 (2014), pp. 423-447, as summarized: "the causal direction likely runs from political identity to religious identity").
- Robert Putnam and David Campbell, *American Grace*, 2010: young Americans came to associate religion with conservative politics, especially on homosexuality, and many left religion. Search-checked (Cascade PBS, "Religious right drives young from churches," October 2010; comment.org review; summaries say the distinguishing characteristic of the post-1991 young nones is their stance on homosexuality). Secondary; the book itself not opened.

## Politics and the Mainline

- "it describes something we did" and "When a church lets its name become shorthand for a party, it loses the people who can't vote for that party." Interpretation drawn from Hout and Fischer.

- Mainline Protestant churches on the whole kept their distance from the religious right. Not checked (textbook; general characterization).
- Mainline churches "have been shrinking longer and faster" than evangelicals. Supported by Kelley 1972 (mainline decline already under way) and the Pew figures below. Interpretation resting on those lines.
- Dean M. Kelley, *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing*, 1972 (Harper & Row), a study commissioned by the National Council of Churches. Search-checked (Wikipedia, "Dean M. Kelley"; ChurchLeaders, "The Myth of Why Conservative Churches Are Growing"; Christian Post). Kelley's denomination is not stated in the essay (not confirmed).
- Kelley's thesis: churches that make demands on members hold them; those that ask little do not. Search-checked (same results: "conservative churches grow because they make demands on members"). Paraphrase.
- Michael Hout, Andrew Greeley and Melissa Wilde, "The Demographic Imperative in Religious Change in the United States," *American Journal of Sociology* 107, no. 2 (2001), pp. 468-500. Search-checked (University of Pennsylvania repository record and abstract).
- For cohorts born 1903 to 1973, 76 percent of the mainline-decline trend is attributed to higher fertility and earlier childbearing among women in conservative denominations. Search-checked (abstract as summarized). The essay says "about three-quarters" and names the cohorts. A 2005 Christian Century piece by Hout gives 70 percent for 1900-1975; the essay does not use that figure.
- Pew: mainline Protestants 18 percent of U.S. adults in 2007, 11 percent in 2023-24. Search-checked (Pew 2025 study chapter as summarized: "declining from 18% of U.S. adults in 2007 to 11% in 2023-24"; Pew 2015 gives 18.1% in 2007 and 14.7% in 2014).

- "So the decline has no political home" and the sentence after it. Interpretation resting on the lines above. "kept their own people no better" rests on the mainline drop being larger than the evangelical one in 2007-2024.

## What the People Who Left Say

- Pew survey of nones, summer 2023 (July 31 to Aug. 6, 2023; 11,201 American Trends Panel respondents including 3,317 nones), published Jan. 24, 2024 as "Religious 'Nones' in America: Who They Are and What They Believe." Search-checked (Pew report pages as summarized; NPR coverage). The essay says "more than 3,300 nones."
- Nones: 63 percent "nothing in particular," 17 percent atheist, 20 percent agnostic. Search-checked (Pew, "Who are the nones," as summarized: 658 atheists, 678 agnostics, 1,981 nothing in particular).
- 60 percent: questioning a lot of religious teachings is an extremely or very important reason they are nonreligious. Search-checked (Pew, "Why are nones nonreligious?" Jan. 24, 2024, as summarized; Baptist Press and Aleteia coverage). The essay paraphrases ("questioning many religious teachings").
- 47 percent: dislike of religious organizations. Search-checked (same).
- 30 percent: bad experiences with religious people. Search-checked (same).
- About four in ten: no need for religion. Search-checked (Pew summary 41 percent; Aleteia 40 percent). The essay says "about four in ten."
- 12 percent: don't have time for religion. Search-checked (same).
- Not used: the 2024 share citing dislike of religious groups' social and political positions (search returned only the 2018 Pew figure, 49 percent).
- Lifeway Research study released in 2019 ("Most Teenagers Drop Out of Church as Young Adults"): 66 percent of young adults who attended a Protestant church regularly for at least a year as teenagers stopped attending regularly for at least a year between 18 and 22. Search-checked (Christian Post, GetReligion, Relevant, Baptist Press coverage; 2,002 respondents). Survey fieldwork dates not used.
- Reasons: moving to college 34 percent; church members seemed judgmental or hypocritical 32 percent; no longer felt connected to people at church 29 percent; disagreed with the church's stance on political or social issues 25 percent; work responsibilities 24 percent. Search-checked (Christian Post coverage as summarized). The essay says "a quarter" for 25 and "about as many" for 24.
- Jim Davis and Michael Graham, *The Great Dechurching*, 2023, built on surveys by political scientists Ryan Burge and Paul Djupe. Search-checked (RNS review, Sept. 2023; Word&Way; Christianity Today coverage names Burge with the authors).
- Dechurched defined as having attended at least monthly in the past and now attending less than once a year. Search-checked (RNS review: survey of 1,043 Americans; second survey of 4,099 dechurched).
- About 40 million Americans dechurched over the last twenty-five years. Search-checked (Equip.org review; Mosaic review; ChurchLeaders "What 40 Million Dechurched Americans Say About Why They Left"). The essay says "some 40 million Americans."
- A minority were badly hurt (abuse, leaders who put the institution first): the "exvangelical" and harmed groups. Search-checked (RNS review: exvangelicals "often harmed by churches and other Christian institutions"; summary listing spiritual and physical abuse and hypocrisy among leaders).
- "by the authors' account most of the dechurched left casually, without a crisis of faith or a deep wound." Search-checked, secondary only (one summary: "roughly 75% of the respondents claimed casual reasons for dechurching"; Jake Meador's July 2023 Atlantic piece drawing on the book argued ordinary life, not scandal or doctrine, is the main reason, per Christianity Today). A critical review argues the casual category may understate doctrinal doubt. unverified against the book's tables; verifier should confirm "most."
- "Their lives changed, and the habit of church never got rebuilt." Interpretation summarizing "casual" dechurching. (One summary named moving as the top reason; not confirmed, so not stated.)
- Pew 2024: nones less likely to volunteer (17 percent volunteered for an organization in the past year, 10 points below the religiously affiliated). Search-checked (Pew, "Are nones less involved in civic life," as summarized). The essay gives no number.
- Pew 2024: in the 2022 midterms, by verified voter records, 39 percent of nones voted vs 51 percent of the religiously affiliated. Search-checked (same).
- The civic gaps are concentrated among the "nothing in particular," not atheists and agnostics. Search-checked (same: "lower levels of civic engagement tend to be found especially among 'nones' who describe their religion as 'nothing in particular'").
- "The larger group looks more like people whose ties to civic life have loosened in general." Interpretation; Pew also found only slight gaps on some measures (nonreligious volunteer groups, contacting officials), so the essay says "loosened," not "cut."
- Robert Putnam, *Bowling Alone*, 2000, traced the decline of American association, including religious participation. Not checked (textbook). Verifier: confirm the book treats religious participation (its chapter on religious participation).
- Not used (contested in search results): Burge's claim that the least educated are most likely to be nones.

## The Faith We Handed Down

- Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, *Soul Searching*, 2005, from the National Study of Youth and Religion, coined "moralistic therapeutic deism." Search-checked (Christianity Today 2009; Albert Mohler, April 11, 2005; DBTS 2018; America magazine review, Sept. 2005).
- The five beliefs (a God who created and watches over the world; wants people good, nice and fair; the central goal of life is to be happy and feel good about oneself; God not particularly involved except to solve problems; good people go to heaven). Search-checked (Mohler 2005 and Christianity Today reproduce the five statements; the third matches "central goal of life is to be happy and to feel good about oneself"). Paraphrased in the essay without quotation marks. unverified against the book's own wording.
- Teens were not in revolt against parents' religion; most said their beliefs were similar to their parents'; parents the strongest influence. Search-checked (reviews of *Soul Searching*: teenage hostility to religion "an entrenched but erroneous stereotype"; most teens' beliefs "somewhat or very similar" to parents'; "the greatest influence shaping teens' religious beliefs is their parents"; the "single most important influence" wording belongs to the follow-up *Souls in Transition*, not quoted).
- "The teenagers had learned what they were taught." Interpretation.
- Luther took part in the visitation of Saxon parishes, October 22, 1528 to January 9, 1529; the preface to the Small Catechism (1529) reports that the common people know almost nothing of Christian doctrine, many pastors are incapable of teaching, and all say they are Christians, have been baptized and receive the sacraments, yet cannot recite the Lord's Prayer, the Creed or the Ten Commandments. Search-checked (Book of Concord historical introduction; LCMS essay by Jonathon Krenz; Concordia Publishing House introduction; KFUO). Paraphrase only. The essay says "In 1528 and 1529."
- Small Catechism meant to be taught by the head of the household. Not checked (textbook): each part is headed in the traditional form "as the head of the family should teach them in a simple way to his household." Search results also note it was aimed at families.
- "A Christian society had carried those villagers for centuries." Interpretation (Saxony had been Christian for centuries by 1528; not checked).
- Vern Bengtson, *Families and Faith: How Religion Is Passed Down across Generations* (Oxford, 2013), from the Longitudinal Study of Generations, begun 1970; more than 350 families, more than 3,500 individuals. Search-checked (Christian Century interview, Dec. 2013; The Mennonite / Anabaptist World "Family warmth helps pass religion"; publisher pages).
- Strongest transmission in warm families, especially where the father is seen as warm and close; taking children to church, role modeling, church involvement and home devotions not enough on their own. Search-checked (Christian Century interview as summarized).
- Asked why fatherly warmth matters so much, Bengtson said he doesn't know. Search-checked (same interview, as summarized). Paraphrase.
- Biography: "I was raised without a father, and I am raising five sons." Confirmed facts per NOTES.md (raised without a father; five sons). No scene invented.
