## Corrections made in the essay (2026-10-28)

1. A Letter from Alexandria, the Hilarion quotation. Was: `In one standard translation it reads: "if it is a male, let it live; if it is a female, expose it."` Now: `In one English translation it reads: "if you bear a child and it is male, let it be; if it is female, cast it out."` Reason: three searches for the old wording found no named translation that prints it (search summaries pointed to Sententiae Antiquae, which reads "if it is a boy, leave it; if it is a girl, throw it out", and to the APIS rendering). The new wording is the APIS translation, read word for word in the papyri.info data (see line 4).
2. Same section, next paragraph. Was: `To expose a child was to leave a newborn outside,`. Now: `To cast out a child, or expose it, as historians usually say, was to leave a newborn outside,`. Reason: the corrected quotation says "cast it out", so the paragraph names the link to the historians' word. The HGV record's own keyword for the letter is "aussetzen" (expose).
3. Did the Early Christians Rescue Abandoned Babies? Was: `Homes for foundlings came later still; a document dated 787 credits an archpriest of Milan named Datheus`. Now: `In the West, homes for foundlings came later still; a document dated 787 credits a priest of Milan named Datheus`. Reasons: the search results seen call him "the priest Dateus" and the home the earliest "in the West"; a search on Byzantine orphanages (Timothy Miller's study, summarized) reports fourth-century Christian orphanages in the East that took in abandoned children, so the unscoped sentence was too broad.
4. Same section. Was: `A law of Constantine in 331 protected people who took in children their fathers had cast out.` Now: `A law of Constantine in 331 let people who took in a child its father had cast out keep it, as a son or as a slave, as they chose.` Reason: three search summaries (a Tel Aviv University article, an Italian article on the 331 measure, and an 1897 commentary on Christian inscriptions) agree that the law gave finders the power to raise the foundling as free or as a slave. "Protected" hid that.
5. What the Church Did with Foundlings, Kertzer. Was: `church policies meant to prevent abortion and infanticide and to protect the honor of unmarried mothers eventually hardened into laws that required single women to give up their babies at birth.` Now: `church rules meant to stop abortion and infanticide and to spare unmarried mothers disgrace became, over time, laws that made single women give up their babies as soon as they were born.` Reason: the old sentence followed the publisher's description almost word for word ("Church policies designed to prevent abortion and infanticide and to save the honor of unwed mothers eventually evolved into laws that required all single women to give up their babies immediately after birth"); the claim is unchanged and now in the essay's own words.
6. Same section, Tuam. Was: `found death records for 796 children who died there between 1925 and 1961.` Now: `found death records for 796 children who died there in the years it was open, from 1925 to 1961.` Reason: a search summary notes that IrishCentral and the Journal give the last recorded death as January 1960; 1925 to 1961 are the home's years of operation.
7. Same section, Fessler. Was: `by the estimate her book uses, more than a million and a half babies were given up for adoption ... ## The Hilarion letter (P.Oxy. 744)

1. Dated 1 BC. Verified: Papyri data, HGV metadata, `origDate when="-0001-06-17"` ("17. Juni 1 v.Chr."); the APIS text ends "The 29th year of Caesar, Pauni 23." Also Search (Caragounis paper: 17 June 1 BC).
2. Hilarion was in Alexandria waiting on his wages; tells Alis not to worry if the others go home and he stays; asks her to take care of the child; will send money when paid. Verified as paraphrase: Papyri data, APIS: "Know that I am still in Alexandria; and do not worry if they wholly set out, I am staying in Alexandria. I ask you and entreat you, take care of the child, and if I receive my pay soon, I will send it up to you." The Arabic translation's line 4 reads "if everyone returns", which supports "go home". "The small child they already have" is the usual reading (Search: Caragounis, as summarized, says he had left at home a wife and a child). "Small": the Greek text in the Papyri data (`DDbDP/20/20442.xml`) reads τῷ παιδίῳ, the diminutive.
3. "He calls her his sister, but most readers take her to be his wife." Verified: Papyri data, APIS: "Hilarion to his sister Alis"; Search (Biblical Archaeology Society library page: "writes to his pregnant wife Alis"; Diotima summary: a husband writing to his wife; Caragounis treats her as his wife). The earlier clause about "sister" meaning wife was cut (correction 14).
4. Quotation: "if you bear a child and it is male, let it be; if it is female, cast it out." Verified verbatim: Papyri data, APIS translation ("Above all, if you bear a child and it is male, let it be; if it is female, cast it out."). The Greek in `DDbDP/20/20442.xml` reads ἐὰν ἦν ἄρσενον ἄφες, ἐὰν ἦν θήλεα ἔκβαλε; ἔκβαλε is "cast out". The essay calls it "one English translation" and does not name APIS. Replaces the writer's unverified rendering (correction 1).
5. "She was expecting a child." Verified by reading: the letter's "if you bear a child" (APIS); HGV keyword "Alis soll das zukünftige Baby aussetzen" (Alis is to expose the future baby); Search (BAS: "pregnant wife").
6. Message through Aphrodisias asking him not to forget her; "How could he forget her?"; begs her once more not to worry. Verified as paraphrase: Papyri data, APIS: "You have told Aphrodisias, 'Do not forget me.' But how can I forget you? Thus I'm asking you not to worry." He had already told her not to worry earlier in the letter, so "once more" is accurate.
7. Found at Oxyrhynchus; scholars know it as P.Oxy. 744. Verified: Papyri data (ids p.oxy;4;744; principal edition P.Oxy. vol. 4 no. 744); Search (Diotima: "Oxyrhynchus papyrus 744"; Caragounis: Hilarion "probably a laborer from Oxyrhynchos"). The HGV record gives Alexandria as the place of writing, which the essay also says. "A town in Middle Egypt" and "its ancient rubbish mounds": verified, Search (closing pass): a Wikipedia for Schools copy (wikigame.di.unito.it) calls Oxyrhynchus a city in Middle Egypt near modern el-Bahnasa; the summary of pages from The Past, Logos, TheCollector and the Biblical Archaeology Society describes Grenfell and Hunt digging its refuse mounds from 1896, after the buildings had been stripped.
8. Exposed babies: some died of cold, hunger or animals; some picked up, many raised as slaves. Verified: ANF (repo), Tertullian, Apology 9 ("exposure to cold and hunger and dogs"); Search (Harris, JRS 1994 abstract as summarized: exposure "very often but by no means always" fatal; it moved potential labour "from freedom to slavery, or at least into de facto slavery"); Search (Boswell, encyclopedia.com summary: most rescued were raised as foster children or in some cases slaves).
9. "As historians usually say": expose is the historians' word. Verified: Search (Sententiae Antiquae summary: "expose" is the standard scholarly term); HGV keyword "aussetzen".

## Why Roman families left children outside

10. No reliable contraception; childbirth dangerous; many infants died in the first year; a laborer's household near the edge; a son might support aged parents; a daughter needed a dowry and left. Verified in general, Search (closing pass), four searches: contraception, the New York Review of Books on Riddle (all but four of Dioscorides's twenty methods judged ineffective; Galen thought most absurd or dangerous) and World History Encyclopedia (cycle tracking unreliable), with Hopkins's dissent that some methods could work noted, so "no reliable way" is the essay's fair reading; childbirth, an estimated 25 maternal deaths per 1,000 live births in premodern societies (same results); infant mortality, estimates of 25 to 40 percent in the first year (World History Encyclopedia, a psychology paper, HistorySkills); dowry and the preference for sons, a Cornell College classics page (a daughter's marriage meant a dowry; sons kept as heirs) and Koskenniemi (sources say sons were preferred and girls abandoned for economic reasons); old-age support, Delfim Leão on Athenian *gerotrophia*, a son's legal duty to feed and house aged parents, and a comparative article on Roman support duties under *pietas*. "A laborer's household": Hilarion is "probably a laborer" (Caragounis, line 2).
11. "There were no orphanages and no adoption agencies." Verified: Search (encyclopedia.com "Abandonment", summarizing Boswell: "in the ancient world there were no institutional arrangements for abandoned children"). The same search reports Christian orphanages only from the fourth century.
12. Boswell argued the English word carries a sense of harm the ancient words lacked, and preferred "abandonment". Verified: Search (a JRS note, as summarized: Boswell rejects "exposure" because it "conveys a sense of risk or harm" absent from ἔκθεσις and *expositio*; the note itself disputes him).
13. Augustine quotation: parents who left their infants "in order to their being cared for by any passer-by." Verified verbatim: Library, `Augustine of Hippo/Letters.html`, headed "LETTER XCVIII. (A.D. 408.) TO BONIFACE, HIS COLLEAGUE IN THE EPISCOPAL OFFICE", section 6: "foundlings which heartless parents have exposed in order to their being cared for by any passer-by".
14. Augustine "writing in North Africa four centuries after Hilarion". Verified: 1 BC (line 1) to AD 408 (line 13). North Africa: verified, Library, `Augustine of Hippo/Letters.html` (NPNF): the introduction calls him "the Bishop of Hippo", and Letter XVII has him write "we are both settled in Africa".
15. "Many Romans also didn't think of exposure as killing." Supported by Boswell's argument (lines 12 and 64) and Search (encyclopedia.com summary: most parents did not intend the child to die). Kept as the essay's reading.
