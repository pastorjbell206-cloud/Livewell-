## What the writer could and could not check

- **Local primary texts worked.** Justin, Tertullian, the Didache, Diognetus and Augustine were read in nineteenth-century or early twentieth-century printed translations held locally or fetched from GitHub's raw file host (see the method key). Every quotation from them in the essay was matched character for character against those files, except where a line says otherwise.
- **Hosts that failed.** papyri.info and diotima-doctafemina.org (WebFetch: host does not resolve; curl: proxy refused). The papyri.info GitHub data repository (papyri/idp.data) returned 404 on raw paths and the GitHub API refused it for this session. Perseus's Latin repository returned 404 for Seneca's *On Anger*, so the Seneca line the writer wanted was left out.
- **Search.** 21 WebSearch calls on 2026-10-28. Every "Search" line rests on the search tool's result summary and the sites it listed; no page was opened.
- **Scripture.** Every ESV quotation was matched against the local ESV copy (2011 text). Only James 1:27 was also searched; the essay quotes only the part of that verse that reads the same in both texts. All other Scripture lines still need the ESV search or API check.
- **Left out because it could not be confirmed:** Seneca, *On Anger* 1.15, on drowning weak or deformed newborns; Aristotle's proposed law against rearing deformed children; the claim that Christians gathered babies from rubbish heaps (the essay names it only as a popular story the early sources don't describe); the "son or slave" detail of Constantine's 331 law; a translator's name for the Hilarion translation; Boswell's page numbers; Stark's sex-ratio figures; Sean Ross Abbey and Bessborough details.

## The Hilarion letter (P.Oxy. 744)

1. Letter dated 1 BC. Search, 2026-10-28 (papyri.info HGV 20442 and trismegistos.org/text/20442 listed; result says P.Oxy. 744, TM 20442, dated 1 BC at Oxyrhynchus; dating line "The 29th year of Caesar, Pauni 23"). Verified by search summary only.
2. Hilarion was working in Alexandria and waiting on his wages; tells Alis not to worry if the others go home and he stays; asks her to take care of the small child; will send money when paid. Search, 2026-10-28 (papyri.info/APIS translation summarized: writes from Alexandria, asks her to care for the child, promises money once his pay arrives). Paraphrase. Partly verified. The "others go home and he stays on" detail rests on the writer's memory of the standard translations ("if they really go home, I will remain in Alexandria"), not on a source seen: unverified.
3. "He calls her his sister" and most readers take her to be his wife; she was expecting a child. Search, 2026-10-28 (Biblical Archaeology Review summary: "writes to his pregnant wife Alis"; results name the letter "Hilarion to his sister Alis"). "Sister ... could mean a wife" in Roman Egypt: General, unverified.
4. Quotation: "if it is a male, let it live; if it is a female, expose it." Search, 2026-10-28 (result summary quoting a translation on romansinfocus.com: "if it is a male, let it live; if it is a female, expose it"; another result summary gives "If you bear offspring, if it is a male, let it live; if it is a female, expose it."). The essay calls it "one standard translation" and names no translator. Verified by search summary only; translator unidentified (possibly the Lefkowitz and Fant *Women's Life in Greece and Rome* rendering cited by Diotima, no. 249). Verifier: confirm against a printed translation if one can be reached.
5. He answers a message she sent through Aphrodisias asking him not to forget her; asks how he could forget her; begs her again not to worry. Search, 2026-10-28 (Caragounis paper summary: letter partly a reply to a message Alis sent through Aphrodisias). Paraphrase, no quotation marks. The "How could he forget her" and closing "not to worry" lines rest on the writer's knowledge of the standard translations; unverified by a source seen.
6. Found at Oxyrhynchus; papyri from its ancient rubbish mounds; known as P.Oxy. 744. P.Oxy. 744 and Oxyrhynchus: Search, 2026-10-28 (several results). "Rubbish mounds" and "a town in Middle Egypt": General, unverified.
7. Exposed babies: some died of cold, hunger or animals; some picked up by strangers, many raised as slaves. Harris JRS abstract (Search, 2026-10-28: exposure "very often but by no means always resulting in death"; served "to transfer potential labour from freedom to slavery"). Tertullian names "cold and hunger and dogs" (ANF (repo), Apology 9). Verified at summary level.

## Why Roman families exposed children

8. No reliable contraception; childbirth dangerous; many infants died in the first year; a laborer's household close to the edge; a son might support aged parents; a daughter needed a dowry and left for another household. General, unverified. (Search, 2026-10-28, a Sententiae Antiquae/romansinfocus summary: "pregnancy for the poor presented not only another mouth to feed, but limited the mother's contribution to the family's resources"; "infant mortality rates were high".)
9. "There were no orphanages and no adoption agencies." Search, 2026-10-28 (encyclopedia.com "Abandonment" summary: "in the ancient world there were no institutional arrangements for abandoned children"). Verified by search summary.
10. John Boswell argued the English word "exposure" carries a sense of harm the ancient terms lacked, and preferred "abandonment". Search, 2026-10-28 (result summarizing Harris's JRS article: Boswell "prefers the term 'abandonment' over 'exposure,' believing that the English word 'exposure' conveys 'a sense of risk or harm … which is absent from the ancient terms'"). Verified by search summary.
11. Augustine quotation: parents who left infants "in order to their being cared for by any passer-by." Writings-Database, Augustine of Hippo/Letters.html, Letter XCVIII (headed "A.D. 408", to Boniface), section 6: "foundlings which heartless parents have exposed in order to their being cared for by any passer-by". Verified verbatim.
12. Augustine wrote "in North Africa" "four centuries after Hilarion". Letter 98 dated A.D. 408 (Writings-Database heading); Augustine was bishop of Hippo (General). 1 BC to AD 408 is 408 years. Verified.

## Did the Christians rescue foundlings?

55. The popular story of Christians gathering babies from rubbish heaps is not what the early writers quoted describe; they describe refusal. Writer's reading of the texts above (Didache, Diognetus, Justin, Tertullian). Verified as a statement about those texts only; the essay makes no wider claim about all early sources.
56. Augustine, letter of 408: foundlings sometimes picked up by consecrated virgins, who had no children of their own, and presented for baptism. Writings-Database, Letter XCVIII (A.D. 408) section 6: "are picked tip [sic, OCR for 'up'] by holy virgins, and are presented for baptism by these persons, who neither have nor desire to have children of their own". Paraphrase. Verified.
57. Council of Vaison, 442, made provision for infants left at churches. Search, 2026-10-28 (Wikipedia "Council of Vaison": generally dated 442; Aleteia: abandoned child to find sanctuary in a church; another source: "full protection for foundlings in churches"). Verified by search summary.
58. A document dated 787 credits the archpriest Datheus of Milan with founding a foundling home; historians have questioned the date. Search, 2026-10-28 (Catholic Encyclopedia "Foundling Asylums": Datheus, Milan, 787; Giorgio and Livia Maggioni, *Medicina nei Secoli*, 2010, "The Dateus Founding Home in Milan: Questionable Date of Establishment"). Verified by search summary.
59. "Nearly a century after Constantine's conversion" parents in a Christian province still left children for passers-by. Augustine Letter 98 (408); Constantine's conversion 312 (General). Verified for the letter; 312 unverified here.
60. A law of Constantine in 331 protected people who took in children their fathers had cast out. Search, 2026-10-28 (Karger, "Exposed and Abandoned: Origins of the Foundling": "Constantine's 331 measure protected people who took in children cast out by their fathers"). Verified by search summary. (Codex Theodosianus 5.9.1, from the writer's memory; not cited in the essay.)
61. In 374 a law under Valentinian I made killing an infant a capital crime. Search, 2026-10-28 (article citing Codex Theodosianus 9.14.1, dated 7 February 374: "it became a capital offence to kill an infant"; a Birmingham Rosetta paper on Byzantine attitudes agrees). Verified by search summary.
62. Historians think exposure itself was probably outlawed the same year, and it went on anyway. Search, 2026-10-28 (a Cambridge abstract: exposure "was subjected to legal prohibition probably in 374, but ... the practice did not cease"; the result did not say which article; likely Harris, JRS 1994, or the Journal of Law and Religion article "Christianity and the Legal Status of Abandoned Children in the Later Roman Empire"). Verified by search summary; attribution left general in the essay.

## Historians and their disagreements

63. Boswell, *The Kindness of Strangers* (1988); the essay gives no year. Search, 2026-10-28 (Kirkus, 1988; Wikipedia).
64. Boswell estimated urban Romans of the first three centuries abandoned 20 to 40 percent of their children, and argued most were rescued. Search, 2026-10-28 (encyclopedia.com "Abandonment" summary: "urban Romans in the first three centuries CE exposed roughly 20 to 40 percent of their children"; "most of them were rescued"). Verified by search summary.
65. Boswell argued abandonment stayed common (and morally accepted) in Christian Europe until the Renaissance, changing form: giving children to monasteries (oblation), later foundling hospitals. Search, 2026-10-28 (sciencia.cat and Brewminate summaries: "child abandonment was a common and morally acceptable practice from antiquity until the Renaissance"; parents "donating them to the church, or later delivering them to foundling hospitals"; Boswell "most interested in the monastic custom of child oblation"). Verified by search summary.
66. W. V. Harris, "Child-Exposure in the Roman Empire", *Journal of Roman Studies* 84 (1994), 1-22: exposure widespread. Search, 2026-10-28 (Cambridge Core abstract). Verified by search summary.
67. Quotation from Harris: "very often but by no means always resulting in death," Search, 2026-10-28 (abstract as quoted in the result: "The exposure of infants, very often but by no means always resulting in death, was widespread in many parts of the Roman Empire"). Verified by search summary only; verifier should see the abstract itself.
68. Harris: exposure moved children born free into slavery. Search, 2026-10-28 (abstract: it served "to transfer potential labour from freedom to slavery (or at any rate to de facto slavery)"). Paraphrase. Verified by search summary.
69. Donald Engels argued (1980) that a high rate of female infanticide was demographically impossible. Search, 2026-10-28 (Engels, "The Problem of Female Infanticide in the Greco-Roman World", *Classical Philology* 75 (1980): 112-120; PDF at gwern.net listed). Verified by search summary.
70. Harris replied that the argument didn't hold. Search, 2026-10-28 (Harris, "The Theoretical Possibility of Extensive Infanticide in the Graeco-Roman World", *Classical Quarterly* 32 (1982): 114-116; a Cambridge note paraphrases his objection to Engels's demographic inference). Verified by search summary. The essay says "Earlier" (1980/1982 before 1994): correct.
71. Rodney Stark argued that pagan families exposed more girls than boys, Christians refused, so the churches had more women of childbearing age and grew faster. Search, 2026-10-28 (Wikipedia "The Rise of Christianity"; a review in Project MUSE/JECS; summaries: "infanticide tended to affect female newborns, so early Christians had a more even sex ratio and therefore a higher percentage of childbearing women"). Verified by search summary. Stark is called a sociologist (General; the approved pilot also calls him one).
72. "His argument rests on the very estimates the historians are still contesting." Writer's judgment from 63 to 70; a 1998 *Journal of Early Christian Studies* response to Stark's chapter on women exists (Search, 2026-10-28) but its content was not seen.
