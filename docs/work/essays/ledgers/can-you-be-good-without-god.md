## Polycarp and Justin Martyr

- Romans called Christians atheists for refusing to honor the gods. Source: Martyrdom of Polycarp ch. 3, 9; Justin, First Apology ch. 5–6 (ANF). WDB.
- Crowd at Smyrna demanded Polycarp's arrest with "Away with the Atheists." Source: Martyrdom of Polycarp ch. 3 (ANF): "Away with the Atheists; let Polycarp be sought out!" The essay quotes only the first clause, without the exclamation point. WDB.
- Proconsul urged him to swear by the fortune of Caesar and say "Away with the Atheists," and offered to set him free. Source: Martyrdom of Polycarp ch. 9 ("Swear by the fortune of Caesar; repent, and say, Away with the Atheists"; "Swear, and I will set thee at liberty"). WDB.
- Polycarp looked sternly at the crowd, waved his hand toward them, groaned, looked up to heaven, and said "Away with the Atheists." Source: ch. 9 ("gazing with a stern countenance on all the multitude ... waving his hand towards them, while with groans he looked up to heaven, said, 'Away with the Atheists.'"). Essay paraphrases and quotes only the three words. WDB.
- "aged bishop." Source: ch. 9 ("Have respect to thy old age"; "Eighty and six years have I served Him"). WDB.
- Account is a letter from the church at Smyrna. Source: opening address, "The Church of God which sojourns at Smyrna, to the Church of God sojourning in Philomelium." WDB.
- "in the middle of that century." Writings-Database metadata gives 155; scholarly dates run c. 155 to the 160s, so the essay stays general. Partially verified.
- Justin's apology addressed to Antoninus Pius. Source: First Apology ch. 1 (ANF). WDB.
- "in the 150s." Standard dating of the First Apology (c. 150–157). Unverified this session.
- Quotation: "Hence are we called atheists. And we confess that we are atheists, so far as gods of this sort are concerned, but not with respect to the most true God, the Father of righteousness and temperance and the other virtues". Source: First Apology ch. 6 (ANF; the sentence continues "who is free from all impurity"). WDB, verbatim.
- Justin asked the emperor to judge Christians by their actions and not their name. Source: First Apology ch. 3–4 ("Claim of Judicial Investigation"; "Christians Unjustly Condemned for Their Mere Name"). WDB. Paraphrase.

## Locke and Bayle

- Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration* published in English in 1689 (William Popple's translation). Search (Wikipedia; freespeechhistory.com).
- Described as one of the founding arguments for religious liberty in the English-speaking world, and as making room for more people than most contemporaries would. Characterization. Unverified.
- Quotation: "Lastly, those are not at all to be tolerated who deny the being of a God. Promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist. The taking away of God, though but even in thought, dissolves all." Search (several sites agree apart from typography, e.g. "Being", "tho"). Popple text not opened.
- Pierre Bayle, French Protestant exile in Rotterdam, argued that an atheist could be virtuous and that a society of atheists was possible, in the comet book (Pensées diverses sur la comète, first edition 1682). "Earlier in that decade." Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Pierre Bayle." Search. First-edition year not confirmed beyond "shortly after arriving in Rotterdam" (1681).
- Bayle later accused of atheism for those arguments (by his former ally Pierre Jurieu). Source: SEP "Pierre Bayle." Search.

## Philosophers

- Wielenberg: the most basic ethical facts hold necessarily and need no foundation; explanations must stop somewhere; he stops at the moral facts as the theist stops at God. Source: secondary summaries of Robust Ethics (Tyndale digital collections; Worldview Bulletin; NDPR). Search. Book not opened. (An earlier draft attributed the example "causing pain for fun" to him; removed as unconfirmed.)
- J. L. Mackie: Australian-born philosopher at Oxford who denied objective moral values. Unverified (textbook: born Sydney 1917; University College, Oxford).
- Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977); the argument from queerness (objective values would be unlike anything else in the universe; knowing them would supply a reason to act). Source: Wikipedia and secondary summaries. Search. Paraphrase only; the familiar quotation was not verified and is not quoted.
- Mackie's later book on the arguments for God (The Miracle of Theism), "published in 1982 after his death" (Mackie died 1981). Source: Wikipedia lists 1982, posthumous; other citations give 1981 or 1983. Search. Partially verified.
- Mackie's concession, paraphrased without quotation marks: objective prescriptive values would be "most unlikely to have arisen in the ordinary course of events, without an all-powerful god to create them" (Miracle of Theism, pp. 115–16, as quoted by the Prindle Institute). Search. Essay paraphrases as "very unlikely to have arisen in the ordinary course of events without a god to create them".
- "He kept his atheism by denying the values." Characterization. Unverified.

## Plato and Aquinas

- *Euthyphro* set in 399 BC outside the office of the magistrate (the king archon) who heard impiety charges, where Socrates comes to answer the charge (brought by Meletus) that led to his death. Source: Wikipedia "Euthyphro" via search; 399 BC trial date textbook.
- Euthyphro is prosecuting his own father over the death of a hired man (a dependant who had killed a household slave, was bound and thrown in a ditch, and died there). Source: Wikipedia and Jowett summary via search (one search summary misread who died; the dialogue says the dependant died in the ditch).
- Socrates' question paraphrased (is the holy holy because the gods love it, or do they love it because it is holy; Euthyphro 10a). Paraphrase, no quotation marks.
- Aquinas: God alone is good by his essence; everything else is good by participation. Source: Summa Theologiae I, q. 6, a. 3 (CCEL, DRBO) via search.
