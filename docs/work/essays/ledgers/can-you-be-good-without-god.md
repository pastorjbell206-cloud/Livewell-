## Corrections and restorations made in this pass

1. **Restored quotation (Myers).** Opening paragraph: the paraphrase "and told him, as he remembered it, that the first was inconceivable and the second unbelievable, and yet the third was peremptory and absolute" became "and, as he remembered it, pronounced "how inconceivable was the first, how unbelievable the second, and yet how peremptory and absolute the third."" Matched word for word in two printed reprints of Myers's passage, Cooke (1883) and Morley. Both italicize *first*, *second*, *third*; the essay drops the italics. This settles the writer's open question: "unbelievable the second," not "unbelievable was the second" (the "was" reading appeared only in a forum transcription, per search).
2. **Date generalized to what the source supports (Justin).** "addressed to the emperor Antoninus Pius in the 150s" became "around 150." Justin's own text (WDB, *First Apology* 46) speaks of Christ as "born one hundred and fifty years ago under Cyrenius," which supports "around 150"; the narrower decade was not confirmed by any source seen.
3. **Clause corrected to what sources support (Bayle).** "and he was later accused of atheism himself for saying so" became "and a fellow Protestant minister later attacked him for it until he lost his professorship." No source reached says Bayle was accused of atheism. Robertson says Pierre Jurieu, "the Protestant Jurieu," "succeeded in having him deprived of his chair of philosophy and history at Rotterdam (1693) on the score of the freethinking of his Pensées sur la Comète," and later had the Consistory censure the *Dictionary* for its "renewed vindications of atheists." EB1911 ("Jurieu") agrees that "the bitterness and persistency of his attacks on his colleague Pierre Bayle led to the latter being deprived of his chair in 1693" and gives Jurieu as pastor of the Walloon church of Rotterdam; EB1911 ("Bayle") puts the quarrel down to envy and to the *Avis important aux refugiés* (1690) rather than the comet book. So "for it" rests on Robertson, and the loss of the chair on both.

## George Eliot and Frederic Myers

- Walk in the Fellows' Garden of Trinity College, Cambridge, with Frederic Myers, on a rainy evening in May 1873. **Verified.** Myers's own words, reprinted in Cooke and Morley: "I walked with her once in the Fellows' Garden of Trinity, on an evening of rainy May" (Morley's text has the misprint "waited"). Year and month: Eliot's journal in Cross, vol. 3, "1873 ... May 19.--We paid a visit to Cambridge at the invitation of Mr. Frederick Myers, and I enjoyed greatly talking with him and some others of the 'Trinity Men'"; a letter of the same week in Cross mentions furs and fires during the visit. Search (George Eliot Review, digitalcommons.unl.edu/ger/34) also places the conversation in 1873.
- Myers "a young scholar." **Verified.** Born 1843 (GITenberg metadata for his *Wordsworth*, PG 8747: birthdate 1843, deathdate 1901; a search result title at the George Eliot Archive gives "Myers, Frederic William Henry (1843-1901)"), so about 29 in May 1873; Eliot's own letter about the visit, printed in Cross, speaks of "a hopeful group of Trinity young men."
- Eliot "by then one of the most admired novelists in England." **Characterization, supported.** Cross, vol. 3, journal for 1 January 1873: of *Middlemarch*, "No former book of mine has been received with more enthusiasm."
- *Middlemarch* had finished its serial run a few months earlier. **Verified.** Cross, vol. 3: journal, "Dec. 1.--This day the first part of 'Middlemarch' was published" (1871); journal, 1 January 1873, "At the beginning of December the eighth and last book of 'Middlemarch' was published."
- Eliot an earnest evangelical as a girl. **Verified.** Cross, vol. 1: her teacher Miss Lewis "was an ardent Evangelical Churchwoman, and exerted a strong influence on her young pupil"; she "had come back from the Miss Franklins' with ultra-Evangelical tendencies."
- Gave up the faith in her early twenties. **Verified.** Cross, vol. 1: after reading Charles Hennell's *Inquiry*, "in the enthusiasm of this first great change ... she determined to give up going to church," a passage Cross places after a letter of 8 December 1841 and before letters of January and February 1842; born 22 November 1819 (Cross, opening entry), so aged 22. Cross adds that after a family rupture "she resumed going to church as before"; the essay says she gave up "the faith," not church-going, which fits.
- Later translated Strauss's *Life of Jesus*, a German study that treated much of the Gospels as myth. **Verified.** Cross, vol. 1: she took over the translation of "Strauss's 'Leben Jesu'" and "on the 15th of [June 1846] the translation of Strauss was published." PG 64037 title page: "Translated from the Fourth German Edition By George Eliot." Pfleiderer's introduction there speaks of "the mythical principle of interpretation which Strauss opposes to the rationalistic one."
- Myers wrote about the walk years afterward. **Verified.** Morley: "Those who know Mr. Myers's essay on George Eliot will not have forgotten its most imposing passage"; Cooke quotes it in 1883. Search result (Wikiquote, Trinity College "History of Women at Trinity" page) dates the essay to *Century Magazine*, November 1881.
- Quotation: "how inconceivable was the first, how unbelievable the second, and yet how peremptory and absolute the third." **Verified, restored** (correction 1). Cooke and Morley, word for word. Context in both: "taking as her text the three words ... the words God, Immortality, Duty,--pronounced, with terrible earnestness [Cooke: emphasis], how inconceivable ..." The essay's "Eliot took three words as her text, God, Immortality and Duty" is a fair paraphrase of that frame.
- Myers had perhaps never heard a sterner statement of an impersonal law that promised no reward. **Verified as paraphrase** (Morley, Cooke, search result quoting Wikiquote; see "Wording available for restoring").
- Eliot's novels morally serious, about small choices adding up to a life and selfishness as a kind of blindness. **Characterization, supported.** *Middlemarch* (PG 145), ch. 21: "We are all of us born in moral stupidity, taking the world as an udder to feed our supreme selves"; Finale: "the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts."
- "She did not think duty needed heaven to back it up. She thought it bound her anyway, absolutely." Reading of Myers's account (the second and third "scrolls of promise" withdrawn, the third left "awful with inevitable fates"). Supported by the same passage.
