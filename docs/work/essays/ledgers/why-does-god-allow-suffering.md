## Lisbon, 1755

- Earthquake on the morning of November 1, 1755, All Saints' Day, while many churches were full for Mass; churches collapsed on worshipers. Source: Britannica ("a large part of the population was attending mass... the churches... collapsed, killing or injuring thousands of worshippers"); EBSCO Research Starters. Search.
- Tsunami about forty minutes later, up the Tagus into the lower city. Source: Wikipedia "1755 Lisbon earthquake" as summarized (wave roughly 40 minutes after the shaking, rushing up the Tagus). Search.
- Fires started by candles and cooking fires burned for days in districts the water did not reach (Wikipedia: five days). Search. The essay says "fallen candles and kitchen hearths" and "for days".
- Dead estimated at about ten thousand to sixty thousand. Sources vary: HISTORY.com 10,000 to 50,000; Wikipedia 30,000 to 50,000 (one version 40,000 to 50,000); Britannica about 60,000 in Lisbon alone; EBSCO 5,000 to over 50,000; an older mirror 60,000 to 100,000. Search. The essay's range is a fair middle of these; verifier may prefer "tens of thousands".
- Lisbon "one of the great Catholic cities of Europe." General characterization. Unverified.
- Gabriel Malagrida, Jesuit, published a pamphlet in 1756 (Juízo da verdadeira causa do terramoto) blaming the earthquake on the city's sins rather than natural causes, and urged processions and penance, opposing the rebuilding work. Source: Wikipedia "Gabriel Malagrida"; archive.org listing of the 1756 pamphlet; Unicamp paper "Paths of Providence." Search.
- Voltaire's long poem (Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne), composed December 1755, published 1756, about 180 lines, against the optimism of "all is well" and the best of all possible worlds. Source: Wikipedia article on the poem; CliffsNotes essay. Search. Essay gives no date for the poem.
- Voltaire asked what crime the children had committed who lay crushed and bleeding on their mothers' breasts. Paraphrase, no quotation marks. Source: translations quoted in search results (Eli Siegel: "What crimes, what bad things have been committed by these children, / Lying on the breasts of their mothers, flattened and bloody?"; Smollett: "babes who on their mothers' bosoms bleed"). Search.

## The argument from Epicurus to Rowe

- The argument is usually credited to Epicurus but survives in nothing Epicurus wrote. Source: Wikipedia "Epicurean paradox" and other secondary sources (the four-fold form is known from Lactantius; Sextus Empiricus has related, earlier arguments; some scholars suggest an Academic skeptic such as Carneades, per Larrimore's reader). Search. Partially verified. The essay says "best-known form", not "earliest".
- Epicurus taught in Athens about three hundred years before Christ (341 to 270 BC; school founded about 306 BC). Unverified (textbook).
- Lactantius quoted it in A Treatise on the Anger of God (De Ira Dei), chapter XIII, "Of the Advantage and Use of the World and of the Seasons," in order to answer it. WDB (ANF vol. 7). Chapter confirmed from the chapter headings in the file.
- "early in the fourth century." Standard dating of De Ira Dei about 313 to 316. Unverified this session.
- "a Christian teacher named Lactantius." Lactantius was a teacher of rhetoric and later tutor to Constantine's son Crispus. Unverified (textbook).
- Block quotation: "If He is willing and is unable, He is feeble, which is not in accordance with the character of God; if He is able and unwilling, He is envious, which is equally at variance with God; if He is neither willing nor able, He is both envious and feeble, and therefore not God; if He is both willing and able, which alone is suitable to God, from what source then are evils? or why does He not remove them?" WDB, verbatim (the ANF sentence before it, "God, he says, either wishes to take away evils...", is omitted, not altered).
- Lactantius's own answer: God does not remove evils because he gives wisdom, and virtue consists in enduring and overcoming them. WDB, same chapter ("He does not take them away, because He at the same time gives wisdom"; "no traces of virtue remain in man, the nature of which consists in enduring and overcoming the bitterness of evils"). Paraphrase.
- Hume, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (published 1779; Part X), a skeptic (Philo) remarks: "Epicurus's old questions are yet unanswered." Search (scholarly quotations; Wikipedia paraphrases it differently). Primary text not opened. Essay names the Dialogues but not Philo or the year.
- "Fourteen centuries later": about 315 to 1779. Arithmetic.
- Twentieth-century philosophers pressed the argument as a strict contradiction (J. L. Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence," Mind, 1955; not named in the essay). Unverified (textbook; the old essay's claim).
- Plantinga's free will defense (God, Freedom, and Evil, 1974; not named in the essay) persuaded many on both sides, atheists among them, that there is no contradiction. Source: Mackie, The Miracle of Theism (1982), p. 154, as quoted in secondary sources, concedes the problem "does not show the central doctrines of theism to be logically inconsistent"; Rowe 1979 and others share the view. Search. Partially verified.
- "God cannot make creatures truly free and also guarantee what they will choose." Plain summary of the free will defense. Interpretation.
- William Rowe, 1979, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism," American Philosophical Quarterly 16.4: 335–341. Search (futilitycloset excerpt with citation). Year verified; journal not named in the essay.
- Rowe an atheist (his "friendly atheism"). Search (Philosophy Now profile and Rowe 2006 article in results). Partially verified.
- The fawn: lightning, forest fire, horribly burned, lies in terrible agony for several days before death. Search (excerpt; Rowe's 2006 retelling says five days, the 1979 text "several days"). Paraphrase, no quotation marks.
- Rowe: so far as we can see, such suffering is pointless (1979, p. 337: "so far as we can see, the fawn's intense suffering is pointless"). Search. Used as unquoted paraphrase.
- Darwin to Asa Gray, letter of 22 May 1860: could not persuade himself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae to feed within the living bodies of caterpillars. Source: secondary quotations; Biodiversity Heritage Library catalog of the letter. Search. Paraphrase. Darwin Correspondence Project not opened. "Botanist" and "more than a century earlier" (1860 vs 1979): textbook and arithmetic.

## The classic answers

- C. S. Lewis, The Problem of Pain, 1940; a shared world needs fixed natural laws. Search (Marginalian write-up; chapter "Divine Omnipotence").
- Paraphrase of Lewis: try to remove every possibility of suffering that comes with a stable natural order and with free wills and you will find you have removed life itself. Lewis's sentence, as quoted (wording varies by site): "Try to exclude the possibility of suffering which the order of nature and the existence of free wills involve, and you find that you have excluded life itself." Search. Attributed paraphrase, no quotation marks.
- "The water that carries a boat can drown a child." Our own illustration, not Lewis's.
- Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, near the end of the second century: God could have made man perfect from the first, as a mother could give strong food to her infant, but the infant could not receive it. WDB (Against Heresies 4.38.1, ANF: "it was possible for God Himself to have made man perfect from the first, but man could not receive this [perfection], being as yet an infant"). Paraphrase. Dating (Against Heresies about 180): unverified (textbook).
- "courage and mercy can only be formed in a world with real danger in it": our summary of the soul-making view (John Hick's name for it, not named in the essay). Interpretation.
- Augustine, Enchiridion, chapter 11: God "would never permit the existence of anything evil among His works, if He were not so omnipotent and good that He can bring good even out of evil." Essay quotes "if He were not so omnipotent and good that He can bring good even out of evil." WDB (NPNF), verbatim.
