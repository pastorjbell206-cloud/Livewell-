## The Church's History

- George Whitefield, the best-known preacher of the First Great Awakening, campaigned to make slavery legal in Georgia. Verified. GITenberg, Tyerman vol. 2: Whitefield's letter to the Georgia Trustees, "GLOUCESTER, _December 6, 1748_," written when "the trustees were about to allow the employment of slaves in Georgia," argues "Georgia never can be a flourishing province, unless negroes are employed" and asks for "a limited use of negroes"; Tyerman vol. 1 says one public reason Mr. Seward, Whitefield's companion, returned to England was "To convince the Trustees of Georgia that three things were necessary to establish the Colony, viz.: 'an allowance of negroes, a free title to the lands, and an independent magistracy.'" Search (2026-10-27): the ban was lifted in 1751, partly through his campaigns and pleas to the Trustees (among the results: Wikipedia's Bethesda Academy entry, the Penn and Slavery Project's "George Whitefield", the Museum of Gloucester). "Best-known preacher" is a general judgment.
- Whitefield owned enslaved people whose labor supported his orphanage there (Bethesda). Verified. GITenberg, Tyerman vol. 2: his letter "it is impossible for the inhabitants to subsist without the use of slaves ... purchasing, in this province, a plantation and slaves, which I purpose to devote to the support of Bethesda ... One negro has been given me. Some more I purpose to purchase this week," and his later memorial offering "all lands, negroes, goods, and chattels, which he now stands possessed of in the Province of Georgia" for a Bethesda College. Search (2026-10-27): the results put his holdings at about 49 enslaved people at his death, passing with the orphanage and plantations to the Countess of Huntingdon.
- Jonathan Edwards bought and owned slaves. Verified, Search (2026-10-27): bill of sale dated June 7, 1731, Richard Perkins of Newport to Edwards, for "a Negro Girle named Venus aged Fourteen years or thereabout," £80 (transcription and Historic Northampton copy); Yale's Jonathan Edwards Center: "the first, a teenaged girl named Venus, he acquired in Newport in 1731"; Desiring God, "How Could Jonathan Edwards Own Slaves?".
- John Newton was converted in 1748. Verified, Search (2026-10-27): Wikipedia (John Newton) and Christianity.com place the conversion on the *Greyhound* in a storm in March 1748 (21 March, the day he later marked). GITenberg, Newton's *Thoughts*: "I arrived at Liverpool in May 1748."
- He kept sailing on slave ships for years afterward, first as a mate, then as a captain. Verified, GITenberg, Newton's *Thoughts*: "I soon revisited the place of my captivity, as mate of a ship, and, in the year 1750, I was appointed commander, in which capacity I made three voyages to the Windward Coast, for Slaves. I first saw the Coast of Guinea in the year 1745, and took my last leave of it in 1754." Search (2026-10-27): Royal Museums Greenwich catalogue names the *Brownlow* (mate) and the *Duke of Argyle* and *African* (captain).
- He didn't publicly oppose the trade until 1788. Verified, GITenberg, Newton's *Thoughts*: imprint "LONDON ... M.DCC.LXXXVIII."; he writes that silence "would, in me, be criminal," calls the pamphlet "a public confession, which, however sincere, comes too late," and says of his years in the trade "I never had a scruple upon this head at the time." Search (2026-10-27): Britannica calls it "a repentant confession of his personal involvement."
- Before the Civil War, Southern pulpits produced a literature defending slavery from Scripture. Verified with examples: GITenberg, *Cotton is King* (Augusta, Georgia, 1860), an anthology of "Pro-Slavery Arguments" including Stringfellow's "The Bible Argument" and Hodge's "Bible Argument on Slavery," whose editor calls those two "a synopsis of the whole theological argument on the subject"; GITenberg, Rev. Fred. A. Ross, D.D., *Slavery Ordained of God* (speeches and letters, epigraph Romans 13:1).
- Thornton Stringfellow, a Virginia Baptist minister, wrote some of the most widely read of it, building on Abraham's household, Leviticus 25 and Paul. Partly verified. GITenberg, *Cotton is King*: "THE BIBLE ARGUMENT: OR, SLAVERY IN THE LIGHT OF DIVINE REVELATION. BY THORNTON STRINGFELLOW, D. D., OF RICHMOND, VIRGINIA," which sets out to show slavery had "the sanction of the Almighty in the Patriarchal age," was "incorporated into the only National Constitution which ever emanated from God," and was recognized "by Jesus Christ in his kingdom"; it treats Abraham at length, quotes "Levit. xxv: 44, 45, 46" and "Levit. xxv: 39 ... 43," and cites Paul's letter to the Ephesians. - Noll's point that on the face-value, verse-by-verse reading most American Protestants had been taught, the defenders of slavery often had the easier argument, and abolitionists had to appeal to the spirit and direction of Scripture, which many devout people heard as evasion. - The Code of Hammurabi, from Babylon some eighteen centuries before Christ, made it a capital crime to help a slave out through the city gate or to hide a runaway in your house. Verified. GITenberg, Johns translation: section 15, "If a man has caused either a palace slave or palace maid, or a slave of a poor man or a poor man's maid, to go out of the gate, he shall be put to death"; section 16, harboring a fugitive "in his house" and not producing him at the commandant's demand, "the owner of that house shall be put to death"; section 19, "If he confine that slave in his house, and afterwards the slave has been seized in his hand, that man shall be put to death." Search (2026-10-27): the Yale Avalon translation and the others returned (Oregon State, Washington State, Macmillan, Liberty Fund) agree on the death penalty in 15, 16 and 19. Date: Johns's 1903 title page uses an outdated chronology (B.C. 2285–2242); Search (2026-10-27) gives about 1754 BC (Middle Chronology), with Wikipedia giving c. 1755–1750 BC, which is the eighteenth century before Christ. Wikipedia lists the stele's original location as Sippar; "from Babylon" names Hammurabi's kingdom, not the find spot.
- Deuteronomy 23:15–16, "You shall not give up to his master a slave who has escaped from his master to you. He shall dwell with you, in your midst, in the place that he shall choose within one of your towns, wherever it suits him." ESV copy, re-read: matched. BSB confirms.
- The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 required Americans to help return escaped slaves. Verified, Search (2026-10-27): section 5 of the act commands that "all good citizens are hereby commanded to aid and assist in the prompt and efficient execution of this law, whenever their services may be required" (Fordham Modern History Sourcebook, University of Groningen, Mass Moments and the National Underground Railroad Freedom Center all reproduce the clause). GITenberg, Child (1860), an appeal to Massachusetts against the act, confirms the duty from the other side.
- Its defenders liked to cite Paul sending Onesimus home. Verified, GITenberg, Child, *The Duty of Disobedience to the Fugitive Slave Act* (1860): "If you turn to the favorite New Testament argument, you will find that Paul requested Philemon to receive Onesimus, 'no longer as a servant, but as a brother beloved.' That is also the essay's point about what Paul asked of Philemon.
- Deuteronomy 5:14–15, rest for the male and female servant "as well as you," and "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt." ESV copy, re-read: both phrases matched. BSB confirms the sense.

## How Abolition Actually Happened

- Gregory of Nyssa, in the late fourth century, was the first Christian writer we know of to condemn slavery itself and not just its cruelties. Partly verified. Late fourth century: GITenberg, *Encyclopaedia Britannica* (1911), "GREGORY, ST, OF NYSSA (c. 331-c. 396)," ordained bishop of Nyssa "in 371 or 372," with his homilies on "the first three Chapters of Ecclesiastes" among his works. That he condemned slavery itself: Commentaries library (both renderings attack a man who thinks himself "the owner of men and women" and say that "by dividing the human species in two with 'slavery' and 'ownership'" he has enslaved the species to itself). That he was the first Christian writer known to do so is a historian's judgment no source in this run stated; it stays hedged "we know of" and is listed under Still open.
- He preached on Ecclesiastes 2:7, "I bought male and female slaves." ESV copy, re-read: matched. BSB confirms the verse. Commentaries library: the file for Ecclesiastes 2:7 is titled "Homilies on Ecclesiastes 4" and treats that verse.
- Gregory asked his congregation what price they thought they had paid for a creature made in the image of God. Paraphrase, not quoted. Verified as paraphrase. Search (2026-10-27): Roger Pearse's post "Gregory of Nyssa fails to adapt to then contemporary attitudes on slavery" quotes the passage from the Hall and Moriarty translation (de Gruyter, 1993, p. 74); the search summary of it says Gregory mocks the buyer for putting a price on human nature and reason and argues that a being made in God's image cannot rightly be sold; a 2024 *New Blackfriars* article (Breedlove and Fogleman) ties his rejection of slavery to the freedom proper to creatures made according to the divine image. Commentaries library: "What will the buyer pay, and what will the vendor accept." The Hall translation's own sentence was not seen, so nothing from it is quoted.
- The church didn't follow him for well over a thousand years. Still open as a general judgment. The span is checked: Gregory died c. 396 (Britannica, above), and the Germantown protest of 1688 (below) is called by Du Bois "one of the first American protests against the slave-trade," about thirteen centuries later.
- Quakers in Germantown, Pennsylvania, protested slavery in 1688 on the strength of the Golden Rule. Verified, GITenberg, Du Bois: "One of the first American protests against the slave-trade came from certain German Friends, in 1688, at a Weekly Meeting held in Germantown, Pennsylvania," quoting the protest: "Is there any that would be done or handled at this manner? ... There is a saying, that we shall doe to all men like as we will be done ourselves; making no difference of what generation, descent or colour they are." Search (2026-10-27): Wikipedia, the National Park Service and Teaching American History agree (signers Pastorius, Hendericks and the op den Graeffs).
- Newton's 1788 pamphlet was a confession; he had been a Christian for forty years. Verified: GITenberg, Newton's *Thoughts* (1788 imprint) calls itself "a public confession"; 1748 to 1788 is forty years.
- William Wilberforce's evangelical conversion came in 1785. Verified, GITenberg, *Private Papers*: "Wilberforce had written to Pitt to tell him of the change in his religious opinions," and Pitt's reply is dated "DOWNING STREET, _December 2, 1785_," speaking of "what has the appearance of a new æra in your life."
- Two years later he wrote in his diary that God Almighty had set before him two great objects, the suppression of the slave trade and the reformation of manners. Marked paraphrase. Verified as paraphrase, Search (2026-10-27): secondary sources date the entry October 28, 1787 and give the two objects as the suppression of the slave trade and the reformation of manners (see "Original wording, for restoring"). 1785 to 1787 is "two years later."
- He brought the cause back to Parliament for nearly twenty years before the trade was abolished in 1807. Verified, GITenberg, Clarkson vol. 2: chapter I ("Continuation from June 1788 to July 1789"): "At length, the twelfth of May arrived. Mr. Wilberforce rose up in the Commons," moving to take into consideration "the petitions, which had been presented against the Slave-trade"; chapter X ("from July 1806 to March 1807 ... receives the royal assent"): royal assent "on Wednesday the twenty-fifth" as "the clock struck twelve." May 1789 to March 1807 is nearly eighteen years, so "nearly twenty" holds.
- In July 1833 he lived just long enough to hear the bill ending slavery across most of the British Empire was assured of passage, and died a few days later. Verified as now worded. GITenberg, Trevelyan: after the second reading carried and the ministry cut apprenticeship from twelve years to seven, Macaulay's letter is "dated the 27th of July"; "On that day week, Wilberforce was carried to his grave in Westminster Abbey ... He died with the promised land full in view. Before the end of August Parliament abolished slavery." Macaulay to his sister, "London: July 31, 1833": "So Wilberforce is gone! ... He was cheerful and full of anecdote only last Saturday." The exact day the news reached him was not found, so "three days later" became "a few days later" (see Changes). "Most of the British Empire" is the cautious word; the act's exclusions were not checked and the claim is true either way.
- The enslaved heard Exodus read, often by preachers hired to teach them obedience, and sang "Go Down, Moses." Partly verified. GITenberg, WPA Georgia narratives: a white preacher "preached to the slaves each Sunday ... The salary of this preacher was paid by Mr. George Allen"; another plantation's slaves heard the text "Obey your masters and your mistresses and your backs won't be whipped." The song title "Go Down, Moses" was not checked against a source and is listed under Still open.
- Douglass: "I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land." Gutenberg (Douglass), re-read: matched word for word, colon included.
- Enlightenment natural-rights arguments, economics and war were other engines of abolition. Still open: a general statement; no source opened.
- The revolt of the enslaved in Saint-Domingue began in 1791 and ended in an independent Haiti. Verified, GITenberg, St. John, *Hayti*: after the colonial assembly met "on the 1st August 1791," "the negroes in the northern province rose in insurrection"; "At Gonaives, on the 1st January 1804, General Dessalines ... had read to them the Act of Independence."
