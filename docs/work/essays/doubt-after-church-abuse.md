# Doubt After Being Abused by a Church Leader

*Abuse by a pastor or church leader often leaves a person doubting God himself, and the Bible has a great deal to say to that doubt.*

If a pastor, priest, youth leader, elder or ministry head used his position to hurt you, you may have found that the damage did not stay where he put it. It followed you into prayer. It is there when a sermon borrows one of his phrases or a song he loved comes on in the car. Some people in your place stopped believing in God. Others still believe but cannot pray without hearing his voice. The questions come in a run, usually at night. Was any of it real? If God was there, why didn't he stop it? If the man who taught me to trust God was a fraud, how do I know God isn't one too? And why did the people who should have protected me protect him?

Most of the leaders in the public cases have been men, and I will write that way. But a woman can do this too, and the person it is done to can be a man or a boy. Whoever you are, what was done to you was not your fault. The doubt that followed is not proof that your faith was weak. It is what this kind of harm does, and the Bible describes it in the opening chapters of 1 Samuel.

First, a few plain things. If you are in danger, or a child is, the first call is to the police or to child protective services, not to the church. Abuse by a church leader should be reported to the authorities, whatever anyone tells you about handling it internally. If the darkness has turned toward not wanting to be alive, call or text 988 in the US to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline tonight. The [Find Help](/help) page lists other places to start. Nothing here is legal or counseling advice.

## What Happened at Shiloh

Early in 1 Samuel, before Israel had a king, the worship of God was centered at Shiloh, where the ark of the covenant was kept. An old priest named Eli presided there, and his sons Hophni and Phinehas served as priests beside him. The narrator does not soften what the sons were: "Now the sons of Eli were worthless men. They did not know the LORD" (1 Samuel 2:12). When Israelites came to sacrifice, the priests' servant took the meat before the fat had been burned to God and threatened to take it by force if anyone objected (1 Samuel 2:13–16). The brothers also "lay with the women who were serving at the entrance to the tent of meeting" (1 Samuel 2:22).

The narrator's judgment is one sentence: "Thus the sin of the young men was very great in the sight of the LORD, for the men treated the offering of the LORD with contempt" (1 Samuel 2:17). Many modern translations take the contempt to be the priests' own. The King James Version reads it another way: "for men abhorred the offering of the LORD." The Hebrew uses a different word for these men than for the young priests, and the older translators read them as the worshipers, who came to despise worship itself because of what the priests were doing. Either reading is true to what abuse at the altar does: it teaches people to recoil from God.

Eli knew. The text says he "kept hearing all that his sons were doing to all Israel." He rebuked them with words, they ignored him, and he did nothing more (1 Samuel 2:22–25). The sons stayed at the altar. The women at the door of the tent were left to them. When God finally spoke, he named the father's failure alongside the sons' sin. He would judge Eli's house "for the iniquity that he knew, because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them" (1 Samuel 3:13).

The same chapter opens with a line anyone who has lived through this will recognize: "And the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision" (1 Samuel 3:1). God seemed silent in the one place built to hear him. When he spoke again, he did not speak to the high priest. He called a boy named Samuel, who served in that compromised house and slept in the temple where the ark was (1 Samuel 3:3–4). The word that broke the silence came to the person with the least standing in the place. It was about what the priests had done (1 Samuel 3:11–14).

The story does not answer every question a survivor carries. It does settle one. God took sides. He was against the priests who used his altar to take what they wanted, and he held the old man who knew responsible as well. Whatever your abuser told you about God being on his side, Scripture said otherwise long before he was born.

## Why This Harm Reaches All the Way to God

Most betrayals cost you a person. This kind costs more because of where the man stood. He spoke in God's name, and he may have been the one who taught you how to listen for God in the first place. He read you the Bible and prayed over you. When a man in that place uses it to exploit you, the harm attaches itself to everything he touched. Prayer starts to sound like him. Scripture reads like his instructions. A church building can set off something in your body before your mind catches up. That is not weakness, and it is not bitterness.

The word abuse should keep its force. Not every hurt a church causes is abuse. Pastors make poor decisions, and churches split over budgets. Those wounds are real. They are not what happened at Shiloh. Abuse is power used against the person it was given to protect: a leader using his position, his access or God's name to control, exploit or harm someone in his care. It can be sexual. It can also be spiritual, and that kind is harder to see.

David Johnson, a pastor, and Jeff VanVonderen, a counselor, wrote a book about it called *The Subtle Power of Spiritual Abuse*. They described it as mistreating someone who came for help in a way that leaves that person weaker and less able to stand before God. They noticed that abusive churches run on unspoken rules, and that the strongest is a rule against talking about the real problem. Whoever names it becomes the problem.

When a pastor draws a woman he is counseling or shepherding into a sexual relationship, churches have often called it an affair, as though two equals had fallen together. They were not equals. He had her trust because of his office, and he used what she told him in confidence. If that was you, the responsibility lies with the one who held the office.

## How True Doctrine Gets Turned Into a Weapon

Spiritual abuse is usually done with true things, which is what makes it so disorienting. An abuser rarely needs heresy. He needs the doctrine of authority, the call to submission, the command to forgive, the warnings against gossip and division, and a congregation that trusts those teachings enough not to ask what they are being used for. He fuses himself to God so closely that disagreeing with him feels like disobeying God. The person who raises a concern is told she has a rebellious spirit. The person who leaves is told he has stepped out from under spiritual covering.

One verse gets used this way more than most. "Touch not mine anointed," in the King James wording, is quoted to people who question a pastor as though it meant ministers may not be criticized. The psalm it comes from is retelling the story of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, when "they were few in number, of little account, and sojourners" in Canaan (Psalm 105:12). God "allowed no one to oppress them; he rebuked kings on their account, saying, 'Touch not my anointed ones, do my prophets no harm!'" (Psalm 105:14–15). The anointed ones are a family of wanderers with no land and no army. The ones being warned are kings. Used to silence a congregant her pastor has harmed, a verse about God shielding the weak from the powerful is turned upside down.

The David story does not say what it is used to say either. David refused to kill King Saul because Saul was "the LORD's anointed" (1 Samuel 24:6). He never took that to mean he had to stay. When Saul "sought to pin David to the wall with the spear," David "fled and escaped that night" (1 Samuel 19:10), and he stayed out of Saul's reach for the rest of Saul's life. When the chance came, he told Saul to his face that he had done him no wrong (1 Samuel 24:9–15). Scripture never calls any of that rebellion. If you left a church to get away from a man who was hurting you, you did what Israel's anointed king did.

Jesus named this kind of leadership and forbade it to his followers: "You know that those who are considered rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. But it shall not be so among you" (Mark 10:42–43). Peter told elders to shepherd the flock "not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock" (1 Peter 5:3). Jesus kept some of his hardest words for religious leaders who "tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4). The man who hurt you had to preach past all of it.

## What the Church Did When It Knew

For a long time many Protestants treated clergy abuse as a Catholic problem. In January 2002 the Boston Globe's Spotlight team began publishing its reporting on John Geoghan, a priest accused of abusing children in parish after parish. Archdiocesan officials had known, and they kept moving him anyway. Cardinal Bernard Law resigned as archbishop of Boston that December. Protestants watching often told themselves the problem was celibacy, or hierarchy, or a clerical culture they didn't share.

They were wrong. In February 2019 the Houston Chronicle and the San Antonio Express-News published "Abuse of Faith." Their investigation found that roughly 380 Southern Baptist church leaders and volunteers had faced allegations of sexual misconduct since 1998, with more than 700 victims, many of them children. In May 2022 an independent investigation by Guidepost Solutions, which the Convention's messengers had voted for the year before, reported that Executive Committee staff had for years kept a private list of ministers accused of abuse. Meanwhile the denomination's leaders resisted calls for a database of abusers on the grounds that the independence of local churches made one impossible.

Baptist churches, mine included, prize that independence, and the conviction has an honorable history. The report showed what it becomes when protecting the institution comes first. It becomes a reason for no one to be responsible.

Some of the accused were defenders of the faith itself. Ravi Zacharias was one of the best-known Christian apologists in the world when he died in May 2020. The following February an investigation commissioned by his own ministry reported that he had sexually abused massage therapists over a period of years. People who had held on to their faith partly through his books had to ask what that meant.

Most of the people who cover for abusers are not cynics. They love their church and fear what a scandal will do to its witness. They believe in grace and restoration and do not want to ruin a man over an accusation they cannot prove. They have seen rumors destroy innocent people. Those are real goods. Eli loved his sons, and he did rebuke them. He would not restrain them.

The psychiatrist Judith Herman, in *Trauma and Recovery*, noticed how little a perpetrator needs from the people around him: "All the perpetrator asks is that the bystander do nothing." The victim asks much more. Most of the failures in these reports were not committed by abusers. They were committed by bystanders, many of them sincere. For a believer, the bystanders' betrayal often lands closer to God than the abuser's did, because it came from the body and not from one man.

A skeptic will say this is all too convenient. If a faith's central texts can be bent this far, this often, maybe the bending is the faith working as designed. A religion that teaches submission, trains people to distrust their own hearts and lets a few men speak for God is going to produce abusers. Pointing to the verses they skipped does not change what the system rewards. That objection has real force. Churches did build cultures that taught people to defer and to fear what speaking would cost. The Boston and Southern Baptist reports were not about a few verses misread. They were about institutions that made silence easy.

But the objection has to set some things aside. The sharpest words in the Bible against abusive religious authority do not come from its critics. They come from its prophets, and from Jesus, who was handed over to death by the religious leaders of his day. The church had those texts the whole time. Abuse also flourishes where there is no religion at all, wherever adults hold unchecked power over people who trust them. That should give a Christian no comfort. The church claims to know better, and it has the Scriptures that say so.

It would be easy for the rest of us to read all of this as other people's failures. We can't. Every pastor knows the pull to protect a church's name and handle things quietly, and to assume the best about a colleague because the alternative is too costly to think about. I hold the same office these men held, and I am not immune to it. That pull is not wisdom. It was Eli's sin, and the women at the door of the tent paid for it. When we choose a church's reputation over the person in front of us, we have decided that the name of Jesus needs protecting more than the people he died for. Scripture never trusted leaders to correct themselves, and [it builds correction in from outside](/writing/the-bible-assumes-you-will-be-wrong) for that reason.

## Was Any of It Real?

Underneath the questions about the church there is usually a more frightening one. If he was a fraud, was my faith a fraud? Was my baptism real? Were the prayers I prayed under his teaching real, or was I being worked on? Did God ever speak to me, or was it always him?

The church has faced a version of that question before. In North Africa in the early fourth century, after the persecution under the emperor Diocletian, some clergy had handed over copies of Scripture to the authorities to save their lives. A party later called the Donatists held that baptisms and ordinations performed by such men were worthless. Their sin was cowardice, not abuse, but the question is the same. Does a minister's sin reach back and undo what God did through him? Augustine of Hippo spent years arguing that it does not, because baptism belongs to Christ and not to the man who performs it.

The Reformers kept that answer. The Second Helvetic Confession, written in the 1560s by the Zurich reformer Heinrich Bullinger, teaches that the sacraments remain effectual for believers even when unworthy ministers administer them. Then it turns to the ministers. Offenders who cannot be corrected "are to be deposed, and like wolves driven away" from the flock. Both halves matter for you. What you received of Christ was Christ's. If you came to trust him under that man's preaching, the trust was real because the one you trusted is real. The man himself should have been driven off like a wolf.

God was not absent from that kind of room, either. The Son of God was handed over by the men who held the highest religious offices of his people. On the night of his arrest the chief priests and the whole council looked for false testimony against him (Matthew 26:59). Then, in the high priest's house, "some began to spit on him and to cover his face and to strike him, saying to him, 'Prophesy!'" (Mark 14:65). Men who claimed to speak for God beat him in the house of God's high priest. Whatever was done to you in God's name, Jesus knows it from the inside.

I won't try to argue your doubt away, and I won't rush you. I would only ask you not to let the man who hurt you decide who God is. He has taken enough. If you are taking your faith apart to see what can survive, [that work is worth doing carefully](/writing/excavation-not-demolition), and some of what you tear down will deserve it. But he does not get the last word about God.

## Forgiveness and Reporting

Someone has probably told you to forgive already. You may have heard it in the same meeting where you reported what happened, before anyone asked whether you were safe. Forgiveness is at the heart of the Christian faith. In these cases, though, the command to forgive has often been used to mean something Scripture never meant by it: be quiet, drop it, let him keep his position, and stop making this hard for everyone else.

Jesus said, "If your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him" (Luke 17:3). The rebuke comes first. Repentance is something the wrongdoer does, not something the victim supplies for him. Forgiveness in Scripture means giving up your claim to pay someone back yourself. It does not mean pretending nothing happened. It is not reconciliation, which needs repentance and trust rebuilt over time, and it is never restoration to the position he abused. A man can be forgiven and still never be allowed near children again. He can be forgiven and still go to prison.

Paul puts the two side by side. "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19). A few sentences later he calls the governing authority "the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4). The person who reports abuse to the police is not taking revenge. She is handing the matter to the authority Paul says God appointed for it.

Churches that discourage reporting sometimes cite 1 Corinthians 6, where Paul tells believers not to sue one another. He is talking there about what he calls "trivial cases" (1 Corinthians 6:2). Crimes against children and vulnerable adults are not trivial cases, and the church was never told to hide them from the authority God put in place to punish them.

Rachael Denhollander, a former gymnast who went public in 2016 with her accusation against the sports doctor Larry Nassar, showed what this looks like in a Michigan courtroom in January 2018. Nassar was not a pastor, but she spoke to him as a Christian. She told him she forgave him, and that if he ever faced what he had done, the gospel offered him a grace he needed far more than her forgiveness. She also asked the judge for the maximum sentence. She saw no contradiction between the two, and the New Testament sees none either.

What would it look like for a church to take this seriously? Any allegation involving a child goes to the police or child protective services before anyone in the church investigates anything. In many states clergy are required by law to report suspected child abuse, and a church should not need the law to tell it so. Accusations against leaders are investigated by people who do not answer to those leaders. No survivor is asked to sign a nondisclosure agreement as the price of help. A leader found guilty does not return to ministry. These steps are not complicated, only costly, and the cost falls on the church instead of on the person who was harmed, which is where it belongs.

## If You Are Carrying This Now

If you were abused, the order matters: safety first, then help, then the slower questions. Putting distance between yourself and the man who hurt you, and the system that protected him, is not a failure of faith. A counselor who understands trauma, and ideally what religious abuse does, can help you in ways a pastor can't. Herman describes recovery as beginning with safety, moving through remembering and mourning, and coming at last to reconnection with ordinary life and other people. She insists it cannot happen in isolation. That is a hard word for someone a community has failed, and a hopeful one.

You may need to stay away from church for a long time. You may need a different church entirely, one where the leaders answer to someone and the vulnerable are believed, before you can sit in a pew without your body going cold. You can still be a Christian while that is true. Several psalms are headed as David's prayers from the years he spent running from Saul. Psalm 57 is one of them, a prayer "when he fled from Saul, in the cave." Some of the most honest prayers in the Bible come from a man who could not go home.

If you can't pray in the words you used to use, the psalms have others. Psalm 55 is the prayer of someone betrayed by a man he worshiped beside: "We used to take sweet counsel together; within God's house we walked in the throng" (Psalm 55:14). It describes the betrayer exactly: "His speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart; his words were softer than oil, yet they were drawn swords" (Psalm 55:21). You are allowed to pray like that. Those words are in the Bible because people in your place needed them. And if God feels far away for a long time, [the church has known that darkness before](/writing/why-does-god-feel-far-away), and it has never meant that he was gone.

## What God Promised the Scattered Sheep

Ezekiel 34 was written to people whose shepherds had failed them. God charges Israel's leaders with feeding themselves instead of the flock: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4). Then he says, "Behold, I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand" (Ezekiel 34:10). And then he promises what no reform of the shepherds could deliver: "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (Ezekiel 34:15).

When Jesus called himself the good shepherd, he was claiming that promise. He set himself against the thief, who "comes only to steal and kill and destroy," and against the hired hand who sees the wolf coming and runs (John 10:10–12). "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The shepherd who hurt you fed himself on the flock. The Shepherd he claimed to represent let himself be struck and spat on in the high priest's house, gave his life for the sheep, and was raised from the dead.

If a man who spoke in God's name hurt you, God has already said whose side he is on: "Behold, I am against the shepherds."

The rest of us, the church that knew or chose not to know, have Eli's story to answer for. There is still time to do what he would not.

And the Shepherd who laid down his life for the sheep is still looking for the ones that were scattered. He said he would bind up the injured himself. He keeps his word.

*"I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak."* (Ezekiel 34:16)

*"The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit."* (Psalm 34:18)

*"Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you; he will never permit the righteous to be moved."* (Psalm 55:22)
