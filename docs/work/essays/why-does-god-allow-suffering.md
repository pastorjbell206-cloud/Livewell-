# Why Does God Allow Suffering?

*Suffering is the hardest objection to the Christian faith, and the Bible faces it more squarely than many of its defenders do.*

On the morning of November 1, 1755, All Saints' Day, an earthquake struck Lisbon while many of its churches were full for Mass. The roofs came down on the worshipers. Within the hour a tsunami surged up the Tagus into the lower city, and fires started by fallen candles and kitchen hearths burned for days in the districts the water didn't reach. Estimates of the dead still vary widely, into the tens of thousands. Lisbon was one of the great Catholic cities of Europe, and it fell on a holy day with its people at prayer.

The explanations came quickly. A Jesuit preacher named Gabriel Malagrida published a pamphlet insisting that the cause was not nature but the city's sins, and he urged Lisbon toward processions and penance before rebuilding. Voltaire took aim at a different explanation. He wrote a long poem against the philosophers who had taught Europe that this was the best of all possible worlds, and he asked what crime had been committed by the children who lay crushed and bleeding on their mothers' breasts. Rousseau answered Voltaire with a more practical point. Nature, he said, had not crowded thousands of tall houses onto that spot. Much of what people called an act of God had been arranged by people.

Versions of those answers are still with us. When a child dies, someone at the funeral home will say that God needed another angel, or that everything happens for a reason, and mean it kindly. And someone else, often a person who used to sit in church, will look at the same small casket and decide that a good and powerful God would never have allowed it. Underneath all of them is one question. If God is good, and if he is able, why does he allow this?

## The Oldest Form of the Argument

The argument is usually credited to Epicurus, who taught in Athens about three hundred years before Christ, but it survives in nothing Epicurus wrote. We have it because a Christian teacher named Lactantius quoted it early in the fourth century, in a treatise on the anger of God, so that he could answer it:

> "If He is willing and is unable, He is feeble, which is not in accordance with the character of God; if He is able and unwilling, He is envious, which is equally at variance with God; if He is neither willing nor able, He is both envious and feeble, and therefore not God; if He is both willing and able, which alone is suitable to God, from what source then are evils? or why does He not remove them?"

The best-known form of the case against God came down to us, then, in a Christian book, copied out in full by a man who took it seriously. His own answer was that God allows evils so that human beings can gain wisdom and virtue, which only grow where there is something hard to overcome. It's a real answer, though it doesn't reach very far into a hospital room.

Fourteen centuries later David Hume had a skeptic in his *Dialogues* remark that "Epicurus's old questions are yet unanswered." In the twentieth century philosophers pressed them as a strict contradiction, until Alvin Plantinga's free will defense persuaded many on both sides, atheists among them, that there is none. His argument was that God cannot make creatures truly free and also guarantee what they will choose.

So the argument changed shape. In 1979 the atheist philosopher William Rowe asked his readers to imagine a fawn caught in a forest fire started by lightning, horribly burned, lying in terrible agony for several days before it dies. Rowe's claim was that a great deal of suffering appears, so far as we can see, to be pointless, which makes a good and all-powerful God hard to believe in. Darwin had felt that weight more than a century earlier. In an 1860 letter to the botanist Asa Gray, he wrote that he could not persuade himself a good and all-powerful God would have designed wasps that feed inside the living bodies of caterpillars.

Then there are the children. In *The Brothers Karamazov*, published in 1880, Dostoevsky gave Ivan Karamazov a chapter called "Rebellion," in which Ivan tells his brother Alyosha about a general who set his hounds on an eight-year-old serf boy in front of the boy's mother, and about a five-year-old girl shut all night in an outhouse in the freezing cold by her own parents. Ivan will allow that a final harmony may explain everything. He wants no part of one bought with that. "It's not God that I don't accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket." Dostoevsky was a believer, and he gave this argument its full strength.

The objection doesn't rest on a misunderstanding. It's built out of things anyone can see, and many of the answers Christians reach for first never touch it. I came to faith as an adult, out of atheism, and I have never thought the people who press this question are only being clever. Most of them aren't arguing at all. They're standing beside a hospital bed, or at a graveside a week after the funeral, wondering whether the God they were taught about is there, and if he is, whether he cares.

## What the Classic Answers Can Explain

Christian thinkers have given several answers over the centuries, and they carry real weight. The first is the one Plantinga defended. Much of the world's suffering is something people do to each other. War, fraud, cruelty, the drunk driver, the father who walks out: none of it requires an earthquake. A God who made creatures able to love made creatures able to refuse love, and he doesn't take the gift back every time it's misused.

The second concerns nature itself. C. S. Lewis argued in *The Problem of Pain*, published in 1940, that a world where creatures can meet and act and depend on one another needs a nature with fixed laws, and fixed laws don't bend to spare whoever is in the way. The water that carries a boat can drown a child. Try to remove every possibility of suffering that comes with a stable natural order and with free wills, Lewis concluded, and you will find you have removed life itself.

The third is older. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon near the end of the second century, wrote that God could have made human beings perfect from the start, the way a mother could give solid food to a newborn, except that the infant couldn't take it. We were made young, in order to grow, and courage and mercy can only be formed in a world with real danger in it. Behind all of these stands Augustine's conviction that God would never permit evil "if He were not so omnipotent and good that He can bring good even out of evil."

These answers are true as far as they go, and a skeptic should know them before he decides the church has nothing to say. But look at how far they go. They explain why a world with some suffering in it might be better than a world with none. They don't explain this suffering, or tell a mother why her son, and why this year. Rowe's fawn is out of their reach, and so are the children in the Lisbon rubble, who weren't old enough for their souls to be made by anything. The theories work at the level of the whole world, and people suffer one at a time.

Lewis learned that himself. After his wife, Joy Davidman, died of cancer in July 1960, he published a short record of his grief, *A Grief Observed*, under the made-up name N. W. Clerk. He wrote that the real danger for him was coming to believe dreadful things about God. The conclusion he dreaded was not that there is no God after all, but that this is what God is really like. The arguments of 1940 weren't false, but they answered a different question from the one his grief was asking.

## What Job's Friends Got Wrong

The Bible saw all of this long before Lisbon. Job loses his flocks, his servants, and then his ten children in a single day, the children when a great wind collapses the house where they're eating together. Then his own body breaks out in sores. Three friends come to comfort him, and the first thing they do is the wisest thing they do in the whole book: "And they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great" (Job 2:13).

Then they start talking. Chapter after chapter, they explain Job's suffering to him, and their explanation is the one Malagrida gave Lisbon. God is just, so suffering must be deserved. Eliphaz begins gently: "Remember: who that was innocent ever perished? Or where were the upright cut off?" (Job 4:7). By chapter 22 he is accusing Job of stripping the poor of their clothing and sending widows away empty-handed, sins no one has seen him commit, because his theology needs Job to be guilty. Job refuses the charge. He says terrible things to God and about God, and he demands a hearing.

When God finally speaks, he takes Job's side: "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7). The men who defended God with a formula are rebuked. The man who argued with God to his face is called God's servant, and the friends are sent to Job so that he can pray for them.

God also never tells Job why. The reader knows something Job doesn't, because the first two chapters show a scene in the heavenly court in which Job's faithfulness is being tested, and God never mentions it to him. He answers out of the whirlwind with questions instead, about the foundations of the earth, the lion hunting its prey, the young ravens crying to God for food, and the eagle whose young feed where the slain lie. It's a tour of a wild creation, predators included, and God claims it as his own without explaining it.

That matters for our question. Job shows, inside Scripture, that a person can suffer for a reason that exists and is never told to him. That doesn't answer Rowe. It does mean the Bible never promised what the friends promised, that a sufferer will be able to see why. Job's last speech isn't a claim that he finally understands. "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5). He met God, and he could go on living without the explanation.

## Jesus and the Question of Blame

Jesus faced the friends' question at least twice. Told about Galileans whom Pilate had killed, he raised a disaster of his own, the eighteen people crushed when a tower fell in Siloam: "do you think that they were worse offenders than all the others who lived in Jerusalem? No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all likewise perish" (Luke 13:4–5). When his disciples asked about a man born blind, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" he rejected both options (John 9:2–3). He didn't deny that sin has consequences, but he refused to read anyone's guilt off the size of his suffering, and he turned the question back on the people asking it.

The church keeps going back to Job's friends anyway, in more than one direction. Some preachers still read hurricanes and pandemics as God's judgment on a nation, usually for somebody else's sins. The [prosperity preacher](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel) tells a sick woman that healing is hers if her faith is strong enough, so that if she stays sick, the failure is hers. Softer churches preach a God whose main concern is our happiness, and that God doesn't survive the first diagnosis. Each of these keeps suffering at a safe distance from the person explaining it. Most of us who have stood with a grieving family have felt their pull, because silence is hard to bear in a room like that.

Others take the opposite way out. Rabbi Harold Kushner's son Aaron was diagnosed as a small child with progeria, a disease of rapid aging, and died in his early teens. In *When Bad Things Happen to Good People*, published in 1981, Kushner concluded that God is wholly loving and just but limited in power, and millions of readers found relief in a book that refused to blame a child's death on God's will. But the Christian hope rests on a God with power over death itself. A God who wants to help and can't is kinder than the friends' God. He can't raise the dead.

## Is God in Control?

That leaves the sentence people hear at funerals: *God is in control.* It's true, and said at the wrong moment it sounds as though God picked out the tumor himself. Scripture means something more careful, and its clearest examples are events it calls evil.

After their father died, Joseph's brothers, who had sold him into slavery, came to him afraid. He told them, "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today" (Genesis 50:20). He doesn't call the evil secretly good, and he doesn't excuse his brothers. Two intentions ran through one event, and God's had the last word. Peter says the same about the cross. Jesus was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," and in the same sentence, "you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). The murder was real murder, and it was also the way God saved the world.

Paul says something close to this in a verse often quoted at funerals: "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28). He doesn't say all things are good. He wrote it to people who could expect "tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword" (Romans 8:35). The promise is that God is at work even in the worst things, and that he is working them toward good.

How God governs evil without being its author is a question faithful Christians have answered differently for centuries. The Reformed tradition holds that God ordains whatever comes to pass, including the evil acts of free creatures, while insisting, in the words of the Westminster Confession, that "neither is God the author of sin." The Wesleyan and Arminian traditions hold that God foreknows and permits evil without willing it, because he has given his creatures a real freedom he will not override. Baptists, my own tradition, have had churches on both sides since the seventeenth century. Both sides confess that God "cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one" (James 1:13), and that nothing escapes his rule. Beyond that, churches that share the gospel still differ.

Whichever view a Christian holds, the doctrine was never an explanation to hand a widow at the graveside. It's a promise she may lean on later, that what happened to her husband has not slipped out of God's hands. Said too early, by someone who hasn't first sat with her through the worst of it, it sounds like a sentence passed on her. We make that mistake of timing all the time.

## Natural Evil and Animal Suffering

Natural evil and the suffering of animals press harder still, because no human choice stands behind them. Paul's answer is that the creation itself isn't yet what it was made to be:

> "For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now." (Romans 8:20–22)

Voltaire was right that "all is well" is false, and Paul agrees with him. The Bible doesn't look at earthquakes and parasites and call the world finished. It calls the present order subjected and bound, in labor and waiting. Then Paul adds that "we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly" (Romans 8:23), and that when we don't know how to pray, "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). Paul doesn't place God outside the groaning, looking in.

Christians disagree, sometimes sharply, about how this fits the history of the natural world. Many read Genesis 3 and Romans 5:12 to mean that death of every kind, animal death included, entered the world through human sin, and most of them hold that the earth is young. Many others accept the scientific dating of the earth, conclude that animals lived and died long before human beings, and read the "futility" of Romans 8 as the condition of a creation made good but not yet complete. The creeds don't settle it, and faithful churches divide over it. Both readings refuse to call the present order final, and they share the same hope.

Isaiah saw a day when the wolf will dwell with the lamb, and "They shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain" (Isaiah 11:9). Rowe's fawn gets no explanation in this life. What Scripture supplies is a promise that the world in which fawns burn is not the world God means to keep.

## The Psalms of Lament

If the Bible doesn't give the sufferer an explanation, it gives him words. Dozens of psalms are complaints addressed straight to God. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). Psalm 88 never turns to praise. Its last line is "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18), and Israel kept it in the book it sang. These aren't failed prayers. God gave them to his people to pray, which means grief and even anger are things a believer may bring to him.

Many American churches have stopped praying this way. A person in the worst year of his life can sit through a whole service without hearing one line that sounds like his week, and without meaning to, we teach him that grief belongs somewhere else. So he takes it somewhere else, sometimes out the door. Jesus prayed differently. Dying, he prayed the first line of Psalm 22: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). The church's calendar keeps a whole day, [Holy Saturday](/writing/the-theology-of-saturday), for the grief between the cross and the empty tomb.

The Christian philosopher Nicholas Wolterstorff knew the arguments in this essay better than I do. His son Eric died young in a climbing accident, and the book Wolterstorff wrote afterward, *Lament for a Son*, offers no theodicy. He wrote that he had no explanation and could not fit the pieces together. He asked his friends not to tell him it wasn't really so bad, because it was. Death, he insisted, is awful, even demonic. The comforters he wanted were the ones willing to sit beside him in his grief.

That is the ministry Job's friends got right for seven days, and Paul made it a command: "Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15). It looks like a meal left on the porch, a name said out loud a year later when everyone else has stopped saying it, and Psalm 13 prayed with someone who can't pray anything else. It needs a church that doesn't require the grieving to recover on schedule, or to stop asking why.

If you're reading this in the middle of your own grief, you don't have to resolve any of it before you're allowed to pray. Bring the question to God in whatever words you have; the psalms give you permission. Bring it to a person too, a friend or a pastor who will sit with you. If the darkness has grown to the point where you wonder whether there's any reason to go on, reach out today: in the US, you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. [Find Help](/help) lists other places to start.

## The Cross and the Resurrection

The Christian answer finally rests on something the classic arguments leave out: God didn't stay outside the suffering. The church confesses that the eternal Son of God became a man, with a body that could be hurt, and that he grieved and was tortured and killed. When his friend Lazarus died, Jesus came to Bethany already intending to wake him. Yet when he saw Mary weeping, "he was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled" (John 11:33), and "Jesus wept" (John 11:35). He didn't give Mary an explanation. He wept with her, and then he called her brother out of the grave.

On the cross Jesus didn't explain the problem of evil. He took the worst of it into himself. The innocent suffered while the guilty went free, God seemed to have gone silent, and he prayed the psalm of the abandoned. On the third day the tomb was empty. [Whether that happened](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen) is the question everything in this essay finally hangs on. If it did, Christians can say that suffering is evil and also that it will not win. The resurrection doesn't tell us why the child died. It tells us what God means to do about death.

The Bible's last picture of suffering is a promise about what God will do with his own hands: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:4). The tears aren't dismissed. God wipes them away himself, which means he has seen every one.

I can't tell you why God allowed what happened to you, and I'd be wary of anyone who says he can. Job never found out. He was allowed to ask, though, and so are you.

If you aren't the one suffering this year, someone near you is. Will you sit on the ground with them and say nothing for a while, the way Job's friends did before they started explaining?

Lisbon heard that it had been judged, and it heard that all was well. Neither one was the gospel.

The hands that will wipe away every tear still carry the marks of the nails.

*"How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?"* (Psalm 13:1)

*"For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us."* (Romans 8:18)
