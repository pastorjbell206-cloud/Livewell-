# What Christian Nationalism Is, and What It Is Not

*Loving your country isn't the problem. An honest answer has to start there before it can name what is.*

"America is a Christian nation."

Depending on who says that sentence, it can mean at least four different things, and most arguments about Christian nationalism go wrong because nobody stops to ask which one is meant. It can be a statement about population: for nearly all of American history most Americans have called themselves Christians. It can be a statement about inheritance — that the moral vocabulary of the founding and a great deal of American law grew out of a culture shaped by Protestant Christianity, which is largely true, though historians argue about how much. It can be a theological claim, that God stands in a special covenant with this country, so that its rise is His blessing and its troubles are His judgment on a people who drifted from Him. Or it can be a claim about belonging: real Americans are Christians, the law should keep it that way, and everyone else is a guest in someone else's house. The first two are history. The last two are something else.

The phrase *Christian nationalism* is doing two jobs right now. For a lot of people it has become a general insult, a way of dismissing any churchgoer who votes conservative and prays for the country. For a smaller number it has become a badge. Stephen Wolfe published a book in 2022 called *The Case for Christian Nationalism*, and a few politicians now use the label about themselves with pride. Somewhere between the insult and the badge, the question most people are actually asking has gotten lost. What is Christian nationalism? And is it just a new name for being a Christian who loves America?

It isn't. But the place to start is with the person who suspects the phrase is aimed at him, because he has more on his side than his critics usually allow.

## A Love the Bible Assumes

The patriotic Christian's case goes something like this. Love of country is natural, he'd say, and Scripture treats it as natural. Jeremiah told the exiles in Babylon to build houses and plant gardens and to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7). If God's people were told to pray for Babylon, it's hard to see why they shouldn't pray for — and love — the place where they were born. Paul carried "great sorrow and unceasing anguish" for "my kinsmen according to the flesh" (Romans 9:2–3). Nations aren't a mistake to be outgrown. They're part of the way God has arranged human life.

Christianity runs all through the American story, he'd go on, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of distortion. Abolitionists argued from the Bible. In April 1963 Martin Luther King Jr. wrote from a Birmingham jail that he agreed with St. Augustine that "an unjust law is no law at all," and he made a Christian case, in public, for changing American law. If bringing Christian conviction into public life makes someone a Christian nationalist, then King was one. And the term has stopped meaning anything.

His sharpest point is about the phrase itself. It's now used so carelessly that it often amounts to bearing false witness, which the Ninth Commandment forbids in so many words (Exodus 20:16). Plenty of small congregations that feed the hungry and keep a flag near the pulpit have had the phrase thrown at them lately by people who have never walked through their doors.

All of that is right, or close enough that a Christian ought to grant it plainly. If the phrase names gratitude for a country's freedoms, honor for those who died defending them, or the conviction that faith has something to say in public, it should be retired.

## Ordered Love

The Christian tradition has a better tool for drawing the line than the slogans on either side. Augustine began *The City of God* after the Goths sacked Rome in 410, and in that long book he defined virtue in a single phrase: rightly ordered love. Family, city, friendship, and homeland were all real goods to Augustine, made by God. The question was whether those loves stood in their proper order — beneath the love of God and alongside the love of neighbor — or whether one of them had climbed to the top and begun demanding what only God can demand. Disordered love is usually love of a good thing in the wrong place.

George Orwell, who was no believer, drew the same line from the other side. In an essay he wrote in 1945, "Notes on Nationalism," he defined patriotism as "devotion to a particular place and a particular way of life, which one believes to be the best in the world but has no wish to force on other people." Nationalism, by contrast, was "inseparable from the desire for power," and its habit was to place the nation beyond good and evil. Patriotism, on that account, is content to be left alone. Nationalism needs to win. It hears criticism of the nation as betrayal.

Scripture gives the clearest picture of what the right kind of love looks like. As Jesus came down the Mount of Olives toward the city of His own people, Luke says, "he wept over it, saying, 'Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes'" (Luke 19:41–42). That is love of one's own country in its Christian form. It was real enough to bring Him to tears, and it still told the city the truth about itself, including the truth that judgment was coming. Jesus loved Jerusalem the way a Jew loves Jerusalem, and He would not let Jerusalem stand in the place of God.

That is where patriotism and Christian nationalism part ways. The difference has nothing to do with how warmly a person feels about the flag. It has to do with where that love sits in the order, and the simplest test is whether it can stand under judgment. Can your love of America hear the country criticized — by Scripture, or by its own history — without treating the criticism as an attack on God? Can you feel a deeper kinship with a believer in Nigeria than with a fellow citizen who shares your flag but not your faith? Ordered love can. The nationalism Orwell described can't, because it has already placed the nation beyond judgment.

## What the Sociologists Measured

The phrase also has a precise meaning in research, narrower than the insult. The sociologists Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry worked through years of national survey data, chiefly the 2017 Baylor Religion Survey. Their book, *Taking America Back for God*, came out in 2020, and it defines Christian nationalism as "a cultural framework—a collection of myths, traditions, symbols, narratives, and value systems—that idealizes and advocates a fusion of Christianity with American civic life." The word that carries the definition is *fusion*. Christians taking part in civic life isn't fusion. Fusion is what you have when the two identities can no longer be told apart.

They measured it with six statements, asking how strongly people agreed that the federal government should declare the United States a Christian nation, advocate Christian values, allow prayer in public schools, enforce strict separation of church and state, and so on. From the answers they sorted Americans into four groups. About one in five fully embraced the fusion. They called them Ambassadors. About a third leaned toward it — the Accommodators. The rest leaned against it or rejected it outright.

Critics have pushed back, and their objection has weight. Mark David Hall, a political scientist and himself a conservative Christian, wrote a book in 2024 called *Who's Afraid of Christian Nationalism?*, and he argues that some of the statements don't mean what the researchers assume. A person might want government to advocate Christian values because he has justice and freedom of conscience in mind, not an established church. A Baptist who supports voluntary school prayer may be thinking about religious liberty, not national identity. A survey that counts those people as nationalists has cast its net too wide.

But the most important thing in Whitehead and Perry's work doesn't depend on the exact headcount, and it ought to trouble Christians more than anyone. When they set Christian nationalism beside the ordinary marks of devotion — how often a person worships, prays, or reads the Bible — the two often pointed in different directions. On questions about immigrants, about racial and religious minorities, and about who counts as a real American, a high score on Christian nationalism predicted more suspicion of outsiders, while regular practice of the faith frequently predicted the opposite once the two were pulled apart. What the data describe is less a kind of devotion than a kind of belonging. It's a way of drawing the boundary of the nation in Christian vocabulary, and a person can hold it firmly while knowing very little of Christ.

Put plainly, then, Christian nationalism is a conviction held more as identity than as doctrine. That America is Christian in the covenant sense. That its real people are Christians. That its laws and symbols ought to secure that status. That its decline is a spiritual fall, to be reversed by taking power back. It isn't loving your country, voting your convictions, believing the founding drew on a Christian moral inheritance, or wanting religious liberty for your church. The temptation to fuse a political cause with the kingdom of God belongs to no single party — churches on the left can make a platform the measure of faithfulness just as easily. But the phrase names a particular American formation, and its history is more recent than most people assume.

## Two Words Added in 1954

The Constitution of 1787 never invokes God, and it forbids any religious test for federal office. The Pledge of Allegiance came a century later, written in 1892 for a national school celebration of the four hundredth anniversary of Columbus's landing. The man usually credited with it, Francis Bellamy, was a Baptist minister. The pledge he wrote didn't mention God.

The words "under God" came sixty-two years later. On February 7, 1954, with President Eisenhower in the congregation, the Rev. George Docherty preached at New York Avenue Presbyterian Church in Washington that the pledge as it stood could have been recited just as solemnly in Moscow, and that what set America apart was belief in God. The Knights of Columbus had already been campaigning for the change, and Congress moved quickly. Eisenhower signed the bill on Flag Day, June 14, 1954, saying it would strengthen the nation's "spiritual weapons." Two years later, on July 30, 1956, he signed the law making "In God We Trust" the official national motto. It had been on some coins since the Civil War. It reached paper money only in 1957.

None of this was sinister. The Soviet Union was officially atheist and persecuted believers, and Americans who wanted to say their country stood for something different weren't wrong about the difference. But the kind of God being invoked deserves a closer look. In December 1952, a month before his inauguration, Eisenhower told an audience that American government "has no sense unless it is founded in a deeply felt religious faith, and I don't care what it is." He went on to name the Judeo-Christian concept as America's, and he was baptized a Presbyterian soon after taking office, so the line wasn't cynical. Still, the sentence people remember tells us something. Faith was prized as a national asset — a resource in a contest between nations — and the particular God being trusted mattered less than the fact of trusting. That is a long way from the apostle who said, "we preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23). Two of the phrases many Christians now defend as the founders' faith were written into law in the 1950s, and the God they named was valued chiefly for what He could do for the nation.

## Civil Religion and Its Two Faces

The sociologist Robert Bellah gave this a name in 1967, in an essay for the journal *Daedalus* called "Civil Religion in America." He argued that alongside the churches, and distinct from them, the United States had developed a religion of its own — with sacred documents, martyrs, holy days like Memorial Day and Thanksgiving, and a God who presided over the nation's destiny. He opened with John F. Kennedy's inaugural address, which declared that "the rights of man come not from the generosity of the state but from the hand of God." Bellah noticed that Kennedy, the first Catholic president, never mentioned Christ or the church. The God of the civil religion belonged to no denomination. He was a God of order, law, and national purpose, and behind Him stood categories borrowed from Israel: exodus, chosen people, promised land.

Bellah didn't think civil religion was simply bad. At its best, he argued, it could place the nation under judgment, and his great example was Abraham Lincoln. In the Second Inaugural, delivered in March 1865 as the war was ending, Lincoln said of North and South that "both read the same Bible and pray to the same God, and each invokes His aid against the other," and then declined to claim God for the Union: "The Almighty has His own purposes." That is civil religion standing beneath God instead of beside Him. In 1975, eight years after the essay, Bellah wrote a book on the same subject and called it *The Broken Covenant*.

Civil religion and Christian nationalism aren't the same thing. Civil religion is generic, a God anyone can pray to at a ballpark or a funeral. Christian nationalism insists that the nation's God is specifically the Christian God and its real people are His. But civil religion prepares the ground by teaching a whole society to describe itself in Israel's language. When anxious Christians decide that the generic God of the civil religion is theirs, and the nation along with Him, a vague public faith hardens into a tribal one. Lincoln's Second Inaugural wondered what God might be doing to the nation. Christian nationalism assumes it already knows.

## "You Only Have I Known"

Scripture goes deeper than the sociology, and it puts its finger on a mistake about covenant. Underneath Christian nationalism, stated or assumed, is the belief that the nation stands in a special covenant with God. Scripture does describe one nation that way. That makes it worth hearing what the prophets told it.

Amos told Israel that the covenant made them more accountable, not less. "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2). The privilege didn't exempt Israel from judgment. It was the reason for the judgment. Later in the same book Amos went after the founding story itself: "'Are you not like the Cushites to me, O people of Israel?' declares the LORD. 'Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor and the Syrians from Kir?'" (Amos 9:7). The Exodus was the most sacred event in Israel's memory, and God told them through a shepherd from Tekoa that He had moved other nations too. The Exodus didn't put God in their debt. What set them apart was His choosing and His faithfulness — and that faithfulness included judgment.

Christian nationalism takes covenant language that belonged to Israel, applies it to a modern nation that was never given it, and keeps the privilege while losing the judgment. That is why one verse turns up on so many banners at patriotic services and prayer rallies: "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land" (2 Chronicles 7:14). The promise was spoken to Solomon after he dedicated the temple, and it was spoken to Israel. For Christians, the people called by God's name today is the church. And the wicked ways in the verse belong to the people doing the praying, not to their neighbors or their political opponents. Read as a promise to America, the verse quietly moves both the people and the sin somewhere else. Read where it stands, it asks the church to repent of its own sins, and those may well include the habit of expecting from a nation what only God can give.

The New Testament finishes the picture. John's vision of the end puts no nation in the place of honor. He saw "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). An anonymous early Christian, writing the *Letter to Diognetus* probably in the second century, described believers in the same terms: they lived in their own countries as resident foreigners, treating every foreign land as a homeland and every homeland as foreign soil. That doesn't cancel love of country. It does put it in its place. A Christian can love America deeply and still know that the people of God are not Americans first.

## A Better Country

Pastors carry a particular share of this, and I include myself, because the decisions about what gets sung and preached on the Sunday nearest the Fourth of July are made by people like me. My own tradition ought to know better than most. Before the Revolution, Virginia jailed Baptist preachers for preaching without a license. In 1801 the Baptists of Danbury, Connecticut, wrote to the new president complaining that their state treated their religious liberty as a favor rather than a right, and Thomas Jefferson's reply on January 1, 1802, gave Americans the phrase "a wall of separation between Church & State." Baptists didn't want that wall because they wanted faith out of public life. They wanted it because they believed coerced faith wasn't faith at all, and that a church protected by the state would sooner or later belong to it. We haven't always remembered why.

I came to faith out of atheism, and I haven't forgotten how the fusion looks from outside. A Christianity that seems to exist to bless one country is easy to explain away as that country's mythology, and the skeptic is right to distrust a God who happens to want whatever his worshipers' nation wants. The answer to that suspicion isn't a less patriotic Christianity. It's an older one — the faith of people who could love a country without needing it to be chosen.

The letter to the Hebrews says that Abraham, Sarah, and their children, who had been promised a land, died acknowledging "that they were strangers and exiles on the earth," and then it names what they were looking for: "But as it is, they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city" (Hebrews 11:13, 16). The promise that family lived on was a promise about land. Yet the writer says their deepest longing was for a country no army could take. That's the place from which a Christian can love America well — wanting a better country, and therefore able to love this one without asking it to be God's.

None of this settles how a Christian should vote. It settles something prior, which is where the love of country sits. A Christian can sing the anthem, honor the dead, and pray for whoever holds office, and still belong first to a people no president will ever govern. Christian nationalism is what happens when that order is reversed and the nation becomes the thing through which God is known, rather than one of the many places where He is served.

The question isn't whether American Christians love their country. Most do, and they should. The question is whether we can love it the way Jesus loved Jerusalem — enough to weep over it, and honestly enough to tell it the truth — without needing it to be the kingdom.

That kind of love has been rare in every country that ever called itself Christian.

It will cost something in this one.
