# What "Post-Christian" Actually Means

*What has ended in America is Christendom, the old arrangement in which the culture handed people the faith, and Jeremiah's letter to the exiles shows the church how to live after it.*

On April 8, 1966, Time magazine ran a cover with no picture on it, the first in its history. It was just three words in red type on a black field: "Is God Dead?" Five years earlier the theologian Gabriel Vahanian had published a book called *The Death of God*, and its subtitle carried a phrase that would outlast the movement it helped start: *The Culture of Our Post-Christian Era*. In 1968 the sociologist Peter Berger was quoted in the New York Times predicting that by the twenty-first century religious believers would be found only in small sects, huddled together against a secular world. Decades later Berger said publicly that he had been wrong about secularization. What modernity reliably produces, he argued, is something else, and he called it pluralism. In April 2009 Newsweek ran a cover story titled "The End of Christian America."

The obituary has been drafted many times.

The newer numbers carry more weight than magazine covers. In 2021 Gallup reported that fewer than half of American adults belonged to a church, synagogue, or mosque, the first time that had happened in the more than eighty years Gallup had been asking. For most of the twentieth century the figure had stood at around seven in ten, and the slide began only around the turn of the century. Four years later the Pew Research Center released the third round of its large study of American religion, a survey of nearly 37,000 adults conducted between July 2023 and March 2024. Its headline ran the other way. The Christian share of American adults had fallen from 78 percent in 2007 to 62 percent. But it had held roughly steady since about 2019. The decline, Pew said, had slowed and may have leveled off.

Which is it, then? An obituary or a plateau?

Most of us read those headlines through whatever frame we brought to them. Some Christians take the Gallup number as proof that the country has turned its back on God and the Pew number as a sign the tide is turning. Some secular readers see both as stages in the slow retreat of an old superstition. Both use the phrase "post-Christian" as though everyone agreed on what it means. We don't. And the confusion is not harmless, because a church that misreads where it lives will misjudge what faithfulness asks of it there.

## Three Ways to Be Secular

One of the most careful attempts to answer the question is Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age*, published in 2007. Taylor is a Canadian philosopher and a Catholic. The book runs close to nine hundred pages, but its central question fits in one sentence. In effect, he asks why it was nearly impossible not to believe in God in the West around 1500, when by 2000 many people found unbelief easy, and even inescapable.

To answer it, Taylor separates three things people mean when they call a society secular. The first concerns public institutions: government, law, schools, and markets no longer refer to God or need Him to function. The second concerns belief and practice. Fewer people pray, attend worship, or say they believe. The third is the one Taylor cares most about, and it's the hardest to see. It concerns the conditions of belief. A society is secular in this third sense when faith in God has become one option among several. Neighbors who are neither stupid nor wicked contest it, and it is frequently not the easiest option to hold. An English villager in 1500 could no more opt out of the Christian account of the world than he could opt out of the weather. The most devout believer in America today holds his faith knowing that the people on either side of him at work see the world differently. And they seem to get along fine.

Taylor also refused what he called subtraction stories, the idea that secularism is simply what is left once religion's illusions have been scraped away. In his account the secular world was built. It has its own history and its own assumptions. He calls its basic assumption the immanent frame: a shared sense that the world can be explained and lived in on its own terms, without reference to anything beyond it. Believers live inside that frame too. Taylor describes modern people as cross-pressured: the believer haunted by doubt, and the unbeliever haunted, at odd moments, by the sense that there might be more.

That description is uncomfortable for both sides, which is one reason to trust it. Unbelief turns out not to be the neutral ground a person reaches once he grows up, and belief is no longer the default it was for the villager who could not opt out of it. Each is a choice now, made under pressure. That is the pluralism Berger came to put in secularization's place.

C.S. Lewis saw the shape of this early. In his inaugural lecture at Cambridge in 1954, he argued that the deepest break in Western history lay not between the Middle Ages and the Renaissance but somewhere between the age of Jane Austen and his own. Christians and pagans, in his view, had more in common with each other than either had with a post-Christian. The prefix matters. A post-Christian society has heard of Jesus, decided it knows what the church is about, and moved on. That makes it a different mission field from first-century Corinth, and in some respects a harder one.

## Where America Actually Stands

Measure America against Taylor's three senses and the answer comes out differently for each.

In the first sense, the national government was never formally Christian, and the country has spent most of a century leaving an informal Christian establishment behind. The First Amendment barred a national church, and the last state establishments were gone by the 1830s. But a Protestant cultural establishment outlived them by more than a century. Many public schools opened the day with prayer and Bible reading until the Supreme Court ended school-sponsored prayer in *Engel v. Vitale* in 1962 and devotional Bible reading in *Abington School District v. Schempp* in 1963. Congress added "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954. It made "In God We Trust" the national motto in 1956.

The sociologist Will Herberg looked at that decade in a 1955 book called *Protestant-Catholic-Jew* and concluded that the operative faith of Americans was the American Way of Life, with Protestantism, Catholicism, and Judaism as its three respectable expressions. When people say America has become post-Christian, this arrangement is usually what they mean. It is gone, and they're right about that.

In the second sense, the evidence is mixed. The Gallup decline is real, and much of it is generational. In Gallup's figures, membership falls with each younger generation: a clear majority of Americans born before 1946 belonged to a church, and only a minority of millennials did. Gallup tied much of the overall drop to older, churchgoing generations being replaced by younger ones that belong less. Pew found that the religiously unaffiliated have grown from about one adult in six in 2007 to roughly three in ten. But most of the unaffiliated are not atheists or agnostics. The largest group describe their religion as "nothing in particular." The same survey found that large majorities of American adults still believe in God or a universal spirit and believe that people have a soul. About a third attend religious services at least once a month. More than four in ten pray every day.

Whatever the obituaries predicted, this is a country where belief is still widespread and belonging is thinning.

Pew itself stopped short of calling the plateau a recovery. Younger Americans remain far less religious than older ones, and the researchers left open whether the leveling off would last. One reasonable reading is that much of what fell away over the last thirty years was affiliation held for social reasons. On that reading, the people who left were mostly those who had little reason to stay once staying stopped being expected, and the floor now showing is made up more of conviction than of habit. I hold that loosely. It is an interpretation, not a finding. But it fits the shape of the data.

In the third sense, America is fully there. Nobody in this country is a Christian by default anymore. A high school student who believes in the resurrection does so knowing that many of her friends find the idea strange, and knowing too that they are not fools. The pastor preaches knowing that some of the people in the room are halfway out the door, and that the culture will not be sending anyone to replace them. Belief has become what Taylor described: one option among others, and seldom the easiest.

The answer to whether America is post-Christian, then, is that it is post-Christendom. The arrangement under which the culture handed people the faith, or at least the vocabulary of the faith, along with their citizenship is over.

The faith itself is not.

## What the Church Is Grieving

It would be easy to dismiss the Christians who grieve this as people nostalgic for power, but the old arrangement had genuine goods in it. Children who never attended a church still learned the Lord's Prayer and a handful of hymns. The country shared a moral vocabulary of sin, forgiveness, conscience, and neighbor that made certain arguments possible, including arguments against the country's own injustices. Churches founded hospitals, colleges, orphanages, and rescue missions, and the culture assumed that kind of work was simply what Christians did. Those are real losses, and the people who mourn them are not wrong to.

But Herberg's diagnosis is the other half of the story. A great deal of what passed for faith at the height of the American consensus was the religion of being a decent American, dressed for church. When membership is expected, congregations fill with people for whom the gospel is part of respectability. Some of them meet Christ there, and we should thank God for every one. Many never do, and their children notice. Part of what is dying is a counterfeit, and the church should not spend its strength trying to resuscitate it.

Nor was Christendom always kind to the people outside it. It was not until 1961, in *Torcaso v. Watkins*, that the Supreme Court stopped Maryland from denying Roy Torcaso his commission as a notary public because he would not declare a belief in God. A skeptic who hears "post-Christian" as good news about a Christianity that can no longer penalize anyone has history on his side, at least in that respect. Christians ought to be able to say so without flinching, since a faith worth holding is one a person was free to refuse.

We pastors have something else to admit. Most American churches, including the ones that think of themselves as countercultural, were organized for a world where the culture delivered people to the front door. Our programs assumed people already knew the story and needed reminding, and our evangelism appealed to a shared memory of the faith that fewer and fewer people actually had. When the culture stopped making deliveries, many of us read the falling numbers as an attack. Much of it was simply the end of a subsidy we had not noticed we were living on.

## Lesslie Newbigin and the West as a Mission Field

Lesslie Newbigin learned this by coming home. He went to India in 1936 as a missionary of the Church of Scotland, and in 1947 he became one of the first bishops of the newly formed Church of South India. When he retired in 1974 he returned to Britain and taught at the Selly Oak Colleges in Birmingham. Then, at about seventy, he took on the pastorate of a small United Reformed congregation in Winson Green, in inner-city Birmingham. He had spent most of his working life carrying the gospel in a country where Christians had always been a small minority. He came home to find that his own country was no longer Christian either, though its churches still assumed it was.

He spent the rest of his life arguing that the modern West had to be approached the way a missionary approaches any culture, from outside its assumptions. He made the case in two books from the late 1980s, *Foolishness to the Greeks* and *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*. The culture he described had divided the world into public facts, which every reasonable person must accept, and private values, which each person is free to choose. And it had filed the gospel firmly among the values. In his account the Western churches had largely made their peace with that arrangement. But a church content to be a private preference has already surrendered the one thing a missionary can never surrender: the claim that the gospel is true for everyone, whether they choose it or not.

His answer was not a strategy for winning the culture back. It was a congregation. He asked how anyone could come to believe that the power with the last word in human affairs is represented by a man hanging on a cross. Newbigin's reply, in substance, was that the only hermeneutic of the gospel is a congregation whose people believe the gospel and live by it. He meant that in a culture which no longer takes the Christian story for granted, people will not learn how to read the gospel mainly from arguments or campaigns. They will learn it from a community whose common life makes no sense unless the gospel is true.

Under the old arrangement the church could let the surrounding culture do a good deal of its explaining. Now the only commentary on the gospel most of our neighbors will ever read is the life of the congregation down the street. What does the life of your church say the gospel means? What would a neighbor conclude about Jesus from watching it for a year?

## Jeremiah's Letter to the Exiles

Scripture has been here before. In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar carried King Jehoiachin, whom Jeremiah calls Jeconiah, off to Babylon along with the queen mother, the officials of Judah, and the craftsmen of Jerusalem. The temple still stood. It would not fall for another decade. In Jerusalem a prophet named Hananiah stood in the house of the Lord, in front of the priests and the people, and promised a short exile: "Within two years I will bring back to this place all the vessels of the LORD's house" (Jeremiah 28:3). It was exactly what everyone wanted to hear. Prophets among the exiles were saying the same thing.

Jeremiah wrote the exiles a letter with a different word.

> "Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, to all the exiles whom I have sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon: Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease. But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare." (Jeremiah 29:4–7)

The letter is careful about who did the sending. Babylon's army marched them there, but the Lord says the exiles are people "whom I have sent." The exile, in other words, was God's discipline rather than an accident of history or a sign that He had lost control of it, and in a way the exiles could not yet see, it was also His placement. The word the ESV translates "welfare" three times in verse 7 is the Hebrew *shalom*, the word for the full flourishing of a people under God. The exiles were told that their own shalom was bound up with the shalom of the city that had conquered them.

Then Jeremiah warned them about the competing prophets: "Do not let your prophets and your diviners who are among you deceive you, and do not listen to the dreams that they dream, for it is a lie that they are prophesying to you in my name; I did not send them, declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 29:8–9). The prophets of the quick return spoke in the name of the right God, and they got His timing wrong. Had the exiles believed them, they would have spent the rest of their lives living out of their bags.

The famous verse comes right after that, and where it sits changes how it reads. "For thus says the LORD: When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you, and I will fulfill to you my promise and bring you back to this place. For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope" (Jeremiah 29:10–11). We print that verse on graduation cards. It was first addressed to people most of whom would die in Babylon. The plans for shalom were real. But they ran past the lifetimes of most of the people who first heard them, and their part in those plans was to build houses.

The American church faces both temptations Jeremiah refused. The first is Hananiah's: the promise of a quick restoration, two years or one election away, after which the right leaders and the right laws will bring back the temple vessels and the country will be Christian again. The people who preach it usually love their country and their churches, and some of what they want restored is worth wanting. But the promise keeps the church from planting anything that takes longer than a news cycle to grow. The second is the one Jeremiah warned against with the words "do not decrease." It is to settle so comfortably into Babylon that the people of God dissolve into it, keeping the name while living by the city's assumptions about money, sex, power, and the self. The first temptation tends to gather on the political right and the second on the left, though neither stays put, and each is a way of refusing to live where God has actually placed us.

The analogy has limits. I am not saying American Christians are in exile the way Judah was. We have not been deported. No one has burned our temple. Christians remain a majority of the population and enjoy legal protections the exiles could not have imagined. Borrowing the language of exile to describe a loss of cultural advantage, and then using it to claim persecution, would be a false prophecy of its own.

The New Testament uses the language more carefully. Peter wrote to largely Gentile congregations across five regions of Asia Minor, people who had once joined in the idolatry of the towns they still lived in and who were now maligned by their old companions for refusing to (1 Peter 4:3–4). He addressed them as "elect exiles of the dispersion" (1 Peter 1:1). Their exile was a matter of allegiance rather than geography. Their citizenship belonged to another King, whether Caesar approved of them or not. Christendom let the church forget that it had always been exiled in that sense, and the end of Christendom is making it remember.

## How to Live After Christendom

What does faithfulness look like in a country like this one? What does it look like in a normal week, in the town where you actually live? Jeremiah's answer is surprisingly ordinary, and that is the point of it.

Build houses and live in them. The church has to stop treating the present as a temporary interruption before the real America comes back. It has to invest in the place where God has put it, in its schools and neighborhoods and local institutions, as people who expect to stay. Plant gardens and eat their produce. That is the kind of faithfulness that takes years to bear fruit: a community that stays in one place, teaches its children the faith patiently, and is still there when the neighborhood changes around it.

The letter also told the exiles to marry and raise children in Babylon. Those of us raising children in this country have to reckon with what Taylor saw. Whatever faith our children hold as grown men and women, they will hold it in his third sense, as a choice made among other live options, beside friends and coworkers who chose otherwise. There is no reason to panic over that. It is how the gospel has always traveled whenever the church had no culture to carry it, which was the case for its first three centuries, and faith chosen in Babylon is no lesser faith than faith absorbed in Jerusalem. What it does mean is that Christian parents can no longer outsource formation to the surrounding culture, because the surrounding culture is forming our children into something else.

The exiles were told to seek the shalom of Babylon and to pray for the city, though its army had besieged Jerusalem and carried off their king. The American church is being asked something much smaller, and it is finding it hard anyway: to work for the good of neighbors who don't share its faith, to want the city to flourish even when the city does not want the church, and to pray by name for leaders it did not vote for. Peter told his exiles, "Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:12). He assumed they would be spoken against. Instead of telling them to win the argument by gaining power, he told them to live in a way that would eventually be seen.

And do not decrease. Seeking the city's welfare was never permission to become indistinguishable from it. The exiles of Psalm 137 refused to forget Zion by the rivers of Babylon. Daniel went on praying toward Jerusalem three times a day, his windows open, in the capital of an empire full of other gods (Daniel 6:10). Christians after Christendom will need their own distinct practices just as badly: gathered worship, the Lord's Table, the reading of Scripture, a sexual ethic the city finds strange, generosity that costs something, and the habit of forgiving enemies. Those practices are what make the church a hermeneutic of the gospel rather than one more organization with opinions.

The letter never promised the exiles that Babylon would become Jerusalem. It promised that God would keep His word on His own schedule, and it asked them to live in the meantime as people who believed Him. The faith their grandchildren carried home had been kept alive in Babylon by people who built houses, raised children, and prayed for a city that had conquered them.

America may never again be Christian in the way it once assumed it was. The question in front of us is whether, seventy years from now, there will still be a recognizable people of God in this place, seeking its welfare and praying on its behalf, because our generation did what the letter said.

Few of us will live to see the answer.

Most of the exiles never saw theirs, and God kept His word anyway.
