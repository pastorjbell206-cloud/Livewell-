# Was Jesus Real? What Historians Actually Know

*Nearly every historian of the ancient world, believer or not, agrees that Jesus of Nazareth lived and was crucified under Pontius Pilate, and the evidence says more about him than most people expect.*

Early in 1910, the German Monist League staged a public debate in Berlin over whether Jesus had ever lived. The occasion was a book published the year before by Arthur Drews, a professor of philosophy at the technical university in Karlsruhe. In *The Christ Myth*, Drews argued that there was no man behind the Gospels at all, only a figure of religious myth who had later been given a human story. Bruno Bauer had pressed a version of the idea in the century before. But Drews kept making the case, theologians lined up to answer him, and for several years it was a public controversy in Germany.

The question has outlived the debate. In a Barna survey of American adults a decade ago, the overwhelming majority said Jesus was a real person who actually lived, and the youngest adults were a little less likely to say so. Anyone who spends much time online has met the claim that Jesus never existed, often stated with a confidence the historians of the period don't share.

The claim needs a real answer, partly because the people who raise it aren't all cranks and partly because Christians have often answered it badly. Some of us have said the evidence for Jesus is better than the evidence for Julius Caesar, or that no serious person has ever doubted him. Neither is true. A skeptic who catches the overstatement is right to wonder what else has been overstated.

## What the Skeptic Gets Right

Start with what is missing, because a good deal is. No document written during Jesus' lifetime mentions him. There is no inscription with his name, no coin, no official letter, no record of his trial. He left no writings of his own. Every full account of his life that survives was written by people who believed God had raised him from the dead. The earliest Gospel, Mark, is usually dated around the year 70, some forty years after the crucifixion. And Paul, the earliest Christian writer whose work we have, says strikingly little about where Jesus went, what miracles he did, or most of what he taught.

Those gaps are the backbone of the modern case against him. Its most detailed form is Richard Carrier's *On the Historicity of Jesus*, published in 2014 by Sheffield Phoenix Press, a biblical studies publisher. Carrier weighs the evidence with probability theory. He argues that the first Christians believed in a heavenly Jesus known through visions and through their reading of Scripture, and that the Gospels later gave him an earthly biography. Paul's near-silence carries much of the weight. If Jesus had walked the roads of Galilee only a generation earlier, why would the man whose letters fill so much of the New Testament say so little about it?

That's a fair question. But it helps to remember what kind of man we're asking about. Ancient historians wrote about emperors, generals and wars. A village craftsman who taught for a few years and was executed as a criminal in a minor province is exactly the sort of person who leaves no trace in that kind of history. Even the governor who sentenced him, who ran Judea for ten years, appears by name in only one known stone inscription. Almost nobody of Jesus' station left a monument. The real question is whether the sources we have are better explained by a real person or by a myth.

## What Paul Knew

Paul is the place to begin, because his letters are the earliest Christian writings we have. Most come from the 50s, some twenty to twenty-five years after the crucifixion. He was writing to churches about their problems, not composing a biography, so the details of Jesus' life come in sideways. But they come. Jesus was a Jew, "born of woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4). He was "descended from David according to the flesh" (Romans 1:3). He shared a last meal with his followers "on the night when he was betrayed" (1 Corinthians 11:23). He was crucified, a fact Paul returns to again and again.

Paul also knew Jesus' family. Describing his first visit to Jerusalem after his conversion, he writes, "But I saw none of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:19). Carrier and other mythicists read "brother of the Lord" as a title any believer might carry, since Christians called one another brothers. Paul doesn't use it that way. Defending his right to bring a wife on his travels, he asks, "Do we not have the right to take along a believing wife, as do the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord and Cephas?" (1 Corinthians 9:5). The brothers of the Lord are a distinct group there, named beside the apostles and Peter. Mark names four of them when the people of Nazareth take offense at their neighbor: "Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon?" (Mark 6:3).

So Paul met the brother of a man who had lived and died within living memory, and that brother was a leader of the church in Jerusalem. As for Paul's silence, most of his letters answer questions from congregations that had already been taught the story. A pastor writing to his church about a quarrel over money doesn't retell the life of Christ. He assumes they know it.

## What Outsiders Said

The other check on the Christian sources is what non-Christians wrote. The most important of them was a Jew. Flavius Josephus was a priest's son who commanded Jewish forces in Galilee in the revolt against Rome, went over to the Romans, and settled in Rome, where he wrote his people's history. In the last book of his *Antiquities of the Jews*, finished in the 90s, he tells how a high priest named Ananus took advantage of the gap between one Roman governor's death and the next one's arrival, around the year 62. Ananus called the Sanhedrin together and "brought before them the brother of Jesus, the so-called Christ, James by name, together with some others," and had them stoned.

Josephus mentions Jesus only so his readers will know which James he means. A Christian copyist would hardly have called his Lord "the so-called Christ," and the story is about Ananus abusing his office. Most historians, including many with no Christian commitment, accept the passage as what Josephus wrote. A longer passage earlier in the same work is another matter. In the form Eusebius quoted in the fourth century, it calls Jesus "a wise man, if indeed it be proper to call him a man," and says flatly, "He was the Christ." A Jew who never became a Christian would not have written that. Many specialists think Josephus wrote a shorter, cooler notice that Christian copyists later improved. The argument over its wording goes on, but the James passage doesn't depend on it.

Josephus also shows how thin the trail of a man like Jesus could be. In his history of the war he tells of another Jesus, a son of Ananias, a countryman who began about four years before the revolt to cry out against Jerusalem and its temple. Some of the city's leading men had him beaten, and its rulers handed him to the governor Albinus, who had him flogged until his bones showed. He wouldn't answer the governor's questions, and Albinus released him as a madman. He kept up his cry for years, until a stone from a Roman siege engine killed him. If Josephus hadn't happened to tell the story, we'd never know the man existed. That is what the evidence for a prophet from the countryside usually looks like.

The Romans noticed Jesus only because of his followers. Writing early in the second century about the fire that burned much of Rome in 64, the historian Tacitus says Nero blamed it on the Christians, and he explains who they were: "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus, and a most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out," spreading from Judea to Rome itself. Tacitus despised the Christians. Skeptics point out that he may only have been repeating what Christians in Rome said about themselves, and that's possible. Even so, a Roman historian with no sympathy for the movement treated the execution under Pilate as a plain fact.

The first pagan writer we know of who attacked Christianity at length went further. A philosopher named Celsus wrote against the Christians late in the second century, and his words come down to us inside Origen's answer to him. Celsus gave some of his sharpest charges to a Jewish critic, who accuses Jesus of having "invented his birth from a virgin." The real story, the critic says, is that Jesus was "born in a certain Jewish village, of a poor woman of the country, who gained her subsistence by spinning," whose husband, a carpenter, threw her out for adultery. Jesus later hired himself out as a servant in Egypt, acquired some miraculous powers there, and came home calling himself a god.

It's an ugly story. It's also a story about a real village, a real mother and a real trade. The Gospels' own opponents took the same line. They didn't deny what Jesus did. They disputed where his power came from: "He is possessed by Beelzebul," the scribes from Jerusalem said, and "by the prince of demons he casts out the demons" (Mark 3:22). The ancient enemies of the faith called Jesus illegitimate, a sorcerer, a deceiver. As far as the surviving sources show, none of them said he never lived. That argument is modern. (The related claim that Christians borrowed their story from older gods gets its own essay, [Did Christianity Copy Pagan Myths?](/writing/did-christianity-copy-pagan-myths))

## What Historians Agree On

Once a historian grants that Jesus lived, the Gospels become sources to be weighed like any other ancient biography, and they're far fuller than anything else we have. Scholars sift them with ordinary questions. Is a saying or event reported in more than one independent source? Does it fit the Galilee and Judea of the first century? Would the early church have wanted to invent it, or would it rather have left it out? (How far the Gospels can be trusted is taken up in [Can You Trust the Bible?](/writing/why-trust-the-bible))

That last question turns up more than you might expect. Mark and Matthew both say Jesus was baptized by John, whose baptism was a call to repentance. Luke mentions the baptism without saying who performed it, and Matthew seems to feel the awkwardness: "John would have prevented him, saying, 'I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?'" (Matthew 3:14). Mark reports that Jesus' family "went out to seize him, for they were saying, 'He is out of his mind'" (Mark 3:21). He has Jesus say of the end of the age that "no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32).

When a man kneels and calls him "Good Teacher," Mark's Jesus answers, "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18). Matthew, telling the same story, words it differently: "Why do you ask me about what is good?" (Matthew 19:17). You can watch a later writer smoothing a hard saying. The church kept Mark's version anyway.

The hardest fact for the first Christians was the cross. Jews knew the law said "a hanged man is cursed by God" (Deuteronomy 21:23), and Greeks and Romans thought of crucifixion as the death of slaves and rebels. Paul knew how his message sounded: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23). It's very hard to picture anyone inventing a savior in the first century and then choosing that death for him. The crucifixion under Pilate is the fact about Jesus that historians of every persuasion hold most firmly.

Near the start of his 1985 book *Jesus and Judaism*, E. P. Sanders, one of the most influential scholars of Jesus' Jewish world in the last century, set out a short list of facts he thought almost beyond dispute. Jesus was baptized by John. He was a Galilean who preached and healed. He called disciples and spoke of there being twelve. He confined his work to Israel and was involved in a controversy over the temple. He was crucified outside Jerusalem by the Roman authorities. Afterward his followers carried on as an identifiable movement, and some Jews persecuted parts of it. Most historians would add that he announced the kingdom of God, taught in parables, and had a reputation, even among enemies, as a healer and exorcist.

Bart Ehrman, an agnostic and one of the best-known New Testament scholars in America, wrote *Did Jesus Exist?* in 2012 as an argument against the mythicists. His conclusion was that Jesus was a real Jewish preacher crucified under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius. That is very nearly the consensus of the field, among Jewish, Christian and secular scholars who agree on little else. Carrier himself grants that the near-universal judgment of qualified experts is against him. You're free to doubt a consensus. But this one wasn't built by believers protecting their faith.

## The Jesus We Keep Inventing

Scholars who agree that Jesus lived have had far more trouble agreeing on what he was like. The evidence isn't the only reason.

Modern study of the historical Jesus began in the eighteenth century, when Gotthold Lessing published fragments left by a Hamburg scholar, Hermann Samuel Reimarus, arguing that Jesus had been a failed political messiah. Through the nineteenth century German scholars wrote one life of Jesus after another, many of them setting aside the miracles and the talk of the end to find a wise teacher of a moral kingdom. In 1906 a young theologian in Strasbourg named Albert Schweitzer surveyed the whole effort and concluded that the writers had mostly described themselves. Each of them, he said in effect, had made Jesus in the image of his own character, and no work of history lays a man's real self so bare as writing a life of Jesus.

Schweitzer's own conclusion was severe. The Jesus of those books, who founded the kingdom of God on earth and died to consecrate his work, never existed. In Schweitzer's account he was a figure drawn up by rationalism, given life by liberalism and dressed by modern theology in the costume of history. The real Jesus, Schweitzer argued, was a Jewish prophet who expected the world to end soon and went to his death to hasten it. Most Christians have rejected that reconstruction, with good reason. His charge against the others has held up much better.

Americans have done the same thing in our own way. In 1925 an advertising executive named Bruce Barton published *The Man Nobody Knows*, presenting Jesus as the founder of modern business, a born executive who recruited twelve men from the lowest ranks of commerce and built them into an organization that took over the world. It became a best-seller. In 1940 Warner Sallman painted his *Head of Christ*, a soft-lit Jesus with light skin and long brown hair. Christian groups handed pocket-sized copies to American soldiers in the Second World War, and it went on to be printed by the millions. For generations of American Protestants, that was simply what Jesus looked like.

The Jesus Seminar, founded by Robert Funk in 1985, voted on the sayings of Jesus with colored beads, red for words he surely said and black for words he did not. Its 1993 report, *The Five Gospels*, concluded that most of the words the Gospels give him were not his. What remained was a wandering sage of subversive parables, with little of the end-of-the-world preaching Schweitzer had found. Other New Testament scholars objected that the votes came from a self-selected group with a shared point of view.

It would be convenient to leave the problem with the professors and the advertising men. It belongs to the churches too. In some congregations Jesus looks like a man who would win a fight and vote the way the congregation votes. In others he's a gentle progressive who never says anything hard about money, sex or judgment. We keep the parts of him that confirm us and quietly misplace the parts that would correct us, and most of us could name which parts those are. When the Jesus we describe agrees with us about nearly everything, we've usually stopped reading him.

## The Question Jesus Asked Peter

The Gospels saw this coming. Mark tells of a day when Jesus and his disciples were on the way to the villages of Caesarea Philippi, north of the Sea of Galilee, and he asked them, "Who do people say that I am?" They told him what they'd heard: "John the Baptist; and others say, Elijah; and others, one of the prophets." Every answer was respectful. Every answer fit him into a category people already had. Then he asked, "But who do you say that I am?" Peter answered, "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:27–29).

Peter had the title right. But when Jesus began to teach that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected and killed, and after three days rise again, Peter took him aside and rebuked him. Jesus turned, saw the other disciples, and rebuked Peter in front of them: "Get behind me, Satan! For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man" (Mark 8:33). Peter wanted a Messiah who would not be crucified. He reshaped Jesus into the Christ he already had in mind in the same conversation in which he confessed him.

That should change how a Christian hears the question in the title. The fact historians are surest of is the one Peter tried to talk Jesus out of. It's also the fact every invented Jesus loses. Barton's executive doesn't need a cross, and neither does the gentle teacher of the nineteenth century, the sage of the colored beads, or the Jesus who shares our politics. What the evidence keeps handing back is a Jewish man from Nazareth, baptized by a prophet of repentance, whose family worried about his sanity, who said he did not know the day of the end, and who was executed as a criminal by the Roman governor of Judea.

The church tied its faith to that man on purpose. The Apostles' Creed confesses that Jesus "suffered under Pontius Pilate." It's an odd thing to find in a creed. The name of a Roman governor sits between the virgin birth and the resurrection, fixing the faith to a date and a place. Standing before King Agrippa, Paul said the king knew about these things, "for this has not been done in a corner" (Acts 26:26). Christianity has always made itself answerable to history.

History can take you that far and no further. It can tell you that Jesus of Nazareth lived, taught, gathered followers and was crucified under Pilate, and that soon afterward those followers were saying God had raised him from the dead. It can't tell you whether they were right. That question has its own essay, [Did the Resurrection Actually Happen?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen) But someone who has settled the historical question has only reached the place where the Gospels start asking theirs.

When Nathanael first heard about Jesus, he asked a skeptic's question: "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" Philip didn't argue. He said, "Come and see" (John 1:46).

Was Jesus real? He was. The evidence settles that about as firmly as ancient history settles anything about a man of his station.

The harder question is the one he put to Peter, and most of us have been answering it with a face we already liked.

The Jesus of the sources flatters no one. He was crucified under a governor whose name the church still recites.

Christians confess that he did not stay dead. If they're right, his question was never a historian's question. He is asking it of you.

*"But who do you say that I am?"* (Mark 8:29)
