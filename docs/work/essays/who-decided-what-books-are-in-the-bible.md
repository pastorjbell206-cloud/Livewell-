# Who Decided What Books Are in the Bible?

*The books of the Bible were gathered over about three centuries as the churches read them aloud in worship, and the letters and lists that show how it happened can still be read today.*

Every year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt to tell them the date of Easter. Athanasius had held that office for nearly forty years and had been driven out of his city five times. In his letter for Easter of 367, he took up another worry. Teachers in Egypt were passing around books they claimed were ancient and holy, and he feared that "some few of the simple" would be taken in, "led astray by the similarity of their names with the true books." So he wrote down which books the churches read as Scripture.

He named twenty-two books of the Old Testament, counted the way Jews counted them. Then he named the New Testament: four Gospels, Acts, seven letters by James, Peter, John and Jude, fourteen letters of Paul with Hebrews among them, and Revelation. These are the twenty-seven books found in nearly every Christian Bible today. His is the earliest list we have that matches ours exactly. "These are fountains of salvation," he wrote, "that they who thirst may be satisfied with the living words they contain. In these alone is proclaimed the doctrine of godliness. Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these."

Athanasius had been at the Council of Nicaea in 325 as a young man, attending his bishop. If that council had settled which books belonged in the Bible, nobody alive was better placed to say so. In what survives of his letter, he never mentions it. He calls the books "handed down," and he says he is writing "by way of remembrance, of matters with which you are acquainted." He wasn't telling the churches what to read. He was reminding them of what they had been reading for generations.

His list isn't as tidy as it first looks. He left Esther out of the canon, placing it with Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, the Didache and the Shepherd of Hermas among books "appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us." The man who gave us the first exact list of the New Testament had an Old Testament that matches neither the Protestant nor the Catholic one. The real history is slower and untidier than most people have heard, and a good deal less sinister.

## What Happened at Nicaea

Most people who think they know who chose the books learned it somewhere other than church. The best-known version comes from Dan Brown's 2003 novel *The Da Vinci Code*. In it a scholar tells the heroine that the emperor Constantine had a new Bible made and left out the gospels that showed Jesus as a mortal man. Documentaries and online videos repeat some form of the story, usually set at the Council of Nicaea. A Christian who has never heard the real history can feel cornered by it. If an emperor picked the books, what exactly have we been trusting?

Constantine did call the council, and a few hundred bishops came. Their business was Arius, a priest of Alexandria who taught that the Son of God was a created being. They answered him with the creed that carries the council's name. They also settled how the date of Easter would be kept, and they issued twenty canons on church order. None of them deals with the books of the Bible.

One ancient remark does tie Nicaea to a book. Around 400, Jerome said he had translated Judith into Latin partly "since the Nicene Council is considered to have counted this book among the number of sacred Scriptures." It's secondhand, and nothing in the council's surviving decisions bears it out. Judith isn't even in Protestant Bibles.

Constantine's real part was smaller. After he founded Constantinople, he wrote to Eusebius, the bishop of Caesarea, asking for "fifty copies of the sacred Scriptures" for the new churches of the city. They were to be written "on prepared parchment in a legible manner, and in a convenient, portable form." The letter covers parchment, trained copyists and the two public carriages that would bring the finished books. It says nothing about what had to be in them.

The rumor isn't wrong about everything. There were other gospels, and the churches did decide that some writings didn't belong. But many Christians tell a story just as tidy in the other direction, in which the New Testament was settled when the last apostle died and no faithful believer ever doubted a book in it. The ancient sources don't support that one either. They show a process of about three centuries, and it began with a Bible the church already had.

## The Scriptures Jesus Read

The first Christians had a Bible before a single book of the New Testament was written. On the evening of the resurrection, Luke says, Jesus told his disciples, "These are my words that I spoke to you while I was still with you, that everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44). That matches the shape the Hebrew Bible still has: the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings, a section that includes the Psalms. Scholars debate whether "the Psalms" there names the whole third section or only the book, but Jesus is plainly speaking of writings his hearers already knew.

Writing in Rome in the 90s, the Jewish historian Josephus told his Greek readers that the Jews had "only twenty-two books, which contain the records of all the past times; which are justly believed to be divine." He doesn't name the twenty-two. Most scholars think they are the books of the Hebrew Bible, counted in a different way. Paul said it more briefly: "the Jews were entrusted with the oracles of God" (Romans 3:2).

## The Books Catholics and Orthodox Keep

This is where the churches still divide. Most early Christians read the Old Testament in Greek, in the translation called the Septuagint, begun in Alexandria in the third century before Christ. The great Christian copies of the Greek Bible from the fourth and fifth centuries include books not found in the Hebrew Bible, in differing combinations: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, the books of the Maccabees, Baruch, and longer forms of Esther and Daniel. Catholics call these books deuterocanonical. Protestants call them the Apocrypha.

The Catholic and Orthodox case begins with the Bible the early church actually used. Many readers hear in Hebrews 11:35, "Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life," the seven brothers and their mother in 2 Maccabees 7, who die confessing that God will raise them. Augustine counted Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees, Wisdom and Sirach among the canonical books.

The African councils of Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397 counted them too, in the same list that named the twenty-seven books of the New Testament. A Protestant who cites those councils for his New Testament owes some account of why he sets them aside for his Old. The Western church read these books for more than a thousand years before the Reformation, and the Council of Trent defined them as canonical in 1546. The Orthodox churches keep them too, along with a few more.

The Protestant case is just as old. Jesus' Bible was the one entrusted to Israel, and Josephus spoke for his people when he counted twenty-two books. Jerome, who translated the Old Testament from the Hebrew, wrote that the church reads Judith, Tobit and the Maccabees without receiving them among the canonical Scriptures, and that Wisdom and Sirach could be read to strengthen the people but not to establish doctrine.

Nor did the Reformers throw these books away. Luther's German Bible of 1534 printed them in a section of their own between the Testaments, and the King James Version of 1611 included them. Later confessions went further. English Baptists, in the confession they published in 1677 and reaffirmed in 1689, called them "no part of the Canon (or rule) of the Scripture."

Faithful churches have divided over this for five centuries. It sits below the creeds. Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants confess the same Christ in the same Nicene words, whichever Old Testament lies open on the pulpit. Both sides appeal to the ancient church, and both can, because the ancient church did not speak with one voice. Athanasius and Jerome drew the line in one place, Augustine and the African councils in another. A Christian who wants to settle it should read the books themselves, and the best arguments of each tradition, before deciding.

## The Gospels and Paul's Letters in Worship

The New Testament grew out of writings meant to be read aloud. Paul wrote to the Thessalonians, "I put you under oath before the Lord to have this letter read to all the brothers" (1 Thessalonians 5:27). He told the Colossians, "And when this letter has been read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and see that you also read the letter from Laodicea" (Colossians 4:16). Most scholars think that letter from Laodicea is lost. The churches kept what they went on reading. Copies of Paul's letters passed from church to church until they were gathered together.

Around 150, in Rome, Justin Martyr described a Christian Sunday for the emperor. Believers from the city and the countryside gathered in one place, "and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits." Justin explains in the same work that these memoirs are the ones "which are called Gospels." By the middle of the second century, the apostles' writings were read in the same service as the prophets. That is how a book became Scripture in practice. The church read it, week after week, and heard God speak in it.

About thirty years later, Irenaeus, who had become bishop of Lyon after [the persecution there in 177](/writing/the-early-church-under-rome), wrote, "It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are." His reasons sound odd to us: four zones of the world, four winds, four living creatures. But they were a preacher's explanation of something he hadn't invented. He had grown up in Asia Minor and served in Gaul, and he wrote as if the four Gospels were simply what Christians read. A writing some of his opponents called "the Gospel of Truth" he dismissed as "their comparatively recent writing," one that "agrees in nothing with the Gospels of the Apostles."

## Marcion's Shorter Bible

Before Irenaeus wrote, the church had already met a man who wanted a smaller Bible. Marcion of Pontus came to Rome around 140 and broke with the church there around 144. He taught that the God of the Old Testament was a lesser god than the Father of Jesus. Irenaeus summed up Marcion's view of that God as "the author of evils" who took "delight in war." So Marcion set aside the whole Old Testament. He kept one Gospel, an edited Luke, and ten letters of Paul, trimmed to match. Irenaeus said he furnished his followers "not with the Gospel, but merely a fragment of it." His churches spread widely and lasted for generations.

Historians argue over how much Marcion shaped the New Testament. About a century ago Adolf von Harnack argued that the church built its canon largely in answer to him. Others think Marcion only hurried along something already under way. Either way, the church's answer is plain. He offered a shorter Bible, and the church kept a longer one: the whole Old Testament, read as Christian Scripture, four Gospels instead of one, and Paul's letters uncut.

Marcion's habit didn't die with his churches. Plenty of Christians treat the Old Testament as a long preface to skip. Some keep the words of Jesus and quietly set Paul aside. Others keep Paul's letters close and pass over what the prophets say about the poor. We do it from the left and from the right, and most of us own a whole Bible while reading from a much shorter one. I know which passages I return to and which ones I find reasons to leave alone. Around 200, Tertullian put a question to Marcion that we could stand to hear ourselves: "Marcion, by what right do you hew my wood?" How our communities shrink the Bible without noticing is the subject of [How the Bible Gets Translated by the Room You're In](/writing/how-the-bible-gets-translated-by).

## The Books That Were Argued Over

Eusebius, writing his history of the church early in the fourth century, sorted the books into groups. The accepted ones were the four Gospels, Acts, the letters of Paul, 1 John and 1 Peter, and "if it really seem proper, the Apocalypse of John." Disputed, "which are nevertheless recognized by many," were James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2 and 3 John. Then came books he classed as rejected, though not heretical, among them the Shepherd of Hermas and the Teachings of the Apostles, probably the little church manual now called the Didache. Last were the books "cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles," such as the gospels of Peter and Thomas. Those, he said, were to be "cast aside as absurd and impious."

The disputes ran in different directions in different places. In the West the doubts gathered around Hebrews. Eusebius notes that some rejected it, saying the church of Rome disputed it "on the ground that it was not written by Paul." Origen thought its ideas were Paul's and its wording someone else's: "But who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows." In the East the book in question was Revelation. Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria in the middle of the third century, didn't believe the apostle John had written it. He kept it anyway: "But I could not venture to reject the book, as many brethren hold it in high esteem." Cyril of Jerusalem's list from around 350 has no Revelation at all. He told the people he was preparing for baptism, "And whatever books are not read in Churches, these read not even by thyself."

Other books were read and not kept. Around 170, the bishop of Corinth, another Dionysius, wrote to the church at Rome that his congregation had read Rome's new letter on the Lord's day. They would draw advice from it whenever they read it, he said, "as also from the former epistle, which was written to us through Clement." That earlier letter, known today as 1 Clement, was still "publicly used in a great many churches" in Eusebius's day. The Shepherd, he wrote, "has been publicly read in churches." Codex Sinaiticus, a Greek Bible copied in the fourth century, follows Revelation with the Letter of Barnabas and the Shepherd.

A Latin list that may be the oldest we have, now called the Muratorian fragment, shows a church drawing these lines. Ludovico Antonio Muratori found it in the Ambrosian Library in Milan and published it in 1740, from a seventh- or eighth-century copy with its beginning missing. What survives calls Luke "the third book of the Gospel" and John the fourth. It recognizes Acts, thirteen letters of Paul, Jude, two letters of John and the Wisdom of Solomon. It receives the revelations of John and of Peter, "though some amongst us will not have this latter read in the Church." It says nothing of Hebrews, James or Peter's letters. The Shepherd, it says, was written "very recently in our times," while Hermas's brother Pius was bishop of Rome. So it "ought to be read; but it cannot be made public in the Church to the people."

That phrase "in our times" is the main reason most scholars date the list to the late second century, probably in or near Rome. A minority, most fully Geoffrey Hahneman in 1992, argue that it comes from the fourth century in the East. The question is still open.

## How the Churches Judged

How did a church decide whether a book belonged? Who wrote it, and when? Was it read everywhere or only in one city? Did it teach what the apostles taught? A book had to come from an apostle or the apostles' circle and generation. That is why the Shepherd, however useful, was too recent. It had to be read across the churches. Augustine told students of the Bible "to prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive." And it had to agree with the rule of faith, the summary of apostolic teaching that new Christians confessed at their baptism.

The tests worked slowly, and they could correct a mistake. Around 200, Serapion, bishop of Antioch, found the church at Rhossus quarreling over a gospel bearing Peter's name. He hadn't read it, and he told them that if that was all they were divided over, they could read it. Then he learned it had been used to teach a false view of Christ. He got a copy, read it through, and found much in it that was sound and some things added. He wrote to them: "For we, brethren, receive both Peter and the other apostles as Christ; but we reject intelligently the writings falsely ascribed to them, knowing that such were not handed down to us."

When councils finally spoke, they spoke for their own region and in the language of receiving. The North African bishops met at Hippo in 393, with Augustine present, and at Carthage in 397. They named the books to be read in church as Scripture. When they gathered their rulings into one code in 419, they sent the list to Boniface, the bishop of Rome, and to other bishops "that they may confirm this canon, for these are the things which we have received from our fathers to be read in church." The councils didn't create a New Testament. They wrote down the one their churches had been reading.

## The Case for Lost Christianities

The strongest form of the skeptic's case doesn't need Constantine. Bart Ehrman, a New Testament scholar at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, made it in *Lost Christianities* (2003). The second century, he argues, held many Christianities. Some believers kept the Jewish law and saw Jesus as a human messiah chosen by God. Marcion's churches had two gods. Groups often called Gnostic taught secret knowledge and read gospels of their own. One group won. Ehrman calls it proto-orthodox. It set the criteria, judged the books, and told the story afterward through historians like Eusebius. On this reading, the rule of faith is the winners' theology under a reverent name, so of course the books that passed agreed with it. Ehrman builds on Walter Bauer, who argued in 1934 that in some regions what was later called heresy had come first.

Much of that is true. The second century was more varied than many Christians imagine. Eusebius took a side. Some of the losing books survive only in their opponents' summaries, and some came back by accident, like the Gospel of Thomas, found in a buried collection near Nag Hammadi in Egypt in 1945. Even the apostolic test was hard to apply. Eusebius recorded the doubts about 2 Peter: "we have learned that his extant second Epistle does not belong to the canon; yet, as it has appeared profitable to many, it has been used with the other Scriptures." Scholars still argue over who wrote it. Ehrman's work on how the manuscripts were copied is taken up in [Can You Trust the Bible?](/writing/why-trust-the-bible)

But the evidence also limits his case. By the end of the second century the four Gospels and Paul's letters were being read in churches from Gaul to Egypt. That was more than a hundred years before an emperor took any interest, and no central office imposed them. Most scholars date the four Gospels to the first century and the Gospel of Thomas to the second. And the rule of faith is older than the second century's quarrels. The earliest Christian writings we have are Paul's letters from the 50s. In one of them he passes on what he says he himself received, "that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). That confession binds Jesus to Israel's Scriptures and to a real death and resurrection, the very things Marcion and many Gnostic teachers denied. The standard the churches used to judge books came from the oldest texts they had.

A party shaping a collection to win an argument would hardly have kept four Gospels that are hard to fit together, or a letter whose author no one knew. It would not have left its doubts in writing for anyone to find. The early church was diverse, and its judgments were human ones. They were made in the open, by churches that disagreed with each other and said so, about books already read in worship for generations.

## Received More Than Chosen

So who decided? No single person or meeting did. The churches recognized books that had already proved themselves in worship. The lists came after the reading. Protestant and Catholic teaching weigh that recognition differently, but both describe the church as receiving these books, not making them Scripture. The Church of England's articles put it simply: "All the Books of the New Testament, as they are commonly received, we do receive, and account them Canonical." The First Vatican Council taught in 1870 that the church holds these books sacred because they were written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, not because the church approved them afterward.

Paul thanked God that the Thessalonians, when they heard the apostles' preaching, "accepted it not as the word of men but as what it really is, the word of God, which is at work in you believers" (1 Thessalonians 2:13). That is the canon in small. The churches did not make these books the word of God by listing them. They found the books at work among them, Sunday after Sunday, and wrote down what they had already received.

If you're a Christian who felt shaken when someone told you a council voted on your Bible, you can set that fear down. The process was slow, human and argued over. The church never hid that. If you're a skeptic, the history won't make you believe. It does mean the question can't be settled by blaming an emperor. It has to be settled with the books themselves. Read Athanasius's letter, and then read a Gospel straight through in the [Study Bible](/study/bible).

For those who already trust these books, the harder question is closer to home. The Christians Justin described read the apostles and prophets "as long as time permits." Many American churches now read a few verses aloud on a Sunday and move on. We defend the whole canon and live on a small part of it.

The churches held on to these books because they kept hearing a voice in them.

It was the voice of the risen Christ, who on the evening of the resurrection opened the Law and the Prophets and the Psalms to his frightened disciples.

He still speaks through all of it, to anyone who will read the whole.

*"And we also thank God constantly for this, that when you received the word of God, which you heard from us, you accepted it not as the word of men but as what it really is, the word of God, which is at work in you believers."* (1 Thessalonians 2:13)

*"My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me."* (John 10:27)
