# Did God Command Genocide?

*The Old Testament does say that God ordered Israel to destroy the peoples of Canaan, and a Christian who wants to answer the charge has to begin by reading those chapters as they stand.*

In the early morning of May 26, 1637, English soldiers from Connecticut and Massachusetts Bay, with Mohegan and Narragansett fighters alongside them, surrounded a fortified Pequot village near the Mystic River and set it on fire. People who escaped the flames were cut down outside the palisade. Several hundred Pequots died, women and children among them. The next year one of the two English captains, John Underhill, published an account of the war in London called *Newes from America*, and he answered the readers who would ask why Christians had shown so little mercy. "Sometimes the Scripture declareth women and children must perish with their parents," he wrote. "We had sufficient light from the word of God for our proceedings."

Underhill was wrong about what Scripture gave him, and I'll come back to why. But he didn't invent the passages he was leaning on. The Old Testament contains commands, spoken in God's name, to destroy whole peoples, and one of them names infants. That's why the question in the title isn't a trick. A Christian who answers it with a quick "Of course not" probably hasn't read Deuteronomy 20 or 1 Samuel 15 in a while. The plain answer is that the Bible says God commanded it. What that means is the hard part.

The sharpest form of the objection isn't that the Bible contains violence. So does the history of every nation. The objection is about what we can know. We are more sure that it's wrong to kill children for what their parents did than we could ever be that an ancient book speaks for God. So when a book says God ordered children killed, the reasonable conclusion is that the book has God wrong. People have left the faith on that reasoning, and most of them weren't looking for an excuse. They were taking their consciences seriously. Christians tell them to.

What the skeptic may not expect is that the Bible asks his question first. When God tells Abraham that judgment is coming on Sodom, Abraham argues with him: "Far be it from you to do such a thing, to put the righteous to death with the wicked, so that the righteous fare as the wicked! Far be that from you! Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). He bargains from fifty righteous people down to ten, and God agrees to spare the city for ten (Genesis 18:32). The father of the faith assumed that God's justice could be appealed to, and that it knew the guilty from the innocent. Whatever we say about Joshua, we have to say it with Abraham's question still in the room.

## What the Commands Say

The commands come in Deuteronomy, as instructions for the war Israel is about to fight west of the Jordan. For cities far away, the law requires an offer of peace before any attack (Deuteronomy 20:10, 15). For the peoples of the land itself it requires something else:

> "But in the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God is giving you for an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes, but you shall devote them to complete destruction, the Hittites and the Amorites, the Canaanites and the Perizzites, the Hivites and the Jebusites, as the LORD your God has commanded, that they may not teach you to do according to all their abominable practices that they have done for their gods, and so you sin against the LORD your God." (Deuteronomy 20:16–18)

The Hebrew word behind "devote to destruction" is *herem*. It describes something handed over to God entirely and taken out of human use. In war, people under the ban were not taken captive, and goods under it were not kept as plunder. Joshua narrates the policy carried out. At Jericho "they devoted all in the city to destruction, both men and women, young and old, oxen, sheep, and donkeys, with the edge of the sword" (Joshua 6:21). After the southern campaign Joshua "left none remaining, but devoted to destruction all that breathed, just as the LORD God of Israel commanded" (Joshua 10:40). Generations later Samuel sends Saul against Amalek: "Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey" (1 Samuel 15:3).

The word *genocide* is new. Raphael Lemkin, a Polish Jewish lawyer, coined it in 1944, and the United Nations convention against genocide, adopted on December 9, 1948, defines it as acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. It would be convenient to say that an ancient command can't be judged by a modern legal term. But Deuteronomy 20 names six peoples and orders that nothing that breathes be left alive among them, and it gives a religious reason. If a government gave that order today, we would know what to call it.

I have five sons. I can't read "child and infant" as a puzzle in biblical interpretation, and I don't think any Christian should try. Whatever we say about these verses has to be something we could say aloud with a parent listening. That rules out pretending the verses aren't there. It also rules out explaining them in the tone of a man pleased to have found the answer.

## What the Text Says the War Was For

The text insists that the land was not a reward for Israel's goodness. "Not because of your righteousness or the uprightness of your heart are you going in to possess their land, but because of the wickedness of these nations the LORD your God is driving them out from before you" (Deuteronomy 9:5). The war is presented as judgment, and the charges are specific. Leviticus 18 lists the practices that had defiled the land, among them offering children to Molech (Leviticus 18:21), and says that "the land vomited out its inhabitants" (Leviticus 18:25). Deuteronomy says the peoples of Canaan "even burn their sons and their daughters in the fire to their gods" (Deuteronomy 12:31).

The judgment is also described as late. Long before Joshua, when God promised the land to Abraham, he told him his descendants would have to wait, "for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16). On the Bible's own account, the peoples of Canaan were given generations. That doesn't answer anything about the children of Jericho, who had no say in what their parents worshiped. But it describes something other than a tribal god clearing ground for his favorites.

Nor is the line drawn by blood. In Jericho itself a Canaanite woman named Rahab hides Israel's spies and confesses that "the LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath" (Joshua 2:11). She and her family are spared, and "she has lived in Israel to this day" (Joshua 6:25). The Gibeonites, another people of the land, talk their way into a treaty and are kept alive (Joshua 9).

Meanwhile an Israelite of the tribe of Judah named Achan keeps plunder from Jericho that had been devoted to God. He is put to death, and his sons and daughters are brought out with him to the valley where it happens (Joshua 7:24–25). That chapter is as hard as any in the book. It also makes it impossible to treat the ban as a weapon reserved for foreigners. Deuteronomy 13 orders the same destruction against any Israelite town that turns to other gods.

The prophets later turned the same charge on Israel. Through Jeremiah, God accuses the people of Judah of building the high places of Topheth "to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire, which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind" (Jeremiah 7:31). Leviticus had warned Israel that the land would vomit them out as well (Leviticus 18:28), and in 586 BC Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and carried its people into exile. Israel was held to the same standard as Canaan, and the Old Testament records its failure in detail.

A skeptic can grant all of that and still say the obvious thing. "They deserved it" is what armies always say about the towns they burn. Underhill said it about the Pequots. And even on the Bible's own terms, a baby in Jericho had never offered anyone to Molech. The language of judgment tells us how the text understands the war. It doesn't make the death of one particular child easier to look at, and Christians shouldn't use it as if it did.

## How Ancient War Reports Talk

The text also shows something easier to miss. Joshua 11:23 says that "Joshua took the whole land." Two chapters later God tells the aged Joshua that "there remains yet very much land to possess" (Joshua 13:1). Joshua 10 reports that no one in the south was left alive. Yet in Judges the same peoples are still there, and "the people of Israel lived among the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Amorites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites" (Judges 3:5). The Jebusites of Jerusalem were never driven out at all (Joshua 15:63).

Deuteronomy points the same way. Right after the command to "devote them to complete destruction" it says, "You shall make no covenant with them and show no mercy to them. You shall not intermarry with them" (Deuteronomy 7:2–3). Nobody needs a law against marrying people who are all dead. In Exodus God promises to drive the peoples out "little by little" (Exodus 23:30). Amalek follows the same pattern. First Samuel 15 says Saul "devoted to destruction all the people with the edge of the sword" (1 Samuel 15:8). Fifteen chapters later the Amalekites are raiding David's town of Ziklag, and four hundred of their young men get away from him on camels (1 Samuel 30:1, 17).

Scholars of the ancient Near East have pointed out that this is how victory was reported in Israel's world. Around 1208 BC the Egyptian pharaoh Merneptah set up a victory stele that contains the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible, and it boasts that Israel's seed is no more. Around 840 BC Mesha, king of Moab, claimed on his own monument that Israel had perished forever. Israel outlived both boasts. Mesha's stone even uses the Bible's word for the ban: he says he killed the people of Nebo because he had devoted the town to his god. The Old Testament scholar K. Lawson Younger compared Joshua with Egyptian, Hittite and Assyrian campaign reports and found the same habit in all of them, sweeping claims of total victory sitting beside plain evidence that the fighting went on.

The philosopher Nicholas Wolterstorff, reading Joshua closely, concluded that the book itself signals that its totals were never meant as body counts. Paul Copan and Matthew Flannagan built a whole book on that reading, under nearly the same title as this essay. What God required, they argue, was the end of Canaan's religious order and the removal of its peoples from the land, and the language of killing everyone belongs to the idiom of war.

The reading has real support in the text. It also has limits. Jericho is still described as a city where men and women, young and old, were killed, and an exaggerated report of a massacre is still a report of a massacre. Many archaeologists also doubt that the conquest happened on anything like the scale the book describes. That may soften the moral problem while raising other questions about Joshua as history, which believing scholars argue over too (see [Can You Trust the Bible?](/writing/why-trust-the-bible)).

## How Christians Have Answered

The church has argued about these chapters for most of its history. In the middle of the second century a teacher from Pontus named Marcion decided that the God of the Old Testament, the God of law and war, could not be the Father whom Jesus revealed. He compiled a book called the *Antitheses* that set the law against the gospel to show they came from two different gods, and he kept for his followers only an edited Gospel of Luke and ten of Paul's letters. The church rejected him. Tertullian spent five books answering him, and his aim, as he put it, was to show "that Christ Jesus is the revealer of none other god but the Creator."

That matters here. Marcion offered the church the easiest way out of Joshua anyone has ever proposed, and the church refused it. Christians may disagree about how to read these commands, but the historic faith doesn't let us say they came from someone other than the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. When the creed confesses one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, it is confessing the God of Joshua as well as the God of the Gospels.

Late in the fourth century the objection came back from Faustus, a Manichaean bishop in North Africa, who complained that the Old Testament showed a God who "destroyed thousands of men for a slight offense, or for nothing" and who "threatened to come with a sword and spare nobody, righteous or wicked." Augustine's reply is hard to read now. God gave those commands, he said, "not in cruelty, but in righteous retribution." Then he asked what the evil in war really was: "Is it the death of some who will soon die in any case, that others may live in peaceful subjection?"

In the thirteenth century Thomas Aquinas put the traditional answer in its tightest form. All human beings, guilty and innocent, die by God's power because of original sin, and so "by the command of God, death can be inflicted on any man, guilty or innocent, without any injustice whatever." The logic holds if God is the giver of every life, and many Christians still find it sound.

Not everyone took that road. Origen, preaching through Joshua in the third century, told his hearers that unless these physical wars were figures of spiritual ones, he doubted the apostles would ever have handed these histories to the church to be read by followers of a Christ who came to teach peace. For Origen the real enemies in Joshua were sin and the powers behind it. Paul had already pointed that way: "For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood" (Ephesians 6:12).

Christians who hold the whole Bible to be the word of God still divide over these chapters, roughly along three lines. Some keep the traditional reading. God commanded what the text says, as judgment on peoples who burned their children, after long patience, and the giver of life had the right to take it. Its defenders say any other reading lets the modern conscience overrule the text, and that a God who never judges is not the God of either Testament. Others accept that God gave a real command of judgment but read the language of total destruction as the idiom of ancient war. They point to the survivors, the marriage law, Rahab, and the stones of Merneptah and Mesha, and they say their reading takes the book seriously in its own kind of language.

A third group, argued most fully by the pastor and theologian Greg Boyd in *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God*, reads the cross as the final revelation of what God is like. On this reading the violent portraits show God stooping to let his people see him in the terms of their own violent world, bearing their misunderstanding much as Christ bore our sin. Its defenders say they are taking seriously that the Son "is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature" (Hebrews 1:3). Its critics answer that it leaves the reader to decide which parts of Scripture truly show God, which comes close to the door the church shut on Marcion. Boyd's defenders reply that they keep every page as inspired Scripture and read it through Christ, which is the opposite of what Marcion did.

These are arguments among people who confess the same creeds, and faithful churches and careful scholars still come down in different places. I won't pretend the question is settled. What the three readings share matters more than where they part. They agree that the God of Joshua is the Father of Jesus and that he is just. And they agree on the point that matters most for how Christians live: no one may take these chapters as a pattern for anything we do.

## What These Chapters Have Been Used For

That is where Underhill went wrong. The command in Deuteronomy is bounded by its own terms. It names particular peoples in one land at one moment, and the same chapter requires an offer of peace to cities outside that land. Nothing in the New Testament renews it. When the apostles speak of war, they move it to the ground Paul names in Ephesians. Underhill had no command from God. He had someone else's command, carried across an ocean and thousands of years to a people the Bible never mentions, so that English soldiers could play Israel and the Pequots could play Canaan.

Jesus settles it himself. When James and John wanted to call down fire on a Samaritan village that had refused to receive him, "he turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:55). When one of his disciples drew a sword in Gethsemane, Jesus told him, "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). His followers offered him violence on his behalf more than once. He refused it every time. A Christian who goes to Joshua looking for permission has to walk past Jesus to get there.

Underhill was not the last American Christian to read Canaan onto the people whose land he wanted. The church's long habit of blessing that kind of taking is its own story, told in [What Is the Doctrine of Discovery?](/writing/doctrine-of-discovery) The habit survives in smaller forms whenever Christians talk about people on the other side of an argument as a contamination to be cleared out instead of neighbors to be loved. Believers on the right and on the left both know how to do it. Most of us will never carry a sword. But the move Underhill made, from "God judged them" to "God is with us against them," comes easily to the human heart, and it feels like faithfulness while we're making it.

There is a quieter way to get these chapters wrong, and it's more common in ordinary churches. We don't read them. Joshua 6 appears in children's Bibles as the story of the walls falling down, and the verse after the walls fall is usually left out. Plenty of lifelong churchgoers have never heard a sermon on 1 Samuel 15:3. That silence is a small Marcionism, and the skeptics usually notice it first. They're right to ask why a church that calls the whole book the word of God goes quiet on these pages. The church learned in [the slavery debates](/writing/why-didnt-the-bible-ban-slavery) what happens when the hardest passages are left to whoever is most eager to use them.

## A Canaanite Woman

The New Testament doesn't make these chapters easy, but it does something with them most readers miss. The name Jesus is the Greek form of Joshua. The Greek translation of the Old Testament spells Joshua's name exactly as the Gospels spell the name of Jesus. When Hebrews says, "For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on" (Hebrews 4:8), the Greek name is the same one, and the King James Version simply printed it as "Jesus."

Matthew tells a story with that name in the background. Jesus withdraws to the district of Tyre and Sidon, coastland the tribes of Israel never took (Judges 1:31 lists Sidon among the towns Asher did not drive out). A woman from that region comes out crying, "Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David; my daughter is severely oppressed by a demon" (Matthew 15:22). Mark calls her a Gentile, a Syrophoenician by birth. Matthew calls her "a Canaanite woman," and she is the only person in the New Testament given that name.

The disciples want her sent away. Jesus answers at first that he "was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 15:24), and she keeps asking. "Yes, Lord," she says, "yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table" (Matthew 15:27). Then Jesus answers her, "O woman, great is your faith! Be it done for you as you desire" (Matthew 15:28). Her daughter is healed that same hour.

Matthew had already put another Canaanite woman in Jesus' family tree: "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab" (Matthew 1:5). So a Joshua comes into Canaanite country, a Canaanite mother begs him for her daughter, and he gives her what she asks. The story doesn't explain the children of Jericho away. But Christians believe God is seen most fully in this Joshua. When we ask what kind of God gave the commands in Deuteronomy, we are not left alone with Deuteronomy.

Abraham asked whether the Judge of all the earth would do what is just. The New Testament answers at the cross, where Paul says God put Christ forward "to show his righteousness at the present time, so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26). Judgment doesn't vanish from the Bible after Joshua. It falls in the end on the Son, who bore it for people who had earned it, in Israel and Canaan and everywhere else. The God who waited generations for the Amorites is the God who asks, "Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, declares the Lord GOD, and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?" (Ezekiel 18:23). He rebuked Jonah for wanting Nineveh destroyed, a city with "more than 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left" (Jonah 4:11).

So did God command genocide? The Bible says he commanded Israel to destroy the peoples of Canaan, and Christians who love the Bible read those commands in more than one way. No reading makes them painless. The church shouldn't want one that does.

What no reading allows is what Underhill did.

Before we ask whether God was just to the Canaanites, it's worth asking whom we have quietly cast as Canaanites ourselves, and whether we would want Jesus to meet them the way he met the woman from Tyre and Sidon.

He met her that way.

He has met the rest of us the same way, at a cross where the Judge of all the earth did what is just and bore the judgment himself.
