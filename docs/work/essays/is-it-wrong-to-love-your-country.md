# Is It Wrong to Love Your Country?

*Scripture treats love of country as a real good, and the kind of love it honors grieves a country's sins and names them.*

You may have loved America all your life without once wondering whether you should. You stand for the anthem. Perhaps you served, or your father did. You pray for the country by name, and you have never thought of that love as competing with your love for Christ. Lately, though, you keep hearing that it does. Books, sermons and maybe your own grandchildren have told you that patriotism is the doorway to idolatry. You want to know whether the love you've carried all these years was a sin.

Or you may be younger, and the trouble runs the other way. You have watched churches drape the stage in red, white and blue and sing about the nation with more feeling than they bring to the cross, and it embarrasses you. You've started to wonder whether a serious Christian can love any country at all, or whether faithfulness means holding your own at arm's length.

Both readers have plenty of company. Gallup has asked Americans how proud they are of their country since 2001. In June 2026 only 33 percent said they were extremely proud, the lowest share since the question began. Among Republicans the figure was 70 percent, and among Democrats it was 14. In its polls from 2021 through 2025, Gallup found that 41 percent of adults in Generation Z were extremely or very proud to be American, against 75 percent of baby boomers. Love of country has become one more thing Americans sort themselves by. The Christian question is whether Scripture sorts it the same way, and I don't think it does.

Elsewhere I have tried to describe [what Christian nationalism is and what it is not](/writing/what-christian-nationalism-is-and-is-not). That is a narrower question about fusing faith and nation. This one sits underneath it. May a Christian love a country at all? The short answer is yes. The longer answer starts on the older reader's side of the question.

## Why Christians Have Defended Patriotism

Christians who defend love of country usually begin with something nobody has to be taught, which is that love starts with what is near. C. S. Lewis, in *The Four Loves*, began his discussion of patriotism with love of home: the place where you grew up, the people you knew there, the food and the speech and the way of life. That love, he thought, is not aggressive. It mostly wants to be left alone, and if it has any imagination it teaches a person that other people love their homes too, and are right to. Lewis knew how such a love could go bad. He wrote of all the natural loves, adapting a line from Denis de Rougemont, that love "begins to be a demon the moment he begins to be a god." He never concluded that the cure was to stop loving.

G. K. Chesterton made the same case with more noise. In 1901, while he was opposing his own country's war in South Africa in print, he gathered a set of essays into a book called *The Defendant*, and the last of them is "A Defence of Patriotism." It holds his best-known line on the subject: "'My country, right or wrong,' is a thing that no patriot would think of saying except in a desperate case. It is like saying, 'My mother, drunk or sober.'" Chesterton wasn't mocking loyalty. A decent man whose mother took to drink, he wrote, "would share her troubles to the last." What that man would never do is pretend not to care whether she drank. For Chesterton, "the first of all the marks of love is seriousness," and such love "will always esteem the most candid counsellor the best."

Simone Weil wrote about the same love in a much darker time. In 1943, working in London for the Free French while her country lay under German occupation, she wrote the long report later published as *The Need for Roots*. "To be rooted," she wrote, "is perhaps the most important and least recognized need of the human soul." Weil, who was drawn powerfully to Christ in her last years, was nobody's flag-waver. She argued that the right love for one's country is compassion for it, not pride in its greatness. But she never doubted that a soul cut off from every place and people has lost something it needs. She died that August.

Behind these writers stands a much older argument. The fifth commandment reads, "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12), and it ties the honor of parents to a people's life in a land. Thomas Aquinas, in the thirteenth century, drew the line out. After God, he taught, we owe the most to our parents and our country, because from them we received our being and our upbringing, and he called the virtue that repays that debt piety. On this view love of country begins as gratitude for what you didn't choose: the language you think in, the laws that protected you before you could read them. A person who can feel no thanks for any of it hasn't risen above his neighbors' patriotism. He has only lost something they still have.

When Paul stood in the Areopagus in Athens, he told the philosophers there that God "made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place" (Acts 17:26). On Paul's account God made the nations. Their existence, and even their borders and their seasons, fall under His providence.

All of that is true, and the older reader is right to hold on to it. But the people most alarmed about patriotism have not, for the most part, been attacking love of home.

## Why Other Christians Distrust Patriotism

Their argument is about what happens to that love when a state wants something from it, and the twentieth century gave them their evidence. In October 1914, two months into the First World War, ninety-three German scholars, scientists and artists signed a public manifesto defending their country's conduct of the war. A young Swiss pastor named Karl Barth found on the list nearly every theologian who had taught him, the great church historian Adolf von Harnack among them. Barth later said he could no longer follow their ethics or their dogmatics, or their way of reading the Bible and history. Some of the most learned Christian teachers in Europe had been unable to tell love of Germany from the will of God.

By 1933 a movement calling itself the German Christians had set out to fit the Protestant church to Hitler's Reich. In May 1934 the synod at Barmen answered with a declaration Barth largely drafted. It confessed Jesus Christ as the one Word of God the church must hear and obey, and it rejected the claim that other events and powers could stand beside Him as revelation. That church's long slide is [a story of its own](/writing/when-patriotism-becomes-a-gospel).

Christian critics in our own country make a version of the same argument. Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon's 1989 book *Resident Aliens* described the church as a colony of resident aliens and rejected the idea that America or any other country is a Christian nation. The book comes out of a pacifist tradition, and that tradition's sharpest worry is war: that a church which thinks of itself as American first will let the nation tell its members when to kill, and will rarely say no.

The critics can point to Scripture, too. The builders of Babel said, "let us make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4). Israel asked Samuel for a king "like all the nations," and the LORD told him, "they have rejected me from being king over them" (1 Samuel 8:5, 7). Read from this side, the Bible is far more suspicious of nations than most patriotic Christians assume.

The younger reader's unease belongs to this tradition, whether that reader knows it or not, and it is right about more than its elders usually admit. Love of country is one of the easiest loves for power to recruit, because it already feels like virtue. Should a Christian love his country less, then, or love it differently?

## Nehemiah, the Exiles and Ruth

Scripture's answer comes mostly in stories, and the first belongs to a man with a good job in a foreign palace. Nehemiah was cupbearer to Artaxerxes, king of Persia, generations after the exile. When his brother Hanani arrived from Judah, he heard that "the wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates are destroyed by fire" (Nehemiah 1:3). What he did next sets the pattern for a Christian love of country: "As soon as I heard these words I sat down and wept and mourned for days, and I continued fasting and praying before the God of heaven" (1:4). The prayer that followed was a confession of "the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned" (1:6). Nehemiah grieved his people's ruin and told the truth about its cause, with his own family on the list.

Months later the king noticed his face and asked why he was sad when he wasn't sick. "Then I was very much afraid," Nehemiah writes (2:2), since a servant who looked unhappy before a king could pay for it. He answered anyway: "Let the king live forever! Why should not my face be sad, when the city, the place of my fathers' graves, lies in ruins, and its gates have been destroyed by fire?" (2:3). He asked to be sent "to the city of my fathers' graves, that I may rebuild it" (2:5). This is love of country in a form Scripture plainly honors. It was tied to a particular place and to the dead buried there, and years of faithful service to a foreign king never loosened it. Before Nehemiah built anything, he wept and confessed.

Psalm 137 shows the same love from inside the exile, and it shows the danger the critics see. "By the waters of Babylon, there we sat down and wept, when we remembered Zion" (Psalm 137:1). Their captors demanded "one of the songs of Zion," and they could not sing it: "How shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land? If I forget you, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget its skill!" (137:3–5). But the psalm doesn't end there. It ends with a curse on Babylon: "Blessed shall he be who takes your little ones and dashes them against the rock!" (137:9).

Scripture keeps that verse, and it isn't there as a model. It is there because it is true to the human heart. The love that weeps by the river and the rage that wants the enemy's children dead can live in the same person a few verses apart, and the critics of patriotism are right that the distance between them is shorter than we like to think. What the psalm does with the rage matters, though. The exile tells God the worst thing in him instead of acting on it, which is a very different thing from a nation baptizing its revenge.

Ruth's story pushes in another direction. She was a widow from Moab. When her mother-in-law Naomi urged her to follow her sister-in-law, who "has gone back to her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15), Ruth refused: "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (1:16). The law of Moses barred Moabites from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3). Yet Ruth became the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:17), and Matthew names her in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Scripture honors loyalty to a people, and it refuses to make blood the boundary of that people. Israel's great king descended from a foreigner who chose them.

## What Nations Are For

Go back to Paul in Athens. The verse the defender of patriotism quotes runs on into the next one. God made every nation and set their times and boundaries "that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him" (Acts 17:27). Nations are real, but they are not their own purpose. In Paul's account a nation is one of the settings in which human beings look for the God who made them. A patriotism that makes the nation the point has misread the sentence.

Paul also wrote to a city where national pride was in the air. Philippi was "a Roman colony" (Acts 16:12), and Paul's accusers there charged him with advocating "customs that are not lawful for us as Romans to accept or practice" (16:21). He later wrote to the church there, "But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 3:20). The Philippians knew what a colony was: an outpost of a distant capital, living by that capital's law on foreign soil. Yet the same Paul used his Roman citizenship without embarrassment, at Philippi (Acts 16:37) and again in Jerusalem, where he told the tribune he was "a citizen by birth" (22:28). His citizenship in heaven outranked his citizenship in Rome, but it never made him despise the lesser one.

John's vision of the end goes further. In the new Jerusalem, he writes, "the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it" (Revelation 21:24). Two verses later he says it again: "They will bring into it the glory and the honor of the nations" (21:26). The nations are not simply erased at the end of the story. What was glorious in them is carried into the city of God, under one condition: "But nothing unclean will ever enter it, nor anyone who does what is detestable or false" (Revelation 21:27). That is a reason to love what is good in a country, its music and its language, its courage, its hard-won liberties, because on John's telling those things have a future. What is false in a country has none, and a Christian can grieve it without hating the country it disfigures.

## Frederick Douglass and Dietrich Bonhoeffer

On July 5, 1852, Frederick Douglass, who had escaped from slavery in Maryland, spoke at Corinthian Hall in Rochester, New York, at a meeting of the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. He began with praise: "The signers of the Declaration of Independence were brave men." Then he turned. "This Fourth of July is *yours*, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn." He recited the exiles' lament from Psalm 137, "How can we sing the Lord's song in a strange land?", and applied it to the millions still in chains.

"What to the American slave is your Fourth of July?" he asked. "I answer, a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim." He spared the churches nothing. To the slave, he said, "your prayers and hymns, your sermons and thanksgivings, with all your religious parade and solemnity," were "mere bombast, fraud, deception, impiety, and hypocrisy."

It would be easy to hear that speech as contempt. It wasn't. In the same address Douglass argued that the Constitution, read as it ought to be read, was "a GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT," the capitals his own. And near the end he said, "I do not despair of this country." He held America to its own founding words because he believed them. That is why he could tell the nation, at the holiday when it most wanted praise, that it was "false to the past, false to the present." His was the love Chesterton described, the kind that prizes the candid counsellor.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer loved Germany in the same way, without illusion. In June 1939 American friends, Reinhold Niebuhr among them, brought the young Lutheran pastor and theologian to New York, where he would have been safe. Within weeks he decided he had made a mistake. He wrote to Niebuhr, "I must live through this difficult period of our national history with the Christian people of Germany." Christians in Germany, he saw, would have to choose between willing their nation's defeat so that Christian civilization might survive and willing its victory at that civilization's cost. He knew which he had to choose, and he would not choose it from safety.

He sailed for home that July, weeks before the war began. He was arrested in 1943, and on April 9, 1945, a month before the war in Europe ended, he was hanged at Flossenbürg. He and Douglass stood in very different places, one shut out of his nation's promises by its laws, the other bound to a nation ruled by a criminal regime. Each told his country the truth at real cost, and each did it from inside, as a man who still said "this country" or "our national history" and meant it.

## Two Ways to Get It Wrong

There are two ways to fail at this love. The first is worship, which treats the country as beyond judgment and hears criticism as treason. It reads God's favor off the nation's successes and blames its failures on enemies within. In church it can make the flag something close to a sacrament, until loyalty to the nation and faithfulness to Christ are hard to tell apart. In our day this disorder lives mostly on the right, though not only there, and it is the one Barth found in his teachers' signatures. It is the man in Chesterton's line who says his mother is his mother "drunk or sober" and means he doesn't care which.

The second is contempt. It treats the country as nothing but its crimes. It can name every sin in the nation's history and cannot give thanks for a single gift. It looks down on neighbors who still love the place. It is quick to say that they have sinned and slow to say, with Nehemiah, "I and my father's house have sinned." In our day this disorder lives mostly on the left and among the young, though not only there. It feels like moral seriousness. More often than its owners suspect, it is pride, and underneath the pride is ingratitude.

They look like opposites, but they grow from one root. Each expects from a nation something only God can give. The worshiper believes the nation has delivered it. The despiser never quite forgives the country for being a country, a mixed and mortal thing, rather than the kingdom. Gallup's numbers hint at the shared root. Between 2024 and 2025 the share of Republicans who said they were extremely or very proud to be American rose from 85 to 92 percent, while among Democrats it fell from 62 to 36. Whatever else changed that year, the party in the White House did. When love of country rises and falls with an election, it was never quite love of the country.

The two disorders have not done equal damage, and it would be false to pretend they have. Worship of the nation has blessed armies, and in Douglass's America it lent slavery the language of worship. Contempt has far fewer dead to its name. But it does its own harm. It cuts the church off from the neighbors it is sent to love, who can tell when they are despised, and it hands the whole subject of patriotism to the people most likely to abuse it. Most of us carry some of each, and which one shows depends a good deal on who is in power. I know that's true of me.

## Loving a Country Like a Family

The best picture of a Christian love of country is the one Chesterton reached for, which is the love of a family. You didn't choose your family, and you owe it more than you can repay. You stay when it's in trouble. You know its faults better than any outsider does, and you say them out loud, because you want it to be better. And you do not worship it. Jesus set that last limit as plainly as anything He said: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (Matthew 10:37). If love of father and mother has a ceiling, love of country has one too.

Chesterton saw something else in that kind of love. In *Orthodoxy* (1908) he asked what would help a London district he called "a desperate thing," Pimlico, and answered that only love would: "If men loved Pimlico as mothers love children, arbitrarily, because it is THEIRS, Pimlico in a year or two might be fairer than Florence." Then he put it as history: "Men did not love Rome because she was great. She was great because they had loved her." Love comes first, and the improvement follows. That is how Nehemiah loved a city in ruins.

In practice this love is quiet. You pray for your country by name and thank God for particular gifts rather than for an idea of America. You learn its history well enough to know the courage and the cruelty in it, including [what the founders actually built](/writing/was-america-founded-as-a-christian-nation), and you teach your children both. You serve the neighbor down the street, who is the country in its most concrete form, whatever his politics. When the country disappoints you, you stay in the argument instead of leaving it to the people who will only flatter it or only curse it. And on Sunday you keep the church's worship fixed on the One the church gathers to worship.

I'm raising five sons in this country, and I want them to love it. I want them to know its songs and its stories, to be grateful for what they were given here, and to be able to tell it the truth when it needs to hear it. I'm more sure that this is the right love than I am that I know how to teach it.

So is it wrong to love your country? No. God made every nation and gave each of us a place to be born and a people to be born among, and gratitude is the right first answer to a gift.

The harder question is what your love does when your country is wrong. Nehemiah's sat down and wept, confessed the people's sins with his own name among them, and then went to build. Most of us let ours go quiet, or go cold.

Jesus was born into a people. He spoke their language, kept their feasts and prayed their psalms. He was never ashamed of His nation, and He never let it take His Father's place.

On the last day the kings of the earth will carry the glory of their nations into the city of God, and none of them will be its light. The city needs no sun, "for the glory of God gives it light, and its lamp is the Lamb" (Revelation 21:23).
