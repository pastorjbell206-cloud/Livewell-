# When the Church Had Nothing to Lose

*For almost three centuries the church lived under an empire that could kill it whenever it chose, and how it lived and grew there is history a post-Christian America needs to learn.*

## Pliny's Letter to Trajan

Sometime around the year 112, a Roman governor named Pliny the Younger wrote to the emperor Trajan for advice about the Christians. Pliny had been sent to Bithynia and Pontus, along the southern shore of the Black Sea, in what is now northern Turkey. He wrote to Trajan about nearly everything. When he asked to form a company of 150 firemen after a bad fire in Nicomedia, Trajan refused, because groups like that had a way of turning into political clubs. Rome distrusted any group that met on its own terms. The Christian community was exactly that kind of group.

Pliny admitted he had never attended a trial of Christians. He did not know whether the name itself was the crime or only the crimes people assumed went with it. He had improvised. He asked the accused three times whether they were Christians, warning them of the penalty. If they kept saying yes, he ordered them led away to execution, because whatever they believed, he explained, that kind of stubbornness and unbending obstinacy deserved to be punished. Those who denied it had to call on the gods, offer incense and wine before the emperor's statue, and curse Christ, things he had heard no real Christian could be forced to do.

From people who had left the church, some as long as twenty years before, Pliny learned what the Christians actually did. They met on a fixed day before dawn and sang a hymn to Christ "as to a god." They bound themselves by an oath, not to commit some crime but to keep from theft, robbery, and adultery, to keep their word, and to hand back money left in their trust. Later they shared a meal of ordinary, harmless food, and they had stopped even that once Pliny, on Trajan's orders, banned private associations. To check their story, he had two enslaved women tortured, women who were called *ministrae*, a word often translated deaconesses. He found nothing, he said, but a depraved and excessive superstition.

What worried him was how far it had spread. It had reached every age and every rank: men and women, the villages and farms as well as the cities. Since his crackdown, he reported, the temples that had been nearly deserted were filling again, and the trade in sacrificial animals was picking up after a long stretch with few buyers. A community with no legal standing had thinned out the temples of a Roman province enough that the governor could see it in the market.

Trajan approved. Christians were not to be hunted, and anonymous accusations were not to be admitted, but anyone accused and convicted was to be punished unless he proved his denial by worshiping the gods. That set the terms for most of the next century and a half. You could live for years as a Christian in a Roman city and never stand before a magistrate, or be denounced by a business rival and be dead within the month for no crime anyone could name. Pliny never did find a crime. He found people who would not do what everyone else did, and in his court that refusal was enough to kill them.

A letter in our New Testament had been addressed to Christians in that same region a generation or more before Pliny arrived: "To those who are elect exiles of the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1 Peter 1:1). It told them people would call them evildoers, and it told them how to answer: "Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:12). It also prepared them to suffer for the name alone: "Yet if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God in that name" (1 Peter 4:16). Pliny wanted to know whether the name itself was a crime. Decades before he asked, First Peter had told the Christians of Bithynia to expect it to be treated as one, and not to be ashamed when it was.

## How Rome Saw the Christians

It's easy to make Rome the villain of this story and stop there, but the Romans had their reasons. By ancient standards Rome was tolerant of religion. It absorbed the gods of the peoples it conquered and gave them temples. What it asked in return was participation. The harvest, the borders, and the health of the city all depended on what Romans called the *pax deorum*, the peace of the gods, and that peace was kept by public rites everyone was expected to share. Rome didn't much care what you believed in your heart, so long as you took part.

The Christians would not. And so Romans called them atheists. When the old bishop Polycarp was brought into the stadium at Smyrna around 155, the governor urged him to save his life by saying "Away with the atheists" (meaning the Christians). Polycarp waved his hand at the crowd and said it. Tertullian, writing in Carthage around 197, complained that whenever the Tiber flooded or the Nile failed to rise, whenever there was famine or plague, the crowd's answer was to throw the Christians to the lion.

The most serious case against them came from a philosopher named Celsus, who wrote around 170 to 180 the earliest full-length attack on Christianity that survives in any quantity. We have much of it only because Origen of Alexandria quoted it at length in his reply some seventy years later. Celsus sneered that the Christians recruited wool-workers, cobblers, and laundry workers, but his strongest argument was civic. If everyone did what the Christians did, he wrote, the emperor would be left deserted and the world would fall to the most lawless barbarians. The Christians lived under Roman law and enjoyed the Roman peace. What did they owe in return? Was refusing the gods really a matter of private conscience? Or was it, as Celsus believed, a kind of desertion?

Robert Louis Wilken, a historian of early Christianity who wrote a book on how the Christians looked to the Romans, took those critics seriously. They saw something true. Here was a community with officers and a common fund of its own and a Lord who was not Caesar, and it would not let itself be folded into the life of the city. Put most of us in a magistrate's chair with a failed harvest and soldiers needed on the border, and we would have seen it the way Celsus did.

The logic should sound familiar to us: a nation's welfare depends on public honor paid to its God, and the people who will not take part endanger everyone. Two days after September 11, 2001, Jerry Falwell said on national television that the pagans, the abortionists, the feminists, gays and lesbians, the ACLU, and others had helped make the attacks happen. He apologized soon after, but the reflex was older than he was. The instinct that blamed the Christians when the Tiber flooded is one Christians learned to use themselves, once they had a civic religion of their own to protect.

## The Martyrs of Lyon

The persecution at Lyon and nearby Vienne in 177, under the emperor Marcus Aurelius, survives in a letter the churches there sent to the Christians of Asia and Phrygia, much of which Eusebius later copied into his history of the church. It shows how persecution often began. It did not begin in the arena. Christians were first barred from private houses, from the public baths, and from the marketplace. Then came the crowd, with beatings, stoning, and plunder, and then arrest. Pagan slaves from Christian households, afraid of the tortures they had watched the Christians endure, accused their masters of eating children and committing incest.

Pothinus, the bishop, was past ninety. He was beaten by the crowd and died in prison two days later. Sanctus, a deacon, would not give his name, his city, or whether he was slave or free; to every question he answered in Latin, "I am a Christian." Blandina, an enslaved woman whose mistress feared she was too frail to hold out, was tortured from morning until evening, until her torturers were exhausted, and her only answer was that she was a Christian and that nothing wicked was done among them. Hung on a post in the arena, she reminded the others, the letter says, of the one who had been crucified for them. She was the last to die.

The emperor himself ruled that those who confessed should be put to death and those who denied should go free. Afterward the bodies were left under guard for six days, then burned, and the ashes swept into the Rhône so that the Christians, their killers said, would have no hope of rising again.

The same letter records that those who had been brought back to prison from the arena, covered with burns and wounds, refused to be called martyrs. That title, they said, belonged to Christ, "the faithful and true witness" (Revelation 3:14), and to those who had already died. They were only confessors. And they did not despise the ones who broke. They prayed for those who had denied Christ, as they prayed for their torturers, and many who had denied later stood up and confessed. The letter describes the church receiving back alive the children it had counted as dead. Nor did the church in Lyon die with its bishop. A presbyter named Irenaeus succeeded Pothinus and went on to write *Against Heresies*, one of the foundational works of Christian theology.

## Decius and the Certificates of Sacrifice

Around 248, Origen wrote that the Christians who had died for their faith were few and easily counted. He was right. For two centuries persecution had been local and sporadic, usually set off by a mob or a governor rather than by policy from Rome. Lyon was terrible, but it was local. In most places and most years, the cost of being a Christian was suspicion and exclusion.

Decius changed that. Rome had celebrated its thousandth birthday in 248, under the emperor Philip, in the middle of a crisis, with Goths pressing on the Danube and armies making and unmaking emperors. In late 249 or early 250, Decius ordered every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice to the gods before local commissioners and obtain a certificate saying so. Dozens of those certificates survive on papyrus from Egypt. The certificates that survive never mention the Christians, and historians still argue about whether they were the order's target or only its most conspicuous casualties. Decius may simply have wanted the whole empire to stand together before its gods in a dangerous time. Either way, every Christian in the empire now faced the same question.

Fabian, the bishop of Rome, died in January 250. Origen, who had written not long before that the martyrs were few, was imprisoned and tortured and died a few years later. But the larger story of Decius is how many Christians gave way. Some sacrificed. Others bought certificates without going near an altar. Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, wrote afterward with real grief that many did not wait to be arrested but went to the forum and sacrificed on their own, and he named the reason: a long peace had corrupted the church. Christians had become absorbed in adding to their estates, and some bishops had left their congregations to chase profit in distant markets. Cyprian himself had gone into hiding. He took criticism for it.

We like to imagine the early church as a church of heroes. Some of it was. Much of it was ordinary people with families and shops and debts, who had grown used to a faith that cost them little. If I had been a Christian merchant in Carthage in 250, with children at home and a way to buy a certificate without touching the altar, I am not at all sure what I would have done.

After Decius died fighting the Goths in 251, the churches had to decide what to do with the lapsed who wanted to come back. Novatian, a Roman presbyter, argued that the church had no power to restore them. Cornelius, the new bishop of Rome, and Cyprian argued for real penance followed by restoration, and the councils of 251 sided with them.

Soon afterward Cornelius described the Christian community in Rome in a letter Eusebius preserved. He counted forty-six presbyters, seven deacons, seven subdeacons, forty-two acolytes, and fifty-two exorcists, readers, and doorkeepers, along with more than fifteen hundred widows and people in distress, all of them fed by the church. This was a year after the worst persecution it had yet faced. When the emperor Valerian renewed the persecution in 257, Cyprian was sent into exile. In 258 the proconsul sentenced him to death. Cyprian answered, "Thanks be to God."

## How the Church Grew

How did a community so small and so exposed keep growing? The sociologist Rodney Stark estimated in the 1990s that the church grew at about forty percent a decade and that by the year 300 there were around six million Christians, about a tenth of the empire. Historians argue about his figures, as they should, but the shape is widely accepted: a small minority growing steadily for two and a half centuries with no help from the state. Growth like that does not depend on crowds. It moves through networks of households and friendships.

Part of what moved through those networks was care. During a plague in the early 260s, Dionysius, the bishop of Alexandria, wrote that most of the Christians had nursed the sick without sparing themselves, many catching the disease and dying of it, while their pagan neighbors pushed the sick away, fled even from those dearest to them, and left the dead unburied. A bishop writing to his own flock is not a neutral witness. But a century later the emperor Julian, trying to revive the old religion, complained to one of his high priests that the Galileans, as he called the Christians, supported not only their own poor but the pagans' poor as well. Stark's account of the mechanism was plain: simple nursing (food and water and someone who stays) saves a great many lives in an epidemic, so Christian communities came through each plague with more survivors and more neighbors who owed them their lives.

For many Romans, that common life was the only argument for the faith they ever saw. Tertullian described a monthly collection, given freely, that fed and buried the poor, supported orphans and the housebound old, and helped Christians sent to the mines or the prison islands for their faith. "See how they love one another," he reported the pagans saying.

And the church was patient. Alan Kreider, a Mennonite historian, built his account of its growth around that single virtue. The early church left no handbook on evangelism, but Tertullian and Cyprian each wrote a treatise on patience. Outsiders were not admitted to the Lord's Supper. A church order traditionally attributed to Hippolytus of Rome examined a candidate's occupation before instruction began, required some trades to be given up, and expected as long as three years of hearing the word before baptism. The church was not in a hurry. It was forming people who could stand before a magistrate, because it knew that day might come.

That runs against most of what we in the American church assume about growth. The early church made joining slower and harder, and it kept growing anyway.

Through all of it, the Christians stayed in the life of the empire. Tertullian told his readers that Christians sailed, farmed, traded, and even soldiered alongside them, and that they prayed for the emperors, for brave armies, a faithful senate, and a quiet world. Justin Martyr told the emperor around 150 that Christians paid their taxes more readily than anyone, because Christ had told them to, and that while they worshiped God alone, in everything else they gladly served. They were living by the line First Peter had drawn: "Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor" (1 Peter 2:17). Fear, in Peter's list, is kept for God alone. So they gave Caesar their taxes, their prayers, and their labor, and some of them served in his legions, but they would not give him a pinch of incense.

## The Great Persecution

After 260 came more than forty years of peace. Eusebius, who lived through it, described large new churches going up in every city and Christians trusted with provincial governorships, and he described what the freedom did to them: they fell into laxity and sloth, envied and attacked one another, and their leaders quarreled. Cyprian had said much the same half a century earlier, and both bishops read the persecution that followed, at least in part, as God's discipline of a church that peace had made comfortable.

On February 23, 303, soldiers tore down the church at Nicomedia that stood within sight of the imperial palace. The next day the first edict of the Great Persecution ordered churches destroyed, Scriptures handed over and burned, and Christians stripped of rank and of the protection of the courts. Later edicts jailed the clergy, and in 304 everyone was again ordered to sacrifice. In Gaul and Britain, Constantius, the father of Constantine, did little. In the East, under Galerius and then Maximinus Daia, it went on for most of a decade.

On April 30, 311, Galerius, dying of a horrible illness, issued an edict that let Christians exist and meet again, and at the end of it he asked them to pray to their God for his safety and for the safety of the state. He was dead within days. The thing he finally asked of them was the thing they had never stopped doing, because Paul had told Timothy, "First of all, then, I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified in every way" (1 Timothy 2:1–2).

The next year an emperor in the West marched on Rome under a sign he believed the Christian God had given him. Eusebius, whose teacher Pamphilus had been executed in the persecution, would end his life as Constantine's admirer and biographer, describing his reign as an earthly copy of the reign of God. It's hard to blame him. After what he had seen, an emperor who honored bishops and rebuilt churches looked like the answer to every prayer the martyrs had prayed. The church was about to enter a different kind of trial, protection instead of persecution, and Christians have argued ever since over whether, and when, the church recognized it as a trial at all.

## What the Early Church Teaches a Post-Christian America

Someone will object that America is not Rome, and that comparing ourselves to Lyon or Decius flatters us. The objection is right. No one here is being asked for a certificate of sacrifice. The First Amendment still protects the free exercise of religion, and Christians in other parts of the world face prison and death in ways American Christians do not. What is ending here is the cultural default: the old assumption that a respectable American is some kind of Christian. Gallup found that membership in a church, synagogue, or mosque fell to 47 percent of Americans in 2020, below half for the first time in more than eighty years of measurement, down from 70 percent in 1999. Pew reported in 2025 that 62 percent of American adults identify as Christian, down from 78 percent in 2007, though that decline may now have leveled off.

What we face is closer to the way things began in Lyon, before anyone was arrested, when the cost was being shut out of the houses and the baths and the market and being thought strange or dangerous by the neighbors. That was the cost most of the early church bore in most years, and the confessors who bore far worse still would not let anyone call them martyrs. Many of us have called the loss of deference persecution. That should embarrass us. If losing cultural standing feels like losing the faith, then more of our faith was resting on that standing than we knew.

The church before Constantine never had the culture's deference to lose, so its credibility had to come from somewhere else, and it came from the widows' rolls and the plague wards. Celsus read the Christians closely and despised them. Julian watched them feed his own people and complained of it. A church fighting to recover its place is not going to persuade a post-Christian America of anything. If anything persuades it, it will be what Julian saw: a community that stays when others flee and feeds the poor who are not its own.

Cyprian and Eusebius left a warning, and both of them had lived through what they were warning about. Decius did not create the lapsed. A long peace did, and the persecution only revealed them. American Christianity has had a far longer peace than Carthage ever did, and many will fall away under much less than Decius demanded. The early church met that danger by forming people slowly, before the day came, and when people failed anyway, it chose Cornelius over Novatian: real repentance, and then restoration.

Where did those Christians draw the line? They paid their taxes, prayed for the emperor, traded in the market, and obeyed the law wherever the law did not require worship. They never asked Rome to establish their faith, and they had no Christian culture to mourn. What they refused was the pinch of incense: the small public act that placed their final loyalty with the state and its gods.

A post-Christian America will have civic gestures of its own, asked of Christians as the price of respectability, from the left and from the right. Some will be the honor Peter told Christians to give the emperor. Others will be the worship that belongs to God alone. Which is which? And how would we know? We are not well practiced at asking, since for most of American history we were the ones who decided what the civic gestures would be.

The men who swept the Lyon ashes into the Rhône did it, they said, so the Christians would have no hope of rising again. They understood better than many Christians do now where that church's hope lay. Its whole life rested on the claim that Jesus had been raised from the dead and that everyone who belonged to Him would be raised too. That claim is what let Blandina answer the way she did and let Cyprian thank God in front of the man who sentenced him. It made the loss of the baths and the market bearable, because the church had never depended on those.

Having no position to protect left the church before Constantine free to spend itself on neighbors who distrusted it and to pray for emperors who hunted it.

That is the church a post-Christian America is going to need. It is also the church we have had the least practice being, and that includes the church I pastor.

The Christians of Lyon found out what they were standing on when everything else had been taken from them. Most of us have never had to find out.

But nothing they stood on has been taken from us.

The risen Christ whom Blandina confessed in the arena is enough for His church in a country that no longer defers to it.
