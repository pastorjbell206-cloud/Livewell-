# Can You Trust the Bible?

*The evidence for how the Bible was copied, collected and written is stronger than the rumors about it, and the question of trusting it finally comes to rest on Jesus.*

As a young student at Princeton Theological Seminary, Bart Ehrman wrote a paper on a single verse. He had come from Moody Bible Institute and Wheaton College, and he believed, as he had been taught, that the Bible was without error in every detail. The verse was Mark 2:26, where Jesus says David entered the house of God and ate the bread of the Presence "in the time of Abiathar the high priest." The trouble is that 1 Samuel 21 tells that story, and the priest who gave David the bread was Ahimelech, Abiathar's father.

Ehrman built a long and careful argument that Mark didn't mean what he seemed to say. His professor, Cullen Story, wrote one line at the end. Maybe, he suggested, Mark had just made a mistake.

Ehrman has told that story many times, most widely in *Misquoting Jesus*, his 2005 book on how the New Testament was copied, which became a surprise bestseller. Once he admitted that much, he wrote, the floodgates opened. If Mark could be wrong in one place, he could be wrong in others, and Ehrman began to find them. He became one of the best-known New Testament scholars in the country, and eventually an agnostic. He has said plainly that what finally ended his faith was suffering, which he could no longer square with a good and powerful God. The manuscripts weren't the reason. Many of his readers, though, closed his book convinced the Bible couldn't be trusted.

The question usually comes from one of two directions. The skeptic asks it because he has heard that nobody knows what the text first said, that powerful men picked the books long afterward, and that the Gospels contradict each other. The believer asks it because, like the young Ehrman, he was taught that one mistake would bring the whole thing down, and then he found a verse he couldn't make fit. Ehrman matters here because he has asked the question from both sides. He is also right about a good deal.

No original manuscript of any book of the Bible survives. Every copy we have was made by hand from earlier copies, by scribes who got tired and made mistakes. Some of them changed words on purpose. Ehrman's more technical book, *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*, argued that scribes sometimes altered a passage to make it say more plainly what they already believed about Christ. The Gospels do differ in their details, and the church did eventually draw up lists of the books that belonged. A Christian loses nothing by admitting any of it. The question is what those facts add up to.

## Do We Have What They Wrote?

Start with the copies. One of Ehrman's most quoted lines is that our manuscripts contain more variations than there are words in the New Testament. That is true, and it sounds devastating until you ask why the number is so large. We have more than five thousand Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, whole or in part, along with thousands more in early translations such as Latin, Syriac and Coptic. Every difference among them counts as a variant, and with that many copies to compare, the count was always going to be high. Most are spelling, word order, a skipped line, or a slip of the pen, and many don't even show up when the Greek is put into English.

Compare the books historians trust every day. The first six books of Tacitus's *Annals* are one of our main sources for the reign of the emperor Tiberius. They survive in a single manuscript copied in the ninth century, some seven hundred years after Tacitus wrote. Classicists don't conclude that we can't know what Tacitus said. They work from that one copy, fixing the slips they can spot and marking the places they can't settle. New Testament scholars use the same method with far more material. A slip in a single copy can easily go unseen, but among thousands of copies made in Egypt and Syria and Italy by people who never met, a mistake in one place stands out against all the others.

Most of those thousands, though, are medieval, and the earliest centuries, when the text was most open to change, are the ones we see least clearly. Ehrman presses that point, and he's right to. The oldest surviving piece of the New Testament is usually thought to be a scrap of John's Gospel about the size of a credit card, now in Manchester. It is commonly dated to the second century, though some papyrologists argue it could be later. Larger papyri survive from the late second and third centuries, and the oldest complete New Testament, Codex Sinaiticus, was copied in the fourth. There is a gap, and nobody should pretend there isn't. But it is a gap of generations, not the seven centuries that separate Tacitus from his manuscript. The early papyri and the great fourth-century copies carry substantially the same text.

The differences that matter are few, and they aren't hidden. Open almost any modern Bible to the end of Mark and you'll find a note that some of the earliest manuscripts stop at 16:8. Turn to John 8 and the story of the woman caught in adultery is set off in brackets, with a note that the earliest manuscripts don't include it. The translators are showing you exactly where the uncertainty is, so you don't have to take anyone's word for it.

The plainest answer to the charge of a cover-up is a verse whose history few Christians know. In the King James Version, 1 John 5:7 reads, "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." It is the most explicit statement of the Trinity in that Bible. It also doesn't belong there. It came into the text through Latin copies and appears in only a handful of late Greek manuscripts. Erasmus left it out of his printed Greek New Testament in 1516 and added it to his third edition in 1522, and from there it passed into the King James.

The Greek theologians who argued out the doctrine of the Trinity in the fourth century never quoted that verse, because their Bibles didn't have it. Most modern translations, made by scholars who confess the Trinity, have taken it out again. The doctrine never rested on the verse. When the evidence showed it wasn't original, most of the church let it go.

The Old Testament has its own version of this story. The oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, the Leningrad Codex, was copied in 1008. Then, beginning in 1947, Bedouin shepherds and later archaeologists brought hundreds of scrolls out of caves near Qumran, above the Dead Sea. Among them was a complete scroll of Isaiah, usually dated to the second century before Christ. It is about a thousand years older than any Hebrew copy of Isaiah known before. Most of its differences from the later text are spelling and small points of grammar.

The scrolls also showed that a few books, Jeremiah among them, circulated in more than one form before the text settled, and scholars are still working out what that means. That complication is real. But someone reading Isaiah 53 from that scroll is reading substantially the chapter you can read tonight.

Ehrman agrees with more of this than his readers sometimes realize. In a question-and-answer appendix to *Misquoting Jesus*, he said his position did not stand at odds with that of his old teacher, Bruce Metzger, who held that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by the variants in the New Testament manuscripts. Metzger was an ordained Presbyterian minister. Ehrman became an agnostic. On that point, at least, they still agreed.

## Who Chose the Books?

The second story is that the books were chosen late by men with power, and it is usually set at the Council of Nicaea in 325. Nicaea argued about whether the Son is fully God. It didn't draw up a list of books. The real history is slower and less dramatic. Around 180, Irenaeus, the bishop of Lyon, could argue that there are four Gospels, no more and no fewer, and treat it as settled. Paul's letters were being read as Scripture even earlier. Within the New Testament itself, 2 Peter sets them beside "the other Scriptures," and admits along the way that "there are some things in them that are hard to understand" (2 Peter 3:16).

The first list we know of that names exactly the twenty-seven books of our New Testament comes from Athanasius of Alexandria, in his letter to the churches for Easter in 367. Getting there wasn't tidy. Some books, such as Hebrews, James and Revelation, were questioned for a long time in some places, and the church argued about them in the open. Its own historians wrote those arguments down, and they can still be read. The councils that came later mostly put in writing what the churches had already been reading for generations. The longer story is in [Who Decided What Books Are in the Bible?](/writing/who-decided-what-books-are-in-the-bible)

## Did It Happen?

Copying the words correctly doesn't make the words true. A lie can be preserved perfectly. So the next question is historical: do these books describe a real world, and do they describe it accurately? The Bible invites the question. Luke opens his Gospel the way a historian would:

> "Inasmuch as many have undertaken to compile a narrative of the things that have been accomplished among us, just as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word have delivered them to us, it seemed good to me also, having followed all things closely for some time past, to write an orderly account for you, most excellent Theophilus, that you may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught." (Luke 1:1–4)

That is a man who says he checked his sources. He also dates his story the way ancient historians did, by naming who ruled. John the Baptist began preaching in the fifteenth year of Tiberius, when Pontius Pilate governed Judea and Annas and Caiaphas held the high priesthood (Luke 3:1–2). Claims like that can be tested, and some have been.

In 1961, archaeologists at Caesarea Maritima, the seat of the Roman governors on the coast, found a limestone block, reused in a theater, inscribed with Pilate's name and his title, prefect of Judea. An inscription from Delphi in Greece places Gallio's term as proconsul of Achaia around AD 51 to 52. That gives historians one of the firmest dates in Paul's life, his hearing before Gallio in Corinth (Acts 18:12). In 1993, excavators at Tel Dan in northern Israel found part of a ninth-century BC Aramaic inscription in which a foreign king boasts of a victory over the "House of David." As most scholars read it, it is the earliest mention of David's dynasty outside the Bible.

Archaeology doesn't prove the Bible, and Christians who talk as if it does claim more than the evidence gives them. A stone with Pilate's name on it shows that Luke had the governor right. It says nothing about whether Jesus rose from the dead. Some questions are genuinely hard. The archaeology of the exodus and the conquest of Canaan is argued over intensely, and believing scholars disagree among themselves about how to read it. What the evidence does establish is narrower, and it still matters. These books are set among real rulers, offices and places, and where they can be checked, they have often held up.

The center of the New Testament is a claim archaeology can't reach. Writing to the Corinthians in the mid-50s, Paul passes on a summary he says he himself received. Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Peter and then to the Twelve. Then Paul adds a line that reads like an invitation to check: "Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:6).

A few verses later he stakes everything on it. "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). The evidence for that claim needs its own essay, and you'll find it in [Did the Resurrection Actually Happen?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen) What matters here is that the New Testament doesn't ask to be excused from history. Its central claim is one that could be proved false.

## What About the Contradictions?

Then there are the apparent contradictions, which is where Ehrman's trouble started. Many of them are between the Gospels. They're real. Read the four accounts of Easter morning side by side. Matthew has Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary" at the tomb, and an angel sitting on the stone. Mark has three women and a young man in a white robe inside the tomb. Luke has a larger group of women and two men in dazzling clothes. John tells it through Mary Magdalene, who later sees two angels where the body had been. A Christian who says these differences aren't there hasn't read the chapters closely. The skeptic can tell.

The church has always known this. In the second half of the second century, a Syrian Christian named Tatian wove the four Gospels into one continuous account, the *Diatessaron*, and for a long time it was the Gospel many Syriac-speaking churches read. In the fifth century Theodoret, the bishop of Cyrrhus, wrote that he had found more than two hundred copies of it in the churches of his region, and that he had collected them and put the Gospels of the four evangelists in their place. Around 400, Augustine wrote a long work on the agreement of the Gospels, answering critics who had already spotted the differences people point to now. The church had a single, smooth account of Jesus within reach, and it kept the four that don't line up perfectly.

That choice makes sense once you remember what testimony usually looks like. Several people describing the same confusing morning will each remember it a little differently, and investigators grow suspicious when witnesses agree too neatly. Ancient biographers also arranged material by theme, compressed events and summarized speeches as a matter of course, and the Gospel writers wrote within those habits.

That doesn't make every difficulty disappear. Mark 2:26 still has no explanation everyone accepts. Some scholars point out that Mark uses the same Greek construction later in his Gospel to mean "in the passage about" (Mark 12:26), so the phrase may name a section of Scripture rather than a date. Others find that unconvincing. But on what matters most, the four agree. Jesus was crucified and buried, women found his tomb empty, and his followers became convinced they had seen him alive.

The church has to own its part in stories like Ehrman's. Many evangelical churches confess that Scripture is without error. A careful modern statement of that conviction, the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy of 1978, was drafted by scholars who knew these passages well. It says in so many words that inerrancy is not negated by "the topical arrangement of material, variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations." As its own framers stated it, the doctrine never required the four Gospels to tell the story the same way.

But a great many young Christians absorb a faith in which the Bible stands or falls with their ability to win every argument about every verse. When they meet a difficulty they can't solve, they think they have to choose between telling the truth and keeping Christ. We taught them that, usually without meaning to. The Bible never did. It even has one apostle admitting that another is sometimes hard to understand.

## What About the Hard Passages?

It was suffering, and not Abiathar, that finally ended Ehrman's faith. For many skeptics the deepest problem with the Bible isn't that its words were changed. It's that they weren't, and some of them are hard to read: laws that regulate slavery, commands in Joshua to destroy whole towns, psalms that ask God to break the teeth of enemies. Those questions deserve more than a paragraph. One of them gets a full essay in [Why Didn't the Bible Just Ban Slavery?](/writing/why-didnt-the-bible-ban-slavery)

A reader who asks them has moved from "Do we have the text?" to "Is the God of this text good?" That is a better question. The Bible asks it too. Job asks it chapter after chapter, and Jeremiah and Habakkuk put it to God to his face.

Some distrust of the Bible was earned, though not by the book. It was earned by people who quoted it to defend what they wanted, or used it as a club. Many of us have done a smaller version of the same thing. We reach for the verses that support us and pass over the ones that would correct us. If your questions about the Bible arrived with a loss or a wound and not only with an argument, you don't have to sort them out alone. [Find Help](/help) is a place to start.

## Why It Comes Back to Jesus

There is a way of defending the Bible that runs in a circle: it's true because it says it's true. The first Christians couldn't have reasoned that way, because they had no New Testament. They had the Scriptures of Israel and the news that Jesus had been raised. They trusted Jesus because they believed God had vindicated him by raising him from the dead, and then they received the Scriptures the way he did.

Jesus treated the Scriptures as the word of God. In an argument with his opponents he rested his case on a phrase from Psalm 82 and said that "Scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35). But he also told opponents who knew the Scriptures as well as anyone that they had missed what the book was for. "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me, yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (John 5:39–40).

After his resurrection, walking with two of his followers on the road to Emmaus, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27). The Bible's own account of itself is that it points to a person.

The manuscripts, the history of the canon and the archaeology can clear away bad reasons for dismissing the book, and I think they do. They can't make you trust it, any more than a well-documented biography makes you trust the man it describes. Christians trust the Bible because they have come to trust the one it is about. The order matters. You could settle every question about Abiathar and still have to decide what to do with Jesus. Plenty of believers have carried unanswered questions about hard verses for a lifetime and trusted him the whole way.

## What Trust Looks Like

If you're a skeptic, I'd ask one thing of you. Read one Gospel whole, in a sitting or two, the way you would read any ancient source you wanted to understand. Mark is the shortest. Notice what it keeps that anyone managing a reputation would have cut: disciples who don't understand, Peter's denial, women running from the empty tomb too afraid to speak. You may finish unconvinced. But you'll be arguing with the book and not with a rumor about it. The [Study Bible](/study/bible) is a good place to read it with notes beside you.

For those of us who already say we trust it, the harder question is whether our lives show it. A church can defend the reliability of the Bible loudly and read it very little. It can also quote it constantly while almost never letting it correct anything. Scripture itself expects its readers to get it wrong, which is why it keeps sending prophets to people who already have the Scriptures. That is the argument of [The Bible Assumes You Will Be Wrong](/writing/the-bible-assumes-you-will-be-wrong). Trusting the Bible means letting it tell us where we are wrong, and then changing.

The two on the road to Emmaus had known the Scriptures all their lives. What they lacked was the one who could open them. Afterward they asked each other, "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?" (Luke 24:32).

Whatever you make of the manuscripts, there is a question underneath them. It isn't only whether the book was copied well, though it was. It's whether the one it bears witness to is alive.

If he is, you can trust him with the hard verses too.

*"The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever."* (Isaiah 40:8)

*"These are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name."* (John 20:31)
