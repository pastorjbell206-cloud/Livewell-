# Why Are People Leaving the Church?

*Millions of Americans have left the church since the 1990s, and many of them simply drifted away from a faith the church had made too thin to hold them.*

On July 21, 2024, First Baptist Church of Mount Vernon, Illinois, held its last service. The congregation had about 170 members in the late 1990s. It had about fifty when Ryan Burge arrived as a young pastor in the mid-2000s, and fewer than twenty when it closed. Burge gave the closing message, and afterward the members held a short meeting and voted to close the church as of August 1.

Burge is also a political scientist. He has spent years studying the Americans who answer "none" when a survey asks their religion, and in 2021 he published a book about them called *The Nones*. Few pastors could have seen what was happening to their own congregation as clearly as he could. The church closed anyway. Nearly a year later he wrote that he still wasn't over it.

Why are people leaving? Most churchgoers have an answer ready. Many conservatives would say the culture turned. The universities, the entertainment industry and the sexual revolution taught a generation to see faith as backward, and the churches that softened their message to keep up only hurried people toward the door. Many progressives would say the church drove people out. It tied itself to a political party, protected its abusers, and met gay neighbors and doubting teenagers with contempt, so people with a conscience left. There is evidence for each account. But each also locates the problem among the other kind of Christian, which ought to make us wary.

The research bears out parts of both stories. It finds real disbelief, and it finds real injury. What it finds most often is something less dramatic. A great many people didn't storm out of the church. They drifted, and a good share of what they drifted away from was a faith we had made too thin to keep.

## How the Nones Grew

In 1968 a sociologist named Glenn Vernon published an article in the *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion* calling the religious "nones" a neglected category. Researchers, he complained, defined these people by what they lacked and then mostly ignored them. He proposed calling them religious independents. The name never caught on, and for a long time the group hardly seemed large enough to need one. When the General Social Survey began asking Americans about their religious preference in 1972, about 5 percent said they had none. Twenty years later the figure was still in the single digits.

Then it moved. In 2002 the sociologists Michael Hout and Claude Fischer reported that the share of American adults with no religious preference had doubled during the 1990s, from 7 percent to 14 percent. By 2018 the General Social Survey put it near a quarter of adults. Pew's large 2023-24 religion survey found nearly three in ten, and it also found that the climb had leveled off, at least for now. I've written about what those numbers mean for the country in [what "post-Christian" actually means](/writing/what-post-christian-means). The question here is narrower. Who left, and why did they go?

Hout and Fischer's explanation for the 1990s had two parts. Most of the new nones, they found, had not stopped believing in God. They held fairly conventional beliefs and had dropped the label and the institution. Sociologists have a name for people like this: unchurched believers. The increase was also concentrated among political moderates and liberals. Through those same years the religious right had become the public face of American Christianity, and Hout and Fischer argued that people who didn't share its politics began declining a religious identity that now seemed to come bundled with them.

In a 2014 follow-up they went further. Political identity, they concluded, was shaping religious identity, rather than the other way around. Robert Putnam and David Campbell had made a similar case in *American Grace* in 2010. Young Americans, they argued, came to associate religion itself with conservative politics, especially on homosexuality, and many of them walked away from religion.

## Politics and the Mainline

Christians on the right should take that finding seriously, because it describes something we did. When a church lets its name become shorthand for a party, it loses the people who can't vote for that party. Many of the people Hout and Fischer studied had not given up on God. What they turned down was a religious label that had come to stand for a political side.

The mainline Protestant churches, which on the whole kept their distance from the religious right, have been shrinking longer and faster, and Christians on the left should take that just as seriously. In 1972 Dean Kelley, in a study commissioned by the National Council of Churches, published *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing*. He argued that churches which make real demands on their members hold them, and churches which ask little do not. Demographers later complicated his story. A 2001 study by Hout, Andrew Greeley and Melissa Wilde found that for Americans born between 1903 and 1973, about three-quarters of the mainline decline came from birth rates. Women in the conservative denominations had more children, and had them earlier.

That is a real correction, and it means the mainline decline can't be read as simple proof that accommodation empties churches. Even so, when Pew measured in 2007, mainline Protestants made up 18 percent of American adults. By 2023-24 they made up 11 percent. Evangelicals fell over the same years too, from 26 percent to 23. So the decline has no political home. Political religion on the right helped drive many moderates and liberals away from the label altogether, and the churches that made the most room for the culture's changes have kept their own people no better.

## What the People Who Left Say

In the summer of 2023 Pew surveyed more than 3,300 nones. Most of them, 63 percent, described their religion as "nothing in particular." Seventeen percent called themselves atheists and 20 percent agnostics. Asked why they aren't religious, 60 percent said that questioning many religious teachings was an extremely or very important reason. Forty-seven percent said they dislike religious organizations, and 30 percent pointed to bad experiences with religious people. About four in ten said they simply don't need religion, and 12 percent said they don't have time for it.

Lifeway Research asked a narrower question in a study released in 2019. Of young adults who had attended a Protestant church regularly as teenagers, 66 percent had stopped attending regularly for at least a year somewhere between the ages of 18 and 22. The reason they gave most often was moving away to college (34 percent). Close behind came church members who seemed judgmental or hypocritical (32 percent) and no longer feeling connected to the people at church (29 percent). A quarter disagreed with the church's stance on political or social issues, and about as many had work obligations.

The broadest recent study is *The Great Dechurching*, a 2023 book by Jim Davis and Michael Graham built on surveys by Burge and his fellow political scientist Paul Djupe. They counted as dechurched anyone who used to attend at least monthly and now attends less than once a year. By their estimate some 40 million Americans left church in that sense over the previous twenty-five years. Some of them had been badly hurt, by abuse or by the hypocrisy of the people who led them, and those stories deserve every bit of attention they get. But by the authors' account most of the dechurched left casually, without a crisis of faith or a deep wound. Their lives changed, and the habit of church never got rebuilt.

Pew also found that nones are less likely than religious Americans to volunteer. In the 2022 midterm elections, by verified voter records, 39 percent of nones voted, compared with 51 percent of the religiously affiliated. The gaps were concentrated among the "nothing in particular," not among atheists and agnostics. The popular picture of a none is a confident skeptic who thought her way out of faith. Some nones fit it. The larger group looks more like people whose ties to civic life have loosened in general, the same loosening of American association, religious participation included, that Robert Putnam traced in *Bowling Alone* in 2000. For them the church was one institution among several.

## The Faith We Handed Down

The drift ought to trouble the church more than the arguments do. A person who leaves because he no longer believes in the resurrection has at least taken the resurrection seriously. A person who drifts away after a move has told us something harder to hear. What he received in church was not strong enough to survive the move.

Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton saw the problem early. In *Soul Searching*, published in 2005 and drawn from the National Study of Youth and Religion, they described the working faith of many American teenagers, including many raised in church, and called it moralistic therapeutic deism. In their account it runs roughly like this. A God exists who made the world and watches over it. He wants people to be good, nice and fair to each other. The central goal of life is to be happy and to feel good about yourself. God doesn't need to be particularly involved in your life except when you need Him to solve a problem, and good people go to heaven when they die.

Smith and Denton did not find teenagers in revolt against their parents' religion. Most said their beliefs were similar to their parents', and the strongest influence on their faith was their parents. The teenagers had learned what they were taught.

A faith whose main promise is that God will help you feel good about yourself gives no reason to get up on a Sunday morning once nobody expects you to. We tend to speak of the nones as people who left the church's faith. A good many of them left a substitute that churches and families had allowed to stand in its place, and some of them saw through it.

A thin, untaught faith is not a new problem. In 1528 and 1529 Martin Luther took part in a visitation of the parishes of Saxony, and in the preface to his Small Catechism he described what he found. Ordinary people knew almost nothing of Christian teaching, and many pastors were unfit to teach it. Everyone called himself a Christian, had been baptized and received the sacrament, and yet many could not recite the Lord's Prayer, the Creed or the Ten Commandments. Centuries of Christian society had left those villagers baptized and nearly untaught. Luther's answer was a small book meant to be taught at home, line by line, by the head of the household. American Christendom left many Americans in much the same condition, and when it ended they found out how little of the faith they knew.

Vern Bengtson's research comes at the same problem from inside the home. His Longitudinal Study of Generations began in 1970 and eventually followed more than 350 families, more than 3,500 people. In *Families and Faith* he reported that faith passed most strongly from one generation to the next in warm families, and especially where children saw their father as warm and close. Taking the children to church, modeling the faith and holding devotions at home were not enough by themselves. Asked why a father's warmth mattered so much, Bengtson said he didn't know. I was raised without a father, and I am raising five sons. I don't read that finding as somebody else's problem.

## When People Walked Away From Jesus

People walked away from Jesus too. The day after Jesus fed the five thousand, a crowd crossed the Sea of Galilee to find Him, and He told them plainly why they had come: "you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves" (John 6:26). Then, in the synagogue at Capernaum, He taught that He was the bread come down from heaven and that whoever feeds on His flesh and drinks His blood has eternal life. Many of His own disciples answered, "This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?" (John 6:60). John records the outcome in one sentence: "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him" (John 6:66).

Jesus did not chase them with an easier sermon. He turned to the Twelve and asked, "Do you want to go away as well?" Peter answered, "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life, and we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God" (John 6:67–69). Peter didn't say the teaching had become easy. He said there was no one else to go to.

Some people leave the church because of Jesus. They hear what He actually claims about Himself, about sin and judgment, about money and sex and enemies and death, and they cannot accept it. The church can't solve that by removing the hard sayings, and it shouldn't try. For some, the hard saying is the church's teaching on sex and marriage, and a church cannot drop a teaching it believes Christ gave in order to keep a member. What it can do is repent of the contempt that so often came with the teaching, because Christ never taught that.

Paul had a different kind of departure in view when he wrote to the church in Rome about his own people. He addressed a man sure he was "a guide to the blind, a light to those who are in darkness," and asked him, "you then who teach others, do you not teach yourself? While you preach against stealing, do you steal?" Then he quoted the prophets: "The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you" (Romans 2:19, 21, 24). Paul was describing insiders whose conduct taught outsiders to despise the God they claimed to serve.

Others leave because of the church. They saw abusers protected, or heard the gospel preached as a party platform, or brought a real question and got a lecture on their attitude instead. No passage of Scripture asks us to call that faithfulness.

Every church is tempted to file each departure in whichever column flatters it. A conservative congregation hears that its young people have gone and assumes they couldn't bear the truth, so it files them with the crowd in John 6. A progressive congregation assumes they were wounded by somebody else's harshness and files them under Romans 2. The data won't let either filing stand. Plenty of the people who left were not taking a stand at all. They simply stopped coming, which is exactly the danger the letter to the Hebrews names: "not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another" (Hebrews 10:25). Drifting from the gathering was already a habit in the first century. The remedy in that letter is a congregation in which people "consider how to stir up one another to love and good works" (Hebrews 10:24).

There's a verse churches reach for when someone leaves: "They went out from us, but they were not of us" (1 John 2:19). John wrote it about teachers who denied that Jesus is the Christ (1 John 2:22). He was not writing about a nineteen-year-old who moved to a college town and never found a church. Used that way, the verse closes a question the church is supposed to keep open. It lets us stop looking for people the Lord has not stopped looking for.

## What a Church Can Actually Do

The simplest thing is to ask people back. In the Great Dechurching surveys, about half of the dechurched evangelicals said they would be willing to return, and when asked what might bring them back, some named making new friends or being invited by a friend. Most of the casually dechurched didn't leave in anger. A mailing or a new sermon series won't reach them the way a friend will, someone who keeps asking and then sits with them when they come.

Tell the truth about abuse, all of it, and early. When the Boston Globe exposed the cover-up of abusive priests in the Archdiocese of Boston in 2002, it was easy for Protestants to treat it as a Catholic problem. In May 2022 an independent investigation commissioned by the Southern Baptist Convention reported that two former officials of its Executive Committee had kept a private list of accused ministers for years, while survivors who came forward met resistance and even hostility from some committee members. No tradition is safe from this, and the church's credibility on every other subject rides on how it answers.

Abuse by a pastor or any church leader should be reported to the authorities, not handled quietly inside the church. If you have been harmed, you can [find help here](/help).

Keep the pulpit from becoming a campaign office for either party. A congregation can and should hold members of both parties, but it cannot preach a gospel that sounds like one party's platform read aloud and still be faithful. I've written elsewhere about how [conservative](/writing/the-conservative-blind-spot) and [progressive](/writing/the-progressive-blind-spot) Christians each mistake their own community's instincts for the voice of God. The young adults who left over politics were right that something had gone badly wrong.

Teach the faith as though the culture will not teach it for you, because it won't. A church that settles for moralistic therapeutic deism never gives its children anything worth staying for, and it will keep producing nones. Luther's small book still points the way: the Creed, the Commandments and the Lord's Prayer, taught at home and explained one line at a time, with the expectation that a child can learn what the church believes and why. And make room for questions while you teach. Sixty percent of the nones in Pew's survey said questioning religious teachings was a major reason they aren't religious. A church that meets every question with a raised eyebrow is training its children to take their questions somewhere else.

In the home, Bengtson's finding means that the most persuasive case for the faith many children will ever hear is a parent who practices it and is plainly glad to be with them. Nobody in a congregation is excused from that. A single mother can give it, and so can a grandfather, or an older couple who decide that the boy without a father three rows ahead of them is now their concern.

## The Shepherds and the Scattered Sheep

Ezekiel, prophesying among the exiles in Babylon, explained why God's people had been scattered, and he did not begin with the sheep. "Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" (Ezekiel 34:2). The charge is specific: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them." The result follows in the next breath: "So they were scattered, because there was no shepherd" (Ezekiel 34:4–5).

Then the prophet turns to the flock itself. Some of the sheep had grown fat on the good pasture and trampled the rest of it, and God says to them, "you push with side and shoulder, and thrust at all the weak with your horns, till you have scattered them abroad" (Ezekiel 34:21). The scattering wasn't only the shepherds' doing. The comfortable members of the flock had shouldered the weak ones out.

Pastors stand where Ezekiel's shepherds stood, and I am one of them. Long-time members are the strong sheep, and many of us reading this are among them. The scattered are the people in the surveys: the student who moved for college and never found another church, and the woman whose questions met a wall until she stopped bringing them. We can argue about the culture for another thirty years. Ezekiel would ask simpler questions first. Who noticed when they stopped coming? Who went looking? When did you last call someone who used to sit near you on Sunday?

The chapter doesn't end with the shepherds' failure. "For thus says the Lord GOD: Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out" (Ezekiel 34:11). God promised to do Himself what His shepherds had not done, and He kept that promise in His Son. "I am the good shepherd," Jesus said. "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). And He asked which of His hearers, having a hundred sheep and losing one, would not "leave the ninety-nine in the open country, and go after the one that is lost, until he finds it" (Luke 15:4).

Someone you love is probably in these numbers, and you may not know why they left.

The Good Shepherd laid down His life for sheep who wandered off and for sheep who pushed others out, and most of us have been both.

He is still searching for His lost sheep, and He means for us to go looking with Him.
