# The Bible door: what it holds, and the plan to make it a one-stop place

Written for James on 2026-11-18. Every number below was counted by a script
run on the files the site serves (`client/public/bible/`), not estimated. A
"word" is a word a reader would see.

## What you asked for

"I want this Bible drop down to be a one-stop place where people can
understand the Bible." Exhaustive, like a scholar teaching at length, but in
words an ordinary person can read, and illustrated so it is easy to see.

## The short answer

The Bible door is not short of words. It is short of **plain** words and of
**pictures**. The notes already hold about 3 million words. But most of it is
written like a seminary handout: the average sentence is 24 to 28 words long,
and it reads at about the level of a college freshman (grade 11 to 12). A
reader who is new, or who left church at 16, will not get through it. And
almost none of it is drawn: no maps, no timelines, no charts of who is who.

So "exhaustive but easy" here means two jobs, done book by book:

1. **A plain teaching layer on top.** Short sentences. One idea per paragraph.
   Hard words explained the first time. Passage by passage, so a reader can
   follow the chapter in order. The deep notes stay underneath for the reader
   who wants them.
2. **Pictures that teach.** An outline you can tap, a timeline, a map, a chart
   of the people. Drawn in the site's engraved hand, loaded only when a reader
   scrolls to them, so the pages stay fast.

## What each room holds today

### 1. Read the Study Bible (66 books, 1,189 chapters)

**Book pages.** All 66 books have an introduction: 151,259 words in all, the
median book 2,157 words (shortest 3 John, 1,318; longest Luke, 4,675). Each
has the same parts: a one-line tagline, what the book is (median 286 words),
who wrote it (216), when (156), the world it was written in (215), why (126),
where it sits in the story (124), what it shows of Christ (167), how to read
it (183), an outline (189), themes (322), chapters to start with, and
doctrines. Only the four Gospels and Genesis also have the text's history,
how the book has been read in the church, and further reading.

The book page already draws one picture: the outline as a band you can tap
(built in November). It does **not** have: a map, a timeline, a chart of the
people, a list of hard passages, a list of key words, or a contents list.

**Chapter notes.** Every one of the 1,189 chapters has a note: 1.9 million
words, the median chapter 1,228 words. Each has a summary (median 80 words),
where it sits in the story (118), the history (194), the culture (192), how it
is built (138), what it teaches (123), key Hebrew or Greek words (183), how it
points to Christ (140), study questions (77) and, in 1,033 chapters, a hard
question with an answer (191). 156 chapters have no hard question.

The four Gospels are far deeper than the rest: their chapters run 5,000 to
5,500 words each and add how the chapter has been read, the text and
translations, and help for teaching. Outside the Gospels, 1,057 chapters are
under 1,500 words.

**Verse-by-verse notes.** 4,311 notes on 202 chapters in 25 books, 1.1
million words. All of Genesis and the four Gospels; a few chapters of 20
other books; nothing at all in 41 books (Acts has one chapter).

**What is missing, for a serious reader.** For a book: a plain-language
introduction a new reader can finish; a map of where it happens; a timeline;
a chart of the people; the hard passages gathered in one place; the key words
in one place; how the church has read it (61 books lack this). For a
chapter: the chapter taken passage by passage, in order, in plain words (the
notes are arranged by subject, so a reader has to hunt across six drawers to
learn what verses 9 to 11 mean); what the first hearers would have caught;
the Old Testament behind a New Testament passage, said in the same place; the
hard verse faced where it sits.

### 2. Search the Bible

Works: any word or phrase across the Berean Standard Bible, in the browser.
Nothing to write. Later, one picture: a strip of the 66 books showing where a
word falls.

### 3. Guides to reading the Bible (24 guides) and How to study the Bible

24 guides, 101,348 words, each about 4,100 words in seven to eleven parts:
six for starting out (what the Bible is, how the books were chosen, how we got
each testament, translations, chapters and verses), ten on how to read each
kind of writing, and eight on the Bible's world. They read at grade 12 to 13.
None has a picture. How to study the Bible (`/study/how-to-study`) is seven
steps, about 500 words, with a guided walk on any chapter. It is clear and
short and needs little.

### 4. The Bible's story (11 parts)

Eleven parts from creation to new creation. Each part has dates, a note on
its world (133 to 349 words), what to watch for (49 to 126 words), its people
(3 to 23 names, listed) and its chapters to read, which walk all 1,189
chapters once. The story's own framing is 3,207 words. It is the best
doorway for a beginner, and the thinnest room. No pictures.

### 5. Bible background (41 guides)

Being expanded by another builder this week. Not counted or planned here.

### 6. Reading plans

`/study/plans` shows twelve plans as drawn cards (a year, the New Testament
in 90 days, the Psalms in 30 days, Proverbs in 31, the story at your own
pace, four for families, and printable charts). Sound. Later: a plan to read
one book with its new teaching notes ("Mark in 16 days").

## What "exhaustive but easy" means, room by room

**A book page** becomes a teaching introduction of 3,500 to 5,000 words in
headed parts with a contents list at the top: who wrote it and how we know;
when, where and for whom (each view stated fairly); what kind of writing it
is and how to read it; its shape (the tap-able outline); its themes; how it
uses or points to the rest of the Bible and to Christ; what only this book
has; the hard passages, one line each with a link; key words; a timeline; a
map; how the church has read it; then "Go deeper". Every date and ancient
source is checked and listed in a ledger.

**A chapter** gets a "passage by passage" layer, read first: for each passage
(the outline the notes already have), what is happening; what a first hearer
would catch that we miss; the Old Testament behind it; the hard verse faced;
what it asks. About 250 to 400 words a passage, 2,000 to 3,500 a chapter. It
sits on top of the deep notes, which stay.

**A guide** gets a plain summary of each part (two or three sentences) and
one picture per guide (a family tree of the Herods, a chart of the empires, a
calendar wheel of the feasts, a diagram of the canon).

**The story** gets one drawn line of the whole Bible with the eleven parts on
it, a map for each part, and its people as a chart, not a list.

## The order

1,189 chapters cannot all be deepened at once. At 2,500 plain words a
chapter that would be about 3 million new words. So the order follows where
readers start and where the site already has the deepest notes to draw on:

1. **Mark** (the pilot, now): the book page and chapters 1 to 3.
2. **The rest of Mark** (13 chapters).
3. **John**, then **Luke**, then **Matthew** (the Gospels have the deepest
   notes already, and the most readers).
4. **Genesis** (the beginning; fully verse-noted already).
5. **Psalms** (the prayer book; 150 short chapters, about 600 to 1,000
   plain words each).
6. **Romans**, then **Acts** (Acts needs verse notes first).
7. The book pages of all 66 (the introduction and pictures only), before the
   chapters of the rest, so every book has a front door.
8. Then the remaining books in the order the reading plans use them.

## How much writing each step is

| Step | Pieces | New plain words | Built from data already here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark book page | 1 | about 4,500 | the outline band, chapter links, further reading |
| Mark 1 to 3 (pilot) | 3 chapters, 29 passages | about 9,000 | the outline, the deep notes as source |
| Rest of Mark | 13 chapters | about 35,000 | the same |
| Each other Gospel | 1 page + 21 to 28 chapters | 60,000 to 80,000 | the same |
| Genesis | 1 page + 50 chapters | about 120,000 | the same |
| Psalms | 1 page + 150 psalms | about 120,000 | the same |
| 61 more book pages | 61 | about 250,000 | the existing introductions |
| Guide summaries and pictures | 24 | about 15,000 | the guides |
| The story's pictures | 11 maps, 1 line, 11 charts | about 3,000 | story.json |

What can be built from data already in the repository, with no new writing:
the outline diagram (done), the chapter tiles, the hard-question list (each
chapter's existing hard question), the key-word lists (each chapter's Greek
or Hebrew words and the dictionary), the cross-references, "Go deeper", the
story's line of eleven parts. What must be newly written and checked: every
plain teaching part, every map's places and every timeline's dates (each
date and place checked against a named source).

## The pictures

All drawn in the site's one engraved hand (ink lines, hatching for shade, one
flat tone, one mustard mark), loaded only when a reader scrolls near, with
their space held so nothing moves.

- **Outline diagrams.** The book's parts as a band you can tap (done for all
  66).
- **Timelines.** A drawn line of dates for each book: the events it tells
  and when it was written, with each date's source.
- **Maps.** Simple drawn maps: coast, rivers, lakes, the places the book
  names, each one tap-able to the verses where it appears. Mark's first.
- **Charts of who is who.** The Herods, the kings of Israel and Judah, the
  patriarchs' families, the Twelve.
- **The story line.** The whole Bible as one drawn road with eleven stops.

## What needs your word

1. The order of books (above), or another.
2. How long a book introduction and a chapter's plain layer should be. The
   pilot uses about 4,500 words for Mark's book page and about 3,000 for each
   chapter.
3. Whether the site states a view on a contested matter (who wrote a book,
   when, the order of the Gospels, the ending of Mark). The pilot states each
   view fairly, says which most scholars hold and why, and takes no side.
