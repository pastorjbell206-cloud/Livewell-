# Why is there something rather than nothing? Couldn't the universe simply exist without God, or come from nothing by the laws of physics?

## The short answer

The cosmological arguments start from a fact no one disputes, that there is a world at all, and ask what explains it. Gottfried Leibniz argued in 1714 that whatever exists without having to exist needs a reason outside itself, and that the universe is that kind of thing; the kalam argument adds that the universe began. Both meet serious objections, from David Hume, Immanuel Kant and Bertrand Russell, and from physicists who describe how a universe might arise under quantum laws. Those objections show that neither argument compels assent. They don't show that the question is empty or that physics has answered it. If the contingency argument is sound, it shows that there is a necessary being on which everything else depends. That is a large part of what Christians mean by God, and not yet the whole of it.

## The objection at its strongest

The case against starts with David Hume. In his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, published in 1779 after his death, one speaker sets out the argument from a necessarily existent Being and another, Cleanthes, takes it apart. His first point concerns necessity itself: "Whatever we conceive as existent, we can also conceive as non-existent." If that holds of everything, then nothing exists by necessity, and "The words, therefore, necessary existence, have no meaning." His second point turns the argument around: "why may not the material universe be the necessarily existent Being"? If some unknown quality made God's non-existence impossible, the same unknown quality might belong to matter. His third concerns wholes. Each link in a chain of causes is explained by the link before it, and to ask for the cause of the whole chain is like being shown the cause of each of twenty particles and then asking for the cause of the twenty: "This is sufficiently explained in explaining the cause of the parts."

Bertrand Russell pressed the same point in his radio debate with the Jesuit Frederick Copleston on the BBC in 1948. Asked why the universe exists, he answered, "I should say that the universe is just there, and that's all." The demand for a cause, on his view, makes sense of things inside the world, not of the world itself, and to conclude that the whole needs a cause because each part does is to commit the fallacy of composition, the kind of mistake one makes in arguing that a wall must be small because each of its bricks is.

Immanuel Kant added a charge of hidden borrowing. In the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) he argued that an argument from experience can reach at most the idea of something unconditioned, and that to identify that something with a supremely real and necessary being it has to lean on the ontological argument's move from a concept to existence: "Thus it is properly the ontological argument which figures in the cosmological, and constitutes the whole strength of the latter" (in J. M. D. Meiklejohn's translation).

Physics supplies the newest form of the objection. At the quantum scale some events seem to happen without any sufficient cause. Stephen Hawking described in *A Brief History of Time* (1988) an early universe with no boundary and no first instant, and Lawrence Krauss in *A Universe from Nothing* (2012) argued that physical law can account for a universe coming to be without a creator. At full strength the objection says that the argument rests on a principle no one can prove, treats the universe as a thing when it is only the sum of things, and asks a question physics is learning to answer without God.

## What is true in it

Much of it. The principle of sufficient reason, the claim that whatever exists has an explanation, can't be proved from anything more certain than itself; its defenders argue only that every inquiry assumes it. In its strongest form it meets a sharp objection that Peter van Inwagen pressed in 1983: if every contingent truth had to be explained by something that entails it, the explanation of all of them would be either contingent, and so part of what needed explaining, or necessary, and then everything it entailed would be necessary too. Defenders such as Alexander Pruss answer that a free choice explains what it brings about without entailing it, but the objection shows that the principle has to be stated with care.

The kalam's premises are open too. Whether a past without a beginning is possible is argued by philosophers of mathematics and of time, the argument fits most naturally a view of time that is itself disputed, and the physics of the first instant is unsettled. And Kant was right that an argument which ends at "a necessary being" has not yet reached the God of Abraham. The step from one to the other needs further argument, and critics such as Graham Oppy have doubted that it can be made.

Christians have sometimes stated the argument badly, as though it claimed that everything has a cause. If that were the argument, Russell's reply, that God would then need a cause as well, would end it, and this area has a page of its own on that question.

## The evidence

Leibniz set out the contingency argument in *The Principles of Nature and of Grace* (1714). Once it is granted that "nothing takes place without sufficient reason," he wrote, the first question we are entitled to put is this: "Why does something exist rather than nothing? For 'nothing' is simpler and easier than 'something.'" The reason can't be found in "the sequence of contingent things," since each link points to another and "the same question always remains." It must lie in "a necessary being, bearing in itself the reason of its own existence" (sections 7 and 8, in Robert Latta's translation of 1898). Thomas Aquinas had reached the same place by another road. Things that come to be and pass away exist only by receiving existence, and so, in his third way, "we cannot but postulate the existence of some being having of itself its own necessity, and not receiving it from another" (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 2, a. 3, in the English Dominican translation). Aquinas held that reason alone can't show that the world began in time. His argument doesn't need a beginning.

Hume's three points have answers. That we can imagine something not existing shows only that we can imagine it; a person who misunderstands a theorem can imagine it false, which doesn't make it possibly false. On the twenty particles, William Rowe answered that explaining each dependent thing by another dependent thing doesn't explain why there are dependent things at all, and Alexander Pruss has illustrated the point with an endless line of hens and eggs, which accounts for each hen by an egg and each egg by a hen and leaves untouched why there is such a line rather than none. On composition, the fallacy is real, but not every inference from parts to whole commits it: a wall made entirely of brick is a brick wall. If every constituent of the universe, its matter, energy and fields, might not have existed, it is hard to see what would make their sum incapable of not existing. On Kant, defenders reply that the necessity in view is not the necessity of a definition but that of a being which can neither begin nor cease and depends on nothing, and an argument to such a being doesn't borrow the ontological argument's step.

The kalam argument, revived by William Lane Craig in *The Kalam Cosmological Argument* (1979), runs in three lines: (1) whatever begins to exist has a cause; (2) the universe began to exist; (3) so the universe has a cause. In 2003 the physicists Arvind Borde, Alan Guth and Alexander Vilenkin showed that a universe which has, on average, been expanding can't be extended into an infinite past, though physicists still argue over whether quantum effects offer a way around their result. Against the first premise, critics point to quantum events. Craig answers that such events happen within conditions, a field obeying laws, while a beginning of everything would have no conditions at all, and that some interpretations of quantum mechanics are deterministic. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on the argument judges that the dispute turns on questions about quantum indeterminacy that are not settled, and that is a fair verdict on both sides.

The "universe from nothing" proposals need the same care. The nothing in them is a state described by quantum laws, as the philosopher David Albert objected in reviewing Krauss's book in the *New York Times* in March 2012. If they succeed, they bear on the kalam's second premise, not on the contingency argument: a universe with no first moment, or one arising from a quantum state, is still a universe that might not have been.

## The argument, laid out

This is a deductive argument: if its premises are true, its conclusion follows, and the question is whether each premise is more believable than its denial.

1. Every contingent thing, anything that exists but might not have existed, has an explanation of its existence in something other than itself.
2. The universe, all of space, time, matter and energy, together with the physical laws and states from which they arise, is contingent: it might have been otherwise, or not been at all.
3. The explanation of the universe can't lie in anything contingent, because every contingent thing is part of what is being explained.

So: the universe is explained by something that exists necessarily and is not itself made of space, time or matter.

The weight sits on the first step. Russell denies it as applied to the universe, and Hume's twenty particles deny that a whole needs anything beyond its parts; the quantum objection presses on it too, though it concerns events within the world rather than the existence of the world. Hume's question "why may not the material universe be the necessarily existent Being" denies the second step. Kant's objection concerns what the conclusion means rather than whether it follows. A reader who holds that some contingent facts have no explanation at all will reject the first step, and that is where an honest person can get off.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is parity. Every account of reality stops somewhere. The theist stops at a necessary being; the atheist may stop at the universe or its most basic laws, and is no more irrational for doing so. And the theist's stopping place has troubles of its own. If God's necessity is logical, Hume and Kant deny there is such a thing. If it is only the necessity of a being that never began and can't end, J. L. Mackie argued in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982) that God is then logically contingent, and the demand for a reason applies to him as much as to the world.

The answer is that the demand for a reason applies to what might not have been. The difference between the two stopping places is not that one of them is called God. Everything about the universe that can be studied, its particular laws, the values of its constants, the states from which it developed, bears the marks of something that might have been otherwise, which is why the fine-tuning of those values is a question at all. A being whose existence is not received from anything, what the Study Bible's note on Exodus 3 calls aseity, God not "caused, completed, or sustained by anything outside himself," would not raise the question in the same way. Whether there can be such a being is the real crux, and philosophers divide over it. The argument doesn't define God into existence. It asks the reader to compare two final facts and judge which can bear the weight.

If the argument is sound, what it shows is a necessary being that explains the universe. Leibniz went on to argue that this being must have "power, knowledge and will in perfection" (section 9). It does not show that this being is the Father of Jesus Christ, that God is three persons, or that he has spoken through the prophets. Christians confess those things on other grounds, above all the testimony that God raised Jesus from the dead, which is a historical claim to be weighed as history, and the library's area on the historical Jesus and the resurrection takes it up. Paul in Athens moved in that order, from "The God who made the world and everything in it" (Acts 17:24, BSB) to the claim that God "has given proof of this to everyone by raising Him from the dead" (Acts 17:31, BSB).

## What remains unresolved

Whether the principle of sufficient reason is true, and in what form, is unresolved, and it may be the kind of principle that can be trusted but never proved. Whether the past can be infinite, and what physics will say about the first instant, are open. Whether the idea of a necessary being is coherent is one of the oldest disputes in philosophy. And how far an argument from the world's existence can go toward the God of the creeds, without help from other arguments or from history, is still argued among those who accept it.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much these arguments can carry. Some, following Aquinas, hold that reason can show that God exists. Others, among them philosophers in the Reformed tradition such as Alvin Plantinga in *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000), hold that belief in God can be rational without resting on such arguments at all. Some prefer the kalam, others the argument from contingency, which needs no beginning. None of this requires a view on the age of the universe, and the science area's pages take none. What all confess is the first article of the Nicene Creed: "We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible." Scripture puts the dependence of all things in a single line: "He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:17, BSB). And it says where such understanding comes from: "By faith we understand that the universe was formed at God's command" (Hebrews 11:3, BSB).
