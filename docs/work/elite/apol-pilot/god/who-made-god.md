# If everything needs a cause, who made God? And isn't it the believer's job to prove that God exists, not the unbeliever's job to disprove him?

## The short answer

"Who made God?" is a fair question, and it would end the matter if the argument for God claimed that everything needs a cause. The serious arguments don't claim that. They claim that whatever begins to exist, or whatever might not have existed, needs an explanation outside itself, and God, as the creeds confess him, is neither: he is not one more thing in the world but the one on whom everything else depends. Whether there is such a being is the real question, and an unbeliever can reasonably answer no. As for the burden of proof, it falls on anyone who makes a claim, the believer who says God exists and the unbeliever who says the universe needs no explanation. Russell's teapot rightly warns against believing what nothing supports. God is proposed as the explanation of things everyone can see, which is why the question has to be weighed rather than waved away.

## The objection at its strongest

Bertrand Russell told in a lecture in London in 1927, published as *Why I Am Not a Christian*, how he gave up the argument from a first cause. For years he had accepted the argument from a first cause, until at eighteen he read John Stuart Mill's *Autobiography* and found this sentence: "My father taught me that the question, 'Who made me?' cannot be answered, since it immediately suggests the further question, 'Who made God?'" Russell drew the conclusion plainly: "If everything must have a cause, then God must have a cause. If there can be anything without a cause, it may just as well be the world as God." Stephen Hawking asked the same question in *A Brief History of Time* (1988): who created God?

Richard Dawkins gave it a sharper edge in *The God Delusion* (2006). He called his argument the Ultimate Boeing 747 gambit, after the image of a hurricane sweeping through a scrapyard and assembling an airliner by chance, an image Fred Hoyle had used for another purpose. A God able to design a universe, Dawkins argued, would have to be supremely complex, and so at least as improbable as the universe he is invoked to explain. Far from ending the regress of explanations, the hypothesis of God begins a worse one.

The second half of the objection concerns the burden of proof. In an essay of 1952, "Is There a God?", first published in his collected papers long after his death, Russell imagined claiming that a china teapot revolves about the sun between the earth and Mars, too small to be seen even by the most powerful telescopes. No one could disprove it, and no one would be obliged to believe it. The philosopher Antony Flew argued in "The Presumption of Atheism" (1972), as the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on atheism sets out his case, that the reasonable starting point is to hold no belief about God either way, and that the onus of proof lies on whoever affirms. At full strength the objection says that God either needs a cause, and explains nothing, or doesn't, and then the universe may not need one either; and that the believer carries the whole burden of proof and has not met it.

## What is true in it

A good deal. Christians have sometimes put the first-cause argument in the very form Russell attacked, as if it began from the claim that everything has a cause. Stated that way, it refutes itself, and Mill's father was right to see it.

The burden point is right in part as well. Anyone who claims that God exists owes reasons, and "you can't prove he doesn't" is a poor reply. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on atheism makes the fair point that we reasonably disbelieve many things we can't disprove, from Santa Claus to a teapot beyond the sun, and that a demand for absolute disproof would leave skepticism in charge of almost every question. Agnosticism and unbelief are positions thoughtful people hold for reasons, not failures of nerve.

And Dawkins is right about one picture of God. A designer imagined as a very complex being inside the universe, assembled from parts, would need explaining as much as anything he made. Where an argument for God speaks of him as that kind of engineer, the gambit succeeds against it.

## The evidence

Look first at what the serious arguments actually say. The kalam argument's first premise, in William Lane Craig's form, is that whatever begins to exist has a cause; it says nothing about what never began. Leibniz's argument concerns contingent things, things that might not have existed, and it ends at "a necessary being, bearing in itself the reason of its own existence" (*The Principles of Nature and of Grace*, 1714, section 8, in Robert Latta's translation). Thomas Aquinas's third way ends at "some being having of itself its own necessity, and not receiving it from another." None of them says that everything has a cause. Each says that a certain kind of thing does, and argues that the universe is that kind of thing.

That is also how Scripture speaks of God. "Before the mountains were born or You brought forth the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting You are God" (Psalm 90:2, BSB). When Moses asked God's name, the answer was "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14, BSB), and the Study Bible's note on the chapter says that Christian theology drew from that verse the doctrine of God's self-existence: God is not caused, completed or sustained by anything outside himself. The creeds say the same of each person of the Trinity. The Athanasian Creed confesses "The Father uncreate, the Son uncreate, and the Holy Ghost uncreate," and that there are not "three uncreated, but one uncreated."

Dawkins's argument meets an old Christian teaching head on. Aquinas held that God is "altogether simple," without parts, and gave as one reason that "every composite has a cause, for things in themselves different cannot unite unless something causes them to unite. But God is uncaused" (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 3, a. 7, in the English Dominican translation). Complexity of the kind Dawkins means, many parts arranged against the odds, is exactly what classical theism denies of God. The Internet Encyclopedia's article on the New Atheists notes that Dawkins's assumption flies in the face of the long-standing theological view of God as self-existent, and that he owes his reader an account of the kind of complexity he attributes to God; it reports Craig's reply that what God knows may be vast while his mind itself is simple.

On the burden of proof, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on atheism and agnosticism observes that discussion of the default position is often sidetracked by analogies such as the teapot, and that the better question is how probable theism is before the evidence is weighed. Some atheist philosophers argue that it is very improbable, so that absent or ambiguous evidence would justify disbelief. That is a real argument, and it is answered on its own ground, by asking whether a single, necessary, perfect being is a complicated hypothesis or as simple a one as there could be.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the objection hits, not a proof that God exists.

1. "Who made God?" is decisive only against an argument that assumes everything has a cause.
2. The serious arguments assume something narrower, that whatever begins to exist, or whatever might not have existed, has a cause or explanation outside itself, and this restriction marks a real difference, not a special exemption.
3. God, as Christians confess him, did not begin to exist, could not have failed to exist, and has no parts whose union would need a cause.

So: "Who made God?" is a fair question aimed at the wrong target. What it leaves standing is the question whether such a being exists, which has to be argued, and the burden of arguing falls on both sides.

The weight sits on the second step. Russell's reply, "it may just as well be the world as God," denies that the restriction marks any real difference. Dawkins denies the third step, holding that a designing mind must be complex. Mill and Hawking, as Russell reports and repeats them, assume the argument the first step sets aside. A reader who thinks the line between contingent things and a necessary being is drawn only to protect God will reject the second step, and that is the honest place to disagree.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is Russell's parity. You have granted that something exists without a cause outside itself. Then why not the universe, or its most basic laws? They are at least something we know exists. Positing a further being, one we can't observe, to be the uncaused thing is one more step than the evidence requires.

The reply is serious, and it changes the question in the right way. It grants that every account ends with something that has no cause outside itself, so the issue is no longer who made God but which candidate can bear that weight. The universe as physics studies it shows the marks of something that might have been otherwise: particular laws, particular values for its constants, particular states from which it developed. A being with no parts, no beginning and no received existence is a different kind of candidate. Hume suggested that the material world might be necessary in some way we don't understand, and observation can't rule that out; neither can it rule out the necessary being the theist proposes. The choice between them is made by argument, the arguments on this area's other pages among them, not by a question that each side can put to the other.

This is also why the teapot is a poor model. The teapot explains nothing. No one has any reason to expect it, and nothing would be different if it were absent. God is proposed as the explanation of things everyone already sees: that there is a world, that it is ordered and knowable, that some things are really wrong, and, Christians add, the testimony of witnesses that Jesus was raised from the dead in Jerusalem in the first century. A hypothesis offered to explain public facts can be tested against them. That is a burden, and the believer carries it. The unbeliever who says the universe needs no explanation carries one too.

## What remains unresolved

Whether the idea of a necessary being is coherent is unresolved, and with it whether Hume's possibility, a necessary universe, is a real alternative. Whether God's simplicity can be held together with his knowledge, will and love, as classical theism holds, is argued among Christians as well as by their critics. How to judge the prior probability of theism, or of naturalism, before the evidence is weighed is an open question in the philosophy of religion, and neither side has a method the other must accept.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to state God's simplicity. Many in the tradition of Augustine, Anselm and Aquinas hold it in the strong form, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's article on divine simplicity describes it. Some Christian philosophers think the strong form hard to reconcile with God's being a person with distinct attributes, as Alvin Plantinga argued in *Does God Have a Nature?* (1980), and they answer Dawkins in other ways, by distinguishing the simplicity of a mind from the richness of what it knows. They differ too on how much weight the arguments for God should bear and on where the burden of proof properly lies. What all confess is that God is uncreated and eternal, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and that everything else exists because he wills it: "You created all things; by Your will they exist and came to be" (Revelation 4:11, BSB).
