# Can we trust the text of the New Testament?

The New Testament was written in Greek in the first century and copied by hand for nearly fifteen hundred years before it was printed. None of the original pages survives. The copies we have number in the thousands, most of them medieval, the earliest of them fragments, and they differ from one another in hundreds of thousands of small ways. Those are the facts, and they are not in dispute between believers and skeptics. The question is what they add up to.

The pages in this part of the library take the hardest forms of that question one at a time. Each states the objection the way its strongest proponent would, most often Bart Ehrman, whose books have done more than anyone's to bring these questions to ordinary readers. Each says plainly what is true in it, sets out the evidence with names and dates a reader can check, lays out the argument in steps, answers the best reply, and says what is still unresolved and where Christians themselves disagree. Each ends with books on both sides.

The case these pages make is a modest one, and it is meant to be. They don't claim that we have every word the apostles wrote, or that no copyist ever changed anything on purpose. They argue that the New Testament's text is known with high confidence in nearly every place, that the places where it is uncertain are known and marked, and that none of the faith the church confesses rests on a reading in doubt. Whether what the text says is true is a different question, and the site's other pages take it up.

## The questions

1. **There are more differences among the manuscripts than words in the New Testament. So how can anyone trust it?** The number of variants, what kinds they are, and how many matter.
2. **We don't have the originals, only copies of copies. Isn't it a telephone game?** How copying actually worked, the earliest papyri and the debates over their dates, and what the telephone picture gets right.
3. **Two famous passages were not in the earliest Bibles: the ending of Mark and the woman caught in adultery. What else was added?** The passages one by one, and the hard question they leave.
4. **Did scribes change the text to make Jesus look more divine?** The "orthodox corruption" case at full strength, where it is right, and what the earliest letters show.
5. **Christians say the New Testament is better preserved than Homer or Caesar. Is that true, and what does it prove?** The famous comparison with corrected figures, and the one question it answers.
6. **Who decides what the Greek New Testament says, and was there ever a single original to find?** How a critical edition is made, step by step, and the serious question about the word "original".

## Words these pages use

- **Autograph**: the original document an author wrote or dictated. None survives for any book of the Bible.
- **Manuscript**: a handwritten copy. New Testament manuscripts are grouped as papyri (the earliest, mostly fragments), majuscules (written in capital letters, such as Codex Sinaiticus), minuscules (in a smaller running hand, mostly from the ninth century on) and lectionaries (copies arranged for reading in church).
- **Codex**: a book with pages, as opposed to a scroll. Early Christians used the codex for their scriptures from very early on.
- **Variant**: any place where manuscripts differ, from a misspelling to a missing passage.
- **Textual criticism**: the discipline of comparing manuscripts to work out the earliest recoverable wording.
- **Versions**: early translations, such as Latin, Syriac and Coptic, which are witnesses to the Greek they were made from.
- **The church fathers**: early Christian writers whose quotations of the New Testament show what their copies said.
- **Textus Receptus**: the "received text" of the printed Greek New Testament from Erasmus (1516) onward, behind the King James Version.
