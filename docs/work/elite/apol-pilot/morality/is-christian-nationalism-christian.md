# Every nation's laws rest on somebody's morals. Why shouldn't a Christian people want a Christian nation? Is Christian nationalism Christian?

## The short answer

Loving your country is not the problem. Scripture assumes it, and tells believers to pray for their rulers and seek the good of the place where they live. Christian nationalism is something else: the claim that a nation should be Christian as a nation, in its laws, its customs and its rulers' duties. Its defenders make a serious case, and part of it is true. But the New Testament gives no nation that standing. It gives the title "holy nation" to the church, gathered out of every people. Jesus refused a crown, refused the sword, and said his kingdom is not of this world. And when the church has held the ruler's power, it has used it on Jews, on unbelievers and on other Christians. This site doesn't teach Christian nationalism as true. It says so with grief, and it applies the same test to any cause, on any side, that drafts the cross.

## The objection at its strongest

The case for a Christian nation deserves to be heard from people who make it, not from their critics. Stephen Wolfe's *The Case for Christian Nationalism* (2022) defines it, in a sentence two writers on the book quote in the same words, as the whole of a nation's common action, its civil laws and its social customs, carried out by a Christian nation acting as a Christian nation, to gain for itself both earthly and heavenly good in Christ. As one of them reports his argument, nations belong to God's design from creation, and the gospel completes a people's common life instead of setting it aside. A nation can't bring anyone to faith; what it can do is see that the preaching of the word and the sacraments are open to all and that its people are encouraged to receive them. That is not a call for mobs. It's a claim about what a people is for.

The argument has four parts. First, there is no neutral ground. Every code of law says that some things are good and some are wrong, and it has to get that from somewhere. If a nation's law doesn't rest on the Christian account of right and wrong, it will rest on another, and calling the other one "secular" doesn't make it neutral. Second, the Bible speaks to rulers as rulers: "Therefore be wise, O kings; be admonished, O judges of the earth. Serve the LORD with fear" (Psalm 2:10-11, BSB). Paul calls the ruler "God's servant for your good" (Romans 13:4, BSB), and Jesus sends his church to "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19, BSB).

Third, this was the settled view of most Christians for most of their history, and of the Reformers. The Westminster Confession of 1646 says of the civil ruler that "he hath authority, and it is his duty to take order, that unity and peace be preserved in the Church, that the truth of God be kept pure and entire, that all blasphemies and heresies be suppressed." Fourth, Christendom did real good. In 390, as this site's essay on empire tells it, the bishop Ambrose barred the emperor Theodosius from the Lord's table until he did public penance for a massacre. A ruler who knows he answers to God can be called to account. At full strength the case says that a people who are mostly Christian may order their common life by their faith, and that refusing to do so is not humility. It's surrender to someone else's creed.

## What is true in it

More than its critics usually admit. There is no neutral ground. Laws against theft and murder are moral judgments, and Christians who bring their convictions into public argument are doing what every citizen does. The men and women who fought slavery argued from the Bible in public, and they were right to.

Love of country is good. God told the exiles in Babylon, "Seek the prosperity of the city to which I have sent you as exiles. Pray to the LORD on its behalf" (Jeremiah 29:7, BSB). Paul could wish himself cut off for the sake of his own people (Romans 9:3). Nothing on this page asks a Christian to love his country less.

And the label is often used unfairly. It gets thrown at any believer who lets his faith shape his public choices or prays for his town. Many of the people drawn to the idea are grieving real losses, in their churches, their towns and their children's world, and that grief is not a sin. This page is about an idea, and it argues against the idea, not against them.

## The evidence

Start with Scripture, and with the one nation God did call his own. Israel's covenant didn't flatter it: "Only you have I known from all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2, BSB). In the New Testament that language is not handed on to Rome or to any later country. It is given to the church: "you are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation" (1 Peter 2:9, BSB). Paul tells Christians in a Roman colony that "our citizenship is in heaven" (Philippians 3:20, BSB).

Then look at what Jesus did with power. When the devil offered him "all the kingdoms of the world," he refused them (Luke 4:5-8). When a crowd meant "to come and make Him king by force," he "withdrew again to a mountain by Himself" (John 6:15, BSB). When Peter drew a sword to defend him, he said, "Put your sword back in its place" (Matthew 26:52, BSB). And before the Roman governor he said, "My kingdom is not of this world; if it were, My servants would fight" (John 18:36, BSB). He drew a line between two kinds of claim: "Give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's" (Matthew 22:21, BSB). The apostles kept both halves. They told believers to submit to rulers, and when rulers ordered them to stop preaching they answered, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29, BSB).

For three centuries the church had no power at all, and it argued for freedom. Tertullian wrote to a Roman official that "It is assuredly no part of religion to compel religion." Lactantius wrote that "religion is to be defended, not by putting to death, but by dying." Then the church's position changed. In 313 the Edict of Milan gave Christians toleration, and in 380 the emperor Theodosius made Nicene Christianity the official religion of the empire, as this site's essay on empire tells it. Within a generation the state's force was turned on other Christians. Imperial law suppressed the Donatist church in North Africa from 405, and Augustine, who had once opposed coercion, came to defend it with Jesus' own words, "compel them to come in."

The pattern held for a thousand years and survived the Reformation. Protestant Zurich drowned Felix Manz in 1527 for baptizing adults. The Peace of Augsburg settled religion in the German lands by each ruler's right to choose it for his people. The French wars of religion ran from 1562 to 1598, and the Thirty Years' War from 1618 to 1648. These were Christian nations, on any definition, and both sides claimed the cross.

Christians were also the ones who said no. Balthasar Hubmaier wrote against the burning of heretics in 1524. Roger Williams argued in *The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution* (1644) that rulers must leave dissenters and unbelievers alone until God's own harvest. In colonial New England, as this site's essay on the founding tells it, Baptists were taxed to support other men's churches and jailed for preaching without a license, and the Baptist leader Isaac Backus spent his life opposing the established church on the conviction that a faith the magistrate can compel is a faith already dead. They didn't want less Christianity in the country. They wanted it free. In 1965 the Catholic Church taught the same thing: that no one may be forced to believe.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument from Scripture first and from history second.

1. The New Testament gives the titles of God's covenant people to the church, gathered from every nation, and to no nation or state.
2. Jesus refused to take or defend his kingdom with the ruler's sword, and the apostles asked rulers for justice and peace, not for the enforcement of the faith.
3. Where the church has used the ruler's power to make or keep a nation Christian, the result, again and again, has been forced profession, the persecution of Jews, unbelievers and other Christians, and a faith measured by loyalty.

So: Christian nationalism, the joining of the faith to a nation's identity and its ruler's power, is not something the New Testament teaches, and it asks of the ruler's sword what Jesus refused to ask of it; Christians can love their country and argue for just laws without it.

The weight sits on the first step. If some nation after Christ can stand where Israel stood, the rest of the argument weakens. The defenders' best reply, though, goes after the second and third.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply says this page has given the game away. It granted that no law is neutral. Then somebody's morality will rule, and a Christian should want it to be the true one. As for history, abuse doesn't cancel use. Bad marriages don't refute marriage, and bad Christian kings don't refute Christian government. And the dissenters' freedom has had a price: a public life where faith is a private hobby and a country that is forgetting God.

The first point should be granted in full, and it doesn't lead where the reply wants. There is a difference between two things. One is Christians arguing, as citizens among other citizens, for laws that do justice to every neighbor, and winning or losing by persuasion. The other is the claim that the nation as a nation is Christian, and that the state should establish the faith or rank its citizens by it. The first is ordinary Christian faithfulness in public. The second is the thing this page argues against. The line can be hard to see in a given case. It isn't hard to see in the Westminster divines' sentence about suppressing heresies.

On abuse and use, the trouble is how regular the abuse was. Catholic and Protestant, emperor and city council, the fourth century and the seventeenth: give the church the sword to guard the faith, and it has used it on the conscience. That looks less like an accident than like a tool being used for a job it can't do. Tertullian had already said why. Force can produce attendance. It can't produce faith.

The third point names a real loss, and Christians should grieve it. But the New Testament's answer to a forgetful country is a church that is holy and tells the truth, not one that holds the levers. And the same test cuts in every direction. In the words of this site's page on Christian nationalism and politics, "On the right it can fuse faith with the nation; on the left it can make a platform the measure of faithfulness just as easily." A church that measures faithfulness by a progressive program has made the same trade as one that measures it by the nation. Whenever the cross is drafted to serve a flag or a cause, Christ has been made a means to something else. We have done that in more than one direction, and none of us is clean of it.

## What remains unresolved

How far Christian convictions should shape the law of a country where many don't share them is not settled by anything said here, and faithful Christians draw that line in different places. Whether a mild established church can be kept without the old harms is still argued. And what Christians owe a nation that is turning from its Christian past is a question each generation has to answer for itself.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on church and state. Some traditions keep established churches; others, Baptists and Anabaptists among them, hold that the state has no authority over conscience at all. They differ on how to judge Christendom, some seeing a real though flawed good, others a long mistake. Those are questions on which believers can disagree and remain brothers and sisters.

What all should confess is narrower. No nation is God's covenant people. No one may be forced to believe. A Christian's first people is the church of every land, so that a believer in another country is nearer kin than an unbeliever under the same flag. This site names Christian nationalism as an error because it makes the cross the badge of a people's power, and it says the same of any gospel shrunk to fit a cause. It says so as a doctor names an illness, to heal, and with the knowledge that the people it disagrees with are often the ones still filling the pews.

Scripture's last picture of God's people is "a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue" (Revelation 7:9, BSB). No nation is missing from it, and none is in charge.
