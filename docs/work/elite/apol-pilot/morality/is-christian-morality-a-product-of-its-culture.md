# Morals differ from one culture to the next, and Nietzsche traced Christian values to the resentment of the weak. Isn't Christian morality just the product of its culture?

## The short answer

Culture shapes how every people sees right and wrong, and Christians are no exception. That much should be granted at once. But the claim that Christian morality is only the product of its culture runs into a plain fact of history. Its most distinctive teachings cut against the cultures that first heard them: on unwanted infants, on the poor, on who counts as fully human, on enemies. Later they cut against Christian societies too, slowly and with much resistance, on slavery. Nietzsche saw this more clearly than most of the faith's friends. His complaint was not that Christian ethics mirrored the ancient world but that it overturned it. Where a moral belief came from doesn't settle whether it's true. History can't prove that Christian morality came from God. It can show that "just a product of its culture" explains too little.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection comes in two forms, and the second is the stronger.

The first is relativism. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states its two claims. One is a claim of fact: that "there are deep and widespread moral disagreements across different societies, and these disagreements are much more significant than whatever agreements there may be." The other is a claim about truth: that "the truth or falsity of moral judgments, or their justification, is not absolute or universal, but is relative to the traditions, convictions, or practices of a group of persons." In 1947, the encyclopedia records, the American Anthropological Association declared that moral values are relative to cultures and that there is no way of showing that one culture's values are better than another's. On that view Christian morality is the custom of ancient Jews and Greeks, and later of Europeans, carried around the world as if it were everyone's.

The second form is Friedrich Nietzsche's, in *The Genealogy of Morals* (1887). He doesn't ask whether Christian values are true. He asks where they came from and what they are worth: "we need a critique of moral values, the value of these values is for the first time to be called into question." His answer is a history. The strong and noble of the ancient world called themselves good and thought little about the weak. The weak could not win, so they changed the meaning of the words. "The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of resentment becoming creative and giving birth to values." Weakness that can't strike back was renamed goodness, and fear was renamed meekness. Pity, humility and forgiveness, on this account, are what losers praise because they are losers. At full strength the objection says that a morality with a history like that needs no God to explain it, and deserves no obedience.

## What is true in it

A good deal. Moral codes do differ, and Christians have often mistaken their own customs for God's commands. Rules about dress, class and race have been preached as gospel.

Christian ethics does have a history. It grew in Israel, it learned to speak Greek, and every generation since has read it through its own eyes. No Christian stands outside culture, and one who thinks he does is the most captive of all.

Nietzsche is also right that resentment is real and that it dresses up as virtue. Envy calls itself justice. Cowardice calls itself patience. People who can't get even take comfort in picturing their enemies judged. Christians should recognize all of that, because their own Scriptures warn against it.

And the hardest concession is this one. On slavery, most Christians for most of their history followed their culture and not their creed. This library's page on slavery and conquest says it in one sentence: "The church was on both sides, on the wrong side first and for longest." Anyone who claims that Christian morality always stood against its age has that sentence to answer.

## The evidence

Take the test the objection itself sets. If Christian morality were the product of the culture around it, it should look like that culture. Here is where it didn't.

Unwanted children. Aristotle, the most admired moral teacher of the Greek world, wrote: "let there be a law that no deformed child shall live." In Roman Egypt in 1 BC a working man wrote home to the woman expecting his child that if it was a girl she should "cast it out." The first Christians, following Jewish teaching, refused the practice in their earliest writings, as this library's page on abortion and infant exposure sets out.

The poor. In 362 the emperor Julian, who was trying to bring back the old gods, wrote to one of his pagan priests in frustration. In W. C. Wright's translation: "it is disgraceful that, when no Jew ever has to beg, and the impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well, all men see that our people lack aid from us." "Galilaeans" was his name for Christians. He ordered hostels set up in every city and food given to strangers and beggars, and told his priest to teach that this had been the Greeks' practice of old. His own letter shows it wasn't their practice in his day.

Human rank. Aristotle taught that "from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule," and called a slave "a living possession." Paul wrote to his churches: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, BSB). He told the Corinthians that "God chose the weak things of the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27, BSB). And the church worshiped a man who had been crucified, one who, as Paul put it, "emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7, BSB).

Enemies. "Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you" (Luke 6:27, BSB). Whatever else that is, it isn't the voice of a culture praising itself.

Slavery, where the story is long and the church's guilt is part of it. Christians held slaves and defended slavery from the Bible for centuries. But the sustained case against it, as this library's page tells it, was made inside the church and from its own texts, by Bartolomé de las Casas and the Dominicans of Santo Domingo, by William Wilberforce, by Frederick Douglass, "alongside others who argued from other grounds." Here the faith cut against Christian culture itself. It took far too long.

Last, call Nietzsche as a witness. He doesn't say Christianity fitted the ancient world. He writes of a "transvaluation of all values" that triumphed under the sign of the cross, and of "that awful paradox of a 'god on the cross.'" He hated it because it reversed what the ancient world admired. On the facts, he agrees with the Christians.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what a historical explanation can and can't do.

1. If Christian morality were simply the product of the cultures that held it, it would reflect the settled values of those cultures.
2. On unwanted infants, the poor, human rank and enemies it contradicted the settled values of the Greek and Roman world, and on slavery it came to condemn a practice of Christian societies themselves; opponents such as Julian and Nietzsche testify to the contradiction.
3. Where a belief came from doesn't decide whether it is true.

So: Christian morality is not well explained as merely the product of its culture; that doesn't prove it true or revealed, but it removes the word "just."

The weight sits on the second step. Nietzsche accepts it and turns it around: Christian morality wasn't the product of the masters' culture, he says, because it was the product of the slaves'. That is the reply to answer.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is Nietzsche's own. Grant every example, he would say. Of course the poor praise charity and the weak praise mercy. Of course the people with no power are the ones who defend the infant no one wants. A morality that serves the interests of those who hold it has been explained, and a morality that has been explained doesn't need a God. The relativist adds a second point: you find these teachings admirable only because you were raised in a world Christianity had already shaped. Your approval is the effect, so it can't be the evidence.

Part of that should be granted. No one judges from nowhere. Readers formed by centuries of Christian teaching will find mercy obvious, and that proves nothing. And people do believe what suits them.

But the reply has three weaknesses. First, it explains too much. If a morality is discredited by serving its holders' interests, the morality of the strong is discredited in the same way, since nothing suits the powerful better than believing that power is its own warrant. Nietzsche's method cuts every way, his own contempt for pity included. That is step 3 again: showing why someone holds a belief doesn't show the belief false. Nietzsche knew that. His real question, in his preface, is whether these values have "hindered or advanced human well-being." But to answer it he needs a measure of well-being, and his history can't supply one; it assumes the measure of the strong. The weak have every reason to hope that the strong may not crush them. It doesn't follow that the strong may.

Second, it doesn't fit what the teaching asks. A code invented by resentment would bless revenge. This one forbids it, and forbids it to the injured: "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, BSB). Nietzsche answers that this is revenge postponed and handed to God. Perhaps, in some hearts. But a morality that tells its own holders they will be judged by how they treated "the least of these" (Matthew 25:40, BSB) is a strange tool for self-flattery.

Third, relativism costs its user more than it costs the church. If right and wrong are only what a culture approves, then the slave trade was wrong for us and right for the traders. A relativist can still condemn it by his own culture's lights; what he can't say is that the traders, by theirs, were mistaken. And the strongest objections in this part of the library, the Crusades, the Inquisition, the cover-up of abuse, turn into reports of changing taste. The critic who says those things were truly wrong is right. But he has stopped being a relativist.

## What remains unresolved

Whether there are moral truths that hold for every culture is one of philosophy's open questions, and this page hasn't settled it; the library's page on the moral argument takes it further. How much of the change in the treatment of children, the poor and the enslaved belongs to Christian conviction and how much to other causes is argued by historians. Much of what set the first Christians apart was Jewish before it was Christian, and the church has never said otherwise; that moves the question back to why Israel stood out among its neighbors, and doesn't answer it. And Christians can't always tell, in themselves, creed from custom. Every generation of the church has found that some of what it called Christian morality was its own culture, and ours will too.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how moral truth is known. Some stress a natural law that every person can know in part, on the strength of Paul's words that Gentiles "show that the work of the law is written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15, BSB). Others stress that right and wrong are known surely only by God's command. Both expect what history shows: much moral agreement across cultures, and blindness in every one of them. Christians also differ on which of today's disputed questions are matters of creed and which of custom, as other pages in this area set out.

What they confess together is that the measure is not a culture at all, the first century's or ours. It is a person. Paul tells the church to have the mind of Christ, who "humbled Himself and became obedient to death" (Philippians 2:8, BSB), and that death was a cross. Every culture that has carried that story has been judged by it, the ones that called themselves Christian first of all.
