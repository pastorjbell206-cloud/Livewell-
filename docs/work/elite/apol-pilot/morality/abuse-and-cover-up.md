# Church leaders hid the sexual abuse of children and protected the abusers. How can anyone trust a church, or the faith it preaches, after that?

## The short answer

If you have been harmed, or someone you love has, the site's Find Help page (/help) lists places to turn now, and if a child is in danger, call the police; the sexual abuse of a child is a crime to be reported to the police, whatever any church says. The charge in the question is true. Church leaders, Catholic and Protestant alike, leaders of the Southern Baptist Convention among them, knew of abuse and left abusers in place, kept lists out of sight, and protected their institutions instead of children. No argument on this page excuses that, and no one should be asked to trust a church that has not told the truth about it. What this page argues is narrower: the cover-up was not the faith's teaching but a betrayal its own Scriptures condemn in the plainest words they have, and the faith itself demands what the cover-up refused, the truth told, the abuser handed to the authorities, the harmed believed and protected.

## The objection at its strongest

The record speaks for itself, and some of it comes from the church's own commissions. In 2002, after news reports in Boston about priests who had abused many children, and in the face of accusations of a cover-up by bishops on a vast scale, the American Catholic bishops commissioned two studies. A board of Catholic laypeople they appointed, the National Review Board for the Protection of Children and Young People, issued "A Report on the Crisis in the Catholic Church in the United States" on 27 February 2004. As *The New York Times* reported it that day, the board laid blame on "the bishops who turned their backs on victims and protected their priests," and its members could not find "a single expression of outrage" in church correspondence from a supervising bishop about any priest the bishops knew had been accused. The companion study by researchers at John Jay College of Criminal Justice found that 4,392 priests had been accused of abusing more than ten thousand children between 1950 and 2002, and cautioned that even these numbers were an undercount, since many victims had never come forward.

Protestants have no ground to treat this as someone else's scandal. In May 2022 an investigation of how the Southern Baptist Convention, the largest Protestant denomination in the United States, had handled sexual abuse over two decades produced a long report, and the Convention's leaders announced that they would release a list, kept in secret until then, of ministers and church workers they said were credibly accused of sexual abuse.

At full strength the objection says this. The pattern is the same across churches that disagree on almost everything else: abusers left in place instead of reported, victims urged to forgive and stay quiet, the church's good name put before the child. A faith whose leaders behave this way when they think no one will see has shown what it is. Its talk of holiness is a cover, its forgiveness a way to excuse the powerful, and its authority a shelter for predators. No one owes it trust, and its claims about God deserve no more belief than its claims about itself.

## What is true in it

Almost all of it. The cover-up was real, it was widespread, and it was done by men who preached the gospel. They knew, and they chose the institution. The studies were commissioned only after news reporting had brought the facts into the open, and many of those harmed had never come forward at all, out of fear or shame, as the 2004 studies themselves noted. Each theological word the objection names has been bent this way: forgiveness turned into a demand that the harmed stop speaking, unity into a reason not to involve the police, honor for leaders into a wall around them.

The site's own Study Bible says it without softening. In its note on 1 Timothy 5, where Paul tells the church not to accept an accusation against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses, it answers the question whether a report of abuse should therefore be ignored: "Abuse is very often done where there are no other witnesses. Verse 19 is about how a church weighs an accusation in its own discipline. It is never a reason to dismiss a report, to keep it quiet, or to ask a person who has been harmed to stay silent." And it names the misuse: "This passage, like others about discipline, has also been badly misused, and that must be faced."

It is also true that people who leave the church over this are not faithless for leaving. They were harmed, or they watched leaders protect those who harmed. No one has the right to tell them to come back.

## The evidence

The question is whether the cover-up expressed the faith or betrayed it, and Scripture answers in its own words. When the sons of Eli the priest abused their office and the people who came to worship, the text says "the sin of these young men was severe in the sight of the LORD" (1 Samuel 2:17, BSB), and God's judgment fell on the father for what he allowed: "his sons blasphemed God and he did not restrain them" (1 Samuel 3:13, BSB). The leader who knows and does nothing is named and judged with the one who does the harm.

Ezekiel's word to Israel's leaders describes the cover-up's priorities exactly: "Woe to the shepherds of Israel, who only feed themselves! Should not the shepherds feed their flock?" (Ezekiel 34:2, BSB). God's answer is not to protect the shepherds' standing but to take the flock out of their hands: "I will deliver My flock from their mouths, and it will no longer be food for them" (Ezekiel 34:10, BSB). Jesus spoke of anyone who causes one of the little ones who believe in him to stumble: "it would be better for him to have a large millstone hung around his neck and to be drowned in the depths of the sea" (Matthew 18:6, BSB). Paul told the church, "Have no fellowship with the fruitless deeds of darkness, but rather expose them" (Ephesians 5:11, BSB), and Jesus said that "There is nothing concealed that will not be disclosed" (Luke 12:2, BSB).

Nor does the gospel's forgiveness cancel the state's justice. Paul calls the ruler "God's servant, an agent of retribution to the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4, BSB). The Study Bible's note on 1 Timothy 5, like its note on 1 Corinthians 5, draws the conclusion the cover-up refused, and this page repeats the note's words: "Church discipline must never be used to silence someone who has been harmed, to protect someone who has done harm, or to punish someone for asking questions or telling the truth. Abuse and other crimes are reported to the police and the proper authorities, whom Paul calls “God’s servant, an agent of retribution to the wrongdoer” (Romans 13:4). A church meeting is never a substitute for that."

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the cover-up shows. It grants the record in full.

1. Church leaders, Catholic and Protestant, hid the sexual abuse of children, protected abusers and silenced or disbelieved those they harmed, to protect their institutions.
2. The Scriptures those leaders preached condemn exactly this: leaders who feed on the flock, a father who knows and does not restrain, harm to a child who trusts, deeds hidden in darkness, and any use of the church to shield a wrongdoer from the authority God set to punish him.
3. The doctrines invoked to excuse the cover-up, forgiveness, unity and honor for leaders, when read as the same Scriptures teach them, require the truth to be told, the abuser to be handed to the authorities and the harmed to be protected.

So: the cover-up shows that church leaders betrayed the faith they preached and that no institution's claim to holiness may be trusted without the truth; it does not show that the faith taught it, and the faith itself condemns it and demands its exposure.

The weight sits on the third step. The objection's deepest form says that the faith's own doctrines produced the cover-up. If forgiveness, unity and honor for leaders, rightly read, still lead to silence, the argument fails, and that is where it should be pressed.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the cover-up was not a lapse from Christian teaching but an application of it. A church that teaches its people to forgive seventy times seven, to avoid scandal before the world, to settle matters among believers rather than before unbelieving judges and to honor those who lead them has built, in its doctrines, the machinery of silence. The men who moved abusers were not ignorant of their faith. They were using it.

The answer has to begin by granting that they used it, and that churches taught some of these things in ways that made the silence easier. But each doctrine, read as Scripture gives it, says the opposite of what the cover-up made of it. Forgiveness in the New Testament is the release of a debt owed to the one who forgives; it was never given to a bishop to grant on a child's behalf, and it doesn't remove the ruler's sword (Romans 13:4). Paul's rebuke of believers who take each other to court concerns ordinary disputes between them, not crimes, and he writes in the same letter to rebuke a church that tolerated a grave sin out of pride (1 Corinthians 5). The scandal Jesus warned against is harm done to little ones, not harm to the church's reputation. And the honor owed to leaders carries a stricter judgment, not a shelter: "we who teach will be judged more strictly" (James 3:1, BSB).

The reply is right about one thing, and it should be kept. Doctrine can be bent to serve power, and the people who bend it are often sincere. That is why a church's words about itself can't be the measure of whether it is safe. What makes a church trustworthy is what it does when a child or an adult tells the truth: whether the police are called, whether the accused is removed from any place of trust, whether the person who spoke is believed and protected, whether the whole matter is told openly rather than settled in private. A church that does those things is keeping the faith. One that does not is keeping something else. And even a church that does them is owed no one's trust by the person it failed; trust, if it comes back at all, is given, not owed.

## What remains unresolved

How many people were abused in churches, Catholic and Protestant, is not known; every study of it counts only what was reported, and its authors say so. How far the reforms adopted since 2002 and since 2022 have made churches safer is still being tested, and survivors' accounts of how they were received remain the measure that matters. Whether, and how, a person who has been harmed in a church can ever trust a church again is not a question for this page to answer for anyone. A person who cannot is owed understanding, not argument.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how churches should be governed, by bishops, by elders or by congregations, and each form has protected abusers in its own way: the hierarchy by moving them, the independent congregation by having no one above it to call it to account. They differ on what forgiveness requires of the one who forgives. What all must confess together is that abuse is a crime as well as a sin, that it is reported to the police and the proper authorities, that no one who has been harmed is to be told to stay silent, and that "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted" (Psalm 34:18, BSB). The laws on who must report abuse, and to whom, differ from place to place and change over time; nothing on this page is legal advice, and anyone unsure what the law asks of them can ask the police or the child protection service where they live, or a lawyer. A word to anyone who has been hurt: what happened to you was wrong, it was not your fault, and it is not wrong to grieve it. You don't owe anyone your silence. If you or a child is in danger now, call the police. The Find Help page on this site lists places to turn.
