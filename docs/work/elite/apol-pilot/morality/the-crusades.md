# Weren't the Crusades a holy war of conquest, and don't they show what Christianity does when it has power?

## The short answer

The Crusades were a holy war, preached by popes and fought under the cross, and the worst of what was done in them was done in Christ's name: the massacre of the Jewish communities of Worms and Mainz in 1096, the slaughter of Jerusalem's Muslims and the burning of its Jews in their synagogue in July 1099, the sack of Constantinople, a Christian city, in 1204. That has to be said first and without excuse. It is also false that they came from nowhere: Turkish armies had overrun Christian Asia Minor, and a Byzantine emperor had asked the West for soldiers. That correction is made because it is true, and it lessens nothing that was done. What the record shows is not that Christ's teaching produces holy war, but that the church, given armies and the promise of forgiveness to offer, turned his cross into a soldier's badge. The church has confessed that as sin, and it is the warning every Christian who wants the faith to rule by force has to hear.

## The objection at its strongest

David Hume put the verdict that most modern readers share in his *History of England* (1762). The Crusades, he wrote, "have ever since engaged the curiosity of mankind, as the most signal and most durable monument of human folly, that has yet appeared in any age or nation." His account of why they happened is a charge against the church. The clergy had gained "the greatest ascendant over the human mind," and violent men knew no expiation but what their pastors imposed, so "it was easy to represent the holy war as an equivalent for all penances, and an atonement for every violation of justice and humanity." He noticed the symbol as well: the cross, once honored because the pagan world despised it, "became the badge of union, and was affixed to their right shoulder, by all who enlisted themselves in this sacred warfare."

At full strength the objection says more than that some Christians behaved badly. A pope promised forgiveness to men who would go and kill. The first armies to march killed Jews in the cities of the Rhine before they had seen a Muslim. When Jerusalem fell on 15 July 1099 the crusaders killed its Muslim inhabitants without distinction, and Jews who had taken refuge in their synagogue died when the crusaders burned it. A century later an army raised under the cross sacked Constantinople, the capital of the Christian East. If this is what the church did when it held power in Europe, the skeptic says, then the gentle sayings of Jesus are not the faith's real character. The Crusades are. And the claim that they were a corruption is only the excuse every movement makes for its crimes.

## What is true in it

Nearly all of the history is true, and it should be granted without bargaining. The Crusades were not wars that happened to involve Christians. They were preached as acts of devotion. According to the accounts of the Council of Clermont in 1095, Pope Urban II promised that those who went would be forgiven, and participation in the war was treated as a penance that could counterbalance sin. Hume's sentence about atonement for every violation of justice is sharp, but it describes something real.

The killing of Jews in 1096 is not a detail at the edge of the story. Armies on their way east attacked the Jewish communities of the Rhineland, and the Study Bible's note on John 16 names the massacres of Worms and Mainz as a fulfillment of Jesus' warning that "anyone who kills you will think he is offering a service to God" (John 16:2, BSB). Many of the attackers wanted to force Jews to be baptized. Some bishops tried to shelter them, as Gibbon records, but that lessens nothing that was done by men who wore the cross, and Jewish memory of the crusades as a time of martyrdom is the memory of their victims.

The sack of Constantinople in April 1204 was the Fourth Crusade's end, after its army had already taken the Catholic city of Zara in 1202. The crusaders broke their oaths and despoiled the city's churches. Pope Innocent III condemned the attack on Zara, and he rebuked the crusaders for what they did in Constantinople. The church itself, in other words, has granted the objection's central facts.

## The evidence

Before the Crusades came a request. In 1071 the Seljuk Turks defeated and captured the Byzantine emperor at Manzikert, and in the years that followed they overran most of Asia Minor; in the same decade Turkish forces took Jerusalem from the Fatimid rulers of Egypt. Jerusalem had been under Muslim rule since the first conquests of the seventh century, and pilgrimage had been allowed under some rulers and disrupted under others. Early in 1095 envoys of the emperor Alexios I came to a council at Piacenza to ask Urban II for help against the Turks, and that November, at Clermont, Urban preached the expedition. What Alexios seems to have wanted was soldiers for his own army; what Urban preached was something larger. The wars that followed answered real conquests, of Asia Minor above all, and the claim sometimes heard, that the West fell without cause on a world at peace, is false.

That correction matters because the truth matters, and it is stated here only for that reason. It lessens nothing the crusaders did. Defending Eastern Christians against an invader, if that had been all, would have been an ordinary war with ordinary limits. What the preaching added was the promise of forgiveness for fighting and the cross as the soldier's badge, and those additions were what turned a war of defense into a war of devotion that could reach Jews in the Rhineland, Muslims in Jerusalem and Greek Christians in Constantinople alike.

Set beside this what Jesus said when a disciple drew a sword in his defense: "Put your sword back in its place," Jesus said to him. "For all who draw the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52, BSB). When James and John wanted fire on a village that would not receive him, "Jesus turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:55, BSB). Paul wrote, "The weapons of our warfare are not the weapons of the world" (2 Corinthians 10:4, BSB). Nothing in the New Testament promises forgiveness to anyone for killing, and nothing gives any land to the church.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the Crusades show. It grants the history and asks whether the conclusion the objection draws from it follows.

1. The Crusades were preached by the church as acts of devotion, with the promise of forgiveness, and crusaders committed massacres of Jews, Muslims and Eastern Christians under the sign of the cross.
2. The teaching of Jesus and his apostles forbids the use of the sword to advance his kingdom, promises forgiveness to no one for killing, and gives the church no territory to win or hold.
3. A practice that contradicts the founder's explicit teaching, adopted more than a thousand years after him by a church that held armies and land, shows what that church had become, not what the founder taught.

So: the Crusades show that a church joined to military and political power will use Christ's name to sanctify its wars, and that is a judgment on the church; they don't show that holy war is the true character of the faith, whose own Scriptures condemn it.

The weight sits on the third step. The objection's answer to it is that every movement disowns its crimes, so the appeal to the founder proves nothing. That is where a careful reader should press, and it is answered below.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that "that wasn't real Christianity" is a dodge. Catholic popes, Christian bishops and devout knights made the Crusades. If the church of the eleventh and twelfth centuries was not real Christianity, then real Christianity is an ideal no one has ever found, defined after the fact to exclude whatever embarrasses it. And the Old Testament contains wars commanded by God, so the crusaders had texts to point to.

The reply deserves a straight answer, and part of the answer is a concession. The crusaders were Christians, and the church that sent them was the church. This page does not say they were impostors. It says they were Christians who sinned in a way their own Scriptures condemned, and that the measure of the faith is not what Christians have done but what Christ taught, the standard by which the church itself later judged them. That is not a definition made after the fact. Jesus' words about the sword were written down a thousand years before Clermont, and the earliest Christians lived by them under persecution, as the Honest Answer on whether God commanded genocide and this library's page on the command to destroy the Canaanites explain: those commands were tied to one land and one moment, they give no one a warrant for violence, and the New Testament nowhere renews them.

Nor is the distinction between a founder's teaching and his followers' failures a special plea. Everyone uses it. A critic who judges the Crusades wicked is judging them by a standard, and the standard he reaches for, that the weak are not to be slaughtered and enemies may not be killed for their faith, is the standard of the Sermon on the Mount: "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, BSB). Hume's own condemnation borrowed its force from the faith he was condemning.

What the reply gets right is the danger. The error of the Crusades was not peculiar to the Middle Ages. It is the error this site names Christian nationalism: the cross made the badge of a people's power, its enemies treated as God's enemies, and devotion measured by willingness to fight. Those who would make the crusader a hero of their civilization and those who would make him the whole truth about Christianity make the same mistake. Both take a medieval war as the measure of the faith instead of measuring the war by Christ.

## What remains unresolved

Historians still argue over how far the Crusades were driven by devotion and how far by land, wealth and the ambitions of princes, and the honest answer is that the motives were mixed in different men and different campaigns. How many died at Jerusalem in 1099 is disputed, and the medieval chroniclers' numbers are not to be trusted. Whether any war fought in defense of Eastern Christians could have been just, without the promise of forgiveness and the badge of the cross, is a question of just-war reasoning on which Christians still disagree. And what the church owes now, beyond confession, to the descendants of those its armies killed is a question no page can settle.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on war itself. The peace churches hold that Jesus' teaching forbids his followers to take up arms at all; most Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant traditions hold that a ruler may wage a just war under strict limits, and the Study Bible's notes on Matthew 5 and 26 set out both readings without choosing between them. Christians also differ in how they judge the first crusaders' intentions, some finding real devotion mixed with violence and others finding the devotion itself corrupted. What all can confess together is that no one may be killed for his faith, that the church has no sword but the word, and that judgment, as Peter wrote, begins "with the family of God" (1 Peter 4:17, BSB).
