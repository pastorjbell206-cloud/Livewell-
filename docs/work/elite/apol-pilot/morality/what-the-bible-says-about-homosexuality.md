# What does the Bible say about homosexuality, and why do Christians disagree?

## The short answer

If you are gay, or love someone who is, you are met with respect here. If these verses weigh on you, talk with a trusted pastor or friend. The Find Help page on this site lists places to turn. Six passages in the Bible speak of same-sex acts, and each speaks against what it describes. For nearly all of its history the church has read them as ruling out all sexual union outside the marriage of a man and a woman, and most churches in the world still teach that. Since the late twentieth century some Christians have argued that the passages had exploitative practices of the ancient world in view, and don't speak to faithful, lifelong unions. This page sets out both readings in their holders' terms and then says where its author lands. Contempt for gay people has no warrant on either reading.

## The challenge at its strongest

The historic teaching is challenged from outside the church and from inside it. From outside, the charge is simple: the church has called a whole class of people sinful for whom they love, and the results have been cruel. From inside, the challenge is a reading of the Bible, often called the affirming reading, and it deserves to be heard in the form its best scholars give it.

John Boswell made an early form of the argument in *Christianity, Social Tolerance, and Homosexuality* (1980). As the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes him, he argued that many passages now taken as condemnations of homosexuality are more concerned with prostitution, and that where same-sex acts are called "unnatural" the meaning is closer to out of the ordinary than to immoral. James Brownson made the case again in *Bible, Gender, Sexuality* (2013). As the Study Bible's note on Romans 1 summarizes it, he argues that Paul had in view excess, exploitation, or practices tied to status and idolatry in his culture, that Paul's idea of nature reflects ancient assumptions about gender, and that the passage therefore doesn't address faithful, committed same-sex relationships.

At full strength the case has four parts. First, what the ancient world knew was mostly men using boys, slaves and prostitutes, so that is what the Bible's writers condemned, and they were right to. Second, the key words are rare or disputed, and a doctrine that binds people for life shouldn't hang on them. Third, the idea of a lasting orientation, of people who never chose whom they are drawn to, came long after the Bible; the word "homosexuality" itself was coined in the late nineteenth century. Fourth, the church has changed its mind before. As the site's study of the family reports Brownson's argument, the church revised its reading on slavery and on divorce when it saw the fruit of a teaching in people's lives, and the fruit of this one, for many gay Christians, has been loneliness and shame.

## What is true in it

A good deal of it. The church's record toward gay people includes real cruelty, and it should be named plainly. Under the Christian emperor Justinian, the Stanford Encyclopedia records, the law code of 529 made same-sex acts a capital crime, though the repentant could be spared. In the 1730s Dutch authorities used torture to get confessions, and as many as a hundred men and boys were executed. In more recent times families and churches have turned their own children out. None of that was obedience to Jesus. It was sin, and the church committed it.

It's also true that the texts are few. Jesus says nothing recorded about same-sex acts, and the six passages are a handful of verses in a very long book. Churches have often spoken more loudly about these verses than about the greed, gossip and arrogance that stand beside them in Paul's own lists. And the story of Sodom has been misused. The men of Sodom tried to gang-rape two strangers. The Study Bible's notes say that on any reading "nothing in this chapter gives anyone a reason for contempt toward any person."

The point about words is partly true as well. For one of Paul's two words in 1 Corinthians 6:9, no clear use before Paul is known. And the Bible's writers had no word for a person's orientation. A reader who says the Bible never addresses the question exactly as people ask it now is right about that much.

## The evidence

Genesis 19 tells how the men of Sodom surrounded Lot's house and demanded his guests. All Christians agree that what they demanded was gang rape. Many, pointing to Jude 7, where Sodom "pursued strange flesh," see the same-sex nature of the demand as part of what is condemned. Others answer that the prophets name different sins, "arrogant, overfed, and complacent; they did not help the poor and needy" (Ezekiel 16:49, BSB), and that Jude's phrase, "other flesh" in Greek, has been read by some as the angels. The story can't settle the modern question by itself.

Leviticus speaks directly: "You must not lie with a man as with a woman; that is an abomination" (Leviticus 18:22, BSB), and Leviticus 20:13 repeats it with the penalty of death under Israel's law. Those who hold the historic reading note, as the Study Bible's note on Leviticus 18 sets out, that the verse sits among laws Christians still keep, against incest, adultery and child sacrifice, and that its wording names no particular setting such as a temple or an abusive relationship. Others reply that Christians already set aside laws in the same code, such as the ban on mixed fabrics, and that the verse had particular ancient practices in view.

Romans 1 is the longest passage, and the only one that seems to speak of women as well as men: "Even their women exchanged natural relations for unnatural ones. Likewise, the men abandoned natural relations with women and burned with lust for one another" (Romans 1:26-27, BSB). Paul is describing what follows when people trade the Creator for created things. The debated word is "natural." The historic reading holds that "natural" here points back to God's creation of humanity as male and female. The other reading holds that Paul shared an ancient idea of nature and was describing excess.

In 1 Corinthians 6:9 Paul lists those who will not inherit the kingdom of God, among them "men who submit to or perform homosexual acts." That phrase translates two Greek words. The first, *malakoi*, means "soft ones"; it was used of men thought unmanly, and often of the passive partner. The second, *arsenokoitai*, joins the words for "male" and "bed." It appears in the New Testament only here and in 1 Timothy 1:10, and it seems to have been formed from the Greek wording of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13. The historic reading takes that link to show that Paul means what Leviticus means, any man lying with a male. The other reading holds that the words pointed to men's sexual use of boys, slaves or prostitutes. In 1 Timothy 1:10 the same word stands in a list that follows the order of the Ten Commandments, just before "slave traders." The Study Bible's note on 1 Corinthians 6 adds that Paul's list places these acts beside greed, drunkenness, theft and verbal abuse, "sins common to everyone in the room," and that he "writes not to shame one group but to call the whole church to a holiness that Christ has already made possible."

The Study Bible's notes say the traditional reading was "held by the church through most of its history" and "remains the teaching of the Catholic and Orthodox churches and of most Protestant churches worldwide." That teaching doesn't rest on six verses alone. Its center is what Jesus said when he was asked about marriage: "from the beginning the Creator 'made them male and female,'" and "the two will become one flesh" (Matthew 19:4-5, BSB). On this teaching sexual union belongs to the marriage of a man and a woman, and every other sexual act, by anyone, falls outside it. Jesus was answering a question about divorce (Matthew 19:3), and whether his words also settle this question is part of what the two readings argue about. The change is recent. The site's study of the family records that some Protestant bodies in North America and Europe have revised their teaching.

## The argument, laid out

The steps below take no side between the two readings. What they argue is narrower: what kind of disagreement this is.

1. Six passages speak of same-sex acts, and every one speaks against what it describes; no passage speaks of such acts with approval.
2. The disagreement is over what those passages describe: all same-sex acts, as the church has historically read them, or the exploitative and excessive practices of the ancient world, so that faithful, lifelong unions aren't in view.
3. Serious scholars on both sides make their case from the text, its words and its setting, and most on both sides say they are bound by what Scripture teaches.
4. Scripture says every person bears God's image (Genesis 1:27), and Paul's lists set these acts beside greed, envy and gossip and then turn on the reader who judges (Romans 2:1).

So: Christians who disagree here are disagreeing mostly about what the Bible means and how its teaching on creation and marriage applies, and on neither reading does the Bible give anyone a reason for contempt.

The weight sits on the second step. If the passages plainly had only abuse in view, the historic teaching would lose its footing in these texts. If they plainly speak of the acts themselves, whoever does them and however lovingly, the newer reading would. Each side thinks the matter is clearer than this page makes it sound.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

Defenders of the historic teaching say the newer reading can't account for the texts. Richard Hays and Robert Gagnon argue, as the site's study of the family reports them, that the texts rest on the creation pattern Jesus himself cited, that every text that speaks of the practice speaks against it, and that the comparison with slavery fails, because Scripture's own direction moves away from slavery and never moves away from its sexual ethic. Others who hold this reading point to the wording. Paul's argument in Romans 1 reaches back to creation, not to particular abuses, and he speaks of women as well as men and of desire that is mutual, "for one another." Leviticus 20:13 names no victim and holds both partners responsible. And many add that the newer reading arrived at the very moment the surrounding culture changed its mind, which should make any reader suspicious of himself.

Defenders of the newer reading answer that the historic side has its own problem of timing: it read these texts for centuries without asking about people for whom this isn't a choice or a phase, because it didn't know there were such people. They say a teaching should be judged partly by what it does, and that this one has asked gay Christians to carry a weight their teachers never carried. Some who make this case don't dispute what Paul meant. As the note on Leviticus 18 puts it, they "accept that the verse, and Paul, forbid all sexual relations between men, but argue that the church should weigh those texts differently in light of modern accounts of sexual orientation."

The historic side replies that the cost of a teaching doesn't show it false, since Jesus asks costly things of every disciple, and that some who hold the teaching bear its cost themselves. The study of the family names Wesley Hill, who wrote *Washed and Waiting* (2010) as a gay Christian committed to celibacy and has pressed churches to make real room for single people in their common life. Neither reply ends the argument.

## What remains unresolved

How far Paul's words reach is still argued among scholars who know the Greek equally well. What ancient writers knew of lasting same-sex love between equals is argued too. So is the larger question beneath both: how the church should weigh what has been learned about sexual orientation, and whether anything learned about people could change what a text teaches.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on the question itself, and also on what kind of question it is. For most churches it is not a matter on which believers can agree to differ. Other churches treat it as a question on which faithful Christians may disagree and remain in fellowship. The Study Bible's notes set out both readings and say so in these words: "Faithful Christians disagree here, and these notes take no side." This page sets out both readings and then says where its author lands.

Some things all should confess together. Every person bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27). The church's contempt and cruelty toward gay people were sins, and they must be named as sins and turned from. Paul's next sentence after Romans 1 is aimed at the reader who has been nodding along: "You, therefore, have no excuse, you who pass judgment on another" (Romans 2:1, BSB). And faithfulness is costly whichever way it lies. Celibacy is a hard calling, and so is marriage; Paul calls each a "gift from God" (1 Corinthians 7:7, BSB). A church that asks celibacy of anyone owes that person a family.

The author of this site holds the historic teaching: sexual union belongs to the marriage of a man and a woman, and sexual acts outside it, same-sex acts among them, are what Scripture calls sin. It isn't a question the creeds settle. But because, in the words of the site's study of the family, "it concerns what Scripture calls sin and repentance," most churches don't count it among the questions on which believers simply agree to differ, and he stands with them. The newer reading is given above as its own defenders make it, and those who hold it aren't treated here as enemies. The strongest objection to his view, and one this page doesn't claim to have fully answered, is the one that reading presses hardest: the church read these texts for centuries without asking about people who never chose whom they are drawn to, and the cost of the teaching falls on them and not on most of those who teach it. He holds it with the two convictions stated above: that it gives no one a reason for contempt, and that a church which asks celibacy of anyone owes that person a family.

A last word, in the words of the notes. If you are gay, or love someone who is, you are met with respect here. If these verses weigh on you, talk with a trusted pastor or friend. The Find Help page on this site lists places to turn. And if a church has treated you with contempt, that was a wrong done to you. Contempt like that, as this site says elsewhere, "has no warrant anywhere in the teaching of Jesus," who said, "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28, BSB).
