# Christian nations conquered the Americas and built the Atlantic slave trade, and their churches blessed both. Doesn't that discredit the faith they claimed?

## The short answer

It discredits the churches that blessed them, and that has to be said before anything else. Popes granted Christian kings the right to enslave unbelievers and to divide newly found lands. Conquerors who called themselves Christians destroyed peoples in the Americas, and for generations churches in Europe and America defended slavery from the Bible, Baptists among them. What the record does not show is that the faith required it. Atlantic slavery was built on kidnapping, which the law of Moses punished by death, and on a doctrine of race the Bible's account of one human family contradicts. The case against both was made, late and at great cost, by Christians reading the same Bible, among them a Dominican bishop in the Indies and a man who had escaped slavery in Maryland. The church was on both sides, on the wrong side first and for longest.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection begins with the people who suffered. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in Maryland, wrote in the appendix to his *Narrative* (1845) of a country where "The slave auctioneer's bell and the church-going bell chime in with each other," where "The man who wields the blood-clotted cowskin during the week fills the pulpit on Sunday," and where "Revivals of religion and revivals in the slave-trade go hand in hand together." In the Indies three centuries earlier the Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas had described conquerors "who pride themselves in the Name of Christians" setting out to destroy whole peoples (*A Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies*, written in 1542 and published in 1552, in the English translation of 1689).

The critic's argument goes further than the crimes. Hector Avalos argued in *Slavery, Abolitionism, and the Ethics of Biblical Scholarship* (2011) that the Bible endorses slavery and that much modern biblical scholarship still works as an apology for it, as the site's Honest Answer on slavery reports his charge. On this view the slaveholding preachers were not misreading the Bible; they were reading it. The Bible permits buying foreigners as property (Leviticus 25:44-46) and tells slaves to obey their masters (Ephesians 6:5). Popes treated the conquest of unbelievers as a Christian work. When Christians later turned against slavery, they took credit for a change that came from the Enlightenment, from economics and from the revolt of the enslaved themselves. A faith that blessed conquest and slavery for centuries, and found its conscience only when the world around it changed, has no standing to teach anyone what is good.

## What is true in it

Much of it. In 1452 and 1455 Pope Nicholas V issued bulls, *Dum Diversas* and *Romanus Pontifex*, granting Iberian kings the right to reduce Muslims, pagans and other unbelievers to perpetual slavery, and a later papal bull, *Inter caetera*, divided newly found lands between Portugal and Castile. Conquest and conversion were carried by the same ships. This was the error the site calls Christian nationalism in its imperial form: the cross made the banner of a kingdom's expansion, and the gospel made a title to other peoples' land.

The Bible's own texts were used as the critic says. The Study Bible's note on Ephesians 6 says plainly that in the Atlantic slave trade and the American South "slaveholders and many preachers used Ephesians 6:5 to demand obedience from enslaved Africans and to defend slavery as biblical," and that "The church's record was mixed, and it must be named honestly." In 1844 the Baptists' national mission body refused to consider slaveholders as missionaries, and in 1845 Baptists of the slaveholding states met in Augusta, Georgia, and formed the Southern Baptist Convention. In 1995 that Convention resolved that its relationship to African Americans "has been hindered from the beginning by the role that slavery played in the formation of the Southern Baptist Convention," and voted to "apologize to all African-Americans for condoning and/or perpetuating individual and systemic racism." Baptists, and the evangelical churches that shared their views, stand inside this record, not outside it.

And abolition did have many engines. The Honest Answer on slavery names them: the Enlightenment's arguments for natural rights, the revolt in Saint-Domingue that ended in an independent Haiti, economics and finally war. Christians did not end slavery alone, and they should not say they did.

## The evidence

Look first at what the Bible says about the trade that fed Atlantic slavery. "Whoever kidnaps another man must be put to death, whether he sells him or the man is found in his possession" (Exodus 21:16, BSB). Paul lists "slave traders" among those the law exists to restrain (1 Timothy 1:10, BSB). The law forbade returning a slave who had fled to Israel: "Do not return a slave to his master if he has taken refuge with you" (Deuteronomy 23:15, BSB). Atlantic slavery began with the capture of free people and depended on their capture for centuries. Its American form was also bound to a doctrine of race, that Africans and their descendants were fit by nature for bondage. Paul told the Athenians, "From one man He made every nation of men" (Acts 17:26, BSB), and Job asked of his own servants, "Did not He who made me in the womb also make them?" (Job 31:15, BSB). None of this answers why the law of Moses permitted any slavery at all, a question the Honest Answer faces without pretending to settle it. But it shows that the slaveholders' Bible condemned the very things their slavery was built on.

Then look at who first resisted. In the Spanish colonies Dominican friars at Santo Domingo denounced the enslavement of the native peoples as unjust and illegal, and Las Casas, who had come to the Indies as a settler and was present at a massacre in Cuba, turned against the enslavement he had seen. He first proposed bringing African slaves in the native peoples' place, and later changed his mind and became an advocate for the Africans too. His account of the destruction of the Indies helped bring about the New Laws of 1542, which abolished the enslavement of native peoples for the first time in European colonial history, and in 1537 Pope Paul III forbade the enslavement of the peoples of the Americas. Later the British campaign against the trade was led in Parliament by William Wilberforce, an evangelical, after the committee against the trade was formed in 1787; the trade was outlawed throughout the British Empire on 25 March 1807, and slavery itself by the act of 1833. Douglass drew the line that ran through the whole conflict: "I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land."

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the record shows about the faith, granting everything it shows about the churches.

1. Churches and Christian rulers sanctioned and profited from the conquest of the Americas and from Atlantic slavery, and defended both from Scripture.
2. That slavery and that conquest violated the Bible's own explicit commands, against kidnapping and the trade in stolen people, and its teaching that every human being bears God's image and that God made every nation from one man.
3. The sustained case against both was made within the church, from those same texts, by Christians such as Las Casas, the Dominicans of Santo Domingo, Wilberforce and Douglass, alongside others who argued from other grounds.

So: the record convicts the churches that blessed slavery and conquest of reading Scripture through their own interests; it does not show that the faith required either, since the faith they betrayed supplied the case against them; and it gives no Christian any ground for pride.

The weight sits on the second step. Avalos's reply is that the Bible permits slavery, and that the abolitionists found in its spirit what they had brought to it. If the second step only reads modern conscience back into the texts, the argument fails.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is Avalos's, and it should be heard in full. The Bible's permission is not hidden. Leviticus lets Israel buy foreigners and hand them down as property; the New Testament sends no command to free them. The slaveholders could quote chapter and verse, and the abolitionists answered with the spirit of the whole, which is exactly what a reader does when the text says what he wishes it didn't. And if the Bible's spirit was so clear, why did it take the church eighteen centuries to see it?

Part of that has to be granted, as the Honest Answer on slavery grants it. The Bible regulates slavery without forbidding it, and no Christian should pretend otherwise. But the reply proves less than it claims about the slavery that actually existed in the Atlantic world. The slaveholders' Bible did not merely fail to forbid that slavery. It condemned its foundation in so many words: the theft of human beings, punished by death, and the trading of them, named among the deeds of the lawless. The texts the abolitionists cited were not the Bible's spirit against its letter. Exodus 21:16 is letter. And the claim that God made every nation from one man was not a modern sentiment imported into Acts; it was what Paul said in Athens.

The delay is real and it is the church's shame. It is also evidence of something the critic should weigh. The Christians who defended slavery were not simple men misreading an obscure verse; they read the Bible with their profits and their place at stake, and their reading served them. That is a warning to every Christian, not a reason to discard the texts their victims read more truly. The enslaved heard Exodus read by the men who owned them, "I have indeed seen the affliction of My people in Egypt" (Exodus 3:7, BSB), and they knew whose story it was. The same temptation has no party. Whoever reads Scripture in a way that happens to confirm his own power, wealth or side should suspect that reading first.

## What remains unresolved

Why God gave Israel a law that regulated slavery rather than forbidding it outright is not answered by this page, and the Honest Answer on slavery says it does not answer it either. How much of abolition's success belongs to Christian conviction and how much to economics, war and the revolt of the enslaved is argued by historians and will not be settled by Christians claiming credit. And what churches that profited from slavery and conquest owe today, in confession, restitution and repair, is a question many of them are still answering.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to read the Bible's slavery laws: as a permission God gave because of hardness of heart, with creation as the measure, as Jesus said of divorce; as the first steps of a movement the gospel would carry further; or as texts whose difficulty should simply be confessed. They differ on what repair the churches owe for their part in slavery and conquest. What all confess is that every human being is made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27, BSB), that in Christ "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female" (Galatians 3:28, BSB), and that the church that once blessed the trade in human beings was wrong and must say so.
