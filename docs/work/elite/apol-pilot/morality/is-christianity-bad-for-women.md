# The Bible tells wives to submit and women to keep silent, and the church kept women down for centuries. Is Christianity bad for women?

## The short answer

Christians have often been bad for women, and they have used the Bible to do it. That has to be said first. Great teachers of the church wrote of women as the lesser sex, and verses about submission have been used to excuse control and abuse. But the faith itself teaches that women bear God's image as fully as men do. Jesus taught women, relied on them, and sent a woman to announce his resurrection. Paul named women as his fellow workers. Christians still disagree about what the passages on marriage and church leadership ask of them today, and this page sets out the two main readings at equal strength and says where its author lands. Both reject contempt and control, and so does every text that has been bent to excuse them. If you are being hurt, the Find Help page (/help) lists places to turn.

## The objection at its strongest

Elizabeth Cady Stanton put the charge in 1895, in the introduction to *The Woman's Bible*, and it has seldom been put more sharply. When women of her century began to protest their place in society and in the church, she wrote, "they were referred to the Bible for an answer." And she summed up what they were shown: "The Bible teaches that woman brought sin and death into the world, that she precipitated the fall of the race, that she was arraigned before the judgment seat of Heaven, tried, condemned and sentenced. Marriage for her was to be a condition of bondage, maternity a period of suffering and anguish, and in silence and subjection, she was to play the role of a dependent on man's bounty for all her material wants," and for anything she wished to know she was told to ask her husband at home.

The texts she had in mind are easy to find. "Wives, submit to your husbands as to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:22, BSB). "Women are to be silent in the churches" (1 Corinthians 14:34, BSB). "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; she is to remain quiet. For Adam was formed first, and then Eve. And it was not Adam who was deceived, but the woman who was deceived and fell into transgression" (1 Timothy 2:12-14, BSB).

The church's teachers read them as you'd fear. Tertullian, who wrote around the year 200, told Christian women, "You are the devil's gateway." In the thirteenth century Thomas Aquinas, following Aristotle, wrote that "as regards the individual nature, woman is defective and misbegotten." He added that in nature's wider intention, which depends on God, she is no mistake. The first half still says what it says. At full strength the objection says these men weren't misreading their Bible. They were reading it. A faith with those verses will go on producing those teachers, and women have paid for it in every century.

## What is true in it

Most of the history. The quotations above are real, and they are a small sample. For much of church history, the Study Bible's note on 1 Timothy 2 records, the verse about Eve was taken to mean that women are more easily deceived than men; John Chrysostom preached it. Women who asked for more were told that the Bible had settled the matter. Stanton didn't invent that. She lived it.

It's also true, and worse, that the submission texts have been used against women in danger. The site's study of the family counts among the distortions that have done great harm inside the church one that "uses the language of headship or submission to excuse control and abuse." The site's notes on these passages answer it plainly, and this page repeats their words: "Nothing here licenses harshness or abuse. Nothing in this passage asks anyone to stay where they or their children are in danger. If you are in an abusive marriage, seek safety first. Talk to a pastor or a counselor you trust, or call a local domestic violence line. These notes are not legal advice. Abuse and other crimes are to be reported to the police and the proper authorities." And they add: "If you are being hurt in some other place, at home, at work or in a church, the same is true. Tell someone you trust and get help. The Find Help page on this site lists places to turn."

And the hard verses are really there. No honest answer makes them vanish, and Christians who say the Bible has only ever lifted women up haven't read Stanton or the women in their own pews.

## The evidence

Begin with Jesus, because his practice is the church's measure. He taught women as disciples. Mary of Bethany "sat at the Lord's feet listening to His message" (Luke 10:39, BSB), which is how a student sat before a teacher; Paul says he was educated "at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3, BSB). When her sister objected, Jesus said, "Mary has chosen the good portion, and it will not be taken away from her" (Luke 10:42, BSB). Women traveled with him and paid for his work: Luke names Mary Magdalene, Joanna and Susanna, "and many others," who "were ministering to them out of their own means" (Luke 8:3, BSB). His own disciples "were surprised that He was speaking with a woman" (John 4:27, BSB). When most of the men had fled, women stood watching at the cross (Mark 15:40-41). And all four Gospels say that women found the tomb empty. John says Mary Magdalene was sent to tell the others (John 20:17-18). The library's page on the empty tomb shows why that detail matters: in that world a woman's word counted for little as testimony, and no one inventing the story would have chosen her.

Then the first churches. At Pentecost Peter quotes the prophet Joel: "Your sons and daughters will prophesy" (Acts 2:17, BSB). Paul assumes women pray and prophesy aloud in worship (1 Corinthians 11:5). In Romans 16, the Study Bible's note counts, he names at least ten women. Phoebe carries a title that can mean servant or deacon, Prisca is his fellow worker and is named before her husband, and Junia is "outstanding among the apostles" (Romans 16:7, BSB), a phrase whose exact sense is debated. Early in the second century the Roman governor Pliny, investigating Christians, questioned under torture two enslaved women who, in Melmoth's translation, "were said to officiate in their religious rites."

Then the hard texts themselves, read whole. The sentence before "Wives, submit" is "Submit to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21, BSB). The command that follows it is longer and heavier, and it's for the husband: "Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave Himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25, BSB). Colossians adds, "do not be harsh with them" (Colossians 3:19, BSB), and Peter calls wives "fellow heirs of the gracious gift of life" (1 Peter 3:7, BSB). On the marriage bed Paul says the same thing of both: "The wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife" (1 Corinthians 7:4, BSB). And one sentence stands over all of it: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, BSB).

Last, the world these words entered. A letter survives from a working man named Hilarion, away in Alexandria, to a woman named Alis, who was expecting a child. It is dated 1 BC. One English translation reads: "if you bear a child and it is male, let it be; if it is female, cast it out." The first Christians refused that custom for daughters and sons alike, as the library's page on abortion and infant exposure sets out.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the record shows. It grants the history.

1. Christians have treated women as lesser, and have used the household codes and the passages on silence to justify contempt, exclusion and abuse.
2. The same New Testament shows Jesus teaching women as disciples and sending a woman as the first witness of his resurrection, shows women praying, prophesying and working beside the apostles, and lays on husbands a command of self-giving love that leaves no room for harshness.
3. Both main Christian readings of the disputed passages hold that women and men are equal in worth as bearers of God's image, and that no passage licenses domination or abuse.

So: the record shows that Christians have often been bad for women and have bent Scripture to that use; it doesn't show that the faith teaches that women are lesser, because on each of its main readings it teaches the opposite, and it condemns the abuse done in its name.

The weight sits on the third step. If "equal in worth" turns out to be a phrase that changes nothing, the objection stands. That is where it should be pressed.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that step 3 is a way of speaking, not a fact. "Equal in worth but different in role" has been said of every group that was being kept in its place. If one sex is always the head and the other always the helper, the critic says, then talk of equal dignity is decoration. And the egalitarian reading only proves the point: it rescues the Bible by explaining away what it says, and it appeared when the wider culture had already changed its mind.

Part of that should be granted. Equal dignity has often been a phrase. For centuries Christians said it and went on treating women as Tertullian and Aquinas wrote of them. A doctrine is known by what its holders do, and by that measure the church has a great deal to answer for.

But the reply moves too fast in two places. The first concerns what the texts ask. Whatever headship means, the only pattern the passage gives for it is a man who "gave Himself up." A husband who uses Ephesians 5 to get his way has left the text, on the complementarian reading as much as on the other, and complementarians say so: the Study Bible's note records their insistence that "headship is never license for harshness, domination, or abuse, and that the husband's calling to die to himself is the heavier one." Whether a difference of role can be free of any difference in worth is a fair question. Christians who answer yes point to Christ himself, who took the lower place without being the lesser person.

The second concerns the egalitarian reading. It doesn't rest on the modern world alone. It rests on things that were always in the text: women prophesying, Priscilla teaching Apollos (Acts 18:26), Phoebe and Junia, "male nor female." Egalitarians argue that Paul worked inside the household of his day while changing it from within, as he did with slavery, and that following the direction of the gospel is obedience, not escape. That argument may be wrong. It isn't a dodge.

## What remains unresolved

What Paul's word "head" means is still argued, authority or source. So is the rare verb in 1 Timothy 2:12, which may mean to exercise authority or to domineer, and whether Paul's rule there was for Ephesus or for every church. How much of Jesus' way with women the church kept, and why it lost what it lost, is still argued by historians, and it should trouble Christians more than it does.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ here, and the Study Bible's notes call it "a second-order question: it divides faithful churches but not the faith." The two readings are set out here as their holders state them.

Complementarians, in the words of the note on Ephesians 5, "hold that God created men and women equal in dignity and worth but with different roles." In marriage the husband leads in self-sacrificial love modeled on Christ, and in the church the teaching office of elder is kept for qualified men. Their strongest argument is that Paul grounds his words in creation and in Christ, not in Roman custom. The Danvers Statement of 1987 set out this view. Egalitarians "hold that men and women are equal in dignity and in role," that "submit to one another" governs the whole passage on marriage, and that Paul's limit in 1 Timothy addressed false teaching in Ephesus. Their strongest argument is the women Paul himself names and praises. Christians for Biblical Equality, founded the same year, set out this view. The site's study of church leadership records that most mainline Protestant churches opened ordination to women during the twentieth century, and that churches still differ on whether the office of elder or pastor is open to women.

The author of this site holds the complementarian reading: women and men are equal in worth and dignity, and the office of pastor or elder is for qualified men. He holds it as a second-order conviction, on which faithful churches differ, and the egalitarian case is given here as its own defenders make it. The strongest objection to that view, and one this page doesn't claim to have fully answered, is the one raised above: Paul names Phoebe, Prisca and Junia as his fellow workers and honors them by name, and a church that keeps one office for men has to show by what it does, and not by a phrase, that it holds women equal.

What all of them confess is stated in the notes: "the passage gives no cover for control or abuse; and wives and husbands alike stand before Christ as full members of his body." And a last word, for anyone who needs it. If you are being hurt, by a husband, in a church or anywhere else, it is not what God asks you to bear.
