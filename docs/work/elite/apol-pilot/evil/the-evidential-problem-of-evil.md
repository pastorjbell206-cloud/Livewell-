# Even if God and evil don't contradict each other, doesn't so much suffering that serves no purpose anyone can see make God unlikely?

## The short answer

This page weighs an argument; for someone who is hurting now, the pages on suffering (/wisdom/suffering) and on losing a child (/wisdom/losing-a-child), and Find Help (/help), are the better place to start. William Rowe's argument of 1979 is the strongest form of the problem of evil, and it hasn't been refuted. It reasons from suffering for which we can see no purpose to suffering that has none. The main reply, called skeptical theism, answers that we have little reason to expect to see the purposes of an infinite mind, so our not seeing them proves less than it seems. That reply has a cost, and critics press it hard: it can seem to make God's goodness untestable and his silence toward sufferers harder to explain. Christians don't claim to know why any particular suffering was permitted. They claim that God has shown his goodness elsewhere, in the cross, and that suffering will not have the last word.

## The objection at its strongest

Once the charge of contradiction had been met, the argument from evil took a more modest and more dangerous form. William Rowe, an atheist philosopher who called his own position friendly atheism, because he granted that some believers are reasonable in believing, gave it its classic form in "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism," published in the *American Philosophical Quarterly* in 1979.

He asked his readers to picture a single case. In a distant forest, lightning strikes a dead tree and starts a fire. A fawn is trapped in it, horribly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before it dies. No one sees it. So far as anyone can tell, its suffering leads to no good at all. In later essays Rowe set beside the fawn a case of terrible cruelty done to a child, so that the argument would carry both kinds of evil, the natural and the moral.

The argument itself is short, and Rowe pointed out that it is valid. Its first premise, in the site's words, is that there are instances of intense suffering which an all-powerful, all-knowing being could have prevented without losing some greater good or permitting some evil as bad or worse. Its second is that a wholly good, all-knowing being would prevent any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without losing such a good or permitting such an evil. Its conclusion is that there is no all-powerful, all-knowing, wholly good being. The second premise is usually treated, on both sides of the debate, as beyond dispute. Everything turns on the first, and Rowe didn't claim to prove it. He reasoned to it the way people reason every day: we can see no good the fawn's suffering serves, so it is reasonable to believe there is none. That is an inference from what we see to what is, and it is the kind of inference on which all ordinary knowledge depends.

Bart Ehrman, a New Testament scholar who left the Christian faith, made the problem of suffering the subject of a book for general readers, *God's Problem* (2008).

## What is true in it

Most of it. The suffering is real, and much of it looks pointless from where anyone stands. Rowe's inference is not a trick; if a doctor sees no sign of infection, he is often right to conclude there is none. And Christians have too often answered with a phrase, "God has a plan," as though naming a plan explained the cruelty done to a child. Scripture itself refuses that move. When Jesus' disciples asked of a man born blind, "who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2, BSB), he rejected the question's assumption, and when a tower fell in Siloam, he asked whether the eighteen who died were "more sinful than all the others living in Jerusalem," and answered, "No, I tell you" (Luke 13:4-5, BSB). The Bible doesn't explain particular evils, and neither should Christians.

## The evidence

The reply that has shaped the debate since 1984 came from the philosopher Stephen Wykstra. He asked when we are entitled to move from "I see no reason" to "there probably is no reason." His answer, in substance, is that we are entitled to it only when it is reasonable to believe that if there were such a reason, we would probably see it. An everyday comparison shows the point: a man who looks into his garage and sees no car may conclude there is no car there, because a car would be hard to miss. The same man looking across the room for a flea may not conclude there is none, because he wouldn't expect to see one even if it were there. Wykstra's own favored comparison was between God and the parent of a very young infant: the infant can't grasp the parent's reasons, and no one would expect it to. If the God of Christian belief exists, the distance between his understanding and ours is far greater than that, and the goods he might be weighing include goods that stretch beyond this life. So the fact that we can't see God's reasons for permitting the fawn's suffering is about what we should expect whether or not those reasons exist.

William Alston added analogies of his own: a beginner in physics can't follow the reasons for a great physicist's conclusion, and a novice can't see the point of a chess master's move. Skeptical theists, as they are called, don't claim to know God's reasons. They claim that the absence of visible reasons is weak evidence that there are none.

Scripture tells a story that fits this shape exactly. The reader of Job is shown a reason for Job's suffering in the first two chapters, a scene in the heavenly court, and Job is never told it. When God answers him, he gives no explanation at all, only questions: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Tell Me, if you have understanding" (Job 38:4, BSB). The Study Bible's note on Job 1 puts the lesson modestly: the book doesn't claim that this scene explains every human loss, but it teaches that there may be reasons hidden from sufferers. Isaiah gives the principle: "For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so My ways are higher than your ways and My thoughts than your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:9, BSB).

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about the strength of an inference, not a proof that God exists or a claim to know his reasons.

1. Rowe's argument rests on an inference from "we can see no good that justifies this suffering" to "there is no good that justifies it."
2. That inference is strong only if, were there such a good, we should expect to see it.
3. If God exists, the goods he might be weighing, and the ways they might depend on permitting this suffering, could well lie beyond what human beings are placed to see, so we should not confidently expect to see them.
4. The suffering we can see no reason for is therefore weaker evidence against God than it first appears, though it is still evidence, to be weighed with everything else a person has reason to believe about God.

So: the evidential argument is not refuted, but its central inference is much less secure than it looks, and a person may reasonably believe in God while granting that much suffering looks pointless.

The weight sits on the third step. If we had good reason to think that God's reasons, if any, would be visible to us, the inference would stand, and that is exactly what Rowe's defenders argue.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

Rowe's own answer, given in later years, is the strongest. A chess master has good reason not to explain his moves to his opponent, but God, if he exists, is not playing chess with our lives. A loving God, who knew that understanding the reason for a terrible evil would help a person bear it, would have strong reason to help sufferers understand, or at least to assure them that there is a reason. Many who suffer receive no such assurance. Critics add two further costs. If we can never judge what God's reasons might be, they say, we can never judge whether he is good, or whether his promises can be trusted, since a God whose reasons are wholly hidden might have reasons to deceive. And if any horror might serve a hidden good, why should anyone prevent one? The philosophers Michael Almeida and Graham Oppy pressed that last point in 2003: skeptical theism, they argued, threatens to paralyze the moral life.

These are real costs, and a fair page should say that skeptical theism has not escaped all of them. But each can be answered in part. The ignorance claimed is narrow. It is not ignorance of right and wrong, which Christians hold God has made known, and it doesn't license standing back from suffering: the same Scripture that hides God's reasons commands believers to "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15, BSB) and to do justice. Skeptical theists also point out that God may permit not an evil itself but the freedom in which evils become possible, as a parent permits the risk of failure for the sake of a child's growth; on that view no one is told to stand back from preventing a harm.

Rowe's question about assurance is the deepest, and Christians answer it with a claim, not an argument. They hold that God has not left sufferers without assurance but has given it in a form other than an explanation. "God proves His love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, BSB). The Son of God did not explain suffering from a distance; he underwent it, and cried, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Mark 15:34, BSB). If he was raised, then the goodness of God is not read off the balance sheet of the world, where it is hard to read, but learned from a particular history, which can be investigated on its own terms. That answer will not satisfy someone who doubts the history. It does mean that the Christian is not asking the sufferer to trust a reason he can't see, but a person who has carried the wound.

## What remains unresolved

Whether skeptical theism proves too much, by making all reasoning about God's purposes unreliable, is still argued. Whether the goods Christians name, such as freedom, the forming of character and communion with God, could justify the worst evils, or only lesser ones, is unresolved. Whether God's silence toward individual sufferers is itself evidence against him is a question the page on God's hiddenness takes up. And no one has an account of why this child or this fawn, and Christians should not pretend to one.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much of God's reasons can be known. Some, following John Hick's *Evil and the God of Love* (1966), offer a theodicy: the world is a place where persons can grow, through real dangers and real choices, toward goodness and communion with God. Others offer only a defence and hold, with the skeptical theists, that God's reasons are mostly beyond us. Marilyn McCord Adams argued in *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God* (1999), as the Internet Encyclopedia's article on the evidential problem sets out her view, that the worst evils, those that give a person reason to doubt whether their life could be a great good to them, can be overcome only by the good of intimate communion with God, a good beyond comparison with any evil in time, so that a person who comes to it would have reason to count their whole life a great good. The site takes no side among these approaches, which many Christians combine.

What all confess is narrower and firmer: God "does not willingly afflict or grieve the sons of men" (Lamentations 3:33, BSB); evil is real and his enemy; Christ suffered and rose; and nothing, as Paul wrote, "will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:39, BSB).
