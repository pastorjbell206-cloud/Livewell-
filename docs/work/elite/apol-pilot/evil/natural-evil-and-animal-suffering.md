# Earthquakes, cancer and the suffering of animals over ages before there were any people aren't caused by anyone's choice. How can a good God allow them?

## The short answer

This page weighs an argument; for someone facing illness or loss now, the pages on suffering (/wisdom/suffering) and on losing a child (/wisdom/losing-a-child), and Find Help (/help), are the better place to start. Natural evil is the harder half of the problem, because the appeal to human freedom doesn't reach it. Paul Draper argued in 1989 that the pattern of pain and pleasure in nature is what we would expect if nothing behind nature cared about its creatures, and much less what we would expect if a good God made it. Christians have given several answers: that a world of stable laws makes a shared and responsible life possible, that creatures made through long processes may be the cost of creatures at all, and that the present order is not the one God intends to keep. Each has a real cost. None explains any one disaster, and none should be offered to someone in it as the reason.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection is as old as the observation of nature. In Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779) the skeptic Philo surveys the whole of nature, its predators, plagues and famines, and concludes that it shows a blind Nature "pouring forth from her lap, without discernment or parental care, her maimed and abortive children." Charles Darwin, with more evidence in hand, wrote to the botanist Asa Gray on 22 May 1860: "I cannot persuade myself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of Caterpillars, or that a cat should play with mice." The Ichneumonidae are wasps whose young feed inside living caterpillars.

The philosopher Paul Draper gave the objection its most careful modern form in "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists," published in the journal *Noûs* in 1989. He didn't claim that suffering makes God impossible, or even that any single case of it is pointless. He compared two hypotheses. One is theism. The other, which he called the hypothesis of indifference, holds that neither the nature nor the condition of the creatures on earth that can feel is the result of kind or cruel actions by any persons other than human beings. Draper then looked at what we actually observe: pain and pleasure in moral agents and in animals, much of it tied closely to what helps creatures survive, and much of it serving no biological purpose anyone knows of. He argued that this whole pattern is just what the hypothesis of indifference would lead us to expect, and that it is much more surprising if a good and all-powerful God made the world. Facts that are more probable on one hypothesis than another count as evidence for the first.

The objection has a further edge. On the history most geologists and biologists accept, predators and parasites, disease and extinction were at work for hundreds of millions of years before any human being existed. If that is so, no human sin caused them, and the suffering in nature is not the aftermath of a fall but the means by which the living world was made. At full strength the objection says that the God of Christian belief, if he made the world this way, chose suffering as his method.

## What is true in it

Free will doesn't reach the fawn, the tsunami or the cancer ward, and a Christian who answers natural evil with the free-will defence alone has changed the subject. Draper's argument is a fair kind of argument, the kind scientists and historians use every day, and the Christian should answer it in the same coin. And some of what Christians have said about natural disasters is worse than silence. After the earthquake that destroyed Lisbon on All Saints' Day, 1 November 1755, John Wesley, whose hope for the animals this page cites below, asked in his *Serious Thoughts Occasioned by the Late Earthquake at Lisbon* whether God was "now making inquisition for blood," and answered that if so it was "not surprizing" he should begin with that city. Jesus refused that reading of a disaster in his own day. Of the eighteen killed when the tower in Siloam fell, he asked whether they were "more sinful than all the others living in Jerusalem," and answered, "No, I tell you" (Luke 13:4-5, BSB).

Scripture also doesn't hide the wildness of nature that the objection points to. The psalmist says, "The young lions roar for their prey and seek their food from God" (Psalm 104:21, BSB), and when God answers Job he speaks of the eagle whose "young ones feast on blood" (Job 39:30, BSB). The Bible claims the predators as God's creatures, and it gives no explanation of them.

## The evidence

Draper's argument is a comparison, and comparisons depend on what is being compared. It sets a bare hypothesis, a God who is good and all-powerful and nothing more, against indifference. But Christians have never held bare theism. They hold that the creation was made good, that it has been "subjected to futility, not by its own will, but because of the One who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay" (Romans 8:20-21, BSB), that "the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until the present time" (Romans 8:22, BSB), and that God means to reconcile "all things, whether things on earth or things in heaven" (Colossians 1:20, BSB). On that fuller account, a world in which suffering is real and is not yet ended is not the surprise Draper's comparison needs. Philosophers have also questioned his starting point. Draper argued that indifference is at least as likely as theism before the evidence is weighed, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on the problem of evil judges it far from clear why that should be so.

Christians then give several answers to why a good God might make or permit such a world. The first concerns laws. C. S. Creatures who can meet, act and depend on one another need a nature with fixed laws, and fixed laws don't bend to spare whoever is in the way. Richard Swinburne pressed the point in *Providence and the Problem of Evil* (1998): creatures can be responsible for one another only if they can learn what harms and what helps, and they learn it from the regular working of the world. The cost is plain. God could, it seems, intervene each time without abolishing regularity, and the answer reaches human suffering better than animal suffering.

The second concerns growth. John Hick's *Evil and the God of Love* (1966) argued that a world fit for making persons can't be a paradise of pleasure, but must hold real dangers and real failures. Its critics, the same encyclopedia article among them, answer that soul-making gives no account of the pain of animals, who are not being formed in virtue, and that much suffering crushes rather than forms.

The third belongs to Christians who accept the long history of life. Christopher Southgate defended in *The Groaning of Creation* (2008) what has been called an "only way" answer: that a creation unfolding through law and chance over long ages may be not only the best way but the only way for God to bring about the creatures he intends. Its cost is that no one can show it is the only way.

The fourth is the oldest. Many Christians hold that death and disorder entered the creation through human sin, reading Genesis 3 and Romans 8 together, and most of them hold that the earth is young. C. S. Lewis suggested in *The Problem of Pain* (1940), in his chapter on animal pain, that "some mighty created power had already been at work for ill on the material universe" before humanity appeared. Each of these takes the futility of Romans 8 as a corruption of what God made "very good" (Genesis 1:31, BSB); each must answer the evidence of the history of life or the objection that it explains by what can't be observed.

A fifth answer holds that animals feel pain without the inner awareness that makes it suffering. The site's argued answer on natural evil doesn't find it believable, and Jesus spoke of sparrows as creatures his Father attends to: "not one of them will fall to the ground apart from the will of your Father" (Matthew 10:29, BSB).

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about evidence: whether Draper's comparison shows that the suffering in nature favors indifference over the God Christians confess.

1. Draper's argument shows, at most, that the pattern of pain and pleasure in nature is more probable on indifference than on bare theism.
2. Christian belief is not bare theism: it holds that the creation, though good, is subjected to futility in hope, groaning toward a liberation God has promised, and that God has entered its suffering in Christ.
3. On that fuller account, a world whose suffering is real, unexplained case by case and not yet ended is what the account itself leads one to expect, so the pattern, though it still has to be weighed, counts against it less than against bare theism.

So: the suffering in nature is real evidence that a reasonable person must weigh, but it doesn't make Christian belief unreasonable, because it bears most heavily on a God Christians don't confess.

The weight sits on the second step. If the Christian account of a creation subjected to futility in hope is a later patch, added to rescue theism from the evidence, it should be discounted, and that is where the argument should be tested.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the second step is ad hoc. Any hypothesis can be made to fit any evidence by adding enough to it. If Christians may add a fall, a groaning creation and a promised restoration, then their account predicts nothing, and a theory that predicts nothing is confirmed by nothing. Indifference explains the parasite plainly. Theism explains it only with a story added for the purpose.

Part of that should be granted. The doctrine of a creation subjected to futility does not predict the parasite or the pattern of pain; it says only that the pattern is not God's last word. But it was not added to answer Darwin or Draper. Paul wrote Romans 8 in the first century, the Hebrew prophets had already promised a peace that would reach the animals, "The wolf and the lamb will feed together" (Isaiah 65:25, BSB), and John Wesley preached in the early 1780s, in his sermon on Romans 8, "The General Deliverance," that the animals would share in the restoration of all things. A hypothesis is ad hoc when it is invented to absorb an awkward fact. This one was the church's teaching about creation long before the fact was put to it.

A careful critic will keep score another way. Whatever its age, each doctrine added to bare theism makes the whole account less probable before any evidence is weighed, so what it gains by fitting the evidence it may lose in prior probability. That is a fair way to keep score, and it is why the argument above claims only that the suffering in nature counts against Christian belief less than against bare theism, not that it counts in its favor.

The reply also has a cost of its own. If nothing behind nature cares, the fawn's agony is a fact about nerves and fire, and the protest that it ought not to be so has nowhere to stand. That doesn't refute Draper, whose argument needs no moral view. It does mean that the choice is not between a world with suffering and a world without it. Indifference explains the pain well. It can't call it wrong, and it can't promise that it will end.

## What remains unresolved

Which answer, or which combination, is true is unresolved, and the answers depend partly on questions about the history of life on which Christians differ. Why a good God would make a world in which creatures are made through so much death is not explained by any of them. Whether animals themselves will share in the life to come is not said in Scripture, and Christians who hope it, with Wesley, should not say more than it says. And no answer explains the earthquake that killed these people or the cancer that took this child.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on the history of life and on how death relates to the fall, and the site takes no side among them, as the science area's pages also take none. Some hold that the earth is young and that animal death came into the world through human sin. Some hold that the earth is very old and read the futility of Romans 8 as the condition of a creation made good but not yet complete. Some hold that a fall of created spiritual powers marred the world before humanity. Many hold more than one answer together.

What all of them confess is what the Bible confesses: "The LORD is good to all; His compassion rests on all He has made" (Psalm 145:9, BSB); the creation is groaning and will be set free; the Son of God took on the flesh of this creation and died in it; and the One on the throne says, "Behold, I make all things new" (Revelation 21:5, BSB).
