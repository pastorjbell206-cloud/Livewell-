# If a loving God existed, wouldn't he make himself known to everyone who is open to him? Don't sincere unbelievers show that there is no such God?

## The short answer

This page weighs an argument; for someone who feels God's absence in grief or darkness now, the pages on suffering (/wisdom/suffering) and on losing a child (/wisdom/losing-a-child), and Find Help (/help), are the better place to start. J. L. Schellenberg argued in 1993 that a perfectly loving God would always make a relationship with himself possible for anyone capable of it who doesn't resist, and that the existence of sincere, nonresistant unbelievers shows there is no such God. It is one of the most serious arguments of the last generation, and Scripture knows its ache: "Truly You are a God who hides Himself" (Isaiah 45:15, BSB). Christians answer that the love God offers aims at trust freely given and a transformed life, which may be served by a God who can be found by seekers without being forced on anyone. That answer has limits, and it can't say why a particular person has not yet found him.

## The objection at its strongest

The philosopher J. L. Schellenberg set out the argument in *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason* (1993), and more than anyone else he has shaped the debate since. Its form is simple. There are people capable of a personal relationship with God who, through no fault of their own, don't believe that God exists. If there were a perfectly loving God, there would be no such people. So there is no perfectly loving God.

The second premise carries the argument, and Schellenberg defended it from what love is. A perfectly loving God would always be open to a relationship with each person he has made: he would never do anything, or leave anything undone, that would put the relationship out of reach of someone capable of it who wasn't resisting. But a person can't relate to God personally without believing that God exists. So a perfectly loving God would make sure that every capable person who isn't resisting him has the evidence that brings belief. In later work Schellenberg dropped the language of blame and spoke instead of resistance, which made it harder to answer the argument by suggesting that every unbeliever must be at fault. All it needs is one person, at one time, who is open to God and doesn't believe.

At full strength the objection is not that God's existence is unproved. It is that the very absence of proof is evidence against him. A loving parent doesn't hide from a child who is calling for her. If God is love, the world's long silence toward the honest seeker is what we should expect if there is no one there.

## What is true in it

There are honest unbelievers, and Christians should say so. Many people have sought God for years, prayed, read, asked and waited, and have not come to believe. Christians have sometimes answered them by reading Paul's words about God's "eternal power and divine nature" being "clearly seen" (Romans 1:20, BSB) as a verdict that every unbeliever is hiding from what he knows. Paul was diagnosing the human race before God, not reading the heart of the person across the table, and no Christian is in a position to do that.

Christians should also concede that hiddenness is not only a skeptic's complaint. It is a believer's experience. The psalmist prays, "How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever? How long will You hide Your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1, BSB), and Psalm 88 asks, "Why, O LORD, do You reject me? Why do You hide Your face from me?" (Psalm 88:14, BSB) and ends without an answer. The Study Bible's note on Psalm 88 calls it a gift to sufferers, a prayer that brings even despair to God without first tidying it up. Jesus himself prayed the first line of Psalm 22 from the cross. The church has words for the experience, the dark night of the soul among them. A faith with these prayers in its Scripture can't treat the hiddenness of God as a trick question.

## The evidence

Pascal saw the problem three centuries before Schellenberg and built his apology around it. In the *Pensées*, published in 1670 after his death, he wrote that God "has willed to make Himself quite recognisable" by those who seek him, and "to appear openly to those who seek Him with all their heart, and to be hidden from those who flee from Him with all their heart," so that "There is enough light for those who only desire to see, and enough obscurity for those who have a contrary disposition" (*Pensées* 430, in W. F. Trotter's translation). Pascal's point is not that God is absent. It is that God has given signs fitted to a relationship that must be sought and chosen, not compelled.

Christian philosophers since Schellenberg have filled out reasons of that kind, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on the subject sets them out. Some argue that evidence so overwhelming that no one could doubt would make faith a matter of prudence, or would coerce the response it was meant to invite; Michael Murray pressed this in "Coercion and the Hiddenness of God" (1993), and John Hick had argued in 1966 that a world in which God was obvious would leave no room for persons to grow freely toward him. Others argue that a person's openness might rest on motives God means to deepen, such as fear or a wish for approval, so that belief that came at once would come for the wrong reasons; Daniel Howard-Snyder developed this. Kierkegaard, in his *Philosophical Fragments* of 1844, warned of the believer who thinks he has God understood and grows complacent. Paul Moser has argued that God's self-revelation aims at the transformation of the person, not merely at a new belief, and may be held back from someone not yet willing to be changed by it.

The encyclopedia article adds two points worth keeping. No single explanation probably covers every case, and several might together explain what none explains alone. And it may be very hard for anyone, believer or unbeliever, to know of a particular person whether he is truly nonresistant, or what is happening between that person and God over the whole of a life. Scripture describes the seeking as long and the finding as promised: "You will seek Me and find Me when you search for Me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:13, BSB), and God "is not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:27, BSB).

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument against one premise: it asks whether perfect love must do what Schellenberg's second premise says it must.

1. Schellenberg's argument holds only if a perfectly loving God would make sure that every capable, nonresistant person believes in him at every time.
2. The relationship Christians say God offers is not belief that he exists but trust, love and a changed life, freely given and growing over a lifetime, and a God who aims at that may have reasons to let belief come in its own time and through seeking rather than at once and to everyone.
3. No one is placed to judge, of a particular person, whether he is nonresistant in the sense the argument needs, or how God is dealing with him across the whole of his life.

So: the argument from hiddenness is serious, and it shows that God's ways with seekers are not what we would have chosen; but its central premise is open to reasonable doubt, and it doesn't show that a perfectly loving God is impossible.

The weight sits on the second step. If every good that hiddenness might serve could be had as well within a relationship with God that began at once, the step would fail, and that is exactly Schellenberg's reply.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

Schellenberg answers that every good the explanations name, freedom, the right motives, humility, growth, can be had inside a relationship with God, and better there, since a relationship with a perfectly loving God is the greatest good a person can have. A God who withheld it for the sake of a lesser good would not be perfectly loving. Critics add that the explanations seem to fit some people but not others, such as a child who has never resisted anything, or whole peoples who lived and died without hearing of the God of Israel.

That reply is strong, and its first part is not fully answered. Christians answer in part by questioning its picture of relationship. A relationship with God, on the Christian account, is not the same as belief that God exists; "anyone who approaches Him must believe that He exists and that He rewards those who earnestly seek Him" (Hebrews 11:6, BSB), but belief is the door, not the room, and a person can hold the belief and refuse the relationship. If the relationship God seeks is the trust of a person whose whole life is being changed, a love that waits, or speaks in ways that invite rather than compel, need not be a lesser love.

And Christians answer with a claim about where God has chosen to be found. They hold that God did not stay hidden but came near in a way that could be missed. Isaiah describes the servant of the LORD as one "despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief. Like one from whom men hide their faces" (Isaiah 53:3, BSB). Jesus told Philip, "Anyone who has seen Me has seen the Father" (John 14:9, BSB), and was crucified by people who did not see it. Martin Luther's Heidelberg Disputation of 1518 set the theologian of the cross, who knows God hidden in suffering and the cross, against the theologian of glory, who looks for him in visible power and works, as the Study Bible's note on Mark 8 also records. On that view God's hiddenness is not only his absence. It is the manner of his coming, and it asks to be sought.

As for those who never heard, the site's argued answer on that question leaves open what the Bible leaves open, and this page will not say more of any person's standing before God than Scripture does.

## What remains unresolved

Why some who seek do not find, or find only late, is not explained, and Christians who claim to know in a particular case claim too much. Whether there are nonresistant unbelievers at all is disputed by philosophers on both sides, and no one can settle it by looking into another person's heart. Whether God's dealings with a person end at death is a question Christians answer differently, and the page on those who never heard sets out their views. And the believer's experience of God's absence, in the psalms and in the dark night, remains a mystery the church prays through rather than explains.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to describe unbelief. Many in the Reformed tradition read Romans 1 to teach that every person has some knowledge of God, however suppressed, so that no unbelief is wholly innocent, though they agree that no one may judge another's heart. Others hold that there are sincere seekers who have not yet found, and that God's time is not ours. They differ too on what God may do for those who die without hearing of Christ, and the site's answer on that question takes no side. The site takes no side here either.

What all confess is what the psalmist and Paul confess: that God is not far from any of us, that he has made himself known in Jesus Christ, and that nothing, "neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:39, BSB).
