# Samuel and Kings tell the same history as Chronicles, but their numbers often disagree: 800,000 soldiers or 1,100,000, seven hundred charioteers or seven thousand, a king of twenty-two or forty-two. If the Bible can't keep its numbers straight, why trust it on anything?

## The short answer

The differences are real, and some of them are larger than most readers know, because modern translations, the Berean Standard Bible among them, already smooth several of them by following a parallel passage or an ancient translation. In the standard Hebrew text, Ahaziah becomes king at forty-two in Chronicles, older than his father was at his death, and at twenty-two in Kings; Solomon has forty thousand stalls in Kings and four thousand in Chronicles. Many of these differences arose in copying: numbers were easy to miscopy, the ancient Greek translation often reads differently at exactly these places, and some Hebrew readings are impossible on the books' own figures. Others may reflect the Chronicler's sources or purposes, and some have no agreed explanation. None of them touches what these books were written to teach, and they show that the Chronicler wrote with the older books open, not hiding them.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection is old and plain. Thomas Paine, in the second part of *The Age of Reason* (1795), said he would use the books of Kings and Chronicles chiefly by "comparing them with each other, and with other parts of the Bible, to show the confusion, contradiction, and cruelty in this pretended word of God." Every reader can make the comparison, because Chronicles retells much of Samuel and Kings, often word for word, and then gives a different number.

Take one story, David's census. In Samuel, "Joab reported to the king the total number of the troops. In Israel there were 800,000 men of valor who drew the sword, and in Judah there were 500,000" (2 Samuel 24:9, BSB). In Chronicles, "In all Israel there were 1,100,000 men who drew the sword, including 470,000 in Judah" (1 Chronicles 21:5, BSB). The prophet Gad offers David a choice of punishments, and in the Hebrew text of Samuel the first is seven years of famine, where Chronicles has three (1 Chronicles 21:12). David pays for the threshing floor "fifty shekels of silver" in Samuel (2 Samuel 24:24, BSB) and "six hundred shekels of gold for the site" in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 21:25, BSB). Even the instigator differs: in Samuel the LORD "stirred up David against them" (2 Samuel 24:1, BSB), and in Chronicles "Satan rose up against Israel and incited David" (1 Chronicles 21:1, BSB).

The pattern runs through the books. After the war with the Arameans, "David killed seven hundred charioteers" in Samuel (2 Samuel 10:18, BSB) and "seven thousand of their charioteers" in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 19:18, BSB). In the Hebrew of Kings, Solomon has forty thousand stalls for his chariot horses (1 Kings 4:26); in Chronicles, four thousand (2 Chronicles 9:25). Kings says Ahaziah "was twenty-two years old when he became king" (2 Kings 8:26, BSB); the Hebrew of Chronicles says forty-two (2 Chronicles 22:2), though his father Jehoram "was thirty-two years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem eight years" (2 Chronicles 21:20, BSB), and so died at forty. Kings says Jehoiachin "was eighteen years old when he became king" (2 Kings 24:8, BSB); the Hebrew of Chronicles says eight (2 Chronicles 36:9). And in the Hebrew of 2 Samuel 21:19, a man named Elhanan of Bethlehem kills "Goliath the Gittite," though 1 Samuel 17 gives Goliath to David, and Chronicles says Elhanan killed "Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite" (1 Chronicles 20:5, BSB).

Put at full strength, the objection says that a book which cannot agree with itself on how many fought, how old a king was or who killed Goliath is a human record with the ordinary failings of human records, and that the claim that God guarded it from error has been refuted by its own pages.

## What is true in it

The differences are in the text, and a Christian should say so before saying anything else. Some can't both be right: Ahaziah can't have been both twenty-two and forty-two when he began to reign, and on Chronicles' own figures forty-two is impossible. A reader should also know that the English Bible in his hand has already made choices here. The BSB prints three years of famine in 2 Samuel 24:13, "foot soldiers" in 2 Samuel 10:18 where the Hebrew has horsemen, four thousand stalls in 1 Kings 4:26, twenty-two years in 2 Chronicles 22:2 and eighteen in 2 Chronicles 36:9, each following a parallel passage or an ancient translation rather than the standard Hebrew text, and it supplies "the brother of" in 2 Samuel 21:19 from Chronicles. The Study Bible's notes on 1 Kings 4, 2 Chronicles 22 and 2 Samuel 21 say so. A reader who later discovers those choices for himself, without having been told, will reasonably feel that something was kept from him.

## The evidence

Start with how these books relate. The Chronicler wrote after the exile, most scholars think in the Persian period, and he drew heavily on Samuel and Kings; the Study Bible's introduction to 2 Chronicles notes that he used them in a form sometimes closer to Hebrew manuscripts found among the Dead Sea Scrolls than to the traditional Hebrew text. The best-preserved Samuel scroll from Qumran often agrees with the Greek translation against the standard Hebrew text, a sign that the book circulated in more than one textual form before the time of Christ. So when Samuel and Chronicles differ, three things may be at work: differences already present in the copies the Chronicler used, changes made in copying after him, and choices the Chronicler made himself.

The textual evidence points to copying in several of the cases above. The Greek translation, in the form L. C. L. Brenton translated in the nineteenth century, reads three years of famine in 2 Samuel 24:13, agreeing with Chronicles against the Hebrew of Samuel; it reads twenty, not forty-two, for Ahaziah; and it agrees with the Hebrew in giving Jehoiachin eight years, which shows how early that reading entered the tradition. Where one reading is impossible, as with Ahaziah, and the ancient witnesses divide, a copying error is the plainest explanation, and the notes on these chapters say most scholars regard forty-two as one. Numbers were especially easy to get wrong. A single word's difference makes eight into eighteen or four thousand into forty thousand, and nothing in the surrounding sentence warns a copyist that he has slipped.

The Goliath verse shows how a copying error can work with words as well as numbers. In 2 Samuel 21:19 the Hebrew calls Elhanan's father Jaare-oregim and Elhanan "the Bethlehemite," *bet hallahmi*; Chronicles calls the father Jair and names the man Elhanan killed, Lahmi. The Hebrew word *oregim*, weavers, appears again at the end of the same verse, in "a weaver's beam." Many scholars think the Samuel text has been damaged at both points, and that Chronicles preserves the better reading. Others think Samuel preserves an older tradition the Chronicler smoothed, and some suggest that Goliath was a name borne by more than one warrior. The note on 1 Chronicles 20 sets out all three.

Not every difference looks like a copying slip, and other explanations have been offered for those. The Hebrew word usually translated "thousand," *elef*, can sometimes mean a military unit, as the notes on 1 Chronicles 19 and the introduction to 2 Chronicles record, and some large numbers may originally have counted units rather than men. In the census, Chronicles says Joab "did not include Levi and Benjamin in the count" (1 Chronicles 21:6, BSB), so harmonizers propose that the two books report different counts; in the purchase, that Samuel prices the floor and oxen and Chronicles the whole site that would hold the temple. Some differences may be the Chronicler's own emphasis: the note on 1 Chronicles 21 suggests he stressed the value of the temple site, and the introduction to 2 Chronicles allows that some of the book's numbers may be deliberate grandeur. And the change from the LORD to Satan in the census story is a theological difference, not a numerical one, which the Study Bible's notes on both chapters treat at length: many readers hold that God permitted an adversary's temptation as part of a judgment, much as in Job 1 and 2.

Finally, the larger history these books tell can often be checked, and it holds where it can be. The Assyrian king Shalmaneser III recorded Jehu's tribute and his own wars with Hazael of Damascus; an Aramaic inscription found at Tel Dan in the 1990s names the "house of David"; Babylonian ration tablets list oil for Jehoiachin, king of Judah, in Babylon. The numbers are the books' weakest point. The kings, the wars and the exile are not.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what differing numbers in parallel accounts show.

1. Several numbers in Samuel and Kings differ from their parallels in Chronicles, and in some cases one reading is impossible on the books' own figures.
2. Numbers were especially vulnerable in copying, the ancient Greek translation and the Dead Sea Scrolls show that these books circulated in more than one form, and the witnesses divide at several of the very places where the parallels differ; so many of the differences are best explained as arising in transmission, though some may come from the Chronicler's sources or purposes, and some remain unexplained.
3. The differences concern figures and details, not the history the books narrate, which outside records confirm where they reach, and not what the books were written to teach.

So: the numbers show that these books were copied by human hands and retold with purpose, and a fair reader should say so plainly, but they don't show that the history is invented or that what the books teach is untrue.

The weight sits on the second step. If the differences could not be traced to the way the books were transmitted and retold, they would weigh more heavily against the books' reliability, and that is where a reader should test the argument.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that "a copyist did it" can explain anything. Whenever the Bible contradicts itself, the defender blames a scribe, and since the original manuscripts no longer exist, the claim can never be tested. An explanation that would fit any evidence at all explains nothing.

That warning is fair, and Christians have sometimes used the explanation too freely. But here it is not an escape from the evidence; it comes from the evidence. The ancient versions differ at these very places. Some readings are impossible on the books' own figures. Translators of every conviction, working from the same manuscripts, already print the other reading. That is how textual critics reason about Homer or Caesar, and it is how they reason here. Where no such evidence exists, as with the 800,000 and the 1,100,000, honesty says the difference is unexplained, and this page says so. As for trusting the Bible on anything: no historian trusts or distrusts an ancient book wholesale. The books of Kings name rulers whom Assyrian and Aramaic inscriptions also name, and they tell of an exile that Babylonian tablets confirm. A source that slips in its figures but holds where it can be checked is a source to be read carefully, not discarded.

## What remains unresolved

Several differences have no agreed explanation: the census totals, the price of the threshing floor, the seven hundred or seven thousand charioteers and whether Samuel's horsemen were the same troops as Chronicles' foot soldiers. Whether *elef* means a unit in particular passages is argued. Whether 2 Samuel 21:19 has been damaged, preserves another tradition or names a second Goliath is open. The chronology of the kings of Israel and Judah, with its co-regencies and different ways of counting reigns, has several competing reconstructions, as the introduction to 1 Kings records. And how much of the Chronicler's difference is his sources, his copyists or his own design can't always be told apart.

## Where Christians differ

Christians answer these passages according to how they confess Scripture's truthfulness, and the site takes no side among them. Christians who hold to inerrancy, in the form the Chicago Statement of 1978 gave it, confess that Scripture in its original writings is without error in all that it affirms. They hold that differences arising in copying do not touch that confession, since, in the Statement's tenth article, "inspiration, strictly speaking, applies only to the autographic text of Scripture," and "copies and translations of Scripture are the Word of God to the extent that they faithfully represent the original"; the Statement also denies that inerrancy is negated by "the use of hyperbole and round numbers." Christians who hold Scripture to be infallible in what God gave it to teach, the way of salvation and of life with him, hold that the books teach truly what God was doing with Israel and its kings whether or not every figure in them was originally exact. Both confess with the Nicene Creed that the Holy Spirit "spake by the prophets," and both receive Scripture as the church's final authority for faith and life. And all of them read the census story to its end, where David says, "I will not offer to the LORD my God burnt offerings that cost me nothing" (2 Samuel 24:24, BSB), and buys the ground on which the temple would stand.
