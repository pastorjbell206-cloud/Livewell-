# Matthew and Luke give Jesus two different family trees, with different fathers for Joseph. Doesn't that show at least one of them was made up?

## The short answer

The two lists really are different, and the church has known it since antiquity. Matthew runs from David through Solomon and the kings of Judah to "Jacob the father of Joseph"; Luke runs from David through another son, Nathan, to "Joseph, the son of Heli," and the two lines meet only at Shealtiel and Zerubbabel between David and Joseph. The oldest explanation, from Julius Africanus early in the third century, proposed a levirate marriage, and Africanus himself admitted he could offer it without proof. Others hold that Luke gives Mary's line, or that Matthew gives the royal succession and Luke the natural descent. Each solution has a real weakness, and none is established. What the lists don't do is contradict each other on the claim they were written to make: that Jesus, through Joseph, his legal father, was of the house of David, a claim Paul had already made in the 50s.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection is as old as any in this area. The Emperor Julian, in the fourth century, put it to Christians as a plain case of the evangelists contradicting each other, as Jerome recorded in his commentary on Matthew, which speaks of Julian's charge *de dissonantia Evangelistarum*, "concerning the disagreement of the evangelists." David Friedrich Strauss pressed it in 1835 as proof that neither list was history, and Raymond Brown, in *The Birth of the Messiah* (1977), concluded that the two cannot be fully harmonized. Bart Ehrman sets it out for general readers in *Jesus, Interrupted* (2009).

Read the lists side by side and the trouble starts at once. Matthew says "Jacob the father of Joseph, the husband of Mary" (Matthew 1:16, BSB); Luke says Jesus "was regarded as the son of Joseph, the son of Heli, the son of Matthat" (Luke 3:23-24, BSB). From David on, Matthew follows Solomon (Matthew 1:6) and Luke follows Nathan (Luke 3:31). The lines touch at Shealtiel and Zerubbabel and part again, and even there they disagree: Matthew, with 1 Chronicles 3:17, makes Shealtiel the son of Jeconiah, Luke makes him "the son of Neri" (Luke 3:27, BSB), and Chronicles itself calls Zerubbabel a son of Shealtiel's brother Pedaiah (1 Chronicles 3:19). Counting the names between David and Jesus, Matthew has twenty-six and Luke forty-one.

Matthew's list also has problems of its own. He says, "In all, then, there were fourteen generations from Abraham to David, fourteen from David to the exile to Babylon, and fourteen from the exile to the Christ" (Matthew 1:17, BSB). But his line from Joram to Uzziah leaves out three kings whom 1 Chronicles 3:11-12 names, Ahaziah, Joash and Amaziah, and his last group has thirteen generations from Jeconiah to Joseph, as Jerome already counted, so that Jesus himself has to be the fourteenth. And both lists trace the line through Joseph, though both Gospels say Joseph was not Jesus' father by nature, a point Ehrman presses: both are, in fact, genealogies of Joseph.

Put at full strength, the objection says this. Two writers who had a real family record would not give two different grandfathers and fifteen generations' difference. Each built a list to make a theological point, Matthew a royal pattern of three fourteens, Luke a line reaching back to Adam, and at least one of the lists, perhaps both, is a construction rather than a record.

## What is true in it

The differences are real and can't be waved away. Jacob and Heli are different names, Solomon and Nathan different sons, and the gap in length is large. Matthew's pattern does leave out kings who are named elsewhere in Scripture, and he tells the reader so in his own way, since a pattern of fourteens is plainly arranged. Both evangelists did shape their lists for a purpose: Matthew opens his Gospel with "the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1, BSB), and Luke ends his with "the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38, BSB).

Christians have sometimes answered too quickly. Eusebius, who preserved the oldest explanation, said that "every believer, in ignorance of the truth, has been zealous to invent some explanation which shall harmonize the two passages" (*Church History* 1.7.1, in A. C. McGiffert's translation). He was describing an old temptation, and it hasn't gone away. A reader who has been told that the problem was solved long ago deserves to know that the man who offered the first solution did not claim that much for it.

## The evidence

Begin with what ancient genealogies were for. They mattered in Second Temple Judaism. Priests had to prove their descent to serve (Ezra 2:61-63), and Josephus opens his *Life* by setting out his own priestly pedigree as he found it in the public registers. Davidic descent was remembered too, and could be dangerous: Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, tells of grandsons of Jesus' brother Jude brought before the emperor Domitian as descendants of David. Genealogies in Scripture are also often selective. Matthew's omission of three kings is the kind of compression ancient lists made without apology, and Luke's second Cainan (Luke 3:36) comes from the Greek translation of Genesis, not the Hebrew, as the Study Bible's note on Luke 3 records.

Then the oldest solution. Julius Africanus, writing to one Aristides early in the third century, said that in Israel names were reckoned "either according to nature or according to law," and that the law of levirate marriage, by which a man raised up a child to the name of a brother who died childless (Deuteronomy 25:5-6), explained the two lists. His reconstruction is precise. A woman named Estha married Matthan, of Solomon's line, and bore Jacob; widowed, she married Melchi, of Nathan's line, and bore Heli. Heli died childless, Jacob married his widow, and Joseph was born, "his own son by nature, but by law the son of Eli." Africanus said the account came from Jesus' own relatives, the *desposyni*, who kept private records after Herod burned the public ones. And then he wrote, with real honesty: "Whether then the case stand thus or not no one could find a clearer explanation, according to my own opinion and that of every candid person. And let this suffice us, for, although we can urge no testimony in its support, we have nothing better or truer to offer. In any case the Gospel states the truth" (Eusebius, *Church History* 1.7.15). Augustine came to accept the explanation.

Its weakness is in Luke's own text. Africanus makes Melchi the father of Heli, but Luke, as it has come down to us, has "Heli, the son of Matthat, the son of Levi, the son of Melchi" (Luke 3:23-24, BSB), so his scheme needs two generations that Luke's text includes and his account leaves out. It may be that Africanus read a shorter text of Luke; no surviving manuscript supports that.

The second solution holds that Luke gives Mary's line: Heli was Mary's father, and Joseph is called his son as his son-in-law. It was proposed by Annius of Viterbo around 1490 and has been defended by many commentators since, Frédéric Godet among them, who pointed to the way Luke's Greek introduces Joseph's name. Its attraction is that it gives each evangelist one parent, and it lets Luke's line avoid Jeconiah, of whom Jeremiah said, "None of his descendants will prosper to sit on the throne of David" (Jeremiah 22:30, BSB). Its weakness is that Luke names Joseph, not Mary, and that Luke ties David's house to Joseph, not to Mary: Gabriel is sent "to a virgin pledged in marriage to a man named Joseph, who was of the house of David" (Luke 1:27, BSB), and Joseph goes to Bethlehem "since he was from the house and line of David" (Luke 2:4, BSB). Nothing in Luke says Mary was of David's line, and the Study Bible's note on Luke 3 traces the view to Annius, some fourteen centuries after Luke wrote.

The third, argued by J. Gresham Machen in *The Virgin Birth of Christ* (1930), holds that Matthew gives the line of royal succession, the heirs to David's throne, and Luke the actual descent of Joseph. It explains why Matthew's list runs through the kings. Its weakness is that it is a reconstruction too: Matthew's repeated "was the father of" reads naturally as descent, not succession, and the theory needs links that neither Gospel supplies.

The fourth is the critical view of Strauss, Brown and Joseph Fitzmyer, that the evangelists drew on different traditions and shaped them for their own ends. Its strength is that it doesn't force either list. Its weakness, as a verdict on history, is that it can't tell us which list, if either, is wrong, and it leaves untouched what both lists, written independently, share.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the disagreement shows and what it leaves standing.

1. Matthew and Luke give different lines from David to Joseph, and none of the proposed harmonizations is established by evidence; the oldest was offered by its own author as the best available explanation, without proof.
2. Writing independently of each other, both evangelists affirm that Jesus was of the house of David through Joseph, his legal father, and Paul had already called Jesus "a descendant of David according to the flesh" (Romans 1:3, BSB) in a letter written in the 50s.
3. Ancient genealogies were often selective and arranged for a purpose, so a list shaped to make a point is not for that reason invented, though it can't, for that reason, be read as a complete record.

So: the genealogies leave unresolved how the two lines relate, but their disagreement doesn't count against the claim both were written to make, that Jesus came from David's house.

The weight sits on the second step. If the Davidic claim were the evangelists' invention, the agreement of two independent lists would carry less weight, and that is where a reader should test the argument.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the agreement proves nothing, because the claim was what the movement needed. A messiah had to be a son of David, so the first Christians had every reason to give Jesus that descent, and two writers each inventing a pedigree would naturally agree on the one point they both needed while disagreeing on everything else. Paul's statement is no better: he was making the same theological claim, not consulting a family record.

There is force in that, and history can't prove a first-century pedigree; no one now can check the names. But the reply proves less than it seems. The claim of Davidic descent is in the earliest Christian writing we have, Paul's letter to the Romans, within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion, and in two independent Gospels, and it was made about a family that was still known. Jesus' brother James led the Jerusalem church until around 62, and the tradition Hegesippus preserved shows the family remembered as David's descendants a generation later still. A claim of royal descent made about a living family, in a society where pedigrees mattered and could be disputed, was open to contradiction in a way a claim about a distant hero isn't. And the very disagreement of the lists tells against coordinated invention: writers who set out together to manufacture a pedigree would have agreed on Joseph's father.

## What remains unresolved

How the two lists relate is unresolved, and may stay so. Whether Luke's list is Mary's, Joseph's natural line or something else is argued among scholars who share a high view of Scripture as well as among those who don't. Why Luke's line has so many more generations is unexplained. Whether Jeremiah's word against Jeconiah touches Jesus is answered in different ways: Haggai later calls Jeconiah's grandson Zerubbabel the LORD's signet ring (Haggai 2:23), reversing the image Jeremiah had used, and the Study Bible's note on Matthew 1 records a rabbinic tradition that the decree was set aside. And whether Mary was of David's house is a question the New Testament doesn't answer directly.

## Where Christians differ

Christians answer this passage according to how they confess Scripture's truthfulness, and the site takes no side among them. Christians who hold to inerrancy, in the form the Chicago Statement of 1978 gave it, confess that Scripture in its original writings is without error in all that it affirms. They hold that both genealogies are true records of what they set out to give, favor one of the solutions above or hold the question open, and note that the Statement denies that inerrancy is negated by "the topical arrangement of material" or "variant selections of material in parallel accounts." Christians who hold Scripture to be infallible in what God gave it to teach, the way of salvation and of life with him, hold that the genealogies teach truly that Jesus is the promised son of David and son of Abraham, whether or not every link can be reconciled. Both confess with the Nicene Creed that the Holy Spirit "spake by the prophets," and both receive Scripture as the church's final authority for faith and life. All of them confess, with Matthew and Luke alike, that the child Mary bore was conceived by the Holy Spirit, and that God kept his promise to David in him.
