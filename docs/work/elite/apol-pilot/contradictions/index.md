# Contradictions and hard passages

Anyone who reads the Bible slowly, with two accounts of the same event open side by side, will find places where they don't agree. Luke dates Jesus' birth by a census that Josephus puts a decade after Herod's death. Matthew and Luke give Jesus two different grandfathers. Matthew says Judas hanged himself; Acts says he fell and burst open. John seems to put the crucifixion on a different day from the other three Gospels, and at a different hour from Mark. Samuel and Chronicles, telling the same reigns, give different numbers for the same armies. These aren't inventions of skeptics, and they weren't discovered in the modern age. Christians noticed them from the start: Julius Africanus wrote about the genealogies early in the third century, and Augustine wrote a whole book on the agreement of the Gospels around 400.

These pages take the best-known difficulties one at a time. Each sets out the problem in the words of the text itself, from the Berean Standard Bible the site uses, and at the strength its best critics give it. Each sets out the main solutions that have been proposed, with the real weakness of each, and says plainly where a harmonization is strained and where a question is still open. None of them pretends that every difficulty has been solved.

The pages also take no side between the ways Christians confess Scripture's truthfulness. Some hold to inerrancy, that Scripture in its original writings is without error in all that it affirms; others hold that Scripture is infallible in what God gave it to teach, the way of salvation and of life with him, without requiring that every number or date in its histories be harmonized. Each page says what each holds, in words its holders would sign, and what all confess together: that God has spoken in Scripture, and that it is the church's final authority for faith and life. The difficulties matter, and they deserve honest answers. They are also, in nearly every case, about the edges of a story whose center the accounts share.

## The questions

1. **Luke says Jesus was born during a census taken while Quirinius governed Syria. Josephus dates that census to AD 6, about ten years after Herod died. Didn't Luke get it wrong?** What Luke says and what Josephus dates, the governors of Syria, Justin and Tertullian, the five proposed solutions with the weakness of each, and why the question is still open.
2. **Matthew and Luke give Jesus two different family trees, with different fathers for Joseph. Doesn't that show at least one of them was made up?** Both lists set side by side, Julian's charge and Jerome's count, Africanus's levirate explanation in his own words with its weakness, Mary's line and the royal line, and what both lists affirm.

## Words these pages use

- **Inerrancy**: the conviction that Scripture in its original writings is without error in all that it affirms; its best-known modern statement is the Chicago Statement of 1978.
- **Infallibility**: as used here, the conviction that Scripture never fails in what God gave it to teach, the way of salvation and of life with him.
- **Harmonization**: an explanation that shows how two accounts that seem to conflict can both be true.
- **Parallel account**: a second telling of the same event, as Chronicles retells Samuel and Kings, or as the four Gospels tell the passion.
- **Masoretic text**: the standard Hebrew text of the Old Testament, preserved by Jewish scribes; the Leningrad Codex of 1008 is its oldest complete copy.
- **Septuagint**: the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures made by Jews before the time of Jesus, which sometimes preserves a different reading.
