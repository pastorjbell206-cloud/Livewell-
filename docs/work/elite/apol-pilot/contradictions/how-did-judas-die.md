# Matthew and Acts tell Judas's death differently: in one he takes his own life, in the other he falls headlong in a field he had bought. Can both be true?

## The short answer

Perhaps, but not obviously, and the honest answer has to say both. Matthew says Judas threw the silver into the temple and took his own life, and the chief priests used the money to buy a potter's field, called the Field of Blood. Acts says that "with the reward for his wickedness Judas bought a field; there he fell headlong and burst open," and the field was called Akeldama, Field of Blood. The accounts differ on how he died, who bought the field and why it got its name. The common harmonization, that the body fell after the death Matthew describes, is possible but adds what neither text says, and a third account from Papias shows the story was told more than one way early on. What the two accounts share is more solid: a sudden and shameful death, a field near Jerusalem bought with the betrayal money, and a name for it that people there still used.

## The objection at its strongest

Bart Ehrman sets it out as a contradiction in *Jesus, Interrupted* (2009), and it needs no specialist to see it. Read the two passages one after the other.

In Matthew, Judas, "filled with remorse," brings the thirty pieces of silver back to the chief priests and elders. "So Judas threw the silver into the temple and left. Then he went away and hanged himself" (Matthew 27:5, BSB). The priests won't put "blood money" into the treasury, so "they used the money to buy the potter's field as a burial place for foreigners. That is why it has been called the Field of Blood to this day" (Matthew 27:7-8, BSB).

In Acts, Peter tells the believers that Scripture had to be fulfilled concerning Judas, and Luke adds: "Now with the reward for his wickedness Judas bought a field; there he fell headlong and burst open in the middle, and all his intestines spilled out. This became known to all who lived in Jerusalem, so they called that field in their own language Akeldama, that is, Field of Blood" (Acts 1:18-19, BSB).

The differences are not one but three. In Matthew Judas takes his own life; in Acts he dies in a fall. In Matthew the priests buy the field; in Acts Judas buys it. In Matthew the field is the Field of Blood because it was bought with blood money; in Acts because Judas's blood was spilled on it. And Matthew's Judas gives the money back, while the Judas of Acts uses it. Matthew then adds a puzzle of his own: he says the purchase fulfilled "what was spoken through Jeremiah the prophet" (Matthew 27:9, BSB), but the words he quotes about thirty pieces of silver and the potter come mainly from Zechariah 11:12-13.

There is a third account too. Papias, early in the second century, told of a Judas whose body swelled until he could not pass where a chariot could, so that "he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out" (fragment 3, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation). Put at full strength, the objection says that three incompatible deaths for the same man, each shaped by a different writer's use of Scripture, are what legend looks like. The early church remembered that the betrayer came to a bad end; it didn't remember how.

## What is true in it

The differences are real, and a reader who compares the two passages without being told about them will see them at once. The harmonizations offered for them are possible, but each adds something the texts don't say. Both writers do read Judas's end through Scripture: Matthew through Zechariah and Jeremiah, Acts through two psalms, "May his place be deserted" and "May another take his position" (Acts 1:20, BSB). Scripture had another traitor who died the same way, Ahithophel, David's counselor who went over to Absalom (2 Samuel 17:23). And Papias's version shows that by the second century the story could be told with grotesque additions.

Christians have answered this badly when they have answered too confidently. Augustine set a better example. Facing the name of Jeremiah in Matthew 27:9, he rejected the easy answer that some copies leave the name out, since most copies, and the oldest Greek ones he had heard of, contain it, and he wrote that "it may have been the case, that when Matthew was engaged in composing his Gospel, the word Jeremiah occurred to his mind, in accordance with a familiar experience, instead of Zechariah" (*The Harmony of the Gospels* 3.7, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation). He went on to give his own theological reading of why the Spirit let it stand. One may disagree with that reading. His candor about the difficulty is worth keeping.

## The evidence

Start with the harmonizations, each with its weakness.

The most common, which the Study Bible's note on Acts 1 gives first, is that both accounts describe one end: Judas died as Matthew says, and his body afterward fell, as Acts describes. It's physically possible, and it lets each writer report the part of the event that served his telling. Its weakness is that Acts says nothing of the death Matthew describes, and a reader of Acts alone would take the fall to be the death. The harmonization supplies a link that neither author gives.

A second proposal concerns one Greek word. Some scholars have argued that Luke's *prēnēs genomenos*, which the BSB renders "he fell headlong," is a medical term for swelling, so that Acts would describe a body that swelled and burst. That would fit Papias's swelling Judas. Its weakness is that the ordinary sense of the word is "headlong" or "face down," and most translations keep it.

A third concerns the field. The priests, on Matthew's account, bought it with Judas's money, so it could be said in a loose and bitter sense that Judas acquired it: he paid for it with the price of a life. That is a fair reading of how ancient writers could speak of an act done with someone's money. Its weakness is the name. Matthew explains it by the blood money, Acts by the blood spilled on the ground, and harmonizers have to say that both explanations were current, which may be true but can't be shown.

The fourth is the view of many scholars, among them some who hold a high view of Scripture: that Matthew and Luke preserve two independent traditions about Judas's end, which agree on its essentials and differ in detail, and that harmonizing them isn't necessary. The note on Acts 1 sets out both this and the first view without choosing. Its strength is that it lets each text say what it says. Its weakness, for some Christians, is that it accepts a real difference in the history.

As for "Jeremiah," the note on Matthew 27 sets out the main proposals. Most modern commentators hold that Matthew combined Zechariah's thirty pieces of silver and the potter with Jeremiah's visits to the potter's house (Jeremiah 18 and 19) and his purchase of a field for silver (Jeremiah 32:6-15), and named the greater prophet, as Mark 1:2 names Isaiah for a quotation that joins Malachi and Isaiah. Others follow Augustine. The textual evidence is not in doubt: almost all manuscripts name Jeremiah, and editors agree it is what Matthew wrote.

Now set beside the differences what the two accounts share. Luke did not use Matthew's Gospel as a source, on the view most scholars hold, so the two accounts are probably independent. Yet both say that Judas died soon after the betrayal and before the church's public preaching began, that his death was violent and shameful, that the money paid for a field near Jerusalem, and that the field was known as the Field of Blood. Acts even gives its local Aramaic name, Akeldama, and says the story "became known to all who lived in Jerusalem." Matthew says the name was still used "to this day." Christian tradition since at least the fourth century has placed the field at the lower end of the Hinnom Valley, south of the city, where burial caves of the Roman period have been excavated.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what two differing accounts of one event can show.

1. Matthew and Acts give different accounts of how Judas died and of how the field came to be bought and named, and each harmonization adds details that neither text supplies.
2. The two accounts are probably independent of each other, yet they agree on a core: a sudden and shameful death soon after the betrayal, a field near Jerusalem bought with the betrayal money, and its local name, Field of Blood.
3. Independent accounts that diverge in detail around a shared core, tied to a place known to the first readers, are what historians usually find when a real event has been told more than once.

So: the differences are real and how Judas died may not be recoverable in detail, but the shared core points to a real event remembered in Jerusalem, and the differences don't show that the accounts are legend.

The weight sits on the second step. If the two accounts depend on each other, or if the shared core is only a stock motif, the argument loses most of its force, and a reader should test it there.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the shared core is exactly what legend would produce. Every tradition likes its traitors to die badly; Scripture itself tells of Ahithophel's end and of Herod, who "was eaten by worms and died" (Acts 12:23, BSB). A field with a grim name near Jerusalem would naturally attract a story. So agreement on a bad end and a bloody field proves nothing beyond the storytellers' shared instinct.

There is something to that, and the motif is real. But the reply has to explain more than a bad end. Both accounts tie Judas to a particular field bought with particular money, and both give it the same name; one gives that name in the local language and says it was known throughout the city. Stock motifs don't usually come with property and place names that first readers in Jerusalem could check. And the motif cuts the other way too: a legend built on Ahithophel would have given Judas one fitting death, not two different ones that later readers have struggled to fit together. Disagreement in detail is not what coordinated invention produces. It is what independent memory produces.

## What remains unresolved

How Judas died is not settled, and may never be. Whether the two accounts can be fully harmonized is argued among Christians who hold a high view of Scripture as well as between them and their critics. Whether *prēnēs* can mean "swollen" is a minority view that hasn't been ruled out. Why Matthew named Jeremiah has several answers and no agreed one. And how Papias's account relates to the other two is unknown, since it survives only in later quotation.

## Where Christians differ

Christians answer this passage according to how they confess Scripture's truthfulness, and the site takes no side among them. Christians who hold to inerrancy, in the form the Chicago Statement of 1978 gave it, confess that Scripture in its original writings is without error in all that it affirms. Most of them harmonize the two deaths, or hold that both accounts are true in what they affirm though we can't now say exactly how they fit, and they follow the Statement in denying that inerrancy is negated by "variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations." Christians who hold Scripture to be infallible in what God gave it to teach, the way of salvation and of life with him, are freer to say that Matthew and Luke preserve two traditions that differ in detail, and they hold that what both teach, the terrible end of a betrayal and God's purpose carried through it, is true. Both confess with the Nicene Creed that the Holy Spirit "spake by the prophets," and both receive Scripture as the church's final authority for faith and life. And all of them read Judas's story beside Peter's: two disciples who failed their Lord on the same night, and the risen Jesus who sought Peter out and gave him his work again (John 21:15-17).
