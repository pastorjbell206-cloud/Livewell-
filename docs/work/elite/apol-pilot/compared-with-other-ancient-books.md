# Christians say the New Testament is better preserved than Homer or Caesar. Is that true, and what does it prove?

## The short answer

It's true, and it proves less than it's often made to. Counted honestly, the New Testament survives in more manuscripts, in more languages, and closer in time to its writing than any work of classical Greek or Latin literature. The Iliad, the best-attested classical work, survives in about 1,750 manuscripts on a recent count, most of them papyri; the first six books of Tacitus's *Annals* survive in one medieval manuscript. But apologists have often told this story with stale figures and uneven counts, and evangelical textual critics have said so in print. More important, the comparison answers one question only. It shows that the New Testament's wording can be known about as well as any ancient text's can, and better than most. It doesn't show that every word is certain, that the first decades of copying are visible, or that what the books say is true. Those are separate questions, and an honest case keeps them apart.

## The objection at its strongest

For decades, popular Christian apologetics has made a comparison one of its first moves. Historians trust what Caesar and Tacitus wrote, the argument goes, though their works survive in a handful of late copies. The New Testament survives in thousands, some of them within a few generations of its writing. So anyone who doubts the New Testament's text but not Caesar's is applying a double standard. A chart usually follows, listing authors, numbers of copies and the gap in years, with Homer's Iliad in a distant second place.

Bart Ehrman, who teaches at the University of North Carolina and is among the best-known textual critics alive, grants the premise and denies the conclusion. In his textbook *The New Testament: A Historical Introduction to the Early Christian Writings* (6th ed., 2016), he grants that the New Testament is better attested than any other book from the ancient world. But he presses two objections. The first is that abundance isn't the same as earliness. The great majority of the manuscripts are medieval, and we have few from the first centuries, which is when changes were most likely to be made. A thousand copies made in the eleventh century tell you very well what the eleventh century read. The second objection goes deeper. Ehrman asks his readers to suppose that we had more manuscripts of Plato than of the New Testament, and whether that would make Plato more trustworthy. His answer is no. Knowing what an ancient author wrote is one question. Whether what he wrote is true is another, and no pile of manuscripts can answer it.

Put at full strength, the objection says that the famous comparison is a sleight of hand. It takes a fact about how many copies survive, inflates it with old figures for the classics, and lets the reader believe that the reliability of the Gospels has been settled, when at most the fact bears on how well the wording of late copies can be traced back.

## What is true in it

Most of it, and Christians should say so before anyone else does. Start with the numbers. For decades a figure of 643 manuscripts of the Iliad passed from one apologetics book to the next. In 2012 Clay Jones, himself an apologist, published "The Bibliographical Test Updated" in the *Christian Research Journal* and reported the count as 1,757: 1,569 papyri and 188 parchment manuscripts. Counts for other classical authors had grown too. The New Testament still came out far ahead, but the old charts had been repeating figures that nobody had checked in a generation.

The counting was uneven as well. In *Myths and Mistakes in New Testament Textual Criticism* (2019), a book in which evangelical scholars correct bad arguments made on their own side, James Prothro's chapter on classical literature points out that apologists have set an inclusive count of New Testament manuscripts, every fragment and lectionary, beside the pared-down counts classical editors give, which are often only the copies they actually use. In the same book Jacob Peterson's chapter on numbers warns against resting the case on quantities at all. Counts include duplicates and manuscripts that have since been lost, and manuscripts have to be weighed, not merely counted. He puts the number of Greek New Testament manuscripts in existence at about 5,300 and suggests that 5,100 may be a safer figure.

Ehrman is also right about earliness. Of the 5,838 Greek manuscripts Daniel Wallace's center counted in 2013, 2,926 were minuscules and 2,462 were lectionaries, kinds of manuscripts copied mostly from the ninth century onward. And he is right about Plato. A well-preserved text can preserve a mistake perfectly. When the manuscript comparison is presented as though it proved the resurrection, a thoughtful skeptic is entitled to feel handled.

## The evidence

Here is the comparison done carefully, with its limits marked.

The New Testament's books were written in the second half of the first century. The oldest surviving piece is usually thought to be P52, a scrap of John 18 in Manchester, which C. H. Roberts dated to the first half of the second century, though Brent Nongbri has argued that the evidence allows a date as late as the early third. The larger papyri, P66 with much of John and P75 with much of Luke and John, have usually been dated around 200, and Nongbri argues for later dates for both. The first complete New Testament, Codex Sinaiticus, was copied in the middle of the fourth century, and Codex Vaticanus comes from the same century. So the gap to the earliest fragments is a few generations on the usual dating, and the gap to the first complete copies is something like two hundred and fifty to three hundred years. Beside the Greek, Wallace has reported more than 10,000 manuscripts of the Latin translation and as many as 5,000 in other ancient languages, and early Christian writers quoted the books constantly, though a quotation takes care to use, since it may be a paraphrase or a memory.

The Iliad is the best-attested work of classical literature, and its count, 1,757 on Jones's tally, is the only one that comes near. Most of its witnesses are papyri. The earliest, from the third to the first centuries BC, are instructive. Graeme Bird's study *Multitextuality in the Homeric Iliad* (2010) examines these early papyri, which some scholars have called eccentric or even wild because they add lines and word others differently from the text printed in our editions. Bird argues that their readings aren't simple errors but traces of a poem that lived in performance. Classicists don't conclude from this that Homer can't be read. They edit him with the evidence in view.

Julius Caesar's account of his wars in Gaul was written in the first century BC. Its earliest surviving manuscripts were copied in the ninth century AD, some nine centuries later. The first six books of Tacitus's *Annals*, one of our main sources for the reign of the emperor Tiberius, survive in a single medieval manuscript. Historians use both every day, quote them, argue over them, and build on them.

Set side by side, the result is plain even after every correction. On any honest count the New Testament has more witnesses, in more languages, from more places, and its earliest copies stand closer to the writing of the books than those of any classical work. Prothro's and Peterson's cautions narrow the margin. They don't reverse it.

Why the number matters at all is the more interesting point, and it isn't the reason the old charts implied. Copies made in different places by people who never met make different mistakes, and setting them side by side lets an editor see where each one went wrong. A single copy allows no such check. However carefully the one manuscript of the *Annals* was made, where it slipped we can only guess. The New Testament's thousands, most of them late, still give an editor more to check against than any other text from the ancient world, and its early papyri, few as they are, can be compared with the great codices of the fourth century. Where they overlap they carry substantially the same text, as the other pages in this area show.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument from consistency: one historical standard, applied to every ancient text alike.

1. Historians treat the wording of classical works such as Caesar's account of the Gallic war and Tacitus's *Annals* as known well enough to quote, argue over and build on, though those works survive in few copies made many centuries after they were written.
2. Confidence in an ancient text's wording rightly rises with the number, independence and age of its witnesses, weighed and not merely counted, because independent witnesses let an editor find one another's mistakes.
3. Counted carefully and weighed honestly, the New Testament's witnesses are more numerous, more widely spread and earlier than those of any classical work.

So: a historian can't, on one standard, treat Caesar's wording as known and the New Testament's as lost. The comparison establishes how well the text is known. It establishes nothing about whether the text is true.

The weight sits on the third step, and it is the step apologists have most often handled carelessly. A reader can check it against the corrected figures above, which are lower than the old charts and still decisive. The second step is less contested than it sounds. It is the working rule of every editor of ancient texts, classical or biblical.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply sharpens Ehrman's first point. Nobody claims Caesar's account of Gaul is the word of God, it says, so nobody needs its exact wording. A historian can live with uncertainty about Caesar's adjectives. A Christian who says Scripture was given by God can't live so easily with uncertainty about Paul's. The comparison quietly lowers the bar to the level of classical history and then claims to have cleared it.

That is a fair point about what Christians claim, and it should be granted. But two things can be said in answer. First, the question the comparison answers is a historical one, what the authors wrote, and on that question believer and skeptic have to use the same tools. The comparison doesn't lower anyone's bar. It shows where the evidence stands when everyone uses the same one. Second, the higher claim Christians make has never been that every copy is perfect. It is that the books as written are God's word. The evidence can't prove that claim, but it does show that the books as written can be recovered with high confidence in nearly every place, and that the places of real doubt are known and marked, as the first page in this area sets out. Irenaeus and Origen already knew that copies differed and argued about which readings were right. They didn't conclude that Scripture had been lost, and the evidence since gives no reason to.

The second reply is Ehrman's question about Plato, and it needs no answer, because it is right. Knowing what Matthew wrote doesn't tell you whether what he wrote happened. The comparison gets a reader to the text. Whether the text is telling the truth is the work of other pages, on the Gospels as history and on the resurrection, and it shouldn't be smuggled into this one.

## What remains unresolved

Several things stay open. The counts themselves differ, from about 5,100 Greek manuscripts on Peterson's cautious estimate to 5,838 on the 2013 tally, depending on how fragments, duplicates and lost manuscripts are handled. The dates of the earliest papyri are argued, and they set the size of the gap. The comparison with Homer is also less tidy than any chart suggests, because the Iliad came out of a long tradition of performance, and what counts as the original is a harder question for a poem than for a letter of Paul. For the Gospels, too, D. C. Parker has argued that the idea of a single original form may not fit how the early church handed them on, a question another page in this area takes up. And no count of medieval copies reaches the first decades of copying, which is the stretch a skeptic most wants to see and no one can.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much weight this comparison should carry, and this page takes no side. Many apologists make it the first step in a historical case for the faith. Others hold that Scripture's authority rests on God's own witness through it, so that historical arguments, however good, can support faith and clear away objections but can't be its foundation. Christians differ too on how to state Scripture's trustworthiness: many evangelical churches confess that it is without error as first written, while others who also confess it as the word of God put that conviction in other words. Those are family arguments. Every side of them can use the evidence on this page, and none of them needs it to say more than it does.
