# Science keeps explaining what people once put down to God. Hasn't it made God unnecessary?

## The short answer

Science has made one kind of god unnecessary, and Christians should be glad to see him go. A god who is needed only where our explanations run out shrinks every time a laboratory fills a gap, and the church sometimes leaned on that god. But that isn't the God of the creeds. Christians have confessed a Creator who is the reason there is a world of lawful causes at all, not one more cause competing inside it, and Thomas Aquinas answered the "unnecessary God" objection in those terms in the thirteenth century. Science explains how one state of nature leads to another, and it does that superbly. It can't, by its method, tell us why there is a nature with laws, or why those laws can be grasped by human minds. Those questions don't prove God. They show that the success of science, however great, doesn't settle the question it was never built to ask.

## The objection at its strongest

For most of human history, people explained lightning, plague, the motions of the planets and the fitness of living things by pointing to God or the gods. One by one, natural explanations replaced them, and the replacements worked. They predicted, they could be tested, and they built the modern world. When Darwin published *On the Origin of Species* in 1859, he gave a natural account of the most impressive case of all, the way living things fit their lives. Richard Dawkins put the consequence plainly in *The Blind Watchmaker* (1986): before Darwin an atheist could be logically consistent but had no explanation for biological design, and after Darwin he did.

The objection doesn't need the old picture of a war. It needs only the track record. Jerry Coyne, an evolutionary biologist, makes the careful version in *Faith versus Fact* (2015). He doesn't claim that science and religion contradict each other in logic. His charge is about method. Science has ways of finding out that it is wrong, and it uses them. Religion, he argues, holds its claims by faith, scripture and authority, and has no comparable way to correct itself. Every question that has passed from the theologians to the scientists has stayed with the scientists. None has gone back.

Stephen Hawking and Leonard Mlodinow pressed the argument to the edge of the cosmos in *The Grand Design* (2010), arguing that because a law like gravity exists, the universe can create itself from nothing, and that no creator is needed to set it going. Put at full strength, the objection says this: a hypothesis that explains nothing science can't explain better is idle, and an idle hypothesis should be dropped. God is that hypothesis.

The argument is older than its modern advocates. Aquinas set it out himself, as the second objection to his own answer to whether God exists: everything in the world seems accountable to other principles, natural things to nature and voluntary things to human reason and will, and so, in the English Dominican translation, "there is no need to suppose God's existence."

## What is true in it

A good deal, and it should be granted without fuss. Natural explanations have replaced religious ones again and again, and the replacement has rarely gone the other way. Christians have often put God in the gaps of what was known, and each time science filled a gap, the faith that rested there looked smaller. Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw the danger from a prison cell. In a letter of May 1944 he warned against using God as a stopgap for the unfinished parts of our knowledge and wrote, "We are to find God in what we know, not in what we don't know." About a decade later the physicist Charles Coulson made popular the name we still use for the mistake, the God of the gaps.

It's also true that the working rule of the laboratory is right for the laboratory. A chemist who writes "God did it" in the results has stopped doing chemistry. Christians who were scientists have kept that rule as faithfully as anyone. And the church has, at times, set itself against findings it should have welcomed. The next page in this area tells the hardest of those stories.

Coyne's point about method deserves a straight answer too. A faith that could never in principle be wrong wouldn't be knowledge. If Christianity were only a set of feelings about the universe, insulated from every fact, his charge would stand.

## The evidence

Start with what the objection assumes about God. It assumes that God, if he exists, is one of the causes inside nature, the kind of cause science looks for, needed wherever natural causes run out. That is the god of the gaps, and Bonhoeffer was right to refuse him. But it isn't what the church has taught. Aquinas answered his own objection in the same article where he raised it: nature works toward its ends under the direction of a higher agent, and so "whatever is done by nature must needs be traced back to God, as to its first cause." Elsewhere in the *Summa* he taught that God works through the causes he has made, not in competition with them. On that view, natural explanations don't remove God, any more than a full account of the printing press removes the author of a book. The author isn't a part of the press. He is the reason there is anything to print.

Scripture speaks the same way. Of Christ it says, "He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:17, BSB). A God who holds all things together isn't found only where the explanations stop. He is the reason the explanations hold.

Then look at what science itself presupposes. Every scientific explanation takes for granted that nature is ordered by regularities simple enough to be found, often in mathematics, and that human minds can find them. Albert Einstein, who didn't believe in a personal God, wrote in 1936 in an essay called "Physics and Reality" that "the eternal mystery of the world is its comprehensibility," and added, "The fact that it is comprehensible is a miracle." In 1960 the physicist Eugene Wigner wrote of the strange power of mathematics, worked out on paper, to describe the physical world, and called it "a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve." Neither man was arguing for God. Both saw that the success of science rests on something science doesn't explain.

Then consider the history. The historian Peter Harrison, in *The Territories of Science and Religion* (2015), argues that "science" and "religion" as two separate territories are themselves modern categories, and that the idea of natural science as a field set apart from religious and moral questions took its present shape only in the nineteenth century. For most of the history the objection appeals to, the people who did the work didn't think they were taking territory from God. When James Clerk Maxwell's Cavendish Laboratory opened at Cambridge in the 1870s, its doors carried in Latin, by his wish, the words of Psalm 111:2: "Great are the works of the LORD; they are pondered by all who delight in them" (BSB). In 1927 Georges Lemaître, a Belgian physicist and a Catholic priest, argued that the universe is expanding. None of that shows the faith is true. It shows that the story of science steadily evicting God is a story told about the history, not a finding drawn from it.

Finally, take the famous line that is often told on the other side, that the astronomer Laplace told Napoleon he had no need of the God hypothesis. No primary source for the exchange has been found, and the story is better left alone by both sides.

## The argument, laid out

This is a deductive argument about what kind of question science can settle.

1. Science explains events and regularities in nature by other events and regularities in nature, and it is right to do so.
2. The Christian claim about God is not a claim that he is one of those causes. It is the claim that he is the reason there is a lawful nature at all, and that he holds it in being at every moment.
3. An explanation of one part of nature by another, however complete, can't show that nature as a whole needs no explanation, because it already assumes nature and its laws.

So: the success of science can make the god of the gaps unnecessary, and it has. It can't make the Creator unnecessary, because the Creator was never offered as a rival to its explanations. Whether there is such a Creator is a real question. It is a question for philosophy and history, not one the laboratory has answered.

The weight sits on the second step. If the Christian God were one more cause inside the world, the objection would win, and a reader can test the step against the creeds and against Aquinas, who wrote long before anyone thought science was a threat.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that this victory costs everything. If God is never a cause that makes a detectable difference, then belief in him makes no difference to anything we could ever check. A God who explains everything in general explains nothing in particular, and the simplest view is that nature with its laws is simply there. Bertrand Russell said as much in his 1948 radio debate with the Jesuit Frederick Copleston: "I should say that the universe is just there, and that's all." Ockham's razor cuts away the rest.

Part of that should be granted. The argument above doesn't prove that God exists, and a reader who is content to treat the existence of a lawful, intelligible universe as a brute fact is not being irrational. But the reply needs two things it hasn't earned. First, it needs the brute fact to be simpler than the explanation. A razor removes needless causes of the same kind. It doesn't decide whether the existence of everything has an explanation or none, and treating "none" as the default is a choice, not a result. The reasons for and against that choice are set out at length in the site's answer on whether God exists.

Second, the reply says Christian belief makes no difference anyone could check, and that isn't so. The center of Christianity isn't a general claim about the universe kept safely out of the reach of evidence. It is a claim about something that happened in a particular city in a particular decade. Paul staked the faith on it: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile; you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, BSB). That is a faith that can be wrong. The rule of the laboratory, look for natural causes, can't tell anyone whether something happened that natural causes don't explain. That is a question for history, and it can be argued like any other.

## What remains unresolved

Whether the intelligibility of the world really points beyond itself is argued. Alvin Plantinga pressed a version of it in *Where the Conflict Really Lies* (2011), arguing that the conflict between science and belief in God is shallow and the agreement deep, while between science and naturalism, the view that nature is all there is, the agreement is shallow and the conflict deep. His argument has drawn serious replies, and no one should rest everything on its strongest form. Whether a brute-fact universe is an acceptable stopping point is one of the oldest questions in philosophy, and it remains open. And how God acts in particular events in a world whose regularities he upholds is a question Christians themselves still discuss.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much the order of nature can show by itself. Some build a strong natural theology on it, arguing from the existence and order of the world toward a Creator. Others think such arguments show less than their defenders claim, and rest the case for Christianity chiefly on Christ and the testimony to his resurrection. Christians also differ on how to think about God's special acts within nature, and on the age of the earth and the history of life, questions this area takes up on its own page without taking a side. What they confess together is the creed's first article, that God is the maker of heaven and earth, and that confession doesn't depend on any gap in anyone's knowledge.
