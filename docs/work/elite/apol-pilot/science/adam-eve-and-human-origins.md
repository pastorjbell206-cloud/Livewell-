# Fossils and genetics trace human beings back through a long history in Africa, from a population that was never only two people. Doesn't that rule out Adam and Eve, and with them the fall and the need for a Savior?

## The short answer

The evidence is real and should be stated at its strength. Fossils and genetics, as standard reference works set them out, place the origin of our species in Africa a few hundred thousand years ago, within a long branching history, and geneticists such as Dennis Venema argue that our ancestors were never fewer than several thousand. That rules out some readings of Genesis, chiefly a recent first pair who were the only ancestors of everyone. It doesn't rule out the fall or the need for a Savior, because Christians who accept the science hold several views of Adam, from real individuals within a larger population to Adam as a figure for every human being, and each keeps the confession that all have sinned and that Christ alone redeems. What the doctrine of sin requires about Adam is itself where Christians differ. This site takes no side among them.

## The objection at its strongest

Begin with the science as its textbooks give it. OpenStax's *Biology 2e* sets out the fossil record of the hominins, the species more closely related to us than to chimpanzees, from *Australopithecus* through *Homo erectus* to modern humans, and records that the evidence of fossils and DNA "suggests that humans and chimpanzees diverged from a common hominoid ancestor approximately six million years ago." The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on religion and science summarizes the present picture as a "predominantly African origin of our species Homo sapiens (as early as 315,000 years ago) with limited gene-flow from other hominin species such as Neanderthals and Denisovans," a history that "resembles an intricate branching" rather than a line.

Then the genetics of populations. The biologist Dennis Venema, in *Adam and the Genome* (2017), a book he wrote with the theologian Scot McKnight, argued, as the Study Bible's note on Genesis 3 summarizes him, that "human genetic diversity shows our ancestral population never dropped below several thousand individuals, which rules out all humans descending from a single couple within the time frame the traditional reading assumes."

The skeptic draws the conclusion. Paul builds the gospel on Adam: "just as sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin, so also death was passed on to all men, because all sinned" (Romans 5:12, BSB), and "as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22, BSB). The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that these findings "challenge traditional religious accounts of humanity, including the special creation of humans, the imago Dei, the historical Adam and Eve, and original sin," and that the limits of our cognitive capacities "can be naturalistically explained," so that there seems "little explanatory gain" in appealing to the fall. At full strength the objection says: no first pair, no first sin; no first sin, no fall; no fall, nothing for a Savior to undo.

## What is true in it

The science should not be disputed by insinuation. The fossil record of human origins is large and growing, the genetic evidence has been gathered by researchers of every faith and none, and both point to a history much longer and more branched than the reading of Genesis that, as the Study Bible's note on Genesis 3 says, most of the church held through its history. A Christian who tells young people that this evidence is thin, or invented, misleads them, and may teach them that faith depends on a claim they will later find false.

It is also true that the objection touches something the church has said with weight. Romans 5 is not a passing illustration; the Study Bible's note on the chapter records that "Many hold that Paul's argument depends on a real first man whose act had real effects, just as Christ is a real man." Augustine's account of original sin, the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on sin records, was codified by the councils of Carthage (418) and Orange (529), and the note on Romans 5 records that Catholic teaching at Trent (1546) and the Reformers followed it. Christians who accept the science and change their reading of Adam are not making a small adjustment.

## The evidence

Two kinds of evidence bear on the question, and they say different things.

The first is about our species. OpenStax's textbook records that modern humans are thought to have arisen in Africa and migrated out of it about 100,000 years ago, that this timeline "is supported by molecular evidence," and that the mitochondrial DNA of all living people traces to a common ancestor who lived in Africa about 160,000 years ago, and the Y chromosome of all living men to a man who lived there about 140,000 years ago. Those two ancestors, on these estimates, lived some twenty thousand years apart. They were not a couple, and the textbook doesn't present them as the only people of their time. The figures describe lines of descent through a population, not a first pair.

The second is about the size of that population, and here the evidence comes by inference from the variety of human genes. Venema's argument, summarized above, is that so much variety could not have come from two people in the time available. The library's page on evolution records that his estimate has itself been challenged, by Richard Buggs among others, and debated since. What a reader can rely on is the shape of the claim: on the mainstream reading, the genetic evidence rules out a pair who lived recently and were the sole ancestors of everyone. It does not, by itself, speak to everything Christians have said about Adam.

Then Scripture. Genesis says that "the LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground and breathed the breath of life into his nostrils" (Genesis 2:7, BSB) and calls Eve "the mother of all the living" (Genesis 3:20, BSB). Paul tells the Athenians, "From one man He made every nation of men" (Acts 17:26, BSB), and Luke traces Jesus' line to "Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38, BSB). Paul sets two men side by side: "through the disobedience of the one man the many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19, BSB). These are the texts every view must explain. The note on Genesis 3 adds that "Jewish tradition, from the Mishnah onward, has generally read Adam as the first human but without the Western doctrine of inherited guilt."

## The argument, laid out

This is a deductive argument about what the evidence, granted, can refute.

1. What the church confesses about sin is that all human beings have sinned, that sin and death are real enemies, and that Christ alone is the remedy; the creeds confess this without defining how Adam's sin reached his descendants.
2. Christians who accept the mainstream evidence of human origins hold several accounts of Adam, real individuals within a larger population, or a figure for every human being, and each holds all of those confessions without contradiction.
3. Evidence that rules out a recent first pair as the sole ancestors of all refutes that reading of Genesis, not the confessions the readings share.

So: the evidence of human origins, if it holds, can refute one traditional reading of Adam, but not by itself the Christian confession of sin and redemption; whether particular doctrines of original sin can be kept is a further question, on which Christians differ.

The weight sits on the second step. A critic, and many Christians, will say that Paul's argument needs a first man whose single act brought sin to all, and that the other accounts can't hold the confession without emptying Romans 5. That is the reply this page must answer.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply presses on Romans 5 itself. Paul doesn't say that all humans sin. He says that sin "entered the world through one man," and his parallel needs that man as surely as it needs Christ: one trespass, one act of obedience. If Adam is only a figure for everyone, the first half of the parallel becomes a picture and the second half is left resting on it. And the Stanford Encyclopedia records the scientist's challenge to the middle views as well: John Schneider has argued that there is "no genetic or paleoanthropological evidence" for the early community of humans in union with God that some of them propose.

Much of that should be granted. The parallel in Romans 5 is real, and the view that takes Adam as a figure for humanity bears the heaviest burden in explaining it. But the reply proves less than it needs. First, several views keep a real man and a real act. The note on Genesis 3 records that John Walton, William Lane Craig and S. Joshua Swamidass, in their different ways, accept an ancient human population and hold that Adam and Eve were real individuals, as archetypal representatives, as early ancestors of all humans, or as a couple from whom everyone alive in Jesus' time descends genealogically; and the Stanford Encyclopedia records Peter van Inwagen's and James K. Smith's proposal that God guided a first human community that turned from him. Schneider's objection is that no evidence shows such a community; the reply is that an event of that kind would leave little in the bones or the genes, so that the absence of evidence weighs less here than elsewhere. Second, Paul's argument runs from Christ back to Adam, not the other way. Its point, the note on Romans 5 says, is that "the gift is not like the trespass; whatever the loss through Adam, grace abounds much more." Third, the doctrine the objection attacks is not one doctrine. The note records that Augustinian and Reformed traditions speak of inherited guilt or of Adam as representative head, while "Eastern Orthodox tradition stresses inherited mortality and corruption rather than guilt," and the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that "there is no generally accepted Christian doctrine on original sin." The objection's chain, no first pair and so no fall, is strongest against one account and weakest against others.

None of these answers is free of difficulty, and each is disputed by Christians as well as by skeptics. What they show is that the objection's dilemma is not forced.

## What remains unresolved

Who Adam was, when he lived and how he relates to the human population genetics describes is unresolved, and argued among Christians who all confess him. How sin passed from the first sinners to the rest is unresolved in the church's own theology; the Stanford Encyclopedia sets out the federal and realist accounts and the old dispute over inherited guilt. How death before human sin fits the goodness of creation belongs with the problem of natural evil. And the genetic estimates themselves are still being tested.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on Adam, and this site takes no side among them. The note on Genesis 3 and *Four Views on the Historical Adam* (2013), as the argued answer on Genesis 1 sets out its contributors, give the range. Many hold, with most of the church through its history, that Adam and Eve were a real first pair and the ancestors of the whole race, whether on a young earth, as William Barrick argues, or an old one, as C. John Collins does; their hardest difficulty is the genetic evidence as Venema states it. Others hold that Adam and Eve were real individuals within a larger population, archetypes, representatives or genealogical ancestors; their hardest difficulty is to say how the sin of a few reached all, and how that fits Genesis's "mother of all the living." Others, Denis Lamoureux and Peter Enns among them, read Genesis 2 and 3 as a story about every human being in ancient form; their hardest difficulty is Paul's parallel between one man and one man.

What the doctrine of sin requires is where these differences finally lie, and Christians differ there too. Some traditions hold that original sin requires a first human pair from whom all descend; others that it requires a real first sin and a real solidarity of the race with its head, however that came about; Eastern Orthodox theology speaks of inherited mortality and corruption rather than guilt. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on sin observes that "one need not take the story of the fall literally to think that there was a primal sin," and many Christians would not accept that reading. What every view on this page confesses is the confession of Romans itself: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23, BSB), and "as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22, BSB). The creeds rest the gospel on the second man, crucified and raised. How much they rest on the details of the first is the question these Christians have not settled, and they hold it open as brothers and sisters.
