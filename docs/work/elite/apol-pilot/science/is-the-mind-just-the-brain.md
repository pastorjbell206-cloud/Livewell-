# Damage to the brain changes memory, character and even the sense of self, and neuroscience ties more of mental life to the brain every year. Hasn't science shown that the mind is just the brain, and that there is no soul?

## The short answer

Neuroscience has shown that the mind depends on the brain, closely and in detail, and Christians should say so without hedging. It hasn't shown that the mind is nothing but the brain. That further claim, physicalism, rests on philosophical arguments that are serious and still disputed, and it meets what philosophers call the hard problem: no account of what the brain does yet explains why any of it is felt from the inside. Christians themselves hold three views of the person: that the soul is a distinct substance joined to the body, that the soul is the form of a living body, as Thomas Aquinas taught, and that a human being is wholly physical and lives again by the resurrection of the body. This site takes no side among them. All of them confess the same hope, which was never a ghost escaping a machine but the resurrection of the body.

## The objection at its strongest

Every year the case grows. Injury to particular regions of the brain changes memory, speech, judgment and personality, and drugs alter mood and perception. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on the neuroscience of consciousness records what brain lesions take away and what electrical stimulation of the cortex can produce, hallucinations among them, and it begins from the fact on which all of this rests: "Conscious experience in humans depends on brain activity." If everything the mind does can be changed by changing the brain, the objection asks, what is left for a soul to do?

The philosophers give the case its argued form. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on physicalism sets out what it calls "perhaps the dominant argument for physicalism in the literature today," the argument from causal closure: every event that has a cause has a physical cause; mental events, such as wanting to raise your arm, cause physical events, such as the arm rising; and an event is not caused twice over by independent causes. If all three are true, the mind's causes must be physical, or must rest wholly on the physical. Daniel Dennett argued in *Consciousness Explained* (1991), as the encyclopedia's entry on consciousness summarizes him, that the present gaps in explaining experience reflect only the early stage of inquiry and the limits of present theory, not a barrier in reality. At full strength the objection says that the soul was a guess made before anyone could look inside the head, and that now we can.

## What is true in it

The dependence is real and it is intimate. Anyone who has watched a parent's memory fail has seen it, and no Christian account of the person should pretend that the self floats free of the body it lives in. A picture common in popular piety, of a soul that is the real person and a body that is a shell it wears, owes more to Plato than to Genesis, and the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on the afterlife records that many theologians have argued that dualism is "a Platonic import" into the biblical faiths, whose stress falls on bodily life. Christians have sometimes defended the soul by pointing to whatever neuroscience had not yet explained, and that habit, a god of the gaps for the mind, deserved to lose. Nothing on this page is medical advice; anyone facing an illness of the brain, their own or someone else's, should ask a physician.

## The evidence

Begin with what the neuroscience establishes. Its central project, the encyclopedia's entry records, has been to find the neural correlates of consciousness, the brain activity that goes with each kind of experience, and it has found many. The same entry is careful about what that means: identifying correlates "is an important first step in understanding consciousness, but it is an early step," because "correlates are not necessarily explanatory." A correlate shows that experience and a brain state go together. It doesn't by itself show that they are one thing.

Then the problem the correlations leave. David Chalmers, as the encyclopedia's entries on consciousness and on its neuroscience present his argument of 1995, distinguished the "easy problems" of explaining the brain's functions from the hard problem: why the performance of those functions is accompanied by experience at all, why there is something it is like to see deep blue or hear middle C. Explanations in neuroscience, on his argument, are structural or functional, and experience is not reducible to either, so something is left out. Joseph Levine, in 1983, called the same difficulty the explanatory gap. The responses are many. Some philosophers deny that there is a hard problem; the encyclopedia records that "Perhaps the most common attitude for neuroscientists is to set the hard problem aside." Others hold, as it also records, that explanatory gaps are to be expected if we are physical systems with limited understanding, so that the gap would confirm physicalism rather than refute it. The encyclopedia's own summary is that "Discussion and disagreement on these topics remains active and ongoing."

Then the argument from causal closure, examined. The encyclopedia's entry on physicalism, after setting it out, says plainly that "it is somewhat unclear whether it is successful." Each premise has been questioned: some deny that the mental causes anything physical, holding that consciousness accompanies brain events without producing them; others deny that the physical world is closed in the needed sense. The argument is a real argument, and it has not been shown to succeed.

And Scripture's picture of the person. Genesis has God form the man from the dust and breathe into him the breath of life, "and the man became a living being" (Genesis 2:7, BSB). The psalmist praises God who "formed my inmost being" and knit him together in the womb (Psalm 139:13, BSB). Jesus speaks of soul and body as distinct: "Do not be afraid of those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul" (Matthew 10:28, BSB). Paul speaks of being "away from the body and at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8, BSB), and the Study Bible's note on that chapter adds that the final hope is not that state but "mortality swallowed up by life." Scripture gives no theory of neurons. It gives a person who is dust and breath together, made for a bodily life to come.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the evidence shows, and what it would take to show more.

1. Neuroscience shows that conscious experience depends on brain activity and varies with it in detail.
2. Dependence and correlation, however close, do not by themselves show identity; that requires a further philosophical argument, such as the argument from causal closure, and that argument is contested in its premises.
3. No account of the brain's functions yet explains why they are accompanied by experience, and philosophers who agree on the neuroscience disagree about whether one can.

So: neuroscience has not shown that the mind is nothing but the brain; whether it is turns on philosophical arguments that remain open, and the findings, granted in full, refute no Christian view of the person that accepts them.

The weight sits on the second step. If the argument from causal closure succeeds, then whatever the mind is, its causes are physical, and the case for a soul that acts on the body is in serious trouble. That is where the strongest reply presses.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the second step plays for time. The argument from causal closure is valid, the physicalist says, and its premises are what science itself assumes: physics finds physical causes for physical events, and has never needed a soul to close a gap. Denying that the mind causes anything is a desperate move, and denying closure means betting against the most successful program of inquiry there has ever been. As for the hard problem, every hard problem in the history of science looked permanent until it was solved; life itself once seemed to need a vital force.

Part of that should be granted. Closure is a strong working assumption, and a Christian who rests the soul's existence on a hole in physics is making the god-of-the-gaps mistake conceded above. But two things limit the reply. The first concerns the hard problem's kind. The gaps that closed in the past, such as the explanation of life, were gaps in explaining functions, and functions are what science explains well; the hard problem is a question about what remains when every function has been explained, which is why naturalist philosophers themselves divide over it, as this library's page on the argument from reason sets out.

The second matters more, and it is easy to miss. The Christian confession doesn't depend on defeating physicalism. The creeds confess "the resurrection of the body," and some Christian philosophers hold that a human being is wholly physical. The encyclopedia's entry on the afterlife records that Peter van Inwagen, a Christian, argued in 1978 for the possibility of resurrection on materialist terms, that Lynne Rudder Baker proposed that persons are constituted by their bodies without being identical to them, and that Kevin Corcoran and Dean Zimmerman have offered other accounts; it also records that each faces the hard question of how the person who dies and the person raised are the same. If physicalism is true, these Christians lose nothing they confess. If it is false, dualists and Thomists lose nothing either. The objection's last step, that there is then no soul and nothing after death, assumes that Christian hope rests on a separable soul. It rests on the God who raised Jesus from the dead, which no finding about neurons can reach.

## What remains unresolved

Whether consciousness can be explained physically is unresolved, and it is among the most argued questions in philosophy. Whether mental events cause physical ones, and how, is unresolved. For Christians, what happens to a person between death and resurrection, and how the one raised is the same as the one who died, are questions the church has answered in more than one way; Paul's "at home with the Lord" and his "mortality swallowed up by life" are both in the same chapter.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on what a human person is, and this site takes no side among them. Substance dualists hold that the soul is a distinct, immaterial substance joined to the body; Richard Swinburne, in *The Evolution of the Soul* (1986), is among its defenders, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on dualism records. Its strongest point is the unity and inwardness of experience, which it explains as the life of a single subject; its hardest difficulty is how two such different things interact, and the closeness of the mind's dependence on the brain. Thomists hold, with Aquinas, that the soul is the form of the body, so that, as the encyclopedia's entry on dualism puts his view, "The soul, though an immaterial substance, is the person only when united with its body." Its strongest point is that it honors both the body and the intellect; its hardest difficulty is to say how a form survives the body it forms. Christian physicalists hold that a person is wholly physical and rest their hope on the resurrection of the body; their strongest point is how well they fit the neuroscience and the Bible's bodily hope, and their hardest difficulty is the identity of the one raised with the one who died, and the texts that speak of being with Christ before the resurrection (Luke 23:43; Philippians 1:23).

What all of them confess is that human beings are made in God's image, "fearfully and wonderfully made" (Psalm 139:14, BSB), that they will be raised in the body, and that "the perishable must be clothed with the imperishable, and the mortal with immortality" (1 Corinthians 15:53, BSB). The brain is part of that making, not a rival to it.
