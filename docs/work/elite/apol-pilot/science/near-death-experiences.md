# People revived after their hearts stopped report leaving their bodies, a light, and meeting relatives who had died. Do near-death experiences prove that there is life after death?

## The short answer

They do not. If you are thinking about your own death or about ending your life, please don't carry it alone: the site's Find Help page (/help) lists places to turn now. Near-death experiences are real experiences, reported by many people in many times and places, and they deserve to be taken seriously. But what they show is argued, and the reference work this page follows concludes that the evidence is close to an impasse: the reports vary with culture, similar experiences come to people who only believed they were dying, and the most striking cases are disputed. Christians should not rest their hope on them, and the faith never has. Christian hope rests on something of another kind: the claim that Jesus of Nazareth died, was buried and was raised, a claim about history that can be examined, and a promise that death itself, the enemy, will be undone.

## The objection at its strongest

The case that these experiences show life after death is stronger than its popular versions. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on the afterlife sets out why such testimony can seem compelling: large numbers of people report the experiences; they come unbidden to people near death, who did not seek them; and usually no one stands to gain money from them. Their contents form a recurring pattern, "general (but far from invariable)": a sense of being dead, an experience of seeing one's own body from outside, a tunnel and a light, meetings with people who have died, and a review of one's life. Since Raymond Moody's *Life After Life* (1975), the entry records, there have been many studies, some carried out with careful attention to scientific objectivity, among them a study of survivors of cardiac arrest in the Netherlands published in *The Lancet* in 2001.

The strongest form of the case rests on what Gary Habermas called the evidential aspect of these experiences, and J. P. Moreland and Habermas pressed it in *Beyond Death* (1998). The encyclopedia gives three kinds. Some patients, while unconscious, report accurately what was happening around them, sometimes at a distance, which their senses should not have reached. Some report events from periods when the heart had stopped, and even when no brain activity was detected. And some meet relatives in the experience whose deaths they had not yet been told of. The encyclopedia also records a difficulty for the purely medical explanation: climbers falling in what they expected to be fatal falls have had such experiences, which can hardly be put down to drugs or a lack of oxygen. At full strength the case says that if even one report of accurate perception without working senses is true, the mind can act apart from the brain, and death need not be the end of the person.

## What is true in it

The experiences are real, and the people who have them are not lying or foolish. Many describe them as having changed how they live. Dismissing them is neither kind nor honest, and telling people that they imagined it, or that what they met was evil, is no answer.

It is also true that the reductive explanations sometimes claim too much. The encyclopedia observes that "there is no one medical or physiological cause; the experiences occur for persons in a great variety of medical conditions," and it notes that one of the most thorough skeptical surveys, Keith Augustine's, "makes little effort to establish that the factors cited in his naturalistic explanations were actually operative in the various NDE cases." Neither side has closed the question. Nothing on this page is medical advice; questions about a particular illness, resuscitation or the care of someone dying belong with a physician.

## The evidence

The same reference entry, after setting out the case, sets out the replies, and they are strong. First, the experiences vary with culture. Carol Zaleski, in a book of 1987, compared medieval and modern accounts and showed, as the encyclopedia puts it, "that many features of these experiences vary in ways that correspond to cultural expectations": medieval accounts are full of judgment, modern ones mostly not, and research outside the cultures where most studies have been done finds "significantly different patterns." Zaleski ascribes the experiences to the religious imagination, insisting that this enhances rather than diminishes their significance.

Second, the trigger seems to be the belief that one is dying, not death itself. Some features of the experience have been produced by drugs, and the encyclopedia records that similar experiences "sometimes happen to persons who mistakenly believe themselves to be in life-threatening circumstances." Its summary is cautious: "Apparently it is the perceived nearness to death, rather than the actual proximity of the afterworld, that triggers the experiences."

Third, each evidential claim has a reply. People also meet living persons in these experiences, persons who were elsewhere at the time, so that occasional meetings with someone who had just died are to be expected by chance. An electroencephalogram may not detect all activity in the brain, and an experience reported as occurring when the brain was silent may have occurred just before or just after. Reports of accurate perception sometimes prove inaccurate, sometimes grow in the retelling, "a common experience," the entry notes, "that stories often repeated tend to gain new features of interest in the telling," and sometimes may be explained by hearing, since there is evidence that apparently unconscious patients register what is said around them.

The entry's own verdict is that "we may be close to an impasse": supporters will say the naturalistic explanations are speculative and too skeptical of well-confirmed accounts, critics that the evidence cannot bear the extraordinary claims made for it, and "it seems unlikely that the fundamental disagreements can be resolved." That is where the evidence stands, and an honest page has to say so.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what near-death experiences can establish, and about what Christian hope rests on.

1. Near-death experiences vary with culture and expectation, can occur when death is only believed to be near, and have their most striking evidential claims disputed by careful investigators.
2. Evidence so described may be consistent with life after death, but it cannot establish it, and it cannot tell us what that life is.
3. The Christian hope was never a claim that some people glimpse another world when near death; it is the claim that Jesus died, was buried, was raised and appeared, and that the dead will be raised.

So: near-death experiences don't prove an afterlife, and they don't disprove one; whatever they are, they are not the ground of Christian hope, which stands or falls with the resurrection of Jesus.

The weight sits on the second step. If even one case of accurate perception without functioning senses were established beyond reasonable dispute, the experiences would show more than this page allows, and a reader could fairly say that it has been too cautious.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply comes from the skeptic, and it turns the page's caution against the faith. If Christians grant that the best modern evidence for life after death is inconclusive, the skeptic says, then what is left is faith without evidence. And the resurrection appearances look like the same thing told long ago: people in grief and expectation saw their dead teacher, in a culture that believed such things could happen, and the stories grew in the telling. Every reply the page has just used against near-death experiences, the skeptic says, applies to Easter.

That reply deserves an answer, and part of it should be granted: the replies above are the kind of questions any honest reader must put to the Easter reports, and this library's pages on the historical Jesus put them. But the cases differ in ways that matter. The claim of Easter is not that someone near death had an experience. It is that a man who was certainly dead, executed by Rome, did not stay dead, and that his tomb was found empty. The earliest summary, which Paul says he received and passed on, is about events, not visions: "that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that He appeared to Cephas and then to the Twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:3-5, BSB). Whether that happened is a historical question, argued on its own evidence, and the library's pages on the empty tomb and on the appearances and the alternatives argue it, including the explanation from grief. Paul himself staked the faith on it: "if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile" (1 Corinthians 15:17, BSB). He did not stake it on reports from the edge of death, and in Jesus' parable of the rich man the answer to the request for a messenger from the dead is that "they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead" (Luke 16:31, BSB).

## What remains unresolved

What near-death experiences are is unresolved: whether all of them have a natural explanation, and if so which, or whether some show the mind acting apart from the brain. The encyclopedia doubts that further study will settle the disagreement, since it is bound up with larger views of the world. For Christians, what the dead experience before the resurrection is a question Scripture answers only in part, and the church has not settled every question it raises.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to regard near-death experiences, and this site takes no side among them. Some, like Moreland and Habermas, see the best-attested cases as evidence that the person can survive the body, and value them as such. Others hold that they are experiences of a living brain under extreme stress, real and sometimes meaningful, but not reports from beyond death. Some, with Zaleski, see in them the religious imagination at work, which does not make them worthless. Christians also differ, as the library's page on the mind and the brain sets out, on whether a person has a soul that can exist apart from the body.

All of them confess what Scripture confesses about death. It is not a friend or a doorway to be sought: "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26, BSB). Jesus stood at his friend's grave and wept, and the Study Bible's note on John 11 says it plainly: "Jesus knows he will raise Lazarus, yet he weeps (verse 35) and is deeply moved. Grief is not unbelief, and the delay that caused the sisters' grief was not indifference (verses 5 and 6). The Son of God enters human sorrow and stands with those who mourn before he acts." Christians grieve, Paul says, but not "like the rest, who are without hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13, BSB), because their hope is not in what anyone saw at the edge of death but in the one who went through it and came back: "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25, BSB).
