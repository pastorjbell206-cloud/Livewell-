# A miracle would break the laws of nature. Didn't David Hume show that no one can reasonably believe in one?

## The short answer

Hume showed something important, and less than he is usually thought to have shown. In 1748 he argued that a wise person proportions belief to the evidence, that the laws of nature rest on the most uniform experience we have, and that testimony to a miracle can therefore almost never outweigh the evidence against it. The first point is right, and Christians should hold to it more strictly than they sometimes have. Most miracle reports deserve doubt. But Hume's argument either assumes that experience is uniform against miracles, which is the question at issue, or it becomes a matter of weighing particular evidence, and then it can't rule miracles out in advance. Hume himself granted that testimony could establish an event against all past experience. The laws of nature describe what happens when nothing beyond nature acts. Whether anything ever has is a question about God and about history, and Hume's essay doesn't answer it.

## The objection at its strongest

David Hume put the case in the tenth section of *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), "Of Miracles," and most people who doubt miracles today stand inside his conclusion without having read him. He begins with a rule no one should dispute: "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence." Then he defines his terms. "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined." The example he chooses is the one Christianity rests on: "But it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country."

From that he draws what he calls a general maxim: "That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish." When someone tells him a dead man was restored to life, he asks himself "whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened." He always rejects, he says, the greater miracle.

In the second part of the essay Hume argues that the testimony we actually have never meets even that standard. No miracle has been attested by enough people of sense, learning and reputation to rule out delusion and deceit. People love wonder and love to pass it on. Miracle stories, he writes, "are observed chiefly to abound among ignorant and barbarous nations." The miracles of rival religions cancel each other out. He concludes "that no human testimony can have such force as to prove a miracle, and make it a just foundation for any such system of religion."

Robert Fogelin, in *A Defense of Hume on Miracles* (2003), argued that Hume's critics have misread him, and that the argument, rightly understood, still stands. The modern form is simple. Nature runs by laws, and science has never found an exception. Every miracle report we have been able to check has failed. A reasonable person should expect the next one to fail too.

## What is true in it

Hume's first rule is simply right. A belief should be proportioned to the evidence, and a Christian who believes every report of a healing or a weeping statue is not being faithful but credulous. Scripture itself tells believers to test what they hear. Most miracle claims are false, mistaken or exaggerated, and Christians have produced and spread their share of them.

Hume was right, too, about the love of wonder, and about how reports grow in the telling. His own sharpest example was the healings reported at the tomb of the Abbé Paris in France in his lifetime, which were, he wrote, "attested by witnesses of credit and distinction, in a learned age." A Christian reader should feel the force of that. Large numbers of sincere witnesses have reported things that did not happen.

And Hume was right that the regularity of nature is among the best-established things we know. Christians have no interest in denying it. The miracles of Scripture are told as signs precisely because they are not what normally happens. A world without stable laws would make a miracle unrecognizable.

## The evidence

Begin with the definition, because the argument's force depends on it. Hume says there must be "a uniform experience against every miraculous event, otherwise the event would not merit that appellation." But if experience is uniform against miracles, then no miracle has ever happened, and that is the conclusion the argument was meant to prove. If instead experience is uniform only in the sense that most people have never seen one, then the question is open again, and the testimony of those who say they have must be weighed, not dismissed. C. S. Lewis made this point in *Miracles* (1947): we could know that experience is uniform against miracles only if we already knew that all the reports of them are false, and that is "arguing in a circle." Hume's defenders reply that he meant a weighing of probabilities, not a definition. Taken that way, his argument is no longer a proof that miracles can't be believed. It is a warning that the evidence must be very strong.

Hume himself grants as much. In the same essay he writes that "The Indian prince, who refused to believe the first relations concerning the effects of frost, reasoned justly," because frost was outside his experience, though it was real. And he goes further. Suppose, he says, that all authors in all languages agreed that from the first of January 1600 there was total darkness over the whole earth for eight days; then "our present philosophers, instead of doubting the fact, ought to receive it as certain, and ought to search for the causes whence it might be derived." So testimony can establish an event against all past experience. Hume's line is not drawn at the unprecedented. It is drawn at a miracle offered as the foundation of a religion. Of Queen Elizabeth, imagined dead, buried for a month and then restored to the throne, he says he would assert her death "to have been pretended, and that it neither was, nor possibly could be real." That is a judgment about religion brought to the evidence, not a result drawn from it.

Then consider what a law of nature says. Lewis offered a homely picture. If a man puts six pennies in a drawer on Monday and six more on Tuesday, the laws of arithmetic say there will be twelve on Wednesday. If a thief has taken two, he will find ten. The thief hasn't broken arithmetic. He has added a cause the calculation didn't include. A law of nature tells us what happens when nothing outside nature acts. The Christian claim about the resurrection is not that the body's chemistry failed. It is that God acted. Whether that happened depends on whether there is a God who could, and on the evidence that he did.

Then the probability. John Earman, a philosopher of science at the University of Pittsburgh, argued in *Hume's Abject Failure* (2000) that the argument fails by the standards of the probability theory of Hume's own day. Charles Babbage had argued in the 1830s that enough independent witnesses, each more often right than wrong, can together outweigh any improbability, and Earman took up the point. William Vanderburgh replied in *Hume Studies* (2005) that Hume can be defended, and Peter Millican has asked how anyone could ever know that witnesses were truly independent. That debate continues among philosophers. What it shows is that the question is one of weighing, and that Hume's verdict doesn't follow from the rules alone.

Finally, the evidence itself has grown. Craig Keener, in *Miracles* (2011), gathered hundreds of reports of extraordinary healings, many of them from Asia, Africa and Latin America and many from eyewitnesses. Keener doesn't claim that each report proves a miracle. His point is that eyewitness claims of such events are far more widespread than the second part of Hume's essay assumes.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the question of miracles turns on.

1. The laws of nature describe how nature behaves when nothing beyond nature acts upon it; they don't by themselves say whether anything beyond nature exists or acts.
2. So whether a reported miracle is believable depends on two things: how likely it is, given everything else one knows, that a God exists who would act, and how strong the evidence is that he acted in this case.
3. Hume's argument either assumes that experience is uniform against miracles, which begs the question, or it weighs evidence case by case, which can't exclude miracles in advance.

So: miracles are not shown impossible or unbelievable in principle. Each claim has to be weighed, beginning with the question of God, and the claim Christianity rests on, the resurrection of Jesus, has to be weighed on its own evidence.

The weight sits on the second step. It is where Hume's defenders push back, arguing that the probability of any miracle is always so low that no human evidence could reach it, and it is where a reader should test the argument.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply grants all the logic and keeps the conclusion. Fine, Hume's definition begs the question, and a probability can't be zero. But the prior probability of a resurrection is still vanishingly small. Every miracle report we've ever been able to check has turned out false, mistaken or explained. That's a terrible record, and the rational thing is to expect the next report, including an ancient one, to go the same way. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, as Carl Sagan made the slogan, and a few ancient texts written by believers are not extraordinary evidence.

The first half of that is right, and it is how anyone should treat a healer on television. But the reply's prior isn't neutral. How likely a resurrection is depends on what else is true. If there is no God, its probability is about as low as Hume thought. If there is a God who made the world and has purposes in it, an act of God at the center of history isn't absurd, though it is still unusual and still needs evidence. That is why the question of miracles can't be settled apart from the question of God, and why the site treats that question on its own page. As for the record, it is a record of checked reports, and the resurrection is not a report like the others. It was a public claim about a particular tomb in a particular city, made by people who said they had seen the risen Jesus, and the earliest written form of it, in 1 Corinthians 15, lists witnesses by name and number. Paul staked everything on it: "And if Christ has not been raised, our preaching is worthless, and so is your faith" (1 Corinthians 15:14, BSB). That is a claim that can be examined. The site's answer on the resurrection examines it.

## What remains unresolved

How to set the probability of God's acting, before any evidence, has no agreed method, and philosophers on both sides concede that the result depends heavily on it. Whether the laws of nature merely describe regularities or in some sense govern them is a live question in philosophy. Whether Hume's argument is best read as Fogelin reads it or as Earman does is argued. And the reports Keener collected are open to more than one reading, as he himself allows. A careful reader can leave these questions open and still see that Hume's essay did not close the one it is usually thought to have closed.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on whether miracles of the kind Scripture describes continue today, and on how often. Some hold that the sign-miracles of the apostles' time ceased with them; others that they continue wherever God pleases; many hold positions between. They differ too on how God's special acts relate to the ordinary order he upholds. What the church confesses together is narrower and firmer: that God made and upholds the world, that he can act in it, and that he raised Jesus bodily from the dead. That last claim is not a detail. On it, as Paul said, the faith stands or falls.
