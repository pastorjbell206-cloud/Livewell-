# Experiments show the brain preparing an action before a person is aware of deciding to act. Hasn't neuroscience shown that free will is an illusion, and with it moral responsibility?

## The short answer

No. Benjamin Libet's experiments of the early 1980s found a build-up of brain activity, the readiness potential, before people reported the urge to make a simple movement, and some scientists concluded that conscious will is an illusion. But what that activity signifies is disputed; the Stanford Encyclopedia reports that after much further study neuroscientists are uncertain what it means, and that many philosophers, including many who doubt free will on other grounds, reject the argument from these experiments. The deeper question, whether freedom and responsibility can coexist with prior causes, is philosophical, and Christians answer it in more than one way: Reformed theology has held that God ordains all things without doing violence to the will, and the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions that grace frees the will to respond. This site takes no side between them. Both hold that human beings are responsible before God.

## The objection at its strongest

The experiment is simple, and its result was startling. Benjamin Libet, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on epiphenomenalism describes his work, asked people to make an unplanned movement whenever they felt the urge, and to report, using a clock-like instrument, when they first felt it, while their brain waves were recorded. In the study of 1983, the encyclopedia's entry on skepticism about moral responsibility records, the conscious intention came about 200 milliseconds before the movement, but 350 to 400 milliseconds after the readiness potential, "a ramp-like buildup of electrical activity that occurs in the brain and precedes actual movement." Libet and others concluded, the entry records, that "the conscious intention or decision to move cannot be the true cause of action because it comes too late."

The psychologist Daniel Wegner drew the larger lesson in *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (2002). Gathering Libet's findings with others from psychology, cases in which people believe they chose an action that was in fact induced, and patients who act with purpose while sincerely believing they are not the ones acting, he argued, as the encyclopedia's entry on free will summarizes him, that the experience of conscious willing is an illusion. The brain decides; the feeling of deciding comes after, as a story the mind tells itself. At full strength the objection says that if our choices are made by processes we don't control and only later notice, then praise and blame, guilt and repentance, rest on a mistake about what we are.

## What is true in it

More than many Christians would like. The encyclopedia's entry records findings in psychology that "the causes that move us are often less transparent to ourselves than we might assume," that judgments and decisions once thought to need conscious choice "can and often do occur in the absence of conscious choice and guidance," and that implicit biases shape behavior without our knowledge. Even some compatibilists, it records, now grant that free will may be only an occasional thing. Anyone who has acted and only afterwards understood why has met the problem in himself.

Christians should also concede that this is not news to their own Scriptures. "The heart is deceitful above all things and beyond cure. Who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9, BSB). Paul writes, "I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do, I do not do. But what I hate, I do" (Romans 7:15, BSB). A faith that has long spoken of the bondage of the will has no stake in pretending that human choice is transparent, sovereign and self-made.

## The evidence

Begin with the experiments and what has happened to their interpretation. The encyclopedia's entry on free will states it plainly: "Libet's interpretation of the readiness potential has come in for severe criticism. After extensive subsequent study, neuroscientists are uncertain what it signifies." It points to a review of the evidence by Aaron Schurger and colleagues in 2021. Libet himself, the entry on epiphenomenalism records, allowed that a person could still veto the movement after the urge. Later work led by John-Dylan Haynes found brain events that predict simple choices several seconds ahead, but, as the same entry records, those predictions, "while statistically significant, are far from perfect." The critics' points are of several kinds. Alfred Mele, in *Effective Intentions* (2009), argued, as the entry on skepticism summarizes him, that the readiness potential "need not be construed as the cause of the action but rather is best interpreted as the beginning of forming an intention to act," so that the conscious intention can still be a cause. Others have questioned the design. Many of these experiments, the entry on epiphenomenalism notes, involve "meaningless decisions (e.g., when to move a finger or which of two buttons to press) that bear little resemblance to real-life decisions," in which people bring reasons and preferences to bear. The entry records that these objections "have led many contemporary philosophers (including many skeptics) to reject the neuroscientific argument" against moral responsibility.

Then the question behind the experiments. Suppose every choice did have prior causes in the brain. Whether that would make free will an illusion depends on what freedom is, and philosophers have argued that for centuries. Determinism, in the encyclopedia's definition, is "roughly speaking, the idea that every event is necessitated by antecedent events and conditions together with the laws of nature," and the entry adds that there is no agreement over whether it is true. Compatibilists hold that free will is compatible with determinism: a person acts freely when she acts from her own reasons and desires, without compulsion, even if those have causes. Incompatibilists hold that freedom requires that the outcome not be fixed in advance; those among them who believe we are free, called libertarians, hold that some choices are not determined by prior events. Neither position was invented to answer neuroscience, and neither is settled by it. A finding that brain activity precedes awareness would trouble a libertarian only if it showed the choice was fixed before it was made, and it doesn't show that.

And Scripture holds two things together. It speaks of God's purpose in all things: "He was delivered up by God's set plan and foreknowledge, and you, by the hands of the lawless, put Him to death" (Acts 2:23, BSB); "As for you, what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good" (Genesis 50:20, BSB). And it speaks to human beings as answerable: "I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing. Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19, BSB); "each of us will give an account of himself to God" (Romans 14:12, BSB). Paul puts both in one sentence: "continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God who works in you to will and to act on behalf of His good purpose" (Philippians 2:12-13, BSB).

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the experiments can establish.

1. Libet-type experiments measure brain activity that precedes a reported awareness of an urge to make a simple, arbitrary movement.
2. Whether that activity is a decision, a preparation, or something else is disputed among neuroscientists themselves, and the task differs from the deliberate choices on which responsibility turns.
3. Even if every choice had prior neural causes, whether that would rule out free will depends on whether freedom requires that choices be undetermined, a philosophical question that compatibilists and incompatibilists dispute.

So: neuroscience has not shown that free will is an illusion; it has shown that awareness of an urge can lag behind the brain's preparation, and the question of whether freedom and responsibility can coexist with prior causes remains philosophical, and among Christians, theological.

The weight sits on the second step. If the readiness potential were shown to be the decision itself, made before awareness in choices of every kind, the experiments would support the conclusion that conscious deliberation decides nothing, whatever view of freedom one held.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply lets Libet go and keeps the conclusion. The particular experiment may be flawed, the critic says, but the direction of the science is plain: more and more of what we do is explained by causes below awareness, and none of it has been found to need an uncaused choice. And the philosophical retreat to compatibilism only renames the problem. If my reasons and desires were themselves produced by genes, upbringing and circumstance I didn't choose, then the "freedom" to act on them is the freedom of a thermostat. Some philosophers, the encyclopedia records, hold on these grounds that no one is ever truly deserving of blame, with no neuroscience needed.

Part of that should be granted. Agency is smaller and more shaped than the proud picture of the self-made chooser allows, and Christians, of all people, should not defend that picture. But the reply has changed the subject. It no longer claims that neuroscience has shown free will to be an illusion; it claims that a philosophical argument does, and that argument is answered by philosophers in more than one way, as it was before Libet. The compatibilist answers that responsibility requires acting from one's own reasons, responsive to reasons, without compulsion, and that a thermostat has no reasons. The libertarian answers that some choices are not settled in advance, and that nothing in the science shows otherwise. Either answer may be wrong, but the question is open, and the skeptic's conclusion is one position in it, not a finding.

The Christian traditions have their own stake in this debate. Christians who hold that God ordains all things have usually been compatibilists, and the encyclopedia names Jonathan Edwards's *Freedom of Will* (1754) as an example; they have never thought that responsibility required an uncaused will. Christians who hold libertarian freedom have never thought it required a will untouched by sin or untaught by grace.

## What remains unresolved

What the readiness potential signifies is unresolved, as the encyclopedia says. Whether determinism is true is unresolved, and so is whether free will is compatible with it. How much of human action is under conscious guidance, and whether consciousness is needed for responsibility, is argued among philosophers and psychologists. And how God's purpose and human freedom fit together is a question the church has not settled.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on freedom, and this site takes no side between the main accounts. The Reformed tradition holds that God's purpose governs all things without destroying human agency. The Westminster Confession (1646) confesses that God "freely and unchangeably ordain[s] whatsoever comes to pass; yet so as thereby neither is God the author of sin, nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures," and that God "hath endued the will of man with that natural liberty, that is neither forced nor by any absolute necessity of nature determined to good or evil." Its hardest difficulty is to say how a choice God ordains is one for which a person is answerable, and how God is not the author of evil. The Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, as the Study Bible's note on Romans 9 sets them out, hold that God desires all to be saved and that grace goes before and enables a real response; Scripture's "who wants everyone to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4, BSB) is central to them. Their hardest difficulty is to say how God's purpose is sure if human responses are not determined. The note on Genesis 50 records how each tradition reads that chapter: Reformed theologians "speak of God's concurrence, ordaining the event without being the author of the sin," and Arminian and Wesleyan theologians "speak of God permitting the evil and overruling it for good." Thomist and other accounts stand beside these, as the note on Philippians 2 records.

This site's argued answer "Does God choose us, or do we choose him?" sets out the same two cases and doesn't choose between them, and this page agrees with it. What both traditions confess is that human beings are responsible before God, that no one can claim mercy as a right, and that the freedom that finally matters is a gift: "So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36, BSB).
