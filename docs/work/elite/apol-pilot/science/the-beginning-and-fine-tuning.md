# Christians say the Big Bang and the fine-tuning of physics point to God. Can't physics explain both without him?

## The short answer

Physics may yet explain some of it, and Christians should be careful not to claim more than the science gives. The Big Bang doesn't prove Genesis, and it doesn't prove that the universe had an absolute first moment: the theory describes the universe's development from a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago, and physics doesn't yet know what, if anything, came before. Fine-tuning is a finding that many physicists accept, unbelievers among them, though some dispute how far it reaches: that several constants and starting conditions of physics fall within narrow ranges that allow life. But it has serious replies, above all the possibility of a multiverse, and its probabilities are argued. What the two findings can do is narrower and still weighty. They make a universe that began and is fitted for life the kind of world a Creator would make, and the main alternative explanations either leave the deepest question untouched or move it back a step. They are evidence to weigh, not a proof.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection starts by taking the Christian argument seriously. William Lane Craig revived the kalam argument in 1979: whatever begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist, so the universe has a cause. Others argue from fine-tuning. Over the last century physicists have found that several numbers in the laws of nature, the strength of gravity and the energy of empty space among them, sit within narrow ranges that allow stars, chemistry and life. Change many of them modestly and the universe would hold nothing much at all. Design, the argument concludes, explains this better than chance.

The skeptic answers on both counts. Stephen Hawking and Leonard Mlodinow argued in *The Grand Design* (2010) that because a law like gravity exists, the universe can create itself from nothing, so no creator is needed to set it going. Lawrence Krauss made a similar case in *A Universe from Nothing* (2012). The Big Bang, on this view, is a feature of physics, not a doorway out of it.

Fine-tuning has a stronger answer still. Martin Rees, the Astronomer Royal, who holds no religious belief, wrote a whole book on the finely set numbers, *Just Six Numbers* (1999), and took seriously the possibility that our universe is one of a vast number with different values. If there are enough universes, some will allow life, and observers can only ever find themselves in one of those. Brandon Carter gave that observation its name, the anthropic principle, at a symposium in Kraków in 1973 marking Copernicus's five hundredth birthday. Put at full strength, the objection says that fine-tuning is a selection effect, not a sign, and that a designer is one more unobserved entity, and a far less economical one than a law we don't yet know.

## What is true in it

A great deal. The Big Bang theory does not by itself show that time had a first moment. It describes how the universe has developed from a very hot, dense state, and the equations that describe the earliest instants break down before they reach a beginning. What happened at or before that point is a question for a theory of quantum gravity that physics doesn't yet have. Christians who say "the Big Bang proves creation" claim more than cosmology says.

The church has made that mistake before. In an address of 22 November 1951 Pope Pius XII was widely read as saying that the new cosmology confirmed the creation of the world, and Georges Lemaître, the Catholic priest and physicist who first proposed that the universe began from what he called a primeval atom, is reported to have been uneasy with that use of his work. The details of the episode are disputed, but the lesson isn't. A faith that ties itself to a scientific model ties itself to the model's future.

The fine-tuning argument has its own difficulties. A 2001 paper in the journal *Mind* by three philosophers, McGrew, McGrew and Vestrup, argued that if a constant could in principle take any value without limit, no probability can be assigned to its falling in a narrow range, however narrow, and the argument's numbers lose their meaning. And the multiverse is not a dodge. Rees and others take it seriously as physics.

## The evidence

Start with the beginning, stated with its limits. In 1927 Lemaître argued that the universe is expanding, two years before Edwin Hubble's famous paper, and in 1931 he proposed that it began from a primeval atom. Fred Hoyle, who held a steady-state theory instead, called the idea "this 'big bang' idea" on BBC radio in 1949, and the name stuck. In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs in New Jersey detected the faint radiation left over from the early universe, published in 1965, and the steady state gave way. The Planck mission's results put the age of the universe at about 13.8 billion years. In 2003 the physicists Arvind Borde, Alan Guth and Alexander Vilenkin showed that a universe which has, on average, been expanding cannot be extended into an infinite past: its history, as their theorem describes it, has a boundary. Physicists still argue over whether quantum effects open a way around their result, and Sean Carroll is among those who have pressed that objection. So the honest summary is that the evidence is consistent with a beginning and the theorem is often read as pointing to one, but that physics hasn't settled it.

Then notice what the Christian claim needs and what it doesn't. Thomas Aquinas held in the thirteenth century that reason alone can't demonstrate that the world began in time; he believed it began because Scripture says so. For Aquinas the argument for God doesn't depend on a first moment at all. It asks why anything exists, at any moment, rather than nothing, the question Gottfried Leibniz called in 1714 the first we have a right to ask. That is why the Hawking and Mlodinow argument, even if every step of its physics is granted, doesn't reach the question. A law like gravity is not nothing. The philosopher David Albert made a version of this point against Krauss in a review in the *New York Times* in March 2012: the book never justifies calling its starting point, a quantum vacuum with laws of its own, nothing. The question of why there are such laws, and anything for them to govern, is left exactly where it was.

Then fine-tuning, stated at its strength. The sharpest case is the energy of empty space. Steven Weinberg, a Nobel laureate and an unbeliever, wrote in 1989 that the plainest calculation from physics puts it about 120 orders of magnitude above what observation allows, so something must cancel it to an astonishing precision. Fred Hoyle, no Christian either, wrote after his own work on how stars make carbon that a common-sense reading of the facts suggests that a superintellect had monkeyed with physics. Neither man drew a believer's conclusion. Both saw a fact that needs explaining. The astrophysicists Geraint Lewis and Luke Barnes set out the evidence, and the competing explanations, in *A Fortunate Universe* (2016).

There are four broad explanations: necessity, a deeper law that fixes the values; chance; a multiverse with an observer-selection effect; and design. Necessity, if it comes, moves the fine-tuning into the deeper law without removing it. Chance alone is what the argument says is unlikely. The multiverse is the serious rival. The philosopher Robin Collins has argued that any mechanism able to produce many universes with varied constants would need its own finely set laws to produce life-permitting ones at all, so the question moves up a level. A multiverse hasn't been observed, and may not be observable, but neither, a skeptic will rightly say, has God.

## The argument, laid out

This is an inference to the best explanation, and its conclusion is modest by design.

1. The universe as physics describes it is contingent: its existence and its laws are not explained by anything within it, whether or not it had a first moment, and its constants and starting conditions fall within narrow ranges that allow life.
2. The available explanations are a deeper necessity, chance, a multiverse with observer selection, or a Creator, and the first three either leave the existence of a lawful reality unexplained or move the fine-tuning to a further level.
3. A Creator who is rational and purposeful would be expected to make a lawful, intelligible universe fitted for living things, and that expectation is met.

So: the beginning and fine-tuning don't prove God, and they shouldn't be offered as if they did. They fit belief in a Creator well, and the main alternatives carry costs of their own. They are part of a cumulative case, not a demonstration.

The weight sits on the second step, because the multiverse is the strongest rival and the step claims that it doesn't escape the question. A reader who thinks a multiverse of the right kind could be a brute fact needing no further explanation will reject the step, and that is a reasonable place to disagree.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply turns the argument around. You say a multiverse needs explaining. So does God. If a mind able to design a universe is itself unexplained, you have explained the order of the universe by something at least as ordered and complex. Richard Dawkins pressed this in *The God Delusion* (2006): the designer would be at least as improbable as the design. And if you are willing to stop at God as a brute fact, why not stop at the universe? Bertrand Russell said in his 1948 radio debate with Frederick Copleston, "I should say that the universe is just there, and that's all."

That reply is serious, and the reason it doesn't succeed is old. The God of classical Christian theology is not a very complicated object inside some larger reality that needs its own explanation. He is confessed as the one who exists in himself, not by depending on anything else, and whose existence is the ground of everything that does depend. Whether there is such a being is exactly the question, and the argument from contingency on the site's God page takes it up. But the reply mistakes what is being proposed. A multiverse is a physical system, with laws and parameters of its own, and so it raises again the question of why those laws and parameters. A necessary Creator, if there is one, does not. A reader may reject the idea of a necessary being. He can't fairly say the two answers are on the same level.

As for stopping at the universe, it is a coherent choice, and some thoughtful people make it. The argument above doesn't make it irrational. It claims only that the existence of a lawful universe fitted for life is better explained than left unexplained, and that a Creator explains it. Scripture's own claim is no stronger and no weaker: "For since the creation of the world God's invisible qualities, His eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly seen, being understood from His workmanship" (Romans 1:20, BSB). That is a claim about what can be seen, not a proof to be extracted by calculation.

## What remains unresolved

Whether the universe had an absolute beginning is unresolved, and may remain so until physics has a theory of quantum gravity. Whether the probabilities in fine-tuning arguments can be stated rigorously is argued among philosophers, the McGrews' objection among the hardest. Whether any multiverse exists, and whether it could ever be tested, is open. And whether a necessary being is a coherent idea is one of the oldest disputes in philosophy. A reader who leaves all four open has understood the state of the question.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much weight these arguments can bear. Some make the kalam and fine-tuning central to their case; others, following Aquinas, rest more on the argument from contingency, which needs no beginning; others again think arguments from nature show little and rest the case on Christ. They differ on how to relate the Big Bang to the days of Genesis, a question the page on evolution and Genesis sets out without ranking the answers. What they confess together is the creed's first article: one God, the Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible. Whatever physics finds about the first moments, that confession is about why there is anything at all.
