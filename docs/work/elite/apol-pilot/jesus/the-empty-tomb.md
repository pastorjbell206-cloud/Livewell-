# Was Jesus really buried in a known tomb, and was it found empty, or is that a later legend?

## The short answer

This is the most disputed part of the historical case, and the doubts are serious. Rome often left the crucified unburied or threw them into common graves, and the first narrative of the empty tomb, Mark's, was written around 70. But the burial is in the summary Paul received within a few years of the events: "that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day." Jewish law required burial before nightfall, and the bones of a crucified man of the period have been found in a Jerusalem family tomb. The Gospels name the man who buried Jesus as a member of the council and make women the first witnesses, two details an inventor would be unlikely to choose. And as far as our sources show, the earliest opponents didn't say the body was still in the grave; they said it had been stolen. Historians still divide over the empty tomb. It doesn't prove the resurrection. It is a fact any explanation has to meet.

## The objection at its strongest

Bart Ehrman, who taught for many years at the University of North Carolina and describes himself as an agnostic, sets out the case in *How Jesus Became God* (2014). Crucifixion was meant to shame as well as kill. Crucified victims, he argues, were commonly left on their crosses to decay and be eaten by scavengers, and whatever remained might be thrown into a common grave; being denied a decent burial was part of the punishment. Jesus was executed by Pontius Pilate as a man who claimed to be a king, a charge of sedition, and Pilate was not known for mercy. It is hard to see why he would have made an exception and released the body for an honorable burial. John Dominic Crossan made a similar argument in *Jesus: A Revolutionary Biography* (1994). If Jesus was never laid in a known tomb, the stories of an empty one must have grown later.

The documents, on this view, show the growth. Paul's list of witnesses in 1 Corinthians 15, the earliest account of the resurrection, says nothing of women or of a tomb found empty. The first narrative of the empty tomb comes in Mark, written around 70, some forty years after the crucifixion, and in the oldest manuscripts Mark ends with the women fleeing and telling no one, which some critics read as Mark's own explanation for why no one had heard the story before. Matthew then adds a guard at the tomb and an earthquake; the second-century Gospel of Peter adds a centurion's name, seven seals and soldiers who watch Jesus brought out of the tomb. A story that keeps growing in the telling is the mark of legend, and the empty tomb, the objection concludes, is one of its products.

## What is true in it

Much of it. Crucifixion was public, slow and designed to terrify, and Roman practice did deny many of its victims any decent burial. The Syrian legate Varus crucified about two thousand rebels in Judea after Herod's death. Only one crucified man from Judea in that period has been identified among the bones found in its tombs, and one case can't show what was usual. Ehrman is right about that.

The documentary points are true too. Paul's list doesn't mention a tomb found empty or the women, and Mark is the first to tell the story. Matthew's guard story appears in no other Gospel, and many critical scholars, Raymond Brown among them in *The Death of the Messiah* (1994), have judged that it grew up in Christian circles to answer the charge of theft. Christians who treat every detail of the guard story as beyond question have claimed more than the evidence gives. And the argument from the women shouldn't be overstated either. Josephus was describing testimony in a court of law, and the Gospels are not court records.

## The evidence

Start with Paul, because the burial is older than any Gospel. The summary he passed on to the Corinthians, which most scholars, including many with no stake in Christianity, think took shape within a few years of the crucifixion, reads: "that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4, BSB). It doesn't narrate the tomb. But among Jews who believed in resurrection as the raising of the body, "buried" and then "raised" describe what happened to one body, and the summary doesn't need to say the grave was empty for its first hearers to understand that it was.

Then Jewish law and practice. The law of Moses said of an executed man hung on a tree, "you must not leave the body on the tree overnight, but you must be sure to bury him that day" (Deuteronomy 21:23, BSB). Josephus, describing an outrage of the war against Rome, when the dead were cast out unburied, contrasts it with his people's custom: "the Jews used to take so much care of the burial of men, that they took down those that were condemned and crucified, and buried them before the going down of the sun" (*Jewish War* 4.317, in William Whiston's translation). Craig Evans has argued that Roman governors respected this custom in Judea in peacetime, and that Roman law allowed the executed to be buried on request. In 1968 construction work in the Jerusalem neighborhood of Giv'at ha-Mivtar opened a rock-cut tomb, probably a family's, and one of its bone boxes held the bones of a man named Yehohanan, an iron nail still through his heel bone. Ehrman answers that one case tells us little about what usually happened, and he is right that it can't show a rule. What it does show is that a crucified man in Jerusalem in that period could be buried by his family.

Then the man who buried Jesus. Mark calls him "Joseph of Arimathea, a prominent Council member" (Mark 15:43, BSB), a member of the council some of whose leaders had taken part in handing Jesus over to Pilate. Luke adds that he "had not consented to their decision or action" (Luke 23:51, BSB). All four Gospels give him the burial. A Christian inventing a story to give Jesus an honorable grave would more likely have chosen a disciple than a member of that council, and a named man from a named town is a claim that could be checked. Brown, who doubted the guard story, judged the burial by Joseph very probably historical.

Then the women. All four Gospels say that Mary Magdalene, alone or with other women, found the tomb empty early on the first day of the week. In the same century Josephus summarized the law of testimony with the words "let not the testimony of women be admitted" (*Antiquities* 4.219, in Whiston's translation), and in the second century the pagan critic Celsus scoffed at the story's chief witness as "A half-frantic woman, as you state" (*Against Celsus* 2.55, in Frederick Crombie's translation). The formal list in 1 Corinthians 15 leaves the women out, perhaps for that reason. The Gospels put them first anyway. The criterion historians call embarrassment applies here: a detail that cost the storytellers something is unlikely to have been made up by them.

Finally, the opponents. Matthew says the chief priests paid the soldiers to say, "His disciples came by night and stole Him away while we were asleep," and that the story was still being told when he wrote (Matthew 28:13, 15, BSB). Justin Martyr, around 160, says the same charge was still being spread (*Dialogue with Trypho* 108). Tertullian, around 200, mocks the rumors that the disciples stole the body or that a gardener moved it to keep visitors off his lettuces (*On the Shows* 30). Every version explains why the tomb was empty. None says the body was still in it. This was an argument among Jews of the first century, Jesus' followers among them, about a Jewish teacher's grave, and Matthew's charge falls on particular leaders and the soldiers they paid, not on a people. The Study Bible's note on Matthew 28 treats it the same way, as a dispute between the first Christians and their opponents over why the tomb was empty, not whether it was. And the preaching began in Jerusalem within weeks, in the one city where a known grave could be visited.

## The argument, laid out

This is an inference to the best explanation, and it reaches a historical fact, not yet its cause.

1. Jesus was buried, very probably by Joseph of Arimathea, in a tomb whose place was known to his followers and to his opponents.
2. The first preaching that God had raised him took place in Jerusalem within weeks, and the earliest opponents we know of explained the empty tomb by theft rather than denying it was empty.
3. The earliest narrative's details, a council member burying Jesus and women discovering the tomb, are the kind that people inventing a story would avoid.

So: the tomb in which Jesus was buried was probably found empty on the first day of the week. That is a fact to be explained, and the next questions in this area take up the explanations.

The weight sits on the first step. If there was no known tomb, the second and third steps lose most of their force, which is why the burial is where Ehrman and Crossan press hardest, and where a reader should test the argument.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply turns the details against the argument. Joseph of Arimathea, it says, is exactly what an apologist would invent: a respectable insider who can give Jesus the burial Rome denied him, from a town no one could easily visit. The women's silence at the end of Mark explains why the story surfaced so late. And the theft charge is known only from Christian writers, Matthew and Justin, who were reporting their opponents in their own way. Nothing here, the reply concludes, takes us behind the Christian story to an independent fact.

Part of that should be granted. The theft charge does come to us through Christian sources, and that limits what it can prove. But the reply asks too much of each detail. An apologist inventing an honorable burial had better candidates than a member of the council some of whose leaders had handed Jesus over to Pilate, and Matthew had no reason to invent a rumor against his own side; he answered one his readers already knew. Mark's ending says the women were silent in their fear, but the angel had just told them to carry a message to the disciples and Peter, and Paul's summary, which is older than Mark, already moves from burial to raising on the third day. Most of all, the reply has to explain the burial clause in a tradition formed within a few years, the Jewish law and practice that make a burial before nightfall likely rather than strange, and a movement that preached a raised body in the city where the body was laid. Each of those can be doubted. Together they make the empty tomb more probable than not, and it has been accepted outside the church. Pinchas Lapide, an Orthodox Jew, went further and accepted the resurrection itself as an event, while not accepting Jesus as the Messiah.

## What remains unresolved

Whose tomb it was is not certain: Matthew calls it Joseph's own new tomb, and the others say less. Whether Matthew's guard story is history, legend or a mixture is argued among scholars who accept the empty tomb as well as among those who don't. How representative Yehohanan's burial was can't be known from one case. Whether Paul knew a story of the tomb found empty or only assumed it is debated. And an empty tomb, even granted, is not by itself the resurrection. Mary Magdalene's first thought, in John's account, was that someone had taken the body.

## Where Christians differ

Christians who confess the resurrection differ on the guard story, on how to fit together the details of the burial and the first visits to the tomb, and on how much weight the empty tomb should bear in the historical case. Some modern writers, Marcus Borg among them, have held that the truth of Easter doesn't depend on whether the tomb was empty or on what happened to the body. That isn't a second-order difference. The church has confessed from the beginning that Jesus was raised bodily, the same body that was buried, and Paul's own summary runs from "buried" to "raised" without a break. The details are open. The confession is not.
