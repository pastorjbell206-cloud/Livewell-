# Christians say the apostles died for their testimony, and that no one dies for a lie. How much do we actually know about how they died, and what would it prove?

## The short answer

Less than many Christians claim, and less than popular books suggest. For most of the Twelve the stories of their deaths come late, from a time when legend was growing, and they shouldn't be leaned on. For four early leaders the evidence is good: James the son of Zebedee, killed by Herod Agrippa about AD 44 (Acts 12:2); James the Lord's brother, stoned in Jerusalem about 62, as the Jewish historian Josephus records; and Peter and Paul, whose martyrdoms a letter from the church in Rome records before the end of the first century. Paul's list of those to whom the risen Jesus appeared names Peter, James and Paul himself, and includes the Twelve, among them James the son of Zebedee. What their deaths can show is narrow but real: they weren't lying. Men don't spend their lives and lose them for a sighting they know they invented. Their deaths can't show that they weren't mistaken, and the argument should never be made to carry more than that.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection was put with great force by Edward Gibbon in the sixteenth chapter of *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776). "If indeed we were disposed to adopt the traditions of a too credulous antiquity," he wrote, "we might relate the distant peregrinations, the wonderful achievements, and the various deaths of the twelve apostles: but a more accurate inquiry will induce us to doubt, whether any of those persons who had been witnesses to the miracles of Christ were permitted, beyond the limits of Palestine, to seal with their blood the truth of their testimony." In a note he added that in the time of Tertullian and Clement of Alexandria "the glory of martyrdom was confined to St. Peter, St. Paul, and St. James," and that it "was gradually bestowed on the rest of the apostles, by the more recent Greeks, who prudently selected for the theatre of their preaching and sufferings some remote country beyond the limits of the Roman empire."

The modern objection adds a point of logic. Suppose the apostles did die for their faith. People die for false beliefs in every age and every religion, and the sincerity of a martyr is no evidence that what he died for is true. Bart Ehrman, in *How Jesus Became God* (2014), grants, as the Study Bible's note on John 20 records his view, that the disciples sincerely had visions of Jesus after his death; on such views, as the note puts it, "the resurrection is a belief the disciples came to hold, not an event that happened to Jesus." If even a skeptical historian grants their sincerity, the argument from their deaths proves only what no one denies.

## What is true in it

Gibbon was right about the later stories, and Christians should say so. Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, reports that "according to tradition" Parthia was allotted to Thomas and Scythia to Andrew (*Church History* 3.1, in McGiffert's translation); the fuller accounts of how such apostles died come later still, and some popular books still present them as if they were history. The common claim that all the apostles but John were martyred, each refusing to recant, goes far beyond what the sources show. If that is what you were taught, you were taught what Christians have told one another for centuries, usually in good faith, and nothing in the faith depends on it. What can be shown is smaller, and it is enough for the point the story was meant to make.

It is true, too, that even the good evidence often says less than the popular argument needs. Acts says Herod "had James, the brother of John, put to death with the sword" (Acts 12:2, BSB), but not what he was asked or whether he could have saved himself; Luke says Herod "reached out to harm some who belonged to the church" (Acts 12:1, BSB), and politics may have been reason enough. The early letter that records Peter's and Paul's deaths says they died "through envy," not that they were offered life for a denial. And the logical point is simply correct. Sincerity is not truth. A martyr's death shows what he believed, not whether he was right.

## The evidence

Begin with what is well attested, and with the dates. James the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve and one of the three nearest to Jesus, was killed by Herod Agrippa I. Because Agrippa's death can be dated to AD 44, as the Study Bible's note on Acts 12 records, the execution falls shortly before it, about a dozen years after the crucifixion.

Peter's death is attested from three directions. John's Gospel, written late in the century, has Jesus tell him, "when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will dress you and lead you where you do not want to go," and adds, "Jesus said this to indicate the kind of death by which Peter would glorify God" (John 21:18-19, BSB). The letter of the church in Rome to the church in Corinth that we call 1 Clement, which the editors of the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* date, with some hesitation, about 97, after the persecution under Domitian, while granting that it may belong to the persecution under Nero, about 68, says that "Peter, through unrighteous envy, endured not one or two, but numerous labours and when he had at length suffered martyrdom, departed to the place of glory due to him" (chapter 5, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* translation). Eusebius quotes a Roman churchman, Gaius, of the time of Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, who could show visitors "the trophies of the apostles" at the Vatican and on the Ostian Way, and Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, who wrote that Peter and Paul "suffered martyrdom at the same time" (*Church History* 2.25). That Peter was crucified head downward is a later tradition, which Eusebius takes from Origen (3.1), and it should be held more loosely.

Paul's case is the clearest of all, because he describes his own sufferings. "Five times I received from the Jews the forty lashes minus one. Three times I was beaten with rods, once I was stoned, three times I was shipwrecked" (2 Corinthians 11:24-25, BSB). The same letter of Clement says that he was "seven times thrown into captivity," was "stoned," and "suffered martyrdom under the prefects," and Eusebius records the tradition that he was beheaded in Rome under Nero (2.25). Paul tells us why he bore it: "last of all He appeared to me also" (1 Corinthians 15:8, BSB).

James the Lord's brother is the most striking. During Jesus' ministry, John says, "even His own brothers did not believe in Him" (John 7:5, BSB). Paul's list of witnesses says, "Then He appeared to James" (1 Corinthians 15:7, BSB), and Paul met him in Jerusalem as "James, the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:19, BSB). Josephus, a Jewish historian who was not a Christian, tells in his *Antiquities* (20.9.1, in Whiston's translation), written in the 90s, that the high priest Ananus, in the gap between two Roman governors, "assembled the sanhedrim of judges, and brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others," and "delivered them to be stoned"; and that "those who seemed the most equitable of the citizens" objected, so that the king removed Ananus. The study notes date it to AD 62. Josephus says nothing of what James confessed; later and more legendary accounts, which Eusebius preserves from Hegesippus and others (2.23), have him die declaring his faith.

And there is the setting. A generation later, about 112, a Roman governor wrote to the emperor that he released accused Christians who "reviled the name of Christ," and punished those who would not (Pliny, *Letters* 10.96, in Melmoth's translation as revised by Bosanquet). By then, at least, a Christian's confession was a choice that could cost his life.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument from sincerity, and it reaches a narrow conclusion.

1. At least four leaders of the earliest church were put to death within about forty years of the crucifixion, on evidence from the first century: James the son of Zebedee, James the Lord's brother, Peter and Paul, all of them named or included in Paul's list of those to whom the risen Jesus appeared.
2. For years before they died they had proclaimed that claim publicly and suffered for it, and as men who said they had seen him themselves, they were in a position to know whether they were inventing it.
3. People die for mistaken beliefs, but people rarely, if ever, suffer for years and then die for a claim about what they themselves saw that they know to be false.

So: the leading witnesses of the resurrection very probably believed that they had seen Jesus alive after his death; that makes deliberate fraud on their part very unlikely, but it doesn't rule out honest mistake, and what they saw has to be judged on other evidence.

The weight sits on the second step. If there is no good evidence that these men kept up their claim at real cost over years, their deaths might show only that they were caught in the politics of their time.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the second step smuggles in more than the sources give. Acts gives no reason for James's execution. Clement says "envy." Josephus says James was charged as a breaker of the law. None of the sources has an apostle offered his life for a denial of the resurrection. Men can be killed for belonging to a movement without ever being asked to recant, and so the deaths may show nothing about what the apostles would have done if given the choice.

That is fair, and it is why the argument should rest on lives and not only on deaths. The case doesn't need a scene of refusal. It needs men who kept saying the same thing for years, publicly, when saying it cost them, and that is what the earliest sources show. Paul's own catalogue of lashes, beatings, a stoning and imprisonments was written while he was still preaching. Peter and the others, on Luke's account, were flogged in Jerusalem and "did not stop teaching and proclaiming the good news that Jesus is the Christ" (Acts 5:42, BSB). James the brother, a skeptic during Jesus' ministry, led the church in Jerusalem for years under the eyes of its authorities. A fraud can be sustained by a group for a time, but these men scattered across the empire, quarreled with one another, as Paul's letter to the Galatians shows, and none of them, on any source we have, ever said the appearances were invented.

What the argument cannot do is the other half of the objection, and it shouldn't pretend to. Ehrman grants their sincerity and holds that they had visions. The deaths of the apostles answer the fraud theory; they don't answer the vision theory. That has to be weighed on what the witnesses claimed and what else is known: the empty tomb, the variety of the witnesses, a persecutor and a skeptical brother among them, and the kind of claim they made. Other pages in this area weigh those.

## What remains unresolved

How most of the Twelve died is not known, and may never be. Whether Peter was crucified, and in what posture, rests on later tradition. Whether the earliest witnesses were ever offered their lives for a denial can't be shown from the sources. And the sincerity of the first witnesses, which the evidence does establish, leaves open the question of what they experienced.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much weight to put on the later stories of the apostles, which some traditions honor in their calendars and others treat as pious legend, and on how prominent the argument from martyrdom should be in making the case for the faith. Some lean on it heavily; others, with good reason, treat it as a modest support. What all confess is that the faith rests on the risen Christ, not on the courage of his witnesses, and that the witnesses themselves pointed past their own courage to him. "Last of all He appeared to me also," Paul wrote, and that was the whole of his reason.
