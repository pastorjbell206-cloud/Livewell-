# Isn't the virgin birth a legend, built on a mistranslation of Isaiah and borrowed from pagan stories of gods who fathered heroes?

## The short answer

Parts of the objection are true and should be granted. Isaiah's Hebrew word, *almah*, means a young woman, not necessarily a virgin, and the sign was first given to King Ahaz. Mark, John and Paul never tell the story. The ancient world did tell of great men fathered by gods. But the Greek word Matthew quotes was chosen by Jewish translators two or three centuries before Christ, so the church didn't invent the "mistranslation." Matthew and Luke tell the conception from two sides, Joseph's and Mary's, and agree on its core. The pagan stories are of gods who take women; the Gospels tell of no union at all. And Jewish Christians gained nothing by a story that invited the charge of illegitimacy, which opponents soon made. History can't prove a miracle. It can show that this is not the legend the objection describes. The church confesses that Jesus was "conceived by the Holy Ghost, born of the Virgin Mary."

## The objection at its strongest

The objection is as old as the Gospels' first critics. Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, set down the case of a Jewish teacher, Trypho, as Justin presents him: "The Scripture has not, 'Behold, the virgin shall conceive, and bear a son,' but, 'Behold, the young woman shall conceive, and bear a son.'" The prophecy, Trypho says, referred to Hezekiah, and the Christian story looks like the Greek fable in which "Perseus was begotten of Danae, who was a virgin; he who was called among them Zeus having descended on her in the form of a golden shower" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 67, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* translation). The pagan philosopher Celsus, as Origen quotes him, went further and said Jesus "invented his birth from a virgin," and that his mother was "turned out of doors by her husband, a carpenter by trade, because she was convicted of adultery" (*Against Celsus* 1.28, in Crombie's translation).

The modern form is careful and serious. Gerd Lüdemann, in *Virgin Birth?* (1998), concluded, as the Study Bible's note on Matthew 1 records, that Jesus was conceived in the ordinary way, possibly outside marriage. Jane Schaberg, in *The Illegitimacy of Jesus* (1987), argued, as the note on Luke 1 summarizes her argument, that Matthew and Luke inherited a tradition of an illegitimate conception and wrote of God's vindication of Mary. And the case rests on four pillars. Isaiah 7:14 in Hebrew says *almah*, "young woman," and Isaiah 7:16 ties the sign to Ahaz's own day: "before the boy knows enough to reject evil and choose good, the land of the two kings you dread will be laid waste" (Isaiah 7:16, BSB). Mark, the earliest Gospel, has no birth story and calls Jesus "the son of Mary" (Mark 6:3, BSB). Paul, the earliest writer, says only that God's Son was "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4, BSB). And the world around the Gospels told such stories of its great men: Suetonius reports that Augustus's mother fell asleep in Apollo's temple, that a serpent came to her, and that the emperor "was thought to be the son of Apollo" (*Augustus* 94, in Thomson's translation, revised by Forester). On this view a legend grew to honor Jesus as the world honored its emperors, and Matthew found a proof-text in a verse that never meant it.

## What is true in it

The facts about the words are true, and a Christian who denies them hasn't read the notes on his own Bible. On the Hebrew word, Trypho and the Jewish readers after him were right. *Almah*, as the note on Isaiah 7 says, "means a young woman of marriageable age, not specifically a virgin; the word for a virgin in the strict sense is betulah." The verse was first a sign to Ahaz, and modern Jewish scholarship and many Christian critical scholars, Raymond Brown among them in *The Birth of the Messiah* (1977), as the note on Isaiah 7 records, agree that Isaiah spoke of a child of his own time. Mark and John tell no birth story, and Paul never mentions one. And Trypho was right that the parallel had occurred to people: Justin himself, writing to the emperor, said that when Christians affirm Jesus "was born of a virgin, accept this in common with what you accept of Perseus" (*First Apology* 22).

## The evidence

Begin with the word. The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, was made by Jews in Egypt, probably in the second century BC, and it rendered *almah* with *parthenos*, usually "virgin," as the note on Isaiah 7 records. Matthew quotes that translation: "Behold, the virgin will be with child and will give birth to a son, and they will call Him Immanuel" (Matthew 1:23, BSB). The Jewish Greek translators of the second century AD, Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion, chose *neanis*, "young woman," the word Trypho reads. Whatever else is true, the reading Matthew used was not a Christian invention.

Then ask what Matthew is doing with it. He reads Scripture as a story that comes to its fulfillment in Jesus, and he does the same a chapter later with Hosea's line about Israel, "out of Egypt I called My son" (Hosea 11:1, BSB), which he applies to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:15). The note on Matthew 2 grants what Jewish readers have always said, that Hosea speaks of Israel, and that "Matthew's use is typological rather than predictive." Christians read Isaiah 7:14 in more than one way: some, with Brown, take it as a sign in Ahaz's day that Matthew read in the light of what the church believed; others, with Alec Motyer (1993), as the note on Isaiah 7 summarizes his case, point to the definite article, "the *almah*," to a sign announced as deep as Sheol or high as heaven, and to the titles the same child receives in Isaiah 8 and 9, which no ordinary child could bear. On either reading the order matters. Matthew didn't build the story out of Isaiah. He had a story and found it in Isaiah. A writer inventing a virgin birth from a prophecy would have needed a prophecy that plainly predicted one; on the Hebrew, this one doesn't.

Next, the two accounts. Matthew tells the conception from Joseph's side: Mary "was found to be with child through the Holy Spirit" before they came together, and an angel tells Joseph not to be afraid (Matthew 1:18, 20, BSB). Luke tells it from Mary's: "How can this be ... since I am a virgin?" and the answer, "The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you" (Luke 1:34-35, BSB). Gresham Machen, in *The Virgin Birth of Christ* (1930), and Brown argued, as the note on Matthew 1 records, that the two tell the story independently yet agree on the essentials, a betrothed virgin, conception by the Holy Spirit and birth in David's line, which points to an older tradition behind both. And within a generation of the Gospels, Ignatius of Antioch, writing around 110, called the Lord "truly born of a virgin" (*To the Smyrnaeans* 1) and counted "the virginity of Mary" among three mysteries "hidden from the prince of this world" (*To the Ephesians* 19).

Now the pagan stories, which deserve to be read and not waved away. Suetonius's tale of Augustus is of a serpent who comes to a sleeping woman, after which she "purified herself, as usual after the embraces of her husband." Danaë is visited by Zeus in a shower of gold. Celsus's Jew, as Origen reports, compared the Gospels to the fables of "Danae, and Melanippe, and Auge, and Antiope," and Origen himself mentions the story that Plato was the son of Apollo. In every one a god takes a woman. Justin, who granted the resemblance, also named the difference: "a virgin should conceive without intercourse," the power of God having "overshadowed her" (*First Apology* 33). Luke's account has no union and no god in disguise, only the Spirit's overshadowing, the language of God's presence over Israel's tent. Machen and Brown noted, as the note records, that first-century Judaism had no expectation of a virgin-born Messiah that would have invited invention.

Last, the charge of illegitimacy. Mark's "the son of Mary" is, as the note on Mark 6 says, unusual, "since a man was normally identified by his father," and in John his opponents retort, "We are not illegitimate children" (John 8:41, BSB), which some read as aware of a controversy. Celsus repeated the charge in the second century. Origen answered that the slander itself admits, against its will, "that Jesus was born of no ordinary human marriage" (*Against Celsus* 1.32). As the note on Luke 1 puts the point, Jewish Christians would hardly invent a story that exposed Mary to the charge Celsus made.

## The argument, laid out

This is a cumulative historical case. It aims to show that the virgin birth is not the legend the objection describes, not to prove the miracle.

1. The word Matthew relied on was the reading of a Jewish translation made before Christ, and Matthew read Isaiah as fulfilled in a story he already had, as he read Hosea, rather than building the story from the verse.
2. Matthew and Luke tell the conception from different sides, in accounts that show no sign of depending on each other, and agree on its core, and the conception was confessed as part of the faith by about 110.
3. The pagan stories are of gods who unite with women, first-century Judaism expected no virgin-born Messiah, and the story exposed its first tellers to the charge of illegitimacy, which opponents made.

So: the virgin birth is not best explained as a mistranslation, a borrowed myth or a pious embroidery; it is an early tradition, held from two sides, that its first tellers had good reason not to invent, and whether it is true turns on whether the God who raised Jesus could bring him into the world in this way.

The weight sits on the third step. If the stories of divine births were close enough to the Gospels' account, or if the first Christians had motives strong enough to invent it, the first two steps would show only an early legend.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the third step looks only at Jewish Christians, when the Gospels were written for a mixed and largely Greek-speaking church. Matthew and Luke wrote late in the first century, after the faith had spread among Gentiles who knew the stories of Augustus and the heroes. A story of a divine conception would honor Jesus in their eyes as the emperors were honored. The difference between a god's union and the Spirit's overshadowing, on this view, is the refinement of Jewish writers retelling a pagan pattern for their own scruples. And two independent accounts can still both depend on a legend that arose in the decades before them.

That reply is fair, and it can't be refuted with certainty. But its pattern doesn't fit the texts well. Matthew's account is Jewish through and through: a genealogy from Abraham, a righteous man who plans to divorce his betrothed quietly, dreams and an angel of the Lord, a fulfillment of Israel's Scripture. Luke's is written in the language of the Greek Old Testament and set among priests, the temple and the hope of Israel. Neither looks like a pagan story retold. And if the aim was to honor Jesus as the emperors were honored, the tellers chose a strange form for it, one that left his mother open to slander. The best Gentile parallel, Augustus's, came with dreams and portents to flatter a ruler; the Gospel story ends in a manger. Brown, a cautious critic, judged, as the note on Matthew 1 records, that the evidence that can be controlled scientifically cannot settle the question but leans more toward historicity than away from it. That is about as far as history goes.

## What remains unresolved

History can't prove a miracle, and this one, private by its nature, can't be checked as a public event can. Why Mark, John and Paul are silent is answered in more than one way: Mark begins with the adult Jesus, John with the eternal Word, Paul with the cross, and the note on Matthew 1 says their silence "is not denial," but it is still silence. How Isaiah 7:14 related to Ahaz's day is a question on which faithful interpreters differ. There is also a question inside the faith that skeptics have sharpened. Bart Ehrman, in a 2021 interview, as an automatic transcript of it records, pointed out that Luke ties Jesus' sonship to his conception, "So the Holy One to be born will be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35, BSB), and says nothing there of a Son who existed before, while John speaks of the eternal Word and tells no birth story; the creeds hold the two together. Christians have read Luke's "so" as naming what the conception shows rather than what it begins, but the difference in emphasis between the Gospels is real. And for a reader who doubts that God acts at all, the virgin birth will stand or fall with the resurrection, which is the better evidenced of the two and which other pages in this area weigh.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to read Isaiah 7:14, as a direct prophecy, as a double fulfillment or as a sign in Ahaz's day that Matthew read typologically. They differ on Mary's life afterward: Roman Catholic and Orthodox Christians hold her perpetual virginity, while most Protestants in modern times have read Matthew 1:25 and the mention of Jesus' brothers as implying a normal marriage later, as the note on Matthew 1 records, and Christians on both sides agree that Jesus was not Joseph's son by nature. Some Christian scholars have argued, as the notes on Matthew 1 and Luke 1 summarize Andrew Lincoln's *Born of a Virgin?* (2013), that the Gospel writers may not have meant a biological claim and that faith in the incarnation does not depend on it; the historic confession, in the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds, holds that they did. What the church confesses together is that Jesus Christ "was conceived by the Holy Ghost, born of the Virgin Mary," "incarnate by the Holy Ghost of the Virgin Mary, and was made man": the eternal Son whose life did not begin in Mary's womb but entered the world there.
