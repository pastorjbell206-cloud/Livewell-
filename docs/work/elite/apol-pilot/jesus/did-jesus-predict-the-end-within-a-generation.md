# Jesus said "this generation will not pass away until all these things have happened." Didn't he wrongly predict that the world would end within his hearers' lifetime?

## The short answer

The sayings are real, and they are hard. In Mark, Jesus says "this generation" will see "all these things," and in Mark and Matthew he says some standing with him will see the kingdom come before they die. Albert Schweitzer built his picture of Jesus on them: a prophet who expected the end at once and was wrong. The first Christians did hope for a quick return, and the New Testament itself shows them facing the delay. But "all these things" answers a question about the temple, which fell in AD 70, within a generation. The "coming with the clouds" is Daniel's picture of one who comes to God to receive a kingdom. And in the same breath Jesus says that no one knows the day or hour, "nor the Son." Christians read these sayings in more than one way, and this page chooses no scheme of the end. It argues that a failed date is not the only, or the most natural, reading of what Mark kept.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection has a long history and a great champion. Albert Schweitzer, in *The Quest of the Historical Jesus*, published in German in 1906 and in W. Montgomery's English translation in 1910, argued that Jesus lived entirely within the expectation of the end. He built on a saying Matthew records when Jesus sent out the Twelve: "you will not reach all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes" (Matthew 10:23, BSB). Jesus, Schweitzer wrote, "tells them in plain words (Matt. x. 23), that He does not expect to see them back in the present age." And "It is equally clear ... that this prediction was not fulfilled. The disciples returned to Him; and the appearing of the Son of Man had not taken place." On Schweitzer's reading Jesus then resolved to force the end by going to his death, and he gave that resolve its most famous image: Jesus "lays hold of the wheel of the world to set it moving on that last revolution which is to bring all ordinary history to a close. It refuses to turn, and He throws Himself upon it. Then it does turn; and crushes Him."

Bertrand Russell took the same sayings into his lecture of 1927, *Why I Am Not a Christian*: "He certainly thought that His second coming would occur in clouds of glory before the death of all the people who were living at that time." Among living scholars, Bart Ehrman holds a picture of the same family, that Jesus and his first followers were apocalyptic Jews who expected God's kingdom soon, as the English Wikipedia's article on the resurrection of Jesus reports his view.

At full strength the case runs this way. Three sayings, in three settings, put a time limit on the end: "there are some standing here who will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God arrive with power" (Mark 9:1, BSB); "you will not reach all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes" (Matthew 10:23, BSB); and, after a description of the sun darkened and the Son of Man "coming in the clouds with great power and glory," "this generation will not pass away until all these things have happened" (Mark 13:26, 30, BSB). "This generation" in Mark always means Jesus' contemporaries. The first Christians took him at his word: Paul wrote of "we who are alive and remain until the coming of the Lord" (1 Thessalonians 4:15, BSB). When the end didn't come, the church reinterpreted, and that reinterpretation is the history of Christianity. C. S. Lewis, a believer, called Mark 13:30 "the most embarrassing verse in the Bible."

## What is true in it

Much of it. "This generation," as the Study Bible's note on Mark 13 says, means Jesus' contemporaries every other time Mark uses it, and most scholars agree it has that sense here. The first Christians did hope that Jesus would return soon. The New Testament doesn't hide the strain this caused. Second Peter answers scoffers who ask, "Where is the promise of His coming? ... Ever since our fathers fell asleep, everything continues as it has from the beginning of creation" (2 Peter 3:4, BSB), and the note on that chapter grants that "the early expectation that Jesus might return soon had not been fulfilled on the timetable some had assumed, and critics used the delay to argue that there would be no return and no judgment." John's Gospel corrects a rumor that Jesus had said the beloved disciple would not die (John 21:23). And Christians have made the problem worse by fixing dates of their own; the long line of failed date-setters, the note on Mark 13 says, from William Miller's followers in 1844 to Harold Camping in 2011, "has been, in effect, a commentary on verse 32."

## The evidence

Start with the question Jesus was answering. Mark 13 opens with a disciple admiring the temple and Jesus replying, "Not one stone here will be left on another; every one will be thrown down" (Mark 13:2, BSB). The disciples ask, "Tell us, when will these things happen? And what will be the sign that they are about to be fulfilled?" (Mark 13:4, BSB). "These things" are first of all the temple's ruin. Herod's temple was destroyed by Rome in AD 70, about forty years later, within the lifetime of many who heard him, and Eusebius in the early fourth century set Josephus's account of the siege beside Jesus' words to show them fulfilled, as the note on Mark 13 records.

Then the coming with the clouds. The phrase is Daniel's: "I saw One like the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven. He approached the Ancient of Days and was led into His presence. And He was given dominion, glory, and kingship" (Daniel 7:13-14, BSB). In Daniel the figure comes to God, not down to earth. R. T. France, in *The Gospel of Mark* (2002), and N. T. Wright, in *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996), read Mark 13:24-27 in that sense, as the Son of Man's vindication made visible in the judgment on the temple, and the prophets' language of a darkened sun and falling stars as the language they used for judgments within history. Robert Stein, in *Jesus, the Temple and the Coming Son of Man* (2014), defends a mixed reading: "all these things" are the signs and the temple's fall in verses 5 to 23, while the coming in verses 24 to 27 belongs to the undated "that day" of verse 32. Most evangelical and Catholic commentators, the note records, keep a reference to the final return in the chapter.

Then the other two sayings. Mark places "some standing here" immediately before the transfiguration, "After six days" (Mark 9:2, BSB), and from Origen and Chrysostom onward many have read the mountain as at least a first fulfillment, when three of those standing there saw the kingdom's glory in Jesus; 2 Peter 1:16-18 speaks of that mountain as a sight of his power and coming. Matthew 10:23 has been read of the resurrection, when Jesus says all authority has been given him (Matthew 28:18), of the vindication in AD 70, as France reads it, or, by W. D. Davies and Dale Allison and by Ulrich Luz, of the final coming, describing, as the note on Matthew 10 puts it, "an unfinished task rather than a missed date."

Last, the sentence Mark sets beside verse 30: "No one knows about that day or hour, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32, BSB). Lewis, in the same essay in which he called verse 30 embarrassing, set the two sayings side by side and took Mark's keeping of both, decades after Jesus spoke, as the mark of an honest reporter. A man who believed his teacher had fixed the end within forty years, and who wrote when the time was nearly gone, kept a saying in which the teacher refused to fix it.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the sayings most naturally meant to Mark and his first readers, not about which scheme of the end is right.

1. "This generation" means Jesus' contemporaries, and "all these things" answers the disciples' question about the temple's ruin, which took place in AD 70, within that generation.
2. "Coming with the clouds" is Daniel's picture of one who comes to God to receive dominion, and so can describe vindication and enthronement as well as a return to earth; the end itself, in the same discourse, is the undated day no one knows.
3. Mark preserved the time limit and the confession that the Son does not know the day side by side, after much of the generation had passed, which he would hardly have done had he read verse 30 as a failed date for the final end.

So: the sayings do not clearly predict the end of the world within a generation; on readings that careful interpreters hold, what Jesus said would happen within a generation did happen, and he refused to date the final day.

The weight sits on the second step. If the coming of the Son of Man in Mark 13:26-27 can only mean the final return, then step 1 puts that return within a generation, and the objection stands.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the second step is ingenuity. Mark 13:26-27 doesn't stop at clouds; it has the Son of Man send his angels to "gather His elect from the four winds," which sounds like the end, and Paul shows that the first Christians expected exactly that soon. Matthew 10:23 and Mark 9:1 are separate sayings with the same short horizon. Three time limits, in three settings, look less like a misunderstood image and more like a conviction. On this reply, the vindication reading was devised by Christians who couldn't accept that Jesus had been wrong.

That reply has force, and it is why the question is still argued among scholars who share the church's confession. Two things can be said. First, the vindication reading is not modern special pleading: Eusebius read the chapter's warnings as fulfilled in AD 70 in the early fourth century, and the reading of Daniel's figure as coming to God rests on Daniel's own words. Second, each reading carries a burden, and the note on Mark 13 puts it fairly: "The critical view must explain why Mark kept a saying he supposedly knew had failed; the futurist must answer Mark's own use of genea for contemporaries; the preterist and mixed views must account for how closely verse 30 follows verses 24 to 27." None of the burdens is light. But the critical view's is not lighter than the others, and the evidence that Mark and Matthew kept these sayings while the church was already facing the delay, as 2 Peter shows, suggests they did not read them as Schweitzer did.

And if Schweitzer were right, the argument would not end where he ended it. A prophet who threw himself on the wheel of the world and was crushed is a tragic figure, unless the wheel did turn three days later. The question of the resurrection, which other pages in this area weigh, is the one his reading finally leaves.

## What remains unresolved

Which reading of Mark 13 is right is not settled, and careful interpreters who confess the same creed hold different ones. How Jesus' human knowledge, which he says did not include the day, relates to his deity is a mystery the church has confessed without fully explaining. And the history of how quickly the earliest Christians expected the end, and how they adjusted, is still argued.

## Where Christians differ

Preterist, futurist and mixed readings, as the note on Mark 13 says, "are held by careful interpreters who share the church's confession." Some, with Wright and France, read much of the chapter as fulfilled in AD 70; some, with the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), read "generation" as the Jewish people or as the generation that sees the final signs; some hold a mixed reading with Stein; some, with C. H. Dodd in *The Parables of the Kingdom* (1935), have stressed that the kingdom had already come in Jesus. This page takes no side among these schemes. What all confess, with the Nicene Creed, is that Christ "shall come again, with glory, to judge both the quick and the dead; whose kingdom shall have no end," and that verse 32, as the note says, "guards that confession from every attempt to date it."
