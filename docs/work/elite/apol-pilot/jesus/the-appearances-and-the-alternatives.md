# Couldn't the disciples' Easter experiences be explained by grief, legend or fraud, or by Jesus surviving the cross?

## The short answer

Each of those explanations has serious defenders, and each fits part of the evidence. Grieving people do see the dead, legends do grow, religious frauds do happen, and Jesus died sooner than the governor expected. But the evidence they have to explain is unusual. Within a few years of the crucifixion a list was circulating that named individuals and groups who said they had seen Jesus alive, among them a persecutor of the church and a brother who hadn't believed in him. They called what they saw resurrection, a word that meant a body raised, though their world had other words for visions of the dead, and they held to it at cost. Each natural explanation covers some of this and strains at the rest. That God raised Jesus explains all of it, and what keeps a reader from that explanation is usually not the evidence but a prior judgment about whether God acts.

## The objection at its strongest

Four explanations have been offered, and each deserves to be stated as its defenders would state it.

The first is that the disciples saw visions. Gerd Lüdemann, a German New Testament scholar who did not believe Jesus rose, argued in *The Resurrection of Jesus* (1994) that the body decayed and that the appearances were visions: Peter's born of grief and guilt after he had denied his master, Paul's of an inner conflict over the people he was persecuting, and the appearances to groups visions that spread among excited followers. Bart Ehrman suggests that a few followers, Peter, Mary Magdalene and probably James, and later Paul, had visions of the kind bereaved people have and persuaded the rest. The suggestion isn't cheap. In 1971 W. Dewi Rees, a general practitioner in mid-Wales, published a study in the *British Medical Journal* of 293 widowed men and women, and almost half reported hallucinations or illusions of their dead spouse, most often a sense of the spouse's presence, sometimes a sight or a voice, and some for years. Dale Allison, a Christian scholar, has weighed modern reports of apparitions of the dead, among them reports of several people seeing the same figure together, so the old reply that hallucinations are always private can't simply be assumed.

The second is that the stories are legend. The appearance stories grow as the Gospels get later. Mark, the earliest, ends in the oldest manuscripts with the women fleeing the tomb, and narrates no appearance at all. Matthew has a brief meeting and a mountain in Galilee. Luke's risen Jesus walks to Emmaus, invites the disciples to touch him and eats a piece of fish. John's invites Thomas to put his hand into the wound in his side. On this view the earliest belief was that Jesus had been taken up to God and seen in glory, and the solid, touchable body was added decades later to answer doubters.

The third is fraud. Hermann Samuel Reimarus, in fragments Gotthold Lessing published in the 1770s, argued that the disciples took the body and announced a resurrection. The charge is older than Reimarus. Matthew answers a story that the disciples stole the body by night, and in the second century Celsus offered, as the likeliest account of the first witness, "one who desired to impress others with this portent, and by such a falsehood to furnish an occasion to impostors like himself" (*Against Celsus* 2.55, in Frederick Crombie's translation).

The fourth is that Jesus survived. Rationalist critics of the early nineteenth century, H. E. G. Paulus in his life of Jesus of 1828 among them, proposed that Jesus fell into a deathlike trance on the cross, revived in the cool of the tomb, and met his disciples, who took his recovery for a resurrection. Albert Schweitzer reports that Friedrich Schleiermacher held a version of the view. Mark himself says that "Pilate was surprised to hear that Jesus was already dead" (Mark 15:44, BSB).

Put together, the objection says this: four natural explanations are on offer, each with real examples behind it, and any one of them, or some mixture, is more likely than a dead man rising.

## What is true in it

Each explanation names something real. Experiences of the dead are common among the bereaved and often comforting, some are reported as shared, and Rees judged them normal and helpful, not signs of illness. The disciples were bereaved, frightened and ashamed; Peter had sworn, "I do not know this man of whom you speak!" (Mark 14:71, BSB). Legends do grow, and the Gospels show development: the later accounts are fuller, and the second-century Gospel of Peter shows how much fuller a story could become. People have died for beliefs that were false, and the history of religion includes frauds. Pilate's surprise shows that Jesus died sooner than the governor expected. A Christian should grant all of this without hedging, and should grant too that no historical argument can rule every alternative out.

## The evidence

Any explanation has to account for what the earliest sources report. The oldest is the summary Paul handed on, which most scholars, including many with no stake in Christianity, think took shape within a few years of the crucifixion: "He appeared to Cephas and then to the Twelve. After that, He appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once, most of whom are still living, though some have fallen asleep. Then He appeared to James, then to all the apostles. And last of all He appeared to me also, as to one of untimely birth" (1 Corinthians 15:5-8, BSB). Paul had stayed fifteen days with Cephas and met James. "Most of whom are still living" invited anyone who doubted to ask them. E. P. Sanders, no apologist, wrote: "That Jesus' followers (and later Paul) had resurrection experiences is, in my judgement, a fact. What the reality was that gave rise to the experiences I do not know."

Two of the witnesses had no reason to see what they wished. Paul recalls "how severely I persecuted the church of God and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13, BSB). Of Jesus' brothers John writes, "For even His own brothers did not believe in Him" (John 7:5, BSB), yet James went on to lead the church in Jerusalem until he was stoned around 62.

Then the word they chose. Their world had a full vocabulary for seeing the dead. When Peter, freed from prison, knocked at the gate, the believers inside said, "It must be his angel" (Acts 12:15, BSB). When the risen Jesus stood among the disciples, Luke says they were "thinking they had seen a spirit" (Luke 24:37, BSB). Resurrection meant something else. N. T. Wright has argued at length that for Jews of the time it meant the bodily raising of God's people at the end of history, not the survival of a soul or the vision of a ghost. No one expected one man to be raised ahead of everyone while the world went on.

Then what normally happened when a messiah died. In 132 Simon bar Kosiba led a revolt against Rome and Rabbi Akiva hailed him as the messiah. When he died in 135, the sages took his death as proof that he was not, as Maimonides recorded a thousand years later. A messiah killed by the pagans had failed. Jesus' followers had that logic too, and they had a natural successor in James. No one called James the messiah. They said God had raised Jesus.

Now set each rival against this. Visions fit Peter's grief, and perhaps Mary's. They fit Paul poorly, since he wasn't mourning Jesus but hunting his followers, and James, who hadn't believed in him. The shared apparitions Allison weighs are real reports, but a vision of the dead, however vivid and however many see it, was the kind of experience the ancient world already had words for, and those words were not resurrection. The vision theory also needs the tomb to have been unknown or unchecked, which the burial evidence makes hard. Legend explains how details could grow, but the core is in Paul's list, formed within a few years and vouched for by people Paul knew; there wasn't time for the claim itself to be legend, only its telling. Fraud explains an empty tomb, but not the appearances to Paul and James, and it asks us to believe that men who knew where they had put the body suffered for the claim. Herod had James the brother of John "put to death with the sword" (Acts 12:2, BSB), Josephus records the stoning of James the brother of Jesus, and a letter from the church in Rome late in the first century says that Peter and Paul suffered martyrdom. Sanders set fraud aside as no serious explanation. Survival was answered in the nineteenth century by the skeptical critic David Friedrich Strauss: a half-dead man creeping out of the tomb, needing bandages and nursing, could never have convinced anyone that he had conquered death. It produces pity, not Easter.

## The argument, laid out

This is a historical argument, an inference to the best explanation, about why the first Christians said what they said.

1. Soon after Jesus' death, individuals and groups, among them Peter, the Twelve, the persecutor Paul and the unbelieving brother James, became convinced that they had seen him alive, and they said so in Jerusalem within weeks.
2. They described what they had seen as resurrection, a word that meant a body raised, though their world had other words for visions of the dead, and they held to the claim at cost.
3. Each natural rival explains part of this and strains at the rest: visions don't fit Paul, James or the word chosen; legend doesn't fit a list formed within a few years; fraud doesn't fit the appearances or the suffering; survival doesn't fit the conviction that death had been conquered.

So: that God raised Jesus bodily from the dead explains all of the evidence, and each natural explanation leaves part of it unexplained. That conclusion is open to anyone who hasn't ruled out, before looking, that God can act.

The weight sits on the third step. A reader who thinks one of the rivals, or a mixture of them, fits the evidence without strain will reject it, and that is where the argument should be tested.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that no one needs a single theory. Perhaps the body was put in a common grave and the place was soon unknown; Peter, wracked with guilt, saw Jesus in a vision; his conviction spread through a frightened group, as conviction does; Paul, troubled by the people he was hunting, had a vision of his own on the road; James came round when his brother's movement grew; and the stories of the body that could be touched were added later. Each piece has parallels. Any combination of ordinary events, however unusual, is more probable than a miracle.

That reply is the most serious one, and its last sentence is where the real disagreement lies. The combination works by assigning one explanation to each awkward fact, and each assignment is possible. But the pieces multiply, and the support for each is thin where it most needs to be thick: the common grave runs against the burial in Paul's earliest summary, Paul's inner turmoil is a guess about a man whose own letters show a zealot, and nothing explains why grief and visions produced the word resurrection rather than the words the culture already had. The reply's real strength is its final claim, that any natural story beats a miracle. Whether that is true depends on whether there is a God who might raise the dead. If there isn't, some natural story must be right, however strained. If there is, the explanation that fits the evidence is no longer ruled out in advance. History can't settle that question on its own, which is why the last page in this area takes it up.

## What remains unresolved

What exactly the witnesses saw is beyond the reach of history. Paul calls his own experience an appearance "as to one of untimely birth" and speaks of the risen body as a spiritual body, while the Gospels describe a Jesus who can be touched and who eats, and Christians have thought hard about how these fit. How many of the apostles died for the claim is uncertain; the evidence for most is later than for James, Peter and Paul. Whether the modern reports Allison collected are like the Easter appearances in kind is argued. And the historians who grant the experiences, Sanders among them, disagree about what gave rise to them.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much weight to put on historical arguments of this kind. Some build the case for the faith on them; others hold that faith rests on the apostles' testimony and the Spirit's witness, with history as a support. They differ on how to describe the risen body, which Paul says is "raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44, BSB) and which the Gospels show eating and bearing wounds. What the church has confessed together from the beginning isn't open: that Jesus was raised bodily, the body that was buried, and that he appeared to his witnesses. A reading that turns Easter into the disciples' inner experience alone has left that confession behind.
