# Bart Ehrman argues that Jesus' first followers believed God had exalted a man to heaven, and only later that he had been God from the beginning. Didn't the church turn a Jewish preacher into God?

## The short answer

Christian thinking about Jesus did grow. Nicaea's "of one substance with the Father" is not in the New Testament, and some early texts speak of Jesus being declared Son of God at his resurrection. Bart Ehrman builds on them a careful case that the first believers thought God had exalted a man, and he grants that within about twenty years Paul wrote of a Christ who existed before his birth, though, on his reading, as a divine being below God. So the question is what that early faith was. In Paul's letters from the 50s Jesus is prayed to in Aramaic, called on as the Lord of Joel's promise, given a part in creation and the words Isaiah kept for God alone, by Jews who confessed the Shema. Worship of that kind was there almost from the start. Whether it was right depends on who he was and on what happened at Easter.

## The objection at its strongest

Bart Ehrman set out the case in *How Jesus Became God* (2014). As the Study Bible's note on Mark 1 summarizes the book, early Christians first thought Jesus was exalted to divine sonship at his resurrection, then pushed the moment back to his baptism, as Mark does, and later to his birth and to a time before creation. In a 2021 interview, as an automatic transcript of it records, he said that Mark's Jesus is a man who was exalted and became a divine being, with no life before his birth, and that Paul, writing earlier, does speak of a Christ who existed before he became human, though not as God in the later, metaphysical sense. On his blog that year, in a post read aloud in full in a public recording, he argued that the name above every name gives Jesus, at his exaltation and not before, authority equal to God's, and that being equal is not being identical. John's Word who "was God" (John 1:1, BSB) is, on this account, the end of a development. And the cause of the whole movement, as the note on John 20 gives his account, was that the disciples sincerely had visions of Jesus after his death.

The texts the case rests on are real. Romans opens with a summary whose terse lines, as the Study Bible's note on Romans 1 says, sound like a formula: Jesus "was a descendant of David according to the flesh, and who through the Spirit of holiness was declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection from the dead" (Romans 1:3-4, BSB). Peter's first sermon in Acts ends, "God has made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36, BSB). The sermon in Acts 13 applies the psalm's "today I have become Your Father" (Acts 13:33, BSB) to the raising of Jesus. In Mark, the earliest Gospel, there is no birth story and no prologue, and Jesus is first called Son by a voice at his baptism (Mark 1:11). Jewish scholars add, as the note on Mark 1 records, that "Son of God" in the Hebrew Bible is a royal and covenant title that never implied divinity.

Ehrman also names the pattern early Christians found for a pre-existent Christ who was less than God: in the same post he wrote that Christians such as Justin Martyr identified him with the angel of the Lord, who speaks with the Lord's full authority and can carry his name while remaining his messenger. The case can be pressed into Paul's own words. The Galatians, he writes, welcomed him "as if I were an angel of God, as if I were Christ Jesus Himself" (Galatians 4:14, BSB), which a reader can take as placing Christ among the angels, as the highest of them: a great heavenly being, worshipped because God exalted him, but not the one God of Israel.

At full strength the objection says this. The Greek and Roman world was full of men made gods: Justin Martyr, writing to the emperor in his *First Apology*, could point to "the emperors who die among yourselves, whom you deem worthy of deification" (chapter 21, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* translation). The first Jewish followers of Jesus did something milder and more Jewish: they believed God had raised his Messiah and seated him in heaven. On Ehrman's account the next steps came quickly: within a generation the exalted man had become, for Paul, a pre-existent heavenly being. Equality with the Father in the full sense came later, until a council confessed him "very God of very God." On this view the church didn't discover who Jesus was. It promoted him, by stages.

## What is true in it

A great deal. Christian language about Jesus did develop. The Nicene Creed's "being of one substance with the Father" answered fourth-century questions in fourth-century words, and no one should pretend Paul wrote it. John's Jesus says "before Abraham was born, I am" (John 8:58, BSB); Mark's says nothing like it. Some second-century Christians did hold that Jesus was a man on whom the Spirit came at the Jordan; the note on Mark 1 records that Theodotus taught this in Rome and that Bishop Victor excommunicated him for it. And Christians have sometimes defended the deity of Christ by quoting John as if no historian had ever asked when John wrote.

It is also true that the texts Ehrman reads as exaltation say what they say. Romans 1:4 does speak of a declaration at the resurrection, Acts 2:36 does say God "made" Jesus Lord and Christ, and Acts 13:33 does tie "today" to the raising of Jesus. A reader who comes to them with the creed in hand and finds only the creed in them has not read them. And Ehrman is fairer to the early evidence than some critics allow: he doesn't say the high view took three centuries to appear, and his reading of Philippians 2 as describing a Christ who existed before his birth is, as the note on Philippians 2 records, the reading most interpreters hold.

## The evidence

The question is what the earliest layer looked like, and it can be dated. Paul's undisputed letters were written in the 50s, about twenty to twenty-five years after the crucifixion, and they quote material older still: the summary of the gospel he "received" (1 Corinthians 15:3), a passage in Philippians that many scholars, as the note on Philippians 2 says, have taken as a hymn older than the letter, and prayers in Aramaic, the language of the first Jerusalem believers.

In that earliest layer, exaltation and pre-existence stand side by side. The same Romans 1 that speaks of a declaration at the resurrection begins "regarding His Son" (Romans 1:3, BSB), so that, as the note on Romans 1 explains, the one already called Son is installed as Son-of-God-in-power. Paul writes that "God sent His Son, born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4, BSB), which assumes a Son to send. And in Philippians the same passage that says "God exalted Him to the highest place" first says that he was "existing in the form of God" and "did not consider equality with God something to be grasped, but emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in human likeness" (Philippians 2:6-7, 9, BSB). James Dunn, as the note on Philippians 2 summarizes his reading in *Christology in the Making* (1980), took this as a contrast with Adam that need not mean a life before birth; most interpreters, the note records, grant the echo but hold that the passage still describes a Son who existed before he became human.

Then there is worship, which is harder to explain away than titles. Paul ends a letter to Greek-speaking Corinthians with an Aramaic prayer, "Come, O Lord" (1 Corinthians 16:22, BSB), *maranatha*, an early prayer, as the note there says, passed from Aramaic-speaking believers to Gentile churches. Paul takes Joel's promise, "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD will be saved" (Joel 2:32, BSB), and applies it to Jesus: "Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:13, BSB), where the Lord on whom they call is the one confessed in verse 9 as "Jesus is Lord." He reworks the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4) into "one God, the Father, from whom all things came" and "one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things came" (1 Corinthians 8:6, BSB), giving Jesus a part in creation. And he takes God's oath in Isaiah, "Every knee will bow before Me" (Isaiah 45:23, BSB), from a chapter that says "I am God, and there is no other" (Isaiah 45:22, BSB), and writes that "at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Philippians 2:10, BSB). About AD 112 a Roman governor reported that Christians "addressed a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity" (Pliny, *Letters* 10.96, in Melmoth's translation as revised by Bosanquet).

Larry Hurtado, in *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003), argued from this worship that a high view of Jesus was early, as the Study Bible's note on Mark 1 summarizes him; he and Richard Bauckham, in *God Crucified* (1998), argue, in the note on Philippians 2's summary, that the language taken from Isaiah is "not a borrowing of language for a lesser figure" but includes Jesus within the identity of Israel's one God, "while the confession still ends 'to the glory of God the Father'."

## The argument, laid out

This is an inference to the best explanation, about the earliest Christian worship and not yet about whether it was true.

1. Within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion, Paul's letters speak of Jesus as the Son God sent, through whom all things came, who existed in the form of God, and they direct to him prayer, the calling on the name of the Lord and the words Isaiah kept for God alone.
2. Paul gives these not as his own innovations but as what the churches already shared, including Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea, and in the letter where he records how he opposed Peter to his face, he records no dispute about this.
3. Jewish monotheists of the period, so far as their surviving writings show, did not give that kind of worship to angels or exalted human beings.

So: within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion, among Jews who confessed the Shema, Jesus received the worship Israel kept for God alone, which a story of gradual promotion over generations does not explain; what that worship meant, and whether it was true, remain to be weighed.

The weight sits on the third step. If Jewish devotion of the time could give such honour to an exalted figure who was not God, the worship of Jesus proves less than the argument needs, and that is where the strongest reply presses. The argument also claims less than it may seem to. It doesn't rule out an earlier belief that saw only an exalted man, which the formula in Romans 1 may preserve; it shows how soon such a stage, if there was one, was overtaken.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that Judaism in the time of Jesus was not as tidy as the third step assumes. Daniel Boyarin, writing as a Jewish scholar in *The Jewish Gospels* (2012), argues, as the note on Daniel 7 records, that Daniel's vision presents a second divine figure enthroned beside the Ancient of Days and that this expectation was at home in Judaism before Jesus. If so, a Jew could honour an exalted figure as divine in some sense without thinking him the one God, and Paul's language may mean that. Ehrman's own reading points the same way: equal is not identical, and the angel of the Lord could carry God's name without being God. Paul could then have had a high view of Christ, even a pre-existent one, without holding the God of the creeds.

That reply is serious. Ehrman is right that Paul never confuses Jesus with the Father; the confession ends "to the glory of God the Father," and the creeds themselves never say that the Son is the Father. But three things can still be said. First, Boyarin's argument cuts against the slow-development story as much as for it. If a divine figure beside God was a Jewish idea before Jesus, the first Christians needed neither Greek hero cults nor centuries to call Jesus divine; they could do it at once, in Jewish terms, as the letters show. Second, Paul sets Christ apart from the angels rather than among them. He curses even "an angel from heaven" who preaches another gospel (Galatians 1:8, BSB), tells the Corinthians that "we will judge angels" (1 Corinthians 6:3, BSB), and counts angels among the created powers that cannot separate believers "from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38-39, BSB). In Galatians 4:14 the phrase "as if I were Christ Jesus Himself" reads more naturally as a step beyond "an angel of God" than as the same thing said twice. And Paul gives Christ a part in creation (1 Corinthians 8:6), which, as the note on John 1 summarizes Bauckham's and Hurtado's argument, Jewish monotheism reserved for God alone. Third, Jesus was given not only exalted titles but worship: prayer, the invoked name, the oath of Isaiah 45. The note on John 1 records that the rabbis later condemned belief in "two powers in heaven," a polemic Alan Segal traced (1977), which shows that Jewish teachers came to see such devotion as a breach of the Shema. For a Jew who confessed the Shema, worship of a being less than God would itself have been the breach, and Paul shows no sign of thinking he was committing one. The creeds came later. The worship they explain did not.

## What remains unresolved

How much of Nicaea's meaning Paul's words carry is a real question, and the fourth century shows that Christians who prayed to Jesus could still disagree about what he was. Whether Philippians 2 speaks of pre-existence is argued among believing scholars, as Dunn's reading shows, and so is how far Jewish ideas of exalted figures explain Mark's Christology. And the argument above reaches only the earliest Christian worship. Whether he was what they worshipped him as turns on what he claimed and on the resurrection, which other pages in this area take up.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to describe the path from the New Testament to Nicaea: some see the creeds as stating plainly what the apostles confessed, others as a true unfolding of what was there in seed. They differ on how to read Romans 1:4 and Philippians 2 in detail, and on how much weight to give the Gospel of John in a historical argument. What all who confess the historic faith hold together is the confession itself, that Jesus Christ is "the only-begotten Son of God, begotten of his Father before all worlds, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made," and truly man, and that his first followers were not wrong to call on his name.
