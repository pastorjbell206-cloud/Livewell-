# In Matthew, Mark and Luke, Jesus never says "I am God." Isn't the claim that he did a product of John's Gospel, written decades later?

## The short answer

It's true that in the first three Gospels Jesus never says in so many words that he is God, and historians differ over how much of John's long discourses reproduces his own words. But the first three Gospels show him claiming, in deed and in riddle, what Israel's Scriptures reserve for God. He forgives sins in his own right, and the scribes object that only God can. He calls himself lord of the Sabbath. He sets his own word beside the law. He says no one knows the Father except the Son. And at his trial he answers with Daniel's vision, the Son of Man at the right hand of Power coming with the clouds, and is condemned for blasphemy. Each saying can be argued over. Together they make a claim no ordinary teacher or prophet made, and John says openly what Mark shows in action.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection begins with a fact any reader can check. The sayings in which Jesus most plainly speaks of himself as divine, "before Abraham was born, I am" (John 8:58, BSB) and "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30, BSB), come from John, the Gospel usually dated last and treated as the most shaped by later reflection. Bart Ehrman, in *How Jesus Became God* (2014), argues, as the Study Bible's note on John 1 records his view, that John's high view of Jesus is a late development, not what Jesus or his first followers believed. As the note on John 10 puts it, some modern historical critics hold that the historical Jesus made no claim to divinity and that the discourses in John reflect the later church.

Take John away, the objection says, and look at what is left. In Mark, Jesus rebukes a man for calling him good: "Why do you call Me good? ... No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18, BSB). He says of the last day that "No one knows about that day or hour, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32, BSB). He calls himself "the Son of Man," and in Aramaic, as the note on Mark 2 records, "son of man" can simply mean a human being; Geza Vermes, in *Jesus the Jew* (1973), argued that it could be a modest way of saying "I," and Maurice Casey, in *Son of Man* (1979), that the references to Daniel were added by the early church. "Son of God," as the note on Mark 1 records, was a royal and covenant title in the Hebrew Bible that never implied divinity. On this reading the Jesus of history was a Jewish prophet, perhaps one who believed he was the Messiah, and the church, believing God had raised him, later put on his lips what it had come to believe about him.

## What is true in it

Much of it. Jesus never says "I am God" in Matthew, Mark or Luke, and Christians who answer this objection by quoting John alone have not answered it. John's Gospel says plainly that it was written so that its readers "may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (John 20:31, BSB), and its discourses are shaped with great care; scholars who believe what John says about Jesus still differ over how much of the wording is Jesus' own and how much is John's distillation of it. "Son of Man" can mean a human being. "Son of God" did not by itself mean God. The sayings in Mark 10:18 and 13:32 are real, and a Christian should feel their weight rather than hurry past them.

It is also true that some of the strongest scenes are disputed as history. No disciple, on the Gospels' own account, was inside the council when Jesus was questioned. Why his answer counted as blasphemy is itself debated: the Mishnah, as the note on Mark 14 records, defines blasphemy narrowly as pronouncing the divine name, which Jesus did not do, and some scholars think the charge was a pretext.

## The evidence

Set John aside, as the objection asks, and begin with Mark, the Gospel usually dated earliest. Early in it a paralyzed man is let down through a roof, and Jesus says, "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5, BSB). The scribes hear it as more than a word of comfort: "Why does this man speak like this? He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7, BSB). Jesus doesn't say that God has forgiven. He says that "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10, BSB) and heals the man to show it. Daniel Johansson, as the note on Mark 2 records, surveyed early Jewish sources and concluded that forgiveness was everywhere treated as God's prerogative, mediated by priests and intercessors; Tobias Hägerland argues instead that Jesus forgave as a prophet, and Vermes read the words within a Galilean holy man's authority. On either reading the claim is extraordinary, and Mark's scene doesn't have Jesus correct the scribes' inference.

In the same chapter Jesus says that "the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28, BSB), the day God himself hallowed at creation. Casey read "son of man" there as humanity in general, and E. P. Sanders, in *Jesus and Judaism* (1985), argued that disputes over the Sabbath were the kind observant Jews had among themselves. The point is not that one saying settles it. It is that the pattern recurs across very different kinds of material. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus quotes the commandment given at Sinai and sets his own word beside it: "But I tell you" (Matthew 5:22, BSB). He says that "something greater than the temple is here" (Matthew 12:6, BSB). In a saying that Matthew and Luke share, he says, "No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him" (Matthew 11:27, BSB). He asks how the Messiah can be David's son when David calls him "Lord" in Psalm 110 (Mark 12:35-37). And he warns that whoever is ashamed of him, "the Son of Man will also be ashamed of him when He comes in His Father's glory with the holy angels" (Mark 8:38, BSB).

The trial gathers these threads. The high priest asks, "Are You the Christ, the Son of the Blessed One?" Jesus answers, "I am, ... and you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:61-62, BSB). The words join Psalm 110:1, "Sit at My right hand" (BSB), to Daniel's vision of "One like the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven," who is given "dominion, glory, and kingship, that the people of every nation and language should serve Him" (Daniel 7:13-14, BSB). The high priest tears his clothes and calls it blasphemy. Darrell Bock, in *Blasphemy and Exaltation in Judaism and the Final Examination of Jesus* (1998), argued, as the note on Mark 14 records, that claiming to sit at God's right hand and to come as judge was a blasphemous claim to share God's honor, and Adela Yarbro Collins has argued similarly. The Gospels say that some of the Jerusalem authorities handed Jesus over; Raymond Brown concluded, as the note on Mark 15 records, that some did take part, while the decision and the execution were Roman, and the Second Vatican Council's *Nostra Aetate* (1965) taught that what happened cannot be charged against all Jews then living or against Jews today.

Then John. He says outright what Mark shows: Jesus was "calling God His own Father, making Himself equal with God" (John 5:18, BSB), and his opponents say, "You, who are a man, declare Yourself to be God" (John 10:33, BSB). C. H. Dodd, as the note on John 5 records, found an older parable, of a son apprenticed to his father, inside the discourse of John 5. Whatever one concludes about John's wording, his Gospel is not a different Jesus from Mark's. It is the same claim, made in a different voice.

## The argument, laid out

This is an inference to the best explanation, about what Jesus claimed, using the earliest Gospels without relying on John.

1. In Mark and in the sayings Matthew and Luke share, Jesus claims to forgive sins in his own right, to be lord of the Sabbath, to set his word beside the law, to know the Father as no one else does, and to be the Son of Man who will sit at God's right hand and come with the clouds.
2. In their setting, those are claims to do and to be what Israel's Scriptures reserve for God, and Mark shows his hearers taking them so.
3. These sayings are spread across different kinds of material and different sources, and they are tied to the conflict that led to his death, so they are unlikely all to be the later church's invention.

So: though Jesus does not say "I am God" in the first three Gospels, the earliest sources show him claiming a place that belongs to God alone, and John's Gospel states openly what Mark shows in action.

The weight sits on the third step. If the Son of Man sayings and the trial scene were all created by the church, the first two steps describe Mark's theology and not Jesus' claims.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is Casey's and Ehrman's together. Mark was written around forty years after the crucifixion by a believer. The Daniel references could have been added by the church, as Casey argued; no disciple heard the trial; and a Gospel writer who believed Jesus was exalted would naturally tell his story so that Jesus claims what the church claimed for him. On this view the pattern is the church's, not Jesus'.

That reply is fair, and it can't be refuted saying by saying, since nearly every one of these sayings has been doubted by someone. But the pattern is harder to dissolve than any single saying. It appears in a healing story, a Sabbath dispute, the Sermon on the Mount, a saying in material Matthew and Luke share, a riddle about Psalm 110 and a trial scene, sources and forms that critics usually treat as independent. Something about Jesus made the Jerusalem authorities and the Roman governor treat him as a danger, and he was crucified, on the Gospels' account, under a charge that named him "King of the Jews." A prophet who claimed nothing for himself fits that ending less well than a man whose claims about himself were heard as blasphemy.

The same Gospel also keeps sayings that a church inventing claims would hardly have written. "Why do you call Me good?" and "nor the Son, but only the Father" survived in Mark though they were awkward for believers. C. S. Lewis, a believer, called the saying about "this generation" in the same chapter "the most embarrassing verse in the Bible," and the next question in this area takes it up. A tradition that preserved those sayings was not simply putting its creed in Jesus' mouth.

## What remains unresolved

How Jesus understood "Son of Man," and whether he meant Daniel's figure every time, is still argued. How much of John's discourses gives his words and how much John's own rendering of their meaning is a question believing scholars answer differently. And a historian can conclude that Jesus made these claims without concluding that they were true; on the claims alone, the options are that he was mistaken, that he was deceiving, or that he was right, and only the question of the resurrection, which other pages in this area weigh, presses beyond them.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to read John's discourses as history, on how much weight the Son of Man sayings can bear, and on how to hold Jesus' confession of ignorance in Mark 13:32 together with his deity: some speak of the Son's knowledge as man limited in his humility, others of what he chose not to reveal. What all who confess the historic faith hold is the creed, that Jesus Christ is "very God of very God" and was "made man," fully God and fully human, and that what he claimed was true.
