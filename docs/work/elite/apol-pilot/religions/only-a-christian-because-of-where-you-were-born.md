# Aren't you only a Christian because of where you were born? If you'd grown up in another country, wouldn't you hold its religion instead?

## The short answer

Very possibly, and the honest Christian should admit it. Where and to whom a person is born shapes what he first believes, about God and about almost everything else. John Stuart Mill made the point in 1859 and John Hick made it the center of his case for pluralism, and they were right about the fact. But the fact doesn't show that the belief is false. It applies just as much to the skeptic, whose doubts also have a time and a place, and the cause of a belief is a different question from whether it is true. What birthplace rightly demands is that every person test what he was given, and the Christian faith, which began among people who had to leave the religion of their fathers to hold it, asks for exactly that. It rests on a public claim, that Jesus rose, which anyone from anywhere can weigh.

## The objection at its strongest

John Stuart Mill put the objection in *On Liberty* (1859) in a sentence that has never lost its sting. The ordinary believer, he wrote, trusts the opinion of his own world, "his party, his sect, his church, his class of society," and "it never troubles him that mere accident has decided which of these numerous worlds is the object of his reliance, and that the same causes which make him a Churchman in London, would have made him a Buddhist or a Confucian in Pekin." And Mill drew the moral at once: "ages are no more infallible than individuals; every age having held many opinions which subsequent ages have deemed not only false but absurd."

More than a century later the philosopher John Hick made the same observation the hinge of his case that no single religion can be the true one. As the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on religious diversity quotes him, "religious allegiance depends in the great majority of cases on the accident of birth," and so it seems implausible that "being born in our particular part of the world carries with it the privilege of knowing the full religious truth." John Loftus turned it into a test in *The Outsider Test for Faith* (2013), as the site's Honest Answer "What if Christianity is wrong?" reports it: the Christian looks at Hindu or Muslim belief with a skeptic's eye and finds it wanting, so let him turn the same eye on his own.

At full strength the objection runs this way. If a belief would have been different had you been born somewhere else, and you can't show that your birthplace gave you better access to the truth than anyone else's gave them, then your belief tells us about your upbringing, not about God. The Christian in Alabama, the Muslim in Cairo and the Hindu in Varanasi each hold, with equal sincerity, the faith of their grandparents. It would be a remarkable coincidence if the one born in Alabama happened to be right.

## What is true in it

The fact is true, and Christians should say so without hedging. Most people first believe what their families and neighbors believe, and the Christians who grew up in churches are no exception. Many have never asked whether their faith is true, only whether it is theirs. A faith held only because it was handed down is held for a reason that would have made them something else elsewhere, and that should trouble them.

Loftus's test is fair, too, and the site's Honest Answer on whether Christianity is wrong accepts it. A Christian who judges every other faith by the evidence and exempts his own has not been honest. Scripture itself doesn't let him exempt it. Paul told the Thessalonians, "test all things. Hold fast to what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21, BSB), and Luke praised the Bereans because they "examined the Scriptures every day to see if these teachings were true" (Acts 17:11, BSB), testing even the apostle who brought them the message.

## The evidence

Start with the logic. That a belief has a cause in someone's upbringing tells us how he came to hold it. It doesn't tell us whether it is true. A child raised by mathematicians will more likely believe that there are infinitely many primes than a child raised elsewhere, and the belief is no less true for that. Philosophers call the mistake of judging a belief by its origin the genetic fallacy. The objection doesn't quite commit it, since Hick's point is subtler: that a belief so closely tied to birthplace gives its holder little reason to think his own birthplace the one that got it right. But that subtler point has to be applied evenly.

It reaches everyone. Mill's sentence about the Churchman in London and the Buddhist in Pekin is true of the Victorian liberal as well; his confidence in the progress of reason belonged to a particular country and age, and his own line about the fallibility of ages applies to it as well. Unbelief, too, is common in some times and places and rare in others; it has a time and a place like any faith. As the Stanford Encyclopedia sets out the reply made by Alvin Plantinga and others, the correlation between birthplace and belief is real and should give everyone pause, but a person needn't weigh it in isolation from all his other reasons, and it doesn't show that pluralism, or unbelief, is the one view exempt from it. The pluralist and the atheist also hold views most of the world rejects, views their own time and place made more likely.

Then look at where the Christian faith began. It didn't begin as an inherited religion at all. Its first believers were Jews who had to be persuaded that a crucified man was the Messiah, which was, as Paul wrote, "a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, BSB). Paul himself had been "extremely zealous for the traditions of my fathers" (Galatians 1:14, BSB) and persecuted the church until he changed his mind. The Gentiles who came next left the gods of their cities, the very thing Mill's believer never does. Even Abraham's story begins with a command to leave: "Leave your country, your kindred, and your father's household" (Genesis 12:1, BSB), and Joshua reminded Israel that their fathers beyond the river "worshiped other gods" (Joshua 24:2, BSB). The faith has always said that no one is a child of God by birth. John's Gospel speaks of "children born not of blood, nor of the desire or will of man, but born of God" (John 1:13, BSB).

Nor was it ever meant to belong to one place. At its first public preaching, Luke says, the crowd in Jerusalem ranged from "Parthians, Medes, and Elamites" to "Cretans and Arabs," and they asked in astonishment how each heard the message "in our own tongues" (Acts 2:9-11, BSB). The faith was translated from the start, into the languages and lives of people who had no share in the culture it began in, and it has been carried since into peoples who first met it as strangers. A belief tied only to the place of one's birth wouldn't travel that way. That proves nothing about its truth, but it does show that the faith doesn't depend on being born in any particular place.

And the claim on which the faith rests is not the kind that depends on where you stand. It is not that Christians have had a feeling that others lack, but that a particular man, executed in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate, was raised from the dead. That claim is open to anyone, from any country, who will weigh the evidence for it, and the Honest Answer on whether Christianity is wrong says that this is exactly the outsider's test the faith should welcome.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument that birthplace is a reason to examine a belief, not a reason to dismiss it.

1. If the fact that a belief correlates with birthplace undercuts that belief, it undercuts every belief about religion that correlates with birthplace, the pluralist's and the unbeliever's included.
2. Whether a belief is true is settled by the reasons and evidence for it, not by the causes that first brought someone to hold it.
3. Christianity rests on a public claim about an event, that Jesus was raised from the dead, which can be weighed by anyone from anywhere, and the faith itself asks each person to test what he was given.

So: the correlation between birthplace and belief is a reason for every person, Christian or not, to examine what he holds; it isn't a reason to think Christianity false, and the question it leaves is whether the evidence for the resurrection is good.

The weight sits on the first step. The skeptic's best reply is that his view is not like the believer's, because he reached it by reasoning, not by inheritance. That reply is weighed next.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that unbelief isn't a rival tradition but the absence of one. The skeptic doesn't hold a faith that varies with birthplace; he declines all of them for the same reason, lack of evidence, and so the outsider's test, applied evenly, favors him. He is the one who has stepped outside.

There is something to this, and a Christian should grant it: the person who has examined his beliefs, whatever they are, stands differently from the one who never has. But no one stands outside every tradition. The judgment that miracles don't happen, or that religious experience proves nothing, or that the material world is all there is, is also a view with a history, more common in some places and ages than in others, and it too can be held without examination. The test, applied evenly, doesn't favor any side before the evidence is weighed. It sends each person to the evidence. That is all the Christian can ask, and it is what the faith asks for itself.

Mill is still right about one thing. The believer who never asks whether his faith is true has a faith that "mere accident has decided." The answer is not to drop it but to test it. Many people have done that and found the case for Christ stronger than the case against him, and many have done it and walked away. Both are better than never asking.

## What remains unresolved

Why God allows such unequal access to the gospel, so that some hear it from childhood and others never hear it at all, is a real question, and this library takes it up on its page about those who never heard. How far belief of any kind is in a person's control, and how far it is formed in him by his upbringing, is debated by philosophers and psychologists alike. And whether the evidence for the resurrection is strong enough is left to the pages that weigh it.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how faith is passed on: some baptize infants and raise them inside the covenant, trusting that faith grows within a family and a church; others baptize only those who confess the faith for themselves. Both, at their best, hold that faith must at last be a person's own, and both have seen children raised in the church who never made it theirs. What all confess is that faith is a gift of God, "not by works, so that no one can boast" (Ephesians 2:9, BSB), that no one is a Christian by birth, and that the God who "made every nation of men" (Acts 17:26, BSB) "is not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:27, BSB).
