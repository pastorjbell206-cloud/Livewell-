# Aren't all religions basically the same, different paths up one mountain to the same God?

## The short answer

The great religions share more than many Christians admit: prayer, mercy to the poor, a moral law that sounds alike in many tongues, and saints whose lives put the rest of us to shame. But they don't say the same thing about the things they care about most. They differ on whether the ultimate reality is a person, on whether Jesus died on a cross and rose, and on what salvation is. The most careful pluralist, the philosopher John Hick, granted all of that and answered that the ultimate reality lies beyond every concrete description the religions give of it, so that each great faith is a valid path to it and none is the only one. That answer is serious, but it isn't neutral. It tells every believer what his faith really means, from a place none of them stands. So the question is not whether to be humble but which account of the religions is true, and Christianity points to a claim that can be tested, that Jesus rose from the dead.

## The objection at its strongest

The pluralist case is not the lazy thought that nothing matters. At its best it is a generous and carefully argued account of why the religions look the way they do, and its best defender was the philosopher John Hick. In *An Interpretation of Religion* (1989) he argued that there is one ultimate reality, which he called the Real, and that, in his words as the Stanford Encyclopedia's article on the philosophy of religion quotes them, the great world faiths "embody different perceptions and conceptions of, and correspondingly different responses to, the Real from within the major variant ways of being human." As the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on religious diversity sets out his case, Hick held that the differences between the faiths are best seen as the different ways different cultures have conceived and experienced the one ultimate reality. In an article of 1984 he put it plainly: each great religion "constitutes a valid context of salvation/liberation; but none constitutes the one and only such context."

He gave reasons, and they are not weak. The first is what the same article calls transformational parity. A religion that saves, Hick held, doesn't merely issue a ticket to heaven; it begins in this life "the transformation from self-centeredness to Reality-centeredness," and as far as anyone can observe, Christians, Jews, Muslims, Hindus and Buddhists are changed in this way about equally. The second is the plain fact that most people hold the faith of the place they were born, which this library treats on a page of its own. The third is that the whole human race doesn't interpret experience through one pattern of thought but through several, each grown within a great culture, and once that is seen, Hick wrote, a "pluralistic theory becomes inevitable."

The popular form of the case is an old parable. Several blind men touch an elephant: one feels the trunk and says it is a snake, one the leg and says it is a tree, one the side and says it is a wall. Each is partly right and none sees the whole. So it is, the pluralist says, with the religions. Each has touched something real. The one mistake is to think your part is the whole.

## What is true in it

A great deal. Christians have often spoken of other faiths with contempt and without having read a page of them. They've called devout neighbors idolaters while knowing nothing of their prayers. That is not faithfulness. It is ignorance wearing the clothes of conviction, and it has done harm.

The religions do share real goods. When a would-be convert asked the rabbi Hillel, in the time of Herod, to teach him the whole of Judaism while he stood on one foot, Hillel answered, as the Jewish scholar Israel Abrahams tells the story in his short book *Judaism*, "What thou hatest do unto no man; that is the whole Law, the rest is commentary." Christians can only say yes to that. Jews, Christians and Muslims pray to one God who made the world, and they give to the poor as a duty. Paul, standing among the philosophers of Athens, did not begin by mocking their altars. He said, "Men of Athens, I see that in every way you are very religious" (Acts 17:22, BSB), quoted their own poets with approval, and told them that God "is not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:27, BSB).

And the Christian doesn't see everything. Paul himself wrote, "Now we see but a dim reflection as in a mirror" (1 Corinthians 13:12, BSB). Whatever the Christian answer to pluralism is, it can't be the claim that Christians have God figured out.

## The evidence

Begin with what the religions actually say, in their own words. Christians confess that Jesus of Nazareth was crucified under Pontius Pilate and raised bodily on the third day, and Paul staked everything on it: "if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile; you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, BSB). The Qur'an, in Mohammed Marmaduke Pickthall's translation, says of Jesus that "they slew him not nor crucified him, but it appeared so unto them" (Qur'an 4:157). Muslims read the verse within their own long tradition of interpretation, and the common confession is that Jesus was not crucified. Judaism holds that the Messiah has not yet come, since the age of peace he was to bring has not come; the Study Bible's note on Mark 16 records that Jewish readers have generally held exactly this. These are not three angles on one fact. They are three answers to the same question about the same man, and at most one of them can be right.

The differences run deeper than Jesus. Christians, Jews and Muslims confess a personal God, who knows, wills, speaks and judges. Among the Hindu traditions, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's article on God and other ultimates describes them, the school of Shankara has generally been read as holding the ultimate, Brahman, to be impersonal, while Ramanuja, its first great critic, held Brahman to be the Divine Person; Buddhist traditions speak of emptiness and of nirvana. Salvation, too, means different things: in the Christian confession, being made right with God by grace and raised to a life that doesn't end; in other traditions, *moksha* or *nirvana*, a liberation of another kind. Hick saw all this clearly. He wrote, as the Stanford Encyclopedia quotes him, that "the differences of belief between (and within) the traditions are legion."

So the pluralist can't say that the religions teach the same thing. Hick never did. What he said, in a sentence of *An Interpretation of Religion* that the same article quotes, is that "None of the concrete descriptions that apply within the realm of human experience can apply literally to the unexperienceable ground of that realm." The traditions' pictures of God are human responses to a Real beyond them all, true in the sense that they lead people from self toward it. That is a coherent view. But notice what it costs. It tells the Muslim and the Christian, who disagree about whether a man died on a cross, that their disagreement doesn't decide the way to the Real. It tells the Christian that the incarnation is not literally true, as Hick came to read it; the same article reports that he reinterpreted it so that Christians are not committed to the "literal truth" of God becoming flesh. And it tells the Hindu who loves a personal Lord, and the one who denies there is one, that the Real is neither personal nor impersonal as either conceives it. Hick's defenders answer, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's article on mysticism puts it, that he offers a second-order theory about the religions, not an account their members would necessarily endorse. That is fair as far as it goes. But a theory about what every religion is really doing still tells each believer that his own account of it is mistaken.

The parable of the elephant shows the same thing. It is told from the place of someone who isn't blind, who sees the whole elephant and can tell that each of the others has only a part. A pluralist can answer that the teller needn't see the elephant at all, only that the blind men disagree and that their lives bear similar fruit. That answer is better, and it is closer to Hick's own, but it gives up the parable's picture of one who sees the whole, and it leaves the question of which account of the elephant is true where it was. And the Stanford Encyclopedia reports a related criticism from inside the pluralist debate: Mark Heim argued that pluralists such as Hick are inclusivists in disguise, since they recognize only one path to salvation, the turn from self to Reality, and measure every religion by it. Hick's test shows the same difficulty, and without any discredit to him. The virtues the encyclopedia gives as his mark of a true path, love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control, are word for word the list Paul calls "the fruit of the Spirit" (Galatians 5:22-23, BSB), and the encyclopedia reports critics who think any such ethical standard as arbitrary as the exclusivist's. That is no charge of bad faith. It shows that even a generous measure has to come from somewhere.

## The argument, laid out

This is a deductive argument about whether the religions can all be true, followed by a judgment about what pluralism itself claims.

1. The great religions make claims about the same matters, the nature of the ultimate, whether Jesus was crucified and raised, and what salvation is, and on these matters some of their central claims contradict one another.
2. Two claims that contradict each other about the same matter can't both be true.
3. The pluralist answer, that the conflicting teachings are not literally true but are culturally shaped responses to one Real, is itself a substantive claim about ultimate reality, denied by the central teaching of each tradition as its own believers understand it.

So: the religions are not all the same and can't all be true as their followers mean them; pluralism doesn't escape the choice but is one more particular view, to be weighed on its evidence like the others.

Logic gives the second premise, and Hick himself grants the first. The weight sits on the third step, because the pluralist's best reply is that his view isn't one more religion at all, only a humble hypothesis about them. That reply is weighed next.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is Hick's own. The theologian Gavin D'Costa charged that Hick was unfaithful to the religions, and Hick answered him in print ("The Possibility of Religious Pluralism: A Reply to Gavin D'Costa," 1997). His defence, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's article on mysticism puts it, is that he offers a second-order philosophical theory about the religions, not a rival religion or an account of them their members must endorse. The philosopher Philip Quinn, as the Stanford Encyclopedia sets out his view, pressed a gentler version: since our concepts are shaped by culture, a believer can hold that whatever we know of the ultimate is known through our own categories, and so thin his theology to the few claims he must keep. That isn't arrogance; it's modesty.

There is something right here, and Christians should keep it. Every human concept of God falls short of God, and the church has always said so; the creeds confess a mystery, not a diagram. But a hypothesis about the religions can be true or false like any other claim, and this one carries a heavy load. It holds that there is a Real, that it is beyond every concept, that the great traditions all respond to it, and that each is mistaken about it wherever it claims to know it as it is. None of that can be checked from inside any tradition, and none can be checked from outside them all, since no one stands there. Modesty about our concepts is a virtue. A theory that makes every believer's central confession false is not modest. It is confident in a different place.

And here the Christian claim has an odd strength. It doesn't rest finally on a religious experience, which anyone might interpret through his culture, but on an event: that a man was executed outside Jerusalem and raised. That claim can be weighed with the tools historians use, and this library sets out the case for it in its pages on the resurrection. If it is false, Christianity is false, and Paul said so first. If it is true, then the faiths are not equal paths up one mountain, because God has come down it.

## What remains unresolved

How much truth and goodness other religions hold, and how to account for the holiness Christians meet in devout neighbors of other faiths, are questions Christians still answer in more than one way. Whether the change of life Hick pointed to really is the same across traditions is debated; the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that some argue the parity is only apparent, and others that the same change happens in people committed to wholly secular causes, which would weaken it as evidence for any religious view. And a historical argument for the resurrection, however strong, persuades only someone willing to weigh it, which no page can make a reader do.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to regard other religions. Some see in them real but partial knowledge of God, prepared for and fulfilled in Christ, as Paul's speech at Athens suggests; others stress the distance between human religion and God's own self-revelation. Catholic teaching since the Second Vatican Council has held, in *Lumen Gentium* (1964), that those who through no fault of their own do not know the gospel but seek God sincerely may be saved, as the Study Bible's note on John 14 records; many Protestants speak more cautiously. They differ, too, on how God deals with those who never heard of Christ, which this library treats on a page of its own. What all confess together is the historic faith of the creeds: one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, Maker of heaven and earth; Jesus Christ, crucified and risen; and salvation in him alone, a gift of grace, so that no one may boast.
