# What about the people who never heard of Jesus? Would a just God condemn them for being born in the wrong place?

## The short answer

If this question comes to you because of someone you loved who has died, the closing paragraph of this page is written for you, and nothing here speaks of any person's fate. The historic Christian confession doesn't teach that God condemns anyone for where he was born. Christians confess that whoever is saved is saved by Christ alone, that God wants all people to be saved, and that the Judge of all the earth will do what is right. Scripture shows people judged by the light they had, and people rescued by Christ who never knew his name, but it doesn't say how God deals with each person who never heard. Christians answer that in more than one way, and the main answers are set out below in their holders' terms, each with its hardest question. This page takes no side among them. It argues only that the objection, at its strongest, strikes at a view the historic confession doesn't teach.

## The objection at its strongest

The question that unsettles believers most is often not whether God exists but whether he is fair. A woman lived and died a thousand years ago in a village where the name of Jesus had never been spoken. She was honest, she loved her children, she prayed to whatever she knew of God. What becomes of her?

Philosophers have made this into an argument. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on religious diversity sets out the case made by the philosopher Kenneth Himma in "Finding a High Road: The Moral Case for Salvific Pluralism" (2002). A perfectly good God, Himma argues, would not punish people who are not morally to blame. But people who hold non-Christian beliefs are generally not to blame for holding them: no argument that every reasonable person must accept shows any religion's path to be the right one, every great tradition can answer the challenges brought against it, and a person's basic religious beliefs are, to a large extent, beyond the direct control of his will. So a just God could not deny salvation to devout people of other faiths, and any religion that says he does is false.

John Hick, the most influential defender of religious pluralism, added the fact underneath it. The religion a person holds, he wrote in *God Has Many Names* (1980), as the same article quotes him, depends "in the great majority of cases" on "where he was born." If salvation depends on believing in Jesus, it seems to depend on the luck of birth, and a God who saved people by luck would not be just.

## What is true in it

The moral premise is true, and Scripture states it before any philosopher did. When God was about to judge Sodom, Abraham stood before him and pressed: "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" (Genesis 18:25, BSB). God didn't rebuke the question. He answered it, and let Abraham press it again. A God who condemned people for what they could not possibly have done would not be the God Abraham was speaking to.

The fact is also true. Most people who have ever lived never heard the name of Jesus. Some Christians have answered this with a shrug, as if the fate of most of the human race were a footnote to the doctrine of salvation, and some have spoken of the dead of other nations with a certainty Scripture never gave them. Both are wrong. If the Christian answer to this question were "they are all lost because they were born in the wrong place," the objection would stand.

## The evidence

The objection runs two claims together that Scripture keeps apart. One is that no one is saved except by Christ. The church confesses that without hedging: "Salvation exists in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given to men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12, BSB), and "there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5, BSB). The other claim is that no one is saved except by knowing Christ's name and trusting him in this life. That second claim may be true, and many Christians hold it. But it is a different claim, and Scripture itself shows the difference.

Abraham lived long before Bethlehem, yet "Abram believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, BSB). Hebrews says of him and the other faithful of Israel that "All these people died in faith, without having received the things they were promised. However, they saw them and welcomed them from afar" (Hebrews 11:13, BSB). Every Christian tradition holds that they were saved by Christ, by a work done centuries after they died. Scripture also speaks with respect of people outside Israel: Melchizedek, "priest of God Most High" (Genesis 14:18, BSB); Job of Uz, whom God calls "blameless and upright" (Job 1:8, BSB); and Cornelius, a Roman officer who "prayed to God regularly" before he heard the gospel (Acts 10:2, BSB).

Scripture says, too, that judgment is measured by the light a person had. "All who sin apart from the law will also perish apart from the law, and all who sin under the law will be judged by the law" (Romans 2:12, BSB), and Gentiles who never had the law "show that the work of the law is written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15, BSB). Jesus said of the servant who did wrong without knowing his master's will that he "will be beaten with few blows" (Luke 12:48, BSB). No one in Scripture is condemned for never hearing a name. The question it raises is whether a person, judged by the light he had, and having fallen short of it, as Paul says all have (Romans 3:23), can be rescued by a Christ he never knew.

On that, Christians answer in more than one way, and each answer should be heard as its holders give it.

The first answer is often called restrictivism. It holds that a person must hear the gospel and trust Christ in this life to be saved, and it reads Paul's chain at face value: "How then can they call on the One in whom they have not believed? And how can they believe in the One of whom they have not heard? And how can they hear without someone to preach?" (Romans 10:14, BSB). Its holders add that no one is condemned for not hearing, but for sin against the light he had, and that God may bring the gospel to anyone who seeks him, as he sent Peter to Cornelius with "a message by which you and all your household will be saved" (Acts 11:14, BSB). Its hardest question is the one its defenders know best: the multitudes who lived and died with no messenger in reach.

The second answer is often called inclusivism. It holds that no one is saved apart from Christ, but that his work may reach some who never knew his name and who answered, in faith, the light God gave them. Its best-known defender, as the Stanford Encyclopedia describes him, is the theologian Karl Rahner, who held that God, who is love and wants all to be saved, can apply the fruit of Christ's death and resurrection even to those who never heard of him, as he did for the faithful of Israel before Christ came. Clark Pinnock defended a form of it in *A Wideness in God's Mercy* (1992), and the Second Vatican Council's *Lumen Gentium* (1964) affirmed that those who through no fault of their own do not know the gospel but seek God sincerely may be saved, as the Study Bible's note on John 14 records. Its hardest question is why the New Testament presses so hard on hearing and believing, and why even devout Cornelius still had to be told.

Some Christians hold a third answer, that everyone will meet Christ and be able to answer him at death or after it, reading 1 Peter 4:6, "the gospel was preached even to those who are now dead," as a door the church should not close. Its hardest question is Hebrews 9:27, that man is appointed "to die once, and after that to face judgment" (BSB), and the silence of most of Scripture about any later chance.

And some have hoped that in the end God will restore all. Origen in the third century and Gregory of Nyssa held such a hope, and its holders today point to God's purpose "to reconcile to Himself all things" (Colossians 1:20, BSB). The church's councils and most of its confessions have not received it. It was anathematized at Constantinople in the sixth century, though historians dispute whether that sentence belongs to the acts of the Fifth General Council of 553 or was passed by the bishops gathered there before the council opened; either way, it has generally been rejected by the church's councils and confessions, as this library's page on hell sets out. Its hardest question is the plain words of final division in the Gospels, "they will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46, BSB). It is stated here as the church has generally judged it, as a hope outside its confessions, and without contempt for those who hold it.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the Christian confession actually teaches on this question, and whether the objection reaches it.

1. Christians confess that whoever is saved is saved by Christ alone, and that God wants all people to be saved.
2. Scripture shows people saved by Christ who never knew his name, and people judged by the light they had and not by a message they never heard, and it does not say how God deals with each person who never heard of Christ.
3. A just God does not condemn anyone for failing to do what he could not have done.

So: the Christian confession does not teach that anyone is condemned for being born where Christ was never named; the objection strikes a view the historic confession doesn't teach, and the real question, how the one Savior reaches those who never heard, is one faithful Christians answer in more than one way.

The weight sits on the second step. If Scripture did teach that everyone who never heard is lost because he never heard, the objection would stand against the confession itself. The reply below presses exactly there.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that this is a distinction without comfort. Grant that no one is condemned for not hearing but for sin. If all have sinned, and only those who hear of Christ can be rescued, then whether a person can be rescued still depends on where he was born. That is Himma's point in another form, and restrictivism, at least, can't escape it by calling the cause of condemnation sin.

That reply is fair, and it lands on one Christian view, not on the confession. Inclusivists answer it by denying its premise, that only those who hear can be rescued. Restrictivists answer it in more than one way. Some, as the site's Honest Answer on those who never heard reports, have argued that God, who knows what every person would freely do, may have so ordered the world that no one who would have believed is left without the chance; critics answer that this can't be shown true. Others, as the Stanford Encyclopedia notes of many exclusivists, simply confess that God's ways are above ours in this. A reader may find none of these answers sufficient. But the question is then a dispute among Christians about how grace reaches people, not a proof that the God of the creeds condemns people for their birthplace.

And Christians are not left with nothing to say. Scripture names the one who will judge: "the Father judges no one, but has assigned all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22, BSB), the same one who said, "the one who comes to Me I will never drive away" (John 6:37, BSB). The Judge is not a stranger to the question. He crossed every distance himself and went to a cross rather than spare himself. Whatever he does with those who never heard, he will not do it from a distance, and he will not do it unjustly.

## What remains unresolved

Which of these answers is right is not settled by the creeds, and this page doesn't settle it. The fate of children who die before they can believe anything is not answered in a direct sentence in Scripture. David said of his infant son, "I will go to him, but he will not return to me" (2 Samuel 12:23, BSB), and the Stanford Encyclopedia reports that the more common Christian belief today is that those who die in early childhood enter God's presence; that is a hope resting on the character of the Judge, and it should be stated as hope. And no answer removes the church's duty. If Christians do not know that those who never heard are safe, then telling them is not optional.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on this question, and the answers above are held by faithful believers who confess the same creeds; this page takes no side among restrictivism, inclusivism and the hope of a meeting with Christ after death. What all confess together is that salvation is in Christ alone, that God "wants everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4, BSB), and that the Judge will do what is right.

If you are reading this because of someone you loved who has died, whether they never heard of Christ or never believed, hear this before any argument. No page, preacher or tradition knows another person's heart at the end, and this one will not guess. Judgment belongs to Christ, who wept at a friend's grave.
