# Other religions, and those who never heard

One of these pages asks about people who never heard of Jesus, and some readers come to it because of someone they loved who has died. For anyone in that place, the pages on grief (/wisdom/grief) and on heaven and eternity (/wisdom/heaven-and-eternity), a pastor, and Find Help (/help) are better places to begin than an argument, and nothing in this area speaks of any person's fate.

Most people now live among neighbors of other faiths, and the question they meet is not abstract. Isn't one religion as good as another? Isn't it arrogant to say that Jesus is the only way? What becomes of the billions who never heard of him? Aren't Jews and Muslims more faithful to the one God than Christians are? And isn't anyone's faith simply the accident of where he was born? These are fair questions, and some of the people who press them hardest know the world's religions better than many Christians do.

These pages answer them in the same way the rest of the library does. Each sets out the objection in its strongest form, in the words of those who hold it, from the philosopher John Hick's case for pluralism to John Stuart Mill's sentence about the Churchman in London and the Buddhist in Pekin. Each grants what is true in it, including the contempt and ignorance with which Christians have often spoken of other faiths and the wrongs the church has done to Jews and Muslims. Other faiths are described from their own sources, in terms their own adherents would sign, and where the Qur'an or the rabbis are quoted, it is from a named public-domain translation, with the reminder that their adherents read these texts within their own traditions. Then each page argues the Christian answer, says what the argument doesn't show, and names the questions on which faithful Christians disagree.

Two convictions run through the area. The first is that the religions really do differ, about God, about Jesus and about salvation, and that saying so honestly is a form of respect, not a failure of it. The second is that the Christian claim is not that Christians are better than anyone, but that God has acted in Jesus of Nazareth, crucified and raised, and that salvation is a gift in him that no one earns and no one is born into. Whether that claim is true is a question of evidence, and the library's area on the historical Jesus and the resurrection takes it up.

## The questions

1. **Aren't all religions basically the same, different paths up one mountain to the same God?** Hick's pluralism at full strength, the parable of the elephant and what it assumes, what the religions actually say about God, Jesus and salvation, and why pluralism is one more particular view.
2. **Isn't it arrogant to say that Jesus is the only way to God, and that billions of sincere believers in other faiths are wrong?** John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 where they stand, why every view of the religions excludes others, the difference between a claim and the way it is held, and the church's sins in carrying it.
3. **What about the people who never heard of Jesus? Would a just God condemn them for being born in the wrong place?** The moral argument, what Scripture says and doesn't say, the main Christian answers in their holders' terms, each with its hardest question and no side taken, and a word for anyone grieving.
4. **Jews and Muslims both confess one God and refuse to worship Jesus. Isn't Christianity the faith that strayed from the God of Abraham?** Judaism and Islam described from their own sources, what Christianity shares with each and where it truly differs, the church's wrongs against Jews named and rejected, the views of Israel's place set out without choosing, and the earliest Christians' Shema.
5. **Aren't you only a Christian because of where you were born? If you'd grown up in another country, wouldn't you hold its religion instead?** Mill, Hick and the outsider's test, the genetic fallacy and its subtler cousin, a faith that began among converts, and why birthplace is a reason to examine a belief, not to dismiss it.

## Words these pages use

- **Pluralism**: the view that the great religions are equally valid responses to one ultimate reality, none of them the one true way.
- **Exclusivism**: the view that one religion's central claims are true and those that contradict them are false; on salvation, often called restrictivism when it holds that a person must hear the gospel and believe in this life to be saved.
- **Inclusivism**: the view that salvation comes only through Christ, but that Christ may save some who never knew his name.
- **Universal restoration**: the hope that God will in the end reconcile all people to himself; held by Origen and Gregory of Nyssa and by some since, anathematized at Constantinople in the sixth century (whether by the Fifth General Council of 553 itself is disputed among historians), and generally rejected by the church's councils and confessions.
- **Tawhid**: in Islam, the oneness and uniqueness of God, which is central to the faith.
- **Shema**: the confession of Deuteronomy 6:4, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One," which Jesus called the first commandment and which Judaism placed in its public prayer from early times.
- **Torah**: in Judaism, the law of Moses, with the oral tradition the rabbis set down in the Mishnah and the Talmud.
- **Supersessionism**: the teaching, also called replacement theology, that the church has taken Israel's place as God's people; one of several Christian views of Israel's place, which many churches now reject.
- **Genetic fallacy**: the mistake of judging whether a belief is true by how a person came to hold it.
