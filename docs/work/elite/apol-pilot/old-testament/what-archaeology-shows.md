# Archaeologists have found no trace of the exodus, a Jericho without walls, and little sign of a great kingdom under David and Solomon. Does archaeology confirm the Old Testament, or undermine it?

## The short answer

Neither, in the way the question usually means. From the ninth century BC on, inscriptions written by Israel's neighbors and enemies name kings and events the Old Testament records: Omri of Israel on the stone of Mesha of Moab, the "house of David" on a stele found at Tel Dan in 1993, Hezekiah shut up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage" in Sennacherib's annals, and the tunnel Hezekiah cut, with its builders' own inscription. Where these sources overlap with the Bible, they agree on the main facts and differ in detail, as independent sources do. For earlier periods, the patriarchs, the exodus, the conquest and the size of Solomon's kingdom, the evidence is thinner and argued over, and it neither proves the biblical accounts nor shows them false. Claims of proof and claims of disproof both say more than archaeology can.

## The objection at its strongest

Books for believers have long promised that the spade keeps proving the Bible; A. H. Sayce's *Fresh Light from the Ancient Monuments* (second edition, 1884) was subtitled *A Sketch of the Most Striking Confirmations of the Bible*. The objection reverses the story. On the questions that matter most, it says, the spade has come up empty or worse. No trace of a great exodus has been found in Egypt or Sinai; Jericho, on Kathleen Kenyon's dating, which this area's page on Jericho says most archaeologists hold, had no walled city when Joshua would have come; and the united kingdom of David and Solomon, the golden age of Israel's memory, has proved hard to find. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, in *David and Solomon* (2006), argued, as the Study Bible's note on 2 Samuel 7 summarizes them, "that tenth-century Judah was a thinly settled highland chiefdom and that the Bible's picture of a kingdom was magnified later." The monumental gates at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer that an earlier generation credited to Solomon, because 1 Kings 9:15 names those cities among his works, are dated by archaeologists who hold a lower chronology, Finkelstein among them, to the century after him, as the note on 1 Kings 9 records.

At full strength the objection says that the Old Testament becomes checkable history only where the archaeology begins to match it, in the ninth century, under the dynasty of Omri, and that everything before is national memory written down centuries later. And the matches, it adds, are the kind any late writer with access to royal records could produce: names of kings and battles, not the miracles and promises the books exist to tell.

## What is true in it

A great deal. The confident claims of an earlier day did not all survive. A vertical shaft Charles Warren found in Jerusalem in 1867 was long linked with the "water shaft" by which David's men took the city, but, as the Study Bible's note on 2 Samuel 5 records, "later excavations suggest that shaft was not accessible in David's time." The gates once called Solomon's are now disputed. The massive stepped stone structure on the slope of the City of David is real, but "its date and relation to David are debated," the same note says. Books of Sayce's kind belonged to an age that expected the ground to settle what only argument can. And this library's pages on the exodus and on Jericho grant what the objection says there: Egypt is silent, Sinai is silent, and the sudden conquest of the whole land in one generation is hard to defend from the evidence.

It's also true that inscriptions confirm names and events, not meanings. No stele can show that the LORD spoke to Moses, or that the angel of the LORD struck the Assyrian camp.

## The evidence

Begin with the house of David. In 1993 and 1994, excavators at Tel Dan in northern Israel found fragments of an Aramaic victory inscription, most likely set up by Hazael of Damascus in the second half of the ninth century BC, in which a king of Aram boasts of killing a king of Israel and a king of the "house of David." The note on 2 Samuel 7 records that "most scholars take it as the earliest known reference to David's dynasty by name," while noting that it was written "more than a century after David's death." The note on 2 Kings 9 adds the puzzle the stele raises: 2 Kings gives Jehu the credit for those two deaths, and one answer is that "ancient royal inscriptions routinely claimed credit for events their kings did not personally perform." A dynasty known by David's name a century and more after him is good evidence that there was a David who founded it. It says nothing of how large his kingdom was. On that the evidence is argued: the note records that others point to Khirbet Qeiyafa, a fortified town above the Elah Valley excavated from 2007 and dated to the early tenth century, as evidence of organized rule in Judah in David's time.

Then Moab. In 1868, at Dhiban, the biblical Dibon, a black basalt stone was found on which Mesha king of Moab recorded his revolt; it is now in the Louvre. In A. H. Sayce's translation of 1884 it says that Chemosh "made me see my desire upon all my enemies, even upon Omri, king of Israel. Now they afflicted Moab many days, for Chemosh was angry with his land. His son succeeded him; and he also said, I will afflict Moab." Second Kings says that Mesha paid tribute to Israel and "after the death of Ahab, the king of Moab rebelled against the king of Israel" (2 Kings 3:5, BSB). The note on 2 Kings 3 judges that the stele "does not describe the campaign of 2 Kings 3, and the two accounts are hard to fit together in detail, but they agree on the main fact: Moab under Mesha broke free of Israel in this period." Mesha even reads his history as Israel read its own, in terms of his god's anger and favor.

Then Assyria. Shalmaneser III's Kurkh Monolith lists Ahab the Israelite with a large force of chariots at the battle of Qarqar in 853 BC, and his Black Obelisk, found at Nimrud in 1846, shows a figure bowing before him with a caption naming Jehu, as the notes on 1 Kings 22 and 2 Kings 9 record. A century and a half later Sennacherib invaded Judah. Second Kings says that Hezekiah sent to him at Lachish and paid "three hundred talents of silver and thirty talents of gold" (2 Kings 18:14, BSB). Sennacherib's own annals, preserved on clay prisms, say of Hezekiah, in Sayce's translation, that "forty-six of his strong cities" were besieged and captured, that "Hezekiah himself I shut up like a bird in a cage in Jerusalem, his royal city," and that he sent "thirty talents of gold, 800 talents of pure silver." The gold agrees; the silver doesn't. The notes on 2 Kings 18 and 19 add that the annals "never claim the city was taken, an unusual silence in Assyrian royal inscriptions," and that Sennacherib's reliefs of the siege of Lachish, and the Assyrian siege ramp excavated at Lachish, show what happened to Judah's second city.

And Hezekiah's preparations. Second Kings speaks of "how he constructed the pool and the tunnel to bring water into the city" (2 Kings 20:20, BSB), and Chronicles of how he "blocked the upper outlet of the Spring of Gihon and channeled it down to the west side of the City of David" (2 Chronicles 32:30, BSB). The tunnel still runs under the City of David, and an inscription found in it in 1880 tells, in Sayce's translation, how "the excavators had struck pick against pick, one against the other," and "the waters flowed from the spring to the Pool." Earlier still, the note on 1 Kings 14 records the campaign of Sheshonq I of Egypt, the Bible's Shishak, on a relief at Karnak, and the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible, Merneptah's stele of about 1208 BC, is set out on this area's page on the exodus.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what kind of evidence archaeology gives, and how much it can bear.

1. From the ninth century BC on, inscriptions written by Israel's neighbors and enemies name kings of Israel and Judah and events the Old Testament records: Omri and his son, Ahab, Jehu, the house of David, Hezekiah and Sennacherib's campaign, and Hezekiah's tunnel.
2. Where those sources overlap with the Bible, they agree on the main facts and differ in detail and in their kings' self-praise, as independent witnesses do, which is evidence that the books of Kings rest on sources that knew real kings and real events.
3. For earlier periods the evidence is thinner and argued over, and the absence of remains is weak evidence either way for peoples and events that leave little in the ground.

So: archaeology supports the historical setting of much of the Old Testament from the ninth century on, it neither proves nor disproves most of the earlier narratives, and it cannot by itself confirm or refute what the books say about God; both "archaeology proves the Bible" and "archaeology has disproved it" claim more than the evidence shows.

The weight sits on the second step. If the agreements were only the kind a late writer could copy from royal archives, they would show that he used good records, not that the books are faithful history; and that is where the strongest reply presses.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply grants every inscription and asks what it buys. The books of Kings name their sources: the "Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah" (2 Kings 20:20, BSB). A writer in the sixth century with such annals in hand would get the kings' names, the tribute and the tunnel right, and still be free to shape everything else, including the stories of prophets and miracles, and to invent a glorious past for David and Solomon that no annal recorded. On this view the ninth-century agreements are just what a late, theologically motivated history would show.

Much of that should be granted. The inscriptions don't confirm the miracles, and the note on 2 Kings 19 records that Christians themselves read the deliverance of Jerusalem in more than one way: some as a direct act of God, others "who also accept that God acted" as working through an epidemic. A writer with good archives is still a writer who chose and shaped. But the reply concedes the point this page makes. If the writers of Kings worked from contemporary records of real kings and real wars, and where they can be checked they agree with Israel's enemies on the facts while telling them from Israel's side, then they were historians of a kind, not inventors, and their work deserves to be read as ancient history is read: critically, but not with the assumption that whatever can't be checked was made up. That holds for the earlier periods as well. Silence can't establish that David and Solomon ruled a great state, and it can't establish that they ruled a highland chiefdom. The note on 1 Kings 4 states it fairly: "the archaeological picture is incomplete and contested."

## What remains unresolved

The size and character of the kingdom of David and Solomon is unresolved, and so is the chronology on which the tenth-century remains are dated. How Mesha's account and 2 Kings 3 fit together is unresolved. What happened to Sennacherib's army is told by 2 Kings as an act of God, and historians read the silence of his annals in different ways. The exodus and the conquest, set out on their own pages in this area, remain open as those pages say. And every season of digging can change the picture in either direction.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how much archaeology should matter to faith and on how to read the places where it and the Bible are hard to fit. Some expect the evidence, rightly read, to agree with Scripture in every detail and hold their judgment open where it doesn't yet; others accept that some narratives, such as the account of Solomon's splendor, are told with theological purpose and in the conventions of their time, as the note on 1 Kings 4 says that "the biblical writer's main concern is theological." This page takes no side among them. What the church confesses rests on God's acts in history, supremely the resurrection of Jesus, and Paul says plainly what follows if that did not happen: "your faith is futile" (1 Corinthians 15:17, BSB). Archaeology can show that the Old Testament's world was real. It was never going to be the ground of faith, and it hasn't removed it.
