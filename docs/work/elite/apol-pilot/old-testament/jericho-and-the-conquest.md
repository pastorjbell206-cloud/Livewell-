# Archaeologists found no walled city at Jericho when Joshua would have come, and Ai was already a ruin. Wasn't the conquest of Canaan a legend?

## The short answer

Archaeology has made a sudden conquest of the whole land in one generation hard to defend, and an honest answer should say so. Kathleen Kenyon dated the fall of Jericho's last great walled city to the sixteenth century BC, before any date proposed for Joshua, and radiocarbon tests have supported her; the mound usually identified as Ai was empty in the Late Bronze Age. But the evidence is less simple than the objection makes it. Jericho's mound is badly eroded and its dating is still argued, Ai's location is disputed, and Hazor did burn in the thirteenth century. And the book of Joshua itself doesn't describe a total conquest: beside its sweeping summaries it says that "very much of the land remains to be possessed." Christians read these chapters in several ways. What archaeology rules out is a reading the Bible itself complicates.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection begins at Jericho, the first city of the conquest and the most famous. Between 1952 and 1958 the British archaeologist Kathleen Kenyon dug at Tell es-Sultan, the mound of ancient Jericho, with the most careful methods of her day, and published her results for general readers in *Digging Up Jericho* (1957). She found that the great walled city of the Middle Bronze Age was destroyed around the middle of the sixteenth century BC, and that in the Late Bronze Age, when Joshua's Israel would have arrived, there was little or nothing there to conquer. The collapsed walls that John Garstang had found in the 1930s and dated to about 1400 BC, the walls many readers took to be Joshua's, she showed to be far older, from the Early Bronze Age. In 1995 Hendrik Bruins and Johannes van der Plicht published radiocarbon dates from seeds and charcoal of that last Middle Bronze destruction, and placed it in the late seventeenth or the sixteenth century BC.

Ai makes the case worse. The book of Joshua says that Israel burned it "and made it a permanent heap of ruins" (Joshua 8:28, BSB). Its name in Hebrew means "the ruin." The mound usually identified with it, et-Tell, was excavated by Judith Marquet-Krause in the 1930s and by Joseph Callaway in the 1960s, and both found a great city of the Early Bronze Age, destroyed a thousand years and more before Joshua, and no settlement at all in the Late Bronze Age.

Archaeologists have drawn the wider conclusion. William Dever, in *Who Were the Early Israelites and Where Did They Come From?* (2003), and Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, in *The Bible Unearthed* (2001), argue that Israel emerged gradually from within Canaan: the new villages that appear in the central hills around 1200 BC were small, unwalled and Canaanite in their pottery and houses, the work of people who settled the hills rather than an army that took cities. On this view the stories of Joshua grew up later to explain old ruins and to give a people a heroic beginning. Put at full strength, the objection says that the conquest is not merely unproven but contradicted by the excavated sites of the very cities the story names.

## What is true in it

Most of the findings are as the objection states them. Kenyon's work at Jericho was a turning point, the radiocarbon dates of the Middle Bronze destruction are real, and Garstang's walls were not Joshua's. Et-Tell was empty in the Late Bronze Age. The early Israelite villages were small and shared much of Canaan's material culture. A reading of Joshua as the destruction of the whole land's cities in a few years can't be squared with the archaeology of Canaan, and Christians who have taught it that way, or who have announced that Joshua's walls had been found, have set believers up for a fall.

## The evidence

Begin with what the excavations can and can't show. Jericho's mound has been washed by rain and dug by several expeditions for more than a century, and the levels of the Late Bronze Age, near the top, are the ones erosion takes first. Kenyon herself found remains above the burned layer, with a juglet she dated to the late fourteenth century, so the site was not wholly empty in the Late Bronze Age, and Piotr Bienkowski later argued for a small occupation there around 1400 BC. In 1990 Bryant Wood reexamined the pottery excavated at Jericho, in *Biblical Archaeology Review*, and argued that the last walled city fell around 1400 BC, close to the date that 1 Kings 6:1 gives for the exodus counted forward forty years. Bienkowski replied in the same magazine that year that the city was destroyed in the Middle Bronze Age, not the Late Bronze Age, and most archaeologists have sided with Kenyon's date, which the radiocarbon results support. The Study Bible's note on Joshua 6 sets out the dispute and calls the question open. That is the fair verdict: the weight of the published evidence favors Kenyon, and the condition of the mound means the last word may never be said.

Ai's problem is partly one of place. The identification of Ai with et-Tell rests on Joshua's note that Ai lay east of Bethel (Joshua 7:2) and on where Bethel is placed. Some scholars therefore look elsewhere; excavators at nearby Khirbet el-Maqatir have proposed it as Joshua's Ai, though their claim is disputed. Others suggest that the people of Bethel used the old ruin as an outpost, and others that the story explains how a ruin got its name. The Study Bible's note on Joshua 7 lists all three and calls the question unresolved.

Hazor tells a different story. The book of Joshua says it was "formerly the head of all these kingdoms" and that it alone of the northern cities was burned (Joshua 11:10-13, BSB). Hazor was the largest city in Bronze Age Canaan. Yigael Yadin in the 1950s and 1960s, and Amnon Ben-Tor from 1990, uncovered a great destruction by fire at the end of its Late Bronze city, usually dated to the thirteenth century BC, its palace burned. Ben-Tor attributes the burning to the people who became Israel; Sharon Zuckerman argued for an internal revolt; others suggest Egyptians or a rival city. The destruction fits Joshua's account on the thirteenth-century date, and those who hold the earlier date look to an earlier level.

Then the text itself. Joshua contains sweeping summaries: "Joshua conquered the whole region ... leaving no survivors" (Joshua 10:40, BSB). Three chapters later God tells him that "very much of the land remains to be possessed" (Joshua 13:1, BSB). Judah "could not drive out the Jebusites living in Jerusalem" (Joshua 15:63, BSB), and Judges begins with a list of cities the tribes failed to take: "Manasseh failed to drive out the inhabitants of Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Ibleam, Megiddo, and their villages" (Judges 1:27, BSB). Joshua 11:13 says that Israel "did not burn any of the cities built on their mounds, except Hazor," and the book's last chapter has God say, "I gave you a land on which you did not toil and cities that you did not build, and now you live in them" (Joshua 24:13, BSB). A conquest that left most cities standing and most Canaanites in the land would leave few burned layers for archaeologists to find. K. Lawson Younger, in *Ancient Conquest Accounts* (1990), set Joshua's summaries beside Egyptian, Hittite and Assyrian campaign reports and found the same stock language of total victory, which the Honest Answer on the conquest sets out.

So scholars read the conquest narratives as history in several main ways. Some hold the account as a record of a conquest around 1400 BC, and argue with Wood that Jericho fits it. Some, following the line of W. F. Albright and Yadin, place a conquest in the thirteenth century and point to Hazor. Some read Joshua as theological history, written in the war language of its world, describing in sweeping terms a smaller and longer process that Judges also describes. And many archaeologists, following the settlement models of Albrecht Alt (1925), George Mendenhall and Norman Gottwald, or Finkelstein and Dever, hold that Israel emerged largely from within Canaan and that the conquest stories are later tradition.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the archaeology contradicts and what it leaves standing.

1. The excavations at Jericho and et-Tell count heavily against a destruction of walled cities at those sites in the Late Bronze Age, though Jericho's erosion and Ai's disputed location leave room for argument.
2. The book of Joshua, read whole and with Judges, does not describe a sudden and total conquest of the land's cities, but a few destructions, most cities left standing, and a long, incomplete possession, told in the stock war language of the ancient Near East.
3. Archaeology has found a major thirteenth-century destruction at Hazor, the one northern city Joshua says was burned, and a people called Israel in the land by about 1208 BC.

So: archaeology has seriously damaged a reading of Joshua as a sudden destruction of Canaan's cities, and it leaves real difficulties at Jericho and Ai, but it has not shown that Israel's account of its entry into the land is legend, since the account the Bible as a whole gives is not the one archaeology most directly contradicts.

The weight sits on the second step. If the qualifications in Joshua and Judges are not the text's own way of speaking but a later patch over a story of total conquest, then the archaeology contradicts the account as its writers meant it, and that is where the argument should be tested.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that this is a retreat dressed as a reading. Joshua 6 doesn't say Jericho was small or that its wall was already a ruin. It says the wall fell and the city was taken, and Kenyon's evidence says there was no such city to take. Appealing to erosion, to a different Ai, or to the language of ancient war is what one does when the evidence goes against the story, and if the same moves were made for a Hittite epic, no historian would accept them.

Some of that has to be granted. At Jericho and Ai the archaeology as published does not fit the story as told, and a Christian shouldn't pretend that a disputed reanalysis has overturned it. But the reply asks the evidence to do more than it can. An absence of Late Bronze remains on an eroded mound is weaker than an absence on an undisturbed one, and the location of Ai is a real question, not an invented one; the Study Bible's notes, written for every kind of reader, call both open. And the reading of Joshua's summaries as stock war language was not invented to rescue the text from archaeology: it rests on the text's own juxtapositions, which Judges already makes, and on comparison with ancient campaign reports, which use the same idiom. Historians do read Hittite and Assyrian annals that way. What the reply rightly forbids is the claim that archaeology has confirmed the conquest. What it can't establish is that the archaeology has shown the Bible's account to be legend.

## What remains unresolved

The date of Jericho's last destruction is settled for most archaeologists and still argued by a few. Whether anything of a Late Bronze town at Jericho has been eroded away is unknown. Where Ai was is unresolved. Who burned Hazor is argued. The date of the entry into the land depends on the date of the exodus, which is itself open. And how the people who became Israel related to the Canaanites around them, how many came from outside and how many from within, is one of the most debated questions in the history of the period.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how these narratives are to be read as history, and the site takes no side among them. Some hold an early conquest around 1400 BC and read the archaeology at Jericho with Wood. Some hold a thirteenth-century entry and point to Hazor. Some read Joshua as theological history whose sweeping summaries describe a longer and smaller process, and think Jericho's and Ai's difficulties may yet be explained. And some, while still receiving Joshua as Scripture, think its stories preserve memories of a complex emergence told as a heroic beginning. What all confess is what the book itself confesses: the land was God's gift, not Israel's achievement, and the same God who gave it held Israel to his covenant in it. The moral question of the commands to destroy the Canaanites is a separate question, taken up on its own page in this area.
