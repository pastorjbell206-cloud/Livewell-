# The last chapter of Deuteronomy tells of Moses' death and burial, God is called by different names in different stories, and some events are told twice. Weren't the first five books written by many hands, centuries after Moses?

## The short answer

Some of them were, and the books themselves show it. Moses didn't write the account of his own death, and a few notes, like the Edomite kings who reigned "before any king reigned over the Israelites," were written after Israel had kings. Since Spinoza in 1670 and Wellhausen in 1883, many scholars have gone much further, holding that the five books were woven from sources written centuries after Moses; that theory has itself been heavily revised since the 1970s. Christians hold a range of views, from Moses as the substantial author with later editing to fuller critical accounts, and the New Testament's way of naming "the Law of Moses" can be read within more than one of them. What doesn't depend on the answer is what the church confesses: that these books are Scripture, that the God who speaks in them is the God who raised Jesus, and that his promises to Abraham were kept.

## The objection at its strongest

The case began with readers who loved the text closely enough to notice its seams. Baruch Spinoza, in chapter 8 of his *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670, in R. H. M. Elwes's translation), set out what the medieval commentator Abraham ibn Ezra had only hinted. Deuteronomy says "Moses wrote down this law" (Deuteronomy 31:9, BSB), which reads like another man describing him. Genesis says that when Abram came to Shechem, "at that time the Canaanites were in the land" (Genesis 12:6, BSB), which sounds like a writer for whom the Canaanites were gone. Numbers says that "Moses was a very humble man, more so than any man on the face of the earth" (Numbers 12:3, BSB), an odd thing to write of oneself. And Deuteronomy ends with Moses' death, his burial in a grave whose place "no one to this day knows," and the verdict that "no prophet has risen in Israel like Moses" (Deuteronomy 34:6, 10, BSB). From these, Spinoza drew "the plain conclusion that these books were written by another, and not by Moses in person."

The nineteenth century turned the seams into a theory. In 1753 Jean Astruc, a French physician, noticed that Genesis 1 calls God only "God" while other passages use his personal name, the LORD. Exodus seems to explain the change: God tells Moses, "by My name the LORD I did not make Myself known to them" (Exodus 6:3, BSB), though the patriarchs use that name throughout Genesis. Some stories are told more than once. Three times a patriarch passes his wife off as his sister (Genesis 12, 20 and 26). Beersheba is named for an oath twice (Genesis 21:31; 26:33). Read with these clues, the text sorted itself, on the critics' view, into four sources: a narrative that prefers the name the LORD (J), another that prefers God (E), the law book of Deuteronomy (D), and a priestly document full of dates, genealogies and ritual (P).

Julius Wellhausen gave the theory its classic form in his *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* (1883), and he made it a theory about history, not only about style. Read Judges, Samuel and Kings, he argued, and you find the LORD worshipped at many altars without reproach; Deuteronomy demands one place of worship; and the priestly code simply assumes it. So the priestly law, on his reading, came last, not first, and the question was whether the law of Moses "is the starting-point for the history of ancient Israel, or not rather for that of Judaism," the community that survived the exile (in the translation by J. Sutherland Black and Allan Menzies). At full strength the objection says that the five books are a great anthology of Israel's traditions, assembled after the exile, and that "the Law of Moses" names a tradition, not an author.

## What is true in it

More than many Christians have been taught. The books never name their author. Moses did not write the report of his own death, and nearly everyone who holds that he wrote the rest grants it; a tradition in the Talmud, as the Study Bible's note on Deuteronomy 34 records, gives the last eight verses to Joshua. The Study Bible's introduction to Genesis grants that "A few details show at least some later updating, which many conservative scholars readily accept": Abram pursues his enemies "as far as Dan" (Genesis 14:14, BSB), a town that got that name only in the time of the judges, and Genesis 36:31 lists kings of Edom "before any king reigned over the Israelites." The variation in God's names is real, and so is the repetition.

And the critics were not all hostile. Astruc thought Moses had used older sources, and the observation of a seam is not an attack on Scripture. Christians who answered the critics only by denying that there was anything to notice did their readers no favor.

## The evidence

Begin with what the Bible says about Moses and writing. The books themselves say that Moses wrote particular things: the words of the covenant at Sinai ("Moses wrote down all the words of the LORD," Exodus 24:4, BSB), a record of the battle with Amalek (Exodus 17:14), "the words of this law" in a book (Deuteronomy 31:24, BSB), and a song he taught the people (Deuteronomy 31:22). They don't claim that he wrote all five books as they now stand. Later books speak of "the Book of the Law of Moses" (Joshua 8:31; Nehemiah 8:1), and so do Jesus and the apostles. Jesus asks the Sadducees whether they have not read "about the burning bush in the Book of Moses" (Mark 12:26, BSB), says that Moses "wrote about Me" (John 5:46, BSB), and speaks of what is written "in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms" (Luke 24:44, BSB); James says that "Moses has been proclaimed in every city from ancient times and is read in the synagogues on every Sabbath" (Acts 15:21, BSB).

Then the tradition. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s article on the Pentateuch, written early in the twentieth century, gathers the evidence that Jews and Christians alike held Moses to be the author, and records that the medieval Jewish commentators who admitted "certain post-Mosaic additions," Ibn Ezra among them, "still ... upheld its Mosaic authorship." In 1906 the Catholic Church's Pontifical Biblical Commission answered that the critics' arguments did not justify holding that the books were "compiled from sources for the greatest part later than the Mosaic age," and in the same answer allowed that Moses may have used "written documents or oral traditions," may have entrusted the writing to others under his direction, and that the work may have received "post-Mosaic additions" over the centuries.

Then the theory's later history, which the objection usually leaves out. The Study Bible's introduction to Genesis records that from the 1970s, books by John Van Seters (1975), Hans Heinrich Schmid and Rolf Rendtorff (1977) "questioned the old theory," that scholars "have not agreed on what should replace it," and that many now speak more simply of priestly and non-priestly material, while a revised four-source view still has defenders. It also records the reply of the Jewish scholar Umberto Cassuto, in lectures published in English in 1961, that the divine names are chosen for meaning: "God" for the Creator of all, "the LORD" for the God who comes near his people. The note on Exodus 6 sets out both readings of 6:3: a priestly source that thought the name first revealed to Moses, or a statement that the patriarchs had not known God by experience as the One who keeps this promise. And the note on Genesis 20 records that the three wife-sister stories are read by some as versions of one tale and by others as a deliberate pattern of a family repeating its failures.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the evidence settles and what it leaves open.

1. The five books don't name their author, and the passages in which they say Moses wrote refer to particular laws, records and songs, not to the five books as they stand.
2. The books contain material that must be later than Moses (his death, notes such as "to this day" and the kings of Edom), so on any view someone after Moses had a hand in their present form.
3. How much is later, and how the material was gathered, is argued among scholars who agree on the evidence of the text, and the documentary hypothesis in its classic form is no longer the consensus it once was.
4. The New Testament's phrase "the Law of Moses," and Jesus' "Moses wrote," identify the books and their authority by the name of the lawgiver at their center, and a reader can take them as asserting Moses' authorship of what he gave without deciding every question of the books' editing.

So: the evidence shows that the first five books had more than one hand in their final form, and it doesn't settle how much; neither the strict view that Moses wrote every word nor the claim that the critics have proved a post-exilic anthology is established, and Christians who confess the same Scripture hold views across that range.

The weight sits on the fourth step. If Jesus' words are a claim that Moses wrote the whole of the five books as they now stand, then a fuller critical account conflicts with them, and that is how the traditional view has read them.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply comes from both sides at once. The critic says that step 4 is special pleading, a way to keep "Moses wrote" while emptying it. The traditional believer says the same thing for the opposite reason: the *Catholic Encyclopedia* argued that when Jesus said Moses "wrote about Me," he could not "expressly endorse the popular belief, if it was erroneous."

That reply deserves respect, and it is the real question, not a side issue. But it proves less than either party wants. When Jesus says "Moses wrote this commandment for you because of your hardness of heart" (Mark 10:5, BSB), he is speaking of a law, and the law is in Deuteronomy, which on every view contains Mosaic material. When he cites "the Book of Moses" for the burning bush, he names the scroll by its customary title, as Paul says "Moses writes" when he quotes Leviticus (Romans 10:5, BSB). None of these sayings addresses the death notice, the kings of Edom or the date of the priestly code, and nothing in them requires a believer to deny what the text itself shows. The traditionalist is right that the New Testament ties the books' authority to Moses; the critic is right that it doesn't settle the editing. What it settles is that the books are God's word through Moses, and that "he wrote about Me." Christians on every side of this question confess both.

The critic's view has open questions of its own. Wellhausen's reconstruction rested on reading the history of Israel's worship as a line from many altars to one, and the decades since have not agreed on what replaces it. But it should be said fairly that the revisions have not moved the field toward Moses: they questioned the old theory, as the Study Bible's introduction to Genesis records, "especially its early date for the J source." What they show is that the sources and their dates are still argued, which is a reason for modesty about any reconstruction, not a verdict for either side.

## What remains unresolved

How much of the five books comes from Moses himself is unresolved, and so is when and how they reached their final form. Whether the divine names mark sources, meaning, or both is argued. Whether the priestly law is early or late is argued. The Study Bible's introduction to Genesis puts it plainly: "These notes do not settle the question, and the book's authority does not hang on knowing the writer's name."

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ widely here, and this page takes no side. Many hold that Moses wrote the five books in substance, with later updating such as the account of his death; the Pontifical Biblical Commission's answers of 1906 belong to this range, and so do the many conservative scholars who, as the Study Bible's introduction to Genesis records, accept some later updating. Some think of Moses as the source of the law, gathered, supplemented and edited over centuries, as some Catholic scholars of the early twentieth century did by the *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s account. Others, who also confess the Bible as God's word, accept a fuller critical account of sources and late editing; the Study Bible's introduction to Genesis records that "Christians who confess the Bible as God's word hold more than one of these views." What all of them confess is that these books are Scripture, that the God who called Abraham and spoke at Sinai is the God and Father of Jesus Christ, and that "All Scripture is God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16, BSB).
