# The Old Testament: history, archaeology, violence and law

The Old Testament makes claims about history that can be weighed, and over the last two centuries they have been weighed hard. Archaeologists have dug at Jericho, Ai and Hazor and argued about what they found. Egyptologists have searched Egypt's records for the exodus and found silence. The tablets George Smith read in London in December 1872 showed that Babylon told a flood story long before Genesis was written down. Scholars have argued since the third century, when the philosopher Porphyry wrote against the book of Daniel, that its prophecies of the Greek kings were written after the events. And readers of every kind, believers among them, have found the commands to destroy the Canaanites among the hardest words in Scripture.

These pages take those questions one at a time. Each sets out the objection at the strength its best proponents give it, concedes what is true in it, and lays out the evidence: the excavations and their dates, the inscriptions and what they say, the ancient texts quoted from translations that can be checked. Each says where the evidence is strong, where it is thin, and where the question is still open. None of them treats an argument from silence as proof in either direction, and none of them pretends that archaeology has settled what it hasn't.

Christians differ on many of these questions: the date of the exodus, how to read the large numbers, the date of Daniel, the extent of the flood, how the conquest narratives should be read. The pages set out each view in words its holders would sign and take no side among them. What all of them confess is stated as confession: the God of Israel is the Father of Jesus Christ, he acted in Israel's history, and he spoke in its Scriptures.

## The questions

1. **No Egyptian record mentions the exodus, and archaeologists have found no trace of two million people in the Sinai desert. Did it happen at all?** What Egypt's silence does and doesn't show, the Delta, Rameses and Moses' Egyptian name, Merneptah's Israel, the date and the large numbers, and what remains open.
2. **Archaeologists found no walled city at Jericho when Joshua would have come, and Ai was already a ruin. Wasn't the conquest of Canaan a legend?** Kenyon's Jericho, Wood's challenge, et-Tell and the search for Ai, Hazor's burning, what the book of Joshua itself says about the land left unconquered, and the main ways the narratives are read as history.
3. **Deuteronomy tells Israel to leave nothing alive that breathes in the cities of Canaan. How can a good God command that, and how can anyone know he did?** The command and the ban in their setting, the limits the books set on it, Kant's question about knowing a command is God's, the main Christian readings and what each must carry, and why none licenses violence today.
4. **Daniel's visions trace the Greek kings in detail down to Antiochus IV and then seem to go wrong about how he died. Wasn't the book written in the 160s BC, after the events it appears to predict?** Porphyry's case in Jerome's report, the modern case from chapter 11, the loanwords and Darius the Mede, Belshazzar, the Qumran copies, 1 Maccabees and Ben Sira, what the date turns on and what it doesn't decide.
5. **Babylon told of a great flood, a boat, the birds and a sacrifice long before Genesis was written. Didn't Genesis simply borrow a Babylonian myth?** George Smith's tablets, Atrahasis and Gilgamesh quoted from the British Museum's translation, Delitzsch and Finkel, what a shared story can and can't show, the differences that make Genesis an answer, and the readings of the flood's extent.
6. **The last chapter of Deuteronomy tells of Moses' death and burial, God is called by different names in different stories, and some events are told twice. Weren't the first five books written by many hands, centuries after Moses?** Spinoza's seams, Astruc's names and Wellhausen's four sources at full strength, the theory's later revisions, what Jesus and the apostles say of Moses, and the range of Christian views.
7. **The God of the Old Testament sends floods and plagues and commands wars, and the God Jesus called Father is love. Isn't the God of the Old Testament angry and the God of the New loving, and aren't they really two different gods?** Marcion in Irenaeus's and Tertullian's report, Dawkins and Harnack, Exodus 34 and its echoes, judgment in Jesus' own teaching, and why the contrast is false and has fed contempt for Jews.

## Words these pages use

- **Late Bronze Age**: in the archaeology of Canaan, roughly 1550 to 1200 BC, the period in which both dates proposed for the exodus and the entry into Canaan fall.
- **Merneptah Stele**: an Egyptian victory inscription of about 1208 BC, the earliest known mention of Israel outside the Bible, naming Israel as a people in Canaan.
- **Herem**: the ban, in which a city and its people were devoted wholly to a god, with no plunder for the victors; Israel's neighbors practiced it too.
- **Apocalyptic**: a kind of Jewish writing of the last centuries BC, full of visions and symbols, often set in the mouth of a figure from the past; scholars disagree about how much of Daniel belongs to it.
- **Tell**: a mound built up by a town rebuilt again and again on its own ruins; Tell es-Sultan is ancient Jericho.
- **Elef**: the Hebrew word usually translated "thousand," which can also name a clan or a military unit.
- **Documentary hypothesis**: the theory, given its classic form by Julius Wellhausen in 1883, that the first five books were woven from four sources, called J, E, D and P.
