# The Old Testament: history, archaeology, violence and law

The Old Testament makes claims about history that can be weighed, and over the last two centuries they have been weighed hard. Archaeologists have dug at Jericho, Ai and Hazor and argued about what they found. Egyptologists have searched Egypt's records for the exodus and found silence. The tablets George Smith read in London in December 1872 showed that Babylon told a flood story long before Genesis was written down. Scholars have argued since the third century, when the philosopher Porphyry wrote against the book of Daniel, that its prophecies of the Greek kings were written after the events. And readers of every kind, believers among them, have found the commands to destroy the Canaanites among the hardest words in Scripture.

These pages take those questions one at a time. Each sets out the objection at the strength its best proponents give it, concedes what is true in it, and lays out the evidence: the excavations and their dates, the inscriptions and what they say, the ancient texts quoted from translations that can be checked. Each says where the evidence is strong, where it is thin, and where the question is still open. None of them treats an argument from silence as proof in either direction, and none of them pretends that archaeology has settled what it hasn't.

Christians differ on many of these questions: the date of the exodus, how to read the large numbers, the date of Daniel, the extent of the flood, how the conquest narratives should be read. The pages set out each view in words its holders would sign and take no side among them. What all of them confess is stated as confession: the God of Israel is the Father of Jesus Christ, he acted in Israel's history, and he spoke in its Scriptures.

## Words these pages use

- **Late Bronze Age**: in the archaeology of Canaan, roughly 1550 to 1200 BC, the period in which most scholars place the exodus and the entry into Canaan, whichever date they hold.
- **Merneptah Stele**: an Egyptian victory inscription of about 1208 BC, the earliest known mention of Israel outside the Bible, naming Israel as a people in Canaan.
- **Elef**: the Hebrew word usually translated "thousand," which can also name a clan or a military unit.
