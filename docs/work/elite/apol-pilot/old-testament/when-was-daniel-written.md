# Daniel's visions trace the Greek kings in detail down to Antiochus IV and then seem to go wrong about how he died. Wasn't the book written in the 160s BC, after the events it appears to predict?

## The short answer

Most critical scholars think so, and the case is serious. Daniel 11 follows the wars of the Greek kings of Egypt and Syria closely down to the persecution of Antiochus IV in 167 BC, and then describes an end for him that doesn't match his death in Persia in 164; the book's Aramaic, its Greek words for instruments and its puzzling Darius the Mede point to a late date for some. But the argument has always rested partly on a prior judgment, made by the philosopher Porphyry in the third century, that no one can foretell the future, and other evidence points the other way: the book knows Belshazzar, whom later writers had forgotten, and copies of it at Qumran were being made within decades of the date proposed. Christians who confess the same faith hold both dates. The date of Daniel doesn't decide whether God rules history.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection is old. Around the end of the third century AD the Neoplatonist philosopher Porphyry wrote, in the twelfth book of his work against the Christians, that Daniel was not written by the man whose name it bears. Jerome, answering him in his *Commentary on Daniel* (407), reports the argument in Gleason Archer's translation: Porphyry held that the book was composed "by some individual living in Judaea at the time of the Antiochus who was surnamed Epiphanes," that "'Daniel' did not foretell the future so much as he related the past," and that what the book says up to Antiochus is true history, "whereas anything he may have conjectured beyond that point was false, inasmuch as he would not have foreknown the future."

The modern form of the argument is set out with great learning in John J. Collins's commentary *Daniel* (1993), and it no longer rests on Porphyry's premise alone. Chapter 11 traces the Persian kings, Alexander, the division of his empire, and the wars between the kings of the South and the North, the Ptolemies and the Seleucids, in detail that the Study Bible's note on the chapter says interpreters of every school agree matches the history of the fourth to second centuries BC. It names no names but leaves no doubt: Berenice's marriage and murder, the battle of Raphia in 217, the defeat of Antiochus III by Rome, and then "a despicable person" who seizes the kingdom by intrigue (Daniel 11:21, BSB), whose forces "will abolish the daily sacrifice and set up the abomination of desolation" (Daniel 11:31, BSB). That is Antiochus IV and the desecration of the temple in 167 BC. Then the account changes. Verses 40 to 45 describe a last campaign against Egypt and a death "between the sea and the beautiful holy mountain" (Daniel 11:45, BSB). Antiochus died in Persia in 164 BC, far from both. A writer who knew the history down to 167 and then guessed at what came next would produce exactly this.

The critics add further evidence. Three of the instruments in chapter 3 have Greek names, matching the *kithara*, *psalterion* and *symphonia*. Darius the Mede, who receives Babylon in 539 BC, is unknown outside Daniel. The Hebrew Bible places Daniel among the Writings, not the Prophets. And the book is absent from places where one might expect it: Ben Sira, writing around 180 BC, praises Israel's heroes from Enoch to Nehemiah, names Ezekiel and the twelve prophets, and says nothing of Daniel. Put at full strength, the objection says that the evidence of history, language and canon converges on a book completed between 167 and 164 BC, written to strengthen Jews under persecution in the name of a hero of the exile.

## What is true in it

Most of the observations are correct. Chapter 11's precision really does stop where the critics say, and the end it describes does not match what is known of Antiochus's death; even Porphyry's account, as Jerome reports it, has Antiochus die "in Tabes, a town in Persia." The Greek loanwords are there, Darius the Mede is unknown to other sources, and the book's place in the Hebrew canon is among the Writings. Christians have sometimes defended the early date with arguments that don't hold, as if Daniel's place among the Writings were a later accident or as if Darius the Mede had been clearly identified. The Study Bible's note on Daniel 6 says that none of the proposed identifications has won general agreement.

## The evidence

Start with what Porphyry's argument assumed. Jerome saw it plainly: Porphyry "could not deny that they had taken place," so he concluded that the prophecies were written after the events. If predictive prophecy is impossible, a detailed and accurate prophecy is by definition history in disguise, and the date follows from the premise. Many modern scholars hold the late date on other grounds as well, but the question of whether God can make the future known is part of the argument, and it is fair to name it. Isaiah's God says, "I declare the end from the beginning" (Isaiah 46:10, BSB). A reader who thinks that is impossible will date Daniel late however the other evidence falls; a reader who thinks it possible has to weigh the other evidence on its own.

That evidence is mixed. Belshazzar, who rules Babylon on the night it falls in Daniel 5, was for centuries known only from Daniel, and some scholars doubted he existed. Babylonian texts found in the nineteenth century, the Nabonidus Chronicle among them, showed that he governed Babylon for about ten years while his father Nabonidus lived at the oasis of Tema, which explains why he can offer Daniel only the place of "third ruler" in the kingdom (Daniel 5:7). A writer of the 160s could have known it from older tradition, but it is the kind of detail that tells in favor of old material. The Greek instrument names are not decisive either: Greek mercenaries, traders and craftsmen were in the Near East long before Alexander, and instruments travel with their names, and the book uses several Persian words for officials beside its three Greek ones, as the Study Bible's note on Daniel 3 records.

Then the copies. Fragments of eight manuscripts of Daniel, on the usual count, were found in the caves at Qumran. Frank Cross dated the oldest, known as 4QDaniel-c, from its handwriting to the late second century BC, and a study published in 2025, combining radiocarbon dating with a computer analysis of handwriting, placed a Daniel manuscript from the same cave in the second century BC as well. On the critical date, that leaves a few decades between the book's completion and copies kept in the Qumran caves. That is possible, and critics point out that the book was written for exactly such a community of the faithful, but it is fast. First Maccabees, written late in the second century BC, puts in the mouth of the dying Mattathias, father of the Maccabees, a list of heroes that ends, "Ananias, Azarias, and Misael, by believing were saved out of the flame. Daniel for his innocency was delivered from the mouth of lions" (1 Maccabees 2:59-60, in the King James Version), which shows the stories known as tradition by the time that book was written. Josephus, at the end of the first century AD, tells a story of Alexander being shown "the Book of Daniel ... wherein Daniel declared that one of the Greeks should destroy the empire of the Persians" (*Antiquities* 11.337, in William Whiston's translation), but many historians doubt that the visit happened, and the story proves only what Josephus believed.

Ben Sira's silence has a weakness of its own. The same list that leaves out Daniel also leaves out Ezra, whose book no one dates after Ben Sira. And Jesus speaks of "'the abomination of desolation,' described by the prophet Daniel" (Matthew 24:15, BSB), which shows how the book was received in his time, though not when it was written.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what decides the date, and what the date decides.

1. Some evidence favors a completion of the book in the 160s BC (the shape of chapter 11, the Greek loanwords, Darius the Mede, the book's place in the canon), and some favors older material or an earlier date (Belshazzar, the early copies at Qumran, the Persian vocabulary), and none of it is decisive alone.
2. Much of the case for the late date has always depended, and partly still depends, on the judgment that detailed prediction is impossible, so a reader's view of whether God can make the future known properly shapes how the rest of the evidence is weighed.
3. On either date the book's central claims stand or fall on other grounds: that God gives kingdoms and takes them away, that his people must keep faith under persecution, and that the dead will rise.

So: the date of Daniel is not settled by the evidence alone; the late date is a reasonable conclusion for a historian who excludes prediction and an open question for one who doesn't; and the book's faith does not depend on the answer.

The weight sits on the second step. If the case for the late date could be made in full without any assumption about prophecy, the second step would fail and the late date would be as secure as the critics hold, and that is where the argument should be tested.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the critics no longer need Porphyry's premise, because the book's own text tells against it. If God revealed the history, why is the prophecy exact down to 167 and then wrong about how Antiochus died? Deuteronomy itself gives the test: "When a prophet speaks in the name of the LORD and the message does not come to pass or come true, that is a message the LORD has not spoken" (Deuteronomy 18:22, BSB). The pattern of precision followed by error is what a writer of 165 BC would produce, and nothing else explains it as well.

This is the critical case at its strongest, and it should be granted that it is a real argument, not a prejudice. But two things should be weighed against it. First, the reading of verses 40 to 45 as a failed prediction about Antiochus is itself an interpretation. Porphyry, according to Jerome, tried to fit even these verses to Antiochus's last campaigns, while Jerome and most Christian interpreters after him read the text as passing at some point, often at verse 36, beyond Antiochus to a final enemy of God's people, with Antiochus as his foreshadowing; the Study Bible's note on Daniel 11 sets out both. Critics answer that this is special pleading, and traditional readers answer that the chapter's own phrase "the time of the end" (Daniel 11:40, BSB) marks the shift. Neither reading is forced on the text. Second, the reply proves less than it seems about the book's truthfulness, which is a separate question from its date. Christians who hold the late date, among them John Goldingay in his commentary of 1989, still receive the book as Scripture, and many of them read the visions as a recognized form of Jewish writing in which history was presented as revealed to a figure of the past, a form its first readers would not have taken as deceit. Others think that view can't be squared with the book's own claims. That is a disagreement among believers, not a refutation of belief.

## What remains unresolved

The date of the book's completion, and how much of it is older, is unresolved. So is the identity of Darius the Mede. Whether verses 40 to 45 describe Antiochus, a later figure or both is argued. The dating of the oldest Qumran copies is itself debated, and the 2025 study has its critics. Whether the court stories of chapters 1 to 6 circulated long before the visions is held by many on both sides and proved by none. And whether apocalyptic writing in another's name is compatible with a book's being God's word is a question Christians answer differently.

## Where Christians differ

Christians who confess the same faith in God's rule and in Scripture's authority differ on this question, and the site takes no side among them. Many hold the traditional date in the sixth century BC, with Daniel as the book's source; they point to the book's own claims, to Jesus' words about "the prophet Daniel," and to God's power to reveal the future, and Joyce Baldwin's commentary of 1978 weighs the case from that side. Others hold the late date and receive the book as Scripture in the form its first readers knew. Some hold a mediating view, with the stories and some visions from the exile and the book completed later. What all confess is what Daniel teaches: that the Most High rules the kingdoms of men, that faith may say to the king, "even if He does not ... we will not serve your gods" (Daniel 3:18, BSB), and that "many who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2, BSB), the resurrection of the dead the creeds confess.
