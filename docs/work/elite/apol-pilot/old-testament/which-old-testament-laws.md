# Leviticus forbids eating shellfish and wearing clothing made of two kinds of material, and Christians ignore those laws while quoting others from the same chapters. Why do Christians keep some Old Testament laws and not others?

## The short answer

Because the New Testament itself drew a line, though not where careless quoting draws it. The law of Moses was a covenant with Israel, and many of its commands, the food laws and the laws against mixing among them, marked Israel off from the nations, as the law says itself. Within twenty years of Jesus' death the church decided, at the council in Acts 15, that Gentiles enter God's people through Christ without becoming Jews, and Mark draws from Jesus' teaching that "all foods are clean." At the same time the New Testament repeats the commands the Torah itself put at the center, to love God and neighbor. Christians describe the line differently: as moral, ceremonial and civil law, as the whole law fulfilled in Christ and given back as "the law of Christ," or as law and gospel. The charge that they simply pick what they like lands on careless quoting, and it should. It misses the principle.

## The objection at its strongest

Open Leviticus 19 and read two verses together. Verse 18 says, "love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD," and Christians quote it as the heart of their ethics. Verse 19 says, "You shall not crossbreed two different kinds of livestock; you shall not sow your fields with two kinds of seed; and you shall not wear clothing made of two kinds of material" (Leviticus 19:18-19, BSB), and Christians wear cotton blends without a thought. Two chapters earlier, everything in the water "that does not have fins and scales" is "detestable to you" (Leviticus 11:10, BSB), and Christians eat shrimp. The objection says, in the form it is commonly put (no single published author is named for it here), that this is not interpretation but preference. The same God gave both commands in the same book, often in the same paragraph, and Christians who keep one and drop the other are deciding for themselves which parts of God's word to obey.

The oldest written form of the objection comes from a Jew, not a skeptic. In Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho*, from the second century, the Jewish teacher Trypho says what puzzles him most: "that you, professing to be pious, and supposing yourselves better than others, are not in any particular separated from them, and do not alter your mode of living from the nations, in that you observe no festivals or sabbaths, and do not have the rite of circumcision," and yet "expect to obtain some good thing from God, while you do not obey His commandments" (chapter 10, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* translation). Trypho's question is fair. If the law is God's, by what authority does anyone set part of it aside?

## What is true in it

Much of it. Christians do quote Leviticus carelessly, lifting one verse as a rule for others while living freely under the verse beside it, and that deserves the rebuke it gets. The law's own arrangement doesn't help them. As the Study Bible's note on Leviticus 19 says, "Ritual and ethical commands are mixed without apology," so that paying a worker on time and eating an offering on time stand side by side. The familiar division of the law into moral, ceremonial and civil parts is not printed in the text. It was worked out by Christian teachers, from the early church to Thomas Aquinas, as the Study Bible's guide to reading the law records, and some Christians themselves, as the site's page "How Does the Whole Bible Fit Together?" sets out their view, are not confident that it is given in the text rather than read into it.

Trypho is also right about something Christians have often forgotten. The law was Israel's, given in love, and Jewish tradition still keeps it: the note on Leviticus 19 records that "observant Jews still avoid garments mixing wool and linen." Those who keep it have not experienced it as the burden Christians have sometimes described. Solomon Schechter, in *Studies in Judaism* (1896), answered the scholars who called it "a most terrible burden" by pointing to a literature of some twenty-five centuries that bears witness to "the bliss and happiness of living and dying under it." A Christian answer has to explain why the church doesn't keep it without suggesting that those who do are foolish.

## The evidence

Begin with what the law says about itself. It was given to Israel as a covenant, with a purpose: "you will be My treasured possession out of all the nations ... a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:5-6, BSB). The food laws give their own reason: "You are to be holy to Me because I, the LORD, am holy, and I have set you apart from the nations to be My own" (Leviticus 20:26, BSB). And Deuteronomy shows that they were not a diet for all people: a carcass an Israelite must not eat "you may give it to the foreigner residing within your gates, and he may eat it" (Deuteronomy 14:21, BSB). The Study Bible's note on Leviticus 11 draws the conclusion: the laws "marked Israel's calling rather than a universal diet." The note on Leviticus 19 reads the laws of mixtures the same way, as mirroring the created order of things "according to their kinds" and "the separation between Israel and the nations."

Then what the first church decided. When Gentiles began to believe in Jesus, some believers insisted that they "must be circumcised and required to obey the law of Moses" (Acts 15:5, BSB). The council in Jerusalem, about AD 49 by the guide's reckoning, decided otherwise: "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us not to burden you with anything beyond these essential requirements: You must abstain from food sacrificed to idols, from blood, from the meat of strangled animals, and from sexual immorality" (Acts 15:28-29, BSB). Many interpreters, the note on Acts 15 records, see those requirements as drawn from Leviticus 17 and 18, the rules laid on foreigners living among Israel, so that Gentile and Jewish believers could share a table. The note adds that "The council does not declare the law of Moses worthless; Jewish believers continued to keep it."

Then the teaching behind the decision. Jesus said that "Nothing that enters a man from the outside can defile him," and Mark adds, "(Thus all foods are clean.)" (Mark 7:18-19, BSB). The note on Mark 7 records that most commentators take this as Mark's own conclusion, drawn for a church of Jews and Gentiles, and that the Jewish scholar Daniel Boyarin, in *The Jewish Gospels* (2012), reads the dispute as about handwashing, with "all foods" meaning all foods the Torah permits. Peter's vision has the same lesson: "Do not call anything impure that God has made clean" (Acts 10:15, BSB). Hebrews says that "the law is only a shadow of the good things to come" (Hebrews 10:1, BSB), and Paul that food, feasts and Sabbaths "are a shadow of the things to come, but the body that casts it belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:17, BSB).

And what the New Testament keeps. Jesus said, "I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17, BSB), and when asked for the greatest commandment he named two from the Torah: love of God from Deuteronomy, love of neighbor from Leviticus, adding, "All the Law and the Prophets hang on these two commandments" (Matthew 22:40, BSB). Paul lists the commandments against adultery, murder, theft and coveting and says they "are summed up in this one decree: 'Love your neighbor as yourself.' ... Therefore love is the fulfillment of the law" (Romans 13:9-10, BSB). Jeremiah had promised a new covenant in which God would "put My law in their minds and inscribe it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33, BSB), and Paul calls himself "under the law of Christ" (1 Corinthians 9:21, BSB).

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about a principle: whether the New Testament gives Christians a reason, not just a preference, for keeping some of the law's commands and not others.

1. The law of Moses was a covenant with Israel, and many of its commands, among them the food laws and the laws of mixtures, marked Israel off from the nations, as the law itself says and as its permission to foreigners shows.
2. The New Testament holds that Gentiles enter God's people through Christ without becoming Jews, and that the marks of separation, the sacrifices and the feasts point to Christ and are fulfilled in him.
3. The New Testament repeats the commands the Torah itself put at the center, love of God and of neighbor, and the commandments it names as summed up in love, and calls this the law of Christ.

So: Christians who don't keep the food laws or the laws of mixtures but do keep the commands of love are following a principle the New Testament gives, not a preference of their own; how to state that principle, and how it applies to particular commands, is where Christians differ.

The weight sits on the second step. If the New Testament did not set aside the law's marks of separation for Gentile believers, Christians would have no principled reason to keep Leviticus 19:18 and not 19:19, and Trypho's question would stand unanswered.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the principle does its work only after Christians have decided what they want. Matthew's Jesus says that "not a single jot, not a stroke of a pen, will disappear from the Law until everything is accomplished" (Matthew 5:18, BSB). The categories of moral and ceremonial law came centuries later. And Christians apply them inconsistently, keeping some commands from the very chapters where they set others aside, which is exactly the objection.

Part of that should be granted: the categories are later than the New Testament, and the line between "moral" and "ceremonial" is drawn by judgment, not printed in Leviticus. But the principle itself is not later. The decision that Gentiles need not keep the law of Moses to belong to God's people was made, by the New Testament's account, by Jewish apostles within a generation of the cross, and the reasons it gives, that God "made no distinction between us and them, for He cleansed their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9, BSB), and the shadow and the body, are in the texts, not invented by medieval theologians. As for Matthew 5:18, the same sentence ends "until everything is accomplished," and the verse before it says Jesus came "to fulfill"; Christians read those words in more than one way, but every reading has to reckon with the same Jesus declaring all foods clean in Mark and sending his disciples to the nations in Matthew 28.

The charge of inconsistency is right where Christians quote Leviticus to settle questions while ignoring the New Testament's own account of the law. The objection is most often pressed today about sexual ethics. This page does not argue that question or any other contested moral question. Christians who share the principle set out here apply it differently there, and that question needs a page of its own.

## What remains unresolved

How to read Matthew 5:17-20 is unresolved, and so is how far Jesus himself set aside the food laws and how far the church drew the conclusion later, as the note on Mark 7 records. Whether the Sabbath command binds Christians, and in what form, is still argued. How Jewish believers in Jesus should relate to the law today, which the note on Acts 15 says the first Jewish believers kept, is answered differently by different churches.

## Where Christians differ

Christians describe the line in at least three ways, and this page takes no side among them. The threefold division, found in Aquinas, who distinguished "'moral' precepts, which are dictated by the natural law; 'ceremonial' precepts, which are determinations of the Divine worship; and 'judicial' precepts, which are determinations of the justice to be maintained among men" (*Summa Theologiae* I-II, q. 99, a. 4, in the English Dominican translation), and in the Westminster Confession (chapter 19), which holds the ceremonial laws "now abrogated under the New Testament" and the judicial laws expired "together with the State of that people, not obliging any other, now, further than the general equity thereof may require," is, the guide records, the most widely used framework among Protestants and Catholics; its difficulty is that the law doesn't sort itself. New covenant readings, held by dispensationalists and in what is called New Covenant Theology, take the law of Moses as one covenant fulfilled in Christ and given back as the law of Christ; their difficulty is to say why the moral commands carry over with such force. Lutheran theology centers on the distinction between law and gospel: the Formula of Concord (1577) confesses that "the Law is properly a divine doctrine, which teaches what is right and pleasing to God, and reproves everything that is sin," and that believers, though freed from its curse, are not "without Law"; its difficulty is the same question of which commands are the law in that sense. A smaller movement, theonomy, holds that Israel's civil laws still bind governments, a view most Reformed churches have rejected, as the guide records. Some Christians, Seventh-day Adventists among them, keep the food laws, and many Messianic Jewish believers keep them as part of their Jewish identity. All of them confess with Paul that "the law is holy, and the commandment is holy, righteous, and good" (Romans 7:12, BSB), and that righteousness comes through Christ.
