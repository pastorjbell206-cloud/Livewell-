# Babylon told of a great flood, a boat, the birds and a sacrifice long before Genesis was written. Didn't Genesis simply borrow a Babylonian myth?

## The short answer

The likeness is real and close, and Christians shouldn't play it down. Copies of the Babylonian flood story are older than any copy of Genesis: a tablet of the Atrahasis epic was written in the seventeenth century BC, and the eleventh tablet of the Gilgamesh epic, found at Nineveh, has a man warned to build a boat, the birds sent out, and a sacrifice whose smell draws the gods. Genesis almost certainly tells a story its neighbors also told. But "borrowed" settles less than it seems. A shared story can be a shared memory of a real disaster, as the Babylonians themselves held, and the differences are not random: Genesis gives a moral reason for the flood, one God who grieves and judges, a man saved by favor, and a covenant with every living thing. Where Genesis stands apart is the question that matters.

## The objection at its strongest

The story begins in London. On 3 December 1872 George Smith, an assistant at the British Museum, read a paper to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on what he called the Chaldean account of the deluge, a story he had found on tablets from the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. Its hero, warned by a god, builds a great boat, takes his family and living creatures aboard, survives a storm that turns "all mankind" into mud, grounds on a mountain, and sends out birds. In the translation the British Museum published in 1929: "I brought out a dove and let her go free. The dove flew away and [then] came back ... I brought out a raven and let her go free. The raven flew away, she saw the sinking waters." Then he offered a sacrifice, and "The gods smelt the sweet savour. The gods gathered together like flies over him that sacrificed."

Older copies followed. A tablet of the Atrahasis epic, dated by its colophon to the reign of the Babylonian king Ammi-saduqa, tells the same story a thousand years before Ashurbanipal, and a Sumerian version survives on half a tablet published by Arno Poebel in 1914. Genesis, on almost any view of its date, was written down later than the oldest of these.

In 1902 the Assyriologist Friedrich Delitzsch drew the conclusion in lectures published as *Babel und Bibel*, given before Kaiser Wilhelm II: much of the Old Testament, its flood among it, came from Babylon. Irving Finkel, a curator at the British Museum, made a modern form of the case in *The Ark Before Noah* (2014), from a newly read tablet that gives instructions for a round boat, arguing that the story came into the Bible from Babylon. Put at full strength, the objection says that the flood of Genesis is a Babylonian myth retold in Hebrew, and that a reader who knows its source can no longer take it as an account of what happened.

## What is true in it

The parallels are not superficial. The warning, the boat sealed with pitch, the animals, the storm, the grounding on a mountain, the birds, the sacrifice and its pleasing smell occur in both, and the Study Bible's notes on Genesis 6 and 8 call the resemblance unmistakable. The Mesopotamian copies are older than any surviving Hebrew text. And Christians have at times met the discovery badly, either by refusing to look at it or by insisting, against the evidence of the tablets' age, that the Babylonians must have borrowed from Moses.

It should also be granted that the Babylonian story has moral seriousness of its own. When the god Enlil rages that anyone survived, the god Ea rebukes him, "He who is sinful, on him lay his sin, He who transgresseth, on him lay his transgression," and pleads for mercy so that mankind is not blotted out. Ishtar, the Lady of the Gods, grieves that she "commanded evil" and asks, "Did I of myself bring forth my people That they might fill the sea like little fishes?" These are not crude texts, and Genesis shouldn't be praised by caricaturing them.

## The evidence

Begin with what the parallels can show. That two stories share a plot, and even details as specific as birds sent from a grounded boat, shows they belong to one tradition. It does not show which way the dependence ran, whether one text copied another or both drew on older tellings, or whether the tradition remembers an event. The Babylonians themselves treated the flood as history. E. A. Wallis Budge, writing for the British Museum, noted that Sumerian records list kings who reigned before the flood and say that after it kingship came down again from on high; he thought both Sumerians and Semites were commemorating a real disaster, "the knowledge of which, through tradition, was common to both peoples." Excavations at Ur, Kish and Shuruppak found thick flood deposits, which Leonard Woolley in 1929 took for the biblical flood; later work showed they came from different periods and reflected local river floods, a real and recurring terror on the Mesopotamian plain. As the Study Bible's note on Genesis 7 says, such floods may lie behind the region's traditions, though no single deposit can be tied to Genesis.

Then the differences, which are where the comparison becomes interesting. In Atrahasis the gods send plague, drought and finally the flood because humans have grown too many and too noisy and disturb their rest; in Genesis, after the flood, God tells Noah's sons, "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth" (Genesis 9:1, BSB). In Genesis the reason is moral: "the earth was corrupt in the sight of God, and full of violence" (Genesis 6:11, BSB). In the Babylonian story the hero is saved because one god breaks ranks with the others; in Genesis, "Noah, however, found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8, BSB). The Babylonian gods cower during the storm "like a dog" and crowd to the offering because, with no one to feed them, they have gone hungry; in Genesis "the LORD smelled the pleasing aroma" and answers not with appetite but with a promise: "never again will I destroy all living creatures as I have done" (Genesis 8:21, BSB). And the Babylonian story ends with its hero made immortal, while Genesis ends with a covenant God makes with Noah, his descendants and "every living creature" (Genesis 9:10, BSB), and the first law guarding human life because humankind bears God's image (Genesis 9:6).

The same pattern appears in the details. Gilgamesh's boat is usually read as a cube; Noah's is a long barge. Utnapishtim sends a dove, then a swallow, then a raven; Noah sends a raven, then a dove three times, and the second brings back "a freshly plucked olive leaf" (Genesis 8:11, BSB). The Genesis grief is God's own: "the LORD regretted that He had made man on the earth, and He was grieved in His heart" (Genesis 6:6, BSB). Scholars since George Smith have laid these side by side. Whatever one thinks of the relation, Genesis is not a copy. It is an answer.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what a shared story shows.

1. The flood of Genesis and the Mesopotamian flood stories share a plot and specific details, and the Mesopotamian copies are older; so Genesis belongs to a tradition its neighbors shared, whatever the route by which it came to be told in Israel.
2. A shared tradition does not by itself show that the story records no event, or that Genesis copied a particular text, since a common memory of a disaster, told in different ways by different peoples, would produce the same pattern, and the Mesopotamians themselves treated the flood as history.
3. The differences between Genesis and the older stories are consistent and theological: one God instead of many, judgment on violence instead of annoyance at noise, salvation by favor instead of a god's betrayal of his colleagues, and a covenant with all life instead of a quarrel among gods.

So: Genesis tells a story Israel's neighbors told, and probably tells it in conversation with them, which shows the world it was written in rather than that its account is false; what sets it apart is what it says about God, and that is the claim a reader has to weigh.

The weight sits on the second step. If a shared tradition could only mean literary borrowing of an invented tale, the argument would reduce to saying Genesis improved a myth, and that is where it should be tested.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the argument has quietly changed the subject. If Genesis reshaped an older story to teach its own theology, then it is theology in the form of myth, not history, and Christians can't have it both ways. Jesus spoke of "the days of Noah" as an event: "they were oblivious, until the flood came and swept them all away" (Matthew 24:39, BSB). Either Genesis reports a flood that happened as it says, which geology and the tablets make hard to believe, or it doesn't, and then the claim that it is God's word about history has failed at its first great story.

That dilemma is too tidy. Between "reports exactly as it says" and "invented" lie the readings Christians actually hold. Some take the flood as global and hold that the geological record, rightly read, bears witness to it, a view John Whitcomb and Henry Morris set out in *The Genesis Flood* (1961). Some hold it was regional, covering the world Noah knew, since the Hebrew word for "earth" also means "land" and Genesis elsewhere uses universal language of a limited scope. Some see a real flood remembered and told in the universal terms of ancient storytelling to make a claim about the whole human race. The Study Bible's note on Genesis 7 sets out all three. None of them says Genesis invented the event, and none needs the Babylonian tablets to be later than they are. Jesus' point in Matthew 24 is the suddenness of judgment on people who would not see it coming, and readers on every side take it that way, though those who hold a global flood add that he speaks of it as an event. The tablets do rule out one thing: the idea that Genesis fell from the sky with no relation to the world around it. That idea was never the church's.

## What remains unresolved

The relation of Genesis to the Mesopotamian texts is unresolved: whether its writers knew a form of Atrahasis or Gilgamesh, drew on an older shared tradition, or both. When the flood narrative of Genesis took its present form is argued, and Finkel's account of its route from Babylon is one proposal among several. The extent of the flood is a question Christians answer differently. Whether any particular disaster lies behind the tradition is unknown, and the flood deposits found so far can't be tied to it. And why the story of a flood spread so widely is a question historians of religion still argue.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on the flood's extent and on how its account relates to the older stories, and the site takes no side among them. Some hold a global flood; some hold a regional flood, covering the world Noah knew; some read the narrative as a real memory told in the forms of its world. Readers of each view can agree with the Study Bible's note on Genesis 6 that Genesis speaks into a shared memory and that its differences show what it wants to say about God. What all of them confess is what the chapter itself teaches: that God judges human evil and grieves over it, that he remembers those he saves, as "God remembered Noah" (Genesis 8:1, BSB), and that he has bound himself by covenant to the life he made, so that "As long as the earth endures, seedtime and harvest ... shall never cease" (Genesis 8:22, BSB).
