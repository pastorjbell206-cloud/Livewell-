# The Bible has been translated so many times. Hasn't the meaning been lost along the way?

## The short answer

No, because it hasn't been translated over and over in a chain. A modern English Bible is translated directly from the Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek, using editions built from the manuscripts, so the English stands one step from the original languages, not at the end of a line of translations of translations. English versions differ because translators make different choices: which edition of the text to follow, how closely to keep the original's form, which reader to serve. Something is always lost in translation, wordplay and rhythm and sometimes an ambiguity the original leaves open, and the most serious critics of English Bibles, Robert Alter among them, say that modern versions often explain where they should translate. But what one version loses, another can show, and the original languages remain open to anyone who will learn them. And Christianity has never held that God's word lives only in its first language. The New Testament itself was a work of translation.

## The objection at its strongest

The popular form of the objection pictures a photocopy of a photocopy: the Bible passed from Hebrew to Greek to Latin to English, each step blurring the last, until what we read is a faint copy of a faint copy. As it stands, that picture has no scholarly defender, because it isn't how any modern Bible was made. But a serious form of the objection exists, and it comes from people who know the original languages and love the text.

Robert Alter, a scholar of Hebrew literature who has translated the Hebrew Bible himself, argued in *The Art of Bible Translation* (2019) that most modern English versions rest on what he calls a heresy. They use translation to explain the Bible instead of representing it in another language. They smooth what is rough, replace the concrete with the abstract, break up the Hebrew's long chains of "and" into a variety of English connections or drop them altogether, and lose the literary character of the original in the process. Alter admired the King James Version because it accepted that the Bible itself is often not clear in the way its modern translators want it to be.

From a quite different direction, David Bentley Hart, in *The New Testament: A Translation* (2017), set out to render the Greek with deliberate, sometimes uncomfortable literalness, and argued that conventional translations have read later theology into the text. His best-known example is the Greek adjective *aiōnios*, which most Bibles render "eternal". Hart argued that it can just as well mean belonging to an age, and in Matthew 25:46 he has the righteous go to "the life of that Age".

The skeptic can press this. If two learned translators can say that the Bibles most people hold misrepresent the original, then the ordinary reader, who can't check, is at the mercy of committees whose convictions shape the words. And the problem goes back further than English. Jesus taught in a Jewish world where people spoke Aramaic, and Mark preserves some of his words in that language. The Gospels give us his teaching in Greek. Even the first readers of the Gospels were reading a translation.

## What is true in it

Nearly all of it, in one form or another. Every translation loses something. The Hebrew of a psalm has a sound and a compression that no English can keep, and a pun in Greek is rarely a pun in English. Alter is right that translators are tempted to explain, and many have given in. Some reviewers think he overstates the case against versions he doesn't discuss, but the temptation he names is real. Hart is right that theology can shape a translation, and readers should know where a contested word has been decided for them.

The skeptic is also right that some of Jesus' words reach us through translation. In Mark 5:41 Jesus takes a dead girl's hand and says, "Talitha koum," and Mark gives the Greek for his Greek-speaking readers. He does the same with other Aramaic words, including the cry from the cross. The Gospels don't hide this. They show it.

And it is true that most readers can't check their translation for themselves. Christians who speak as though their own favorite version were the only faithful one have sometimes made that problem worse.

## The evidence

Start with how English Bibles have actually been made. William Tyndale's New Testament of 1526 was the first in English translated from the Greek. The King James translators of 1611 worked from the Hebrew and from the printed Greek of their day. Modern versions translate from critical editions of the Hebrew and Greek built on the earliest manuscripts, as other pages in this area describe. None of these is a translation of a translation. Each goes back to the original languages, and so the errors of one English version don't pass into the next. A new translator starts again from the source.

Then look at why English versions differ. Eugene Nida set out in *Toward a Science of Translating* (1964) two orientations a translator can take. Formal equivalence keeps as close as it can to the form of the original, its word order and its images. Dynamic equivalence, later called functional equivalence, aims to produce in a new reader the effect the original had on its first readers, in natural current speech. Nida treated these as poles with many grades between them, and nearly every English Bible stands somewhere on that line. Neither end is simply right. A formal translation can leave a modern reader with English no one speaks. A functional one can decide for the reader what the original left open, which is Alter's complaint.

Now look at the places where the choice really matters, because they are fewer than the objection suggests, and they are argued in the open. In Romans 3:22 Paul writes a Greek phrase, *pistis Christou*, that can mean faith in Christ or the faithfulness of Christ. The King James has "by faith of Jesus Christ". The Berean Standard Bible on this site has "through faith in Jesus Christ". The NET Bible has "through the faithfulness of Jesus Christ", with a footnote giving the other reading. Scholars are divided, and the Study Bible's note on Romans 3 says so. Whichever is right, the gospel Paul preaches in the chapter doesn't change: righteousness comes from God, through Christ, to all who believe. In Matthew 25:46 most Bibles, the BSB among them, say "eternal punishment" and "eternal life", and Hart says "that Age". The Study Bible's note on the word gives both senses, eternal and belonging to the age to come, and the question it raises, about the nature of final judgment, is one Christians have argued about openly, as the site's answer on hell sets out. A reader who meets these differences hasn't found the meaning lost. He has found the places where translators have to decide, and the footnotes tell him where they are.

The New Testament was itself a translator's book. Most early Christians read the Old Testament in Greek, in the translation Jews had begun in Alexandria in the third century before Christ, and the apostles often quoted it. When Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14, he quotes the Greek, which uses *parthenos*, a word that usually implies virginity, where the Hebrew has *almah*, a young woman. The writers knew the original and still treated the translation as Scripture to be quoted.

Lamin Sanneh, who was raised a Muslim in the Gambia, argued in *Translating the Message* (1989) that this habit runs through the whole history of the faith. He contrasted it with Islam, where the Arabic of the Qur'an is held to be the revealed form itself, and wrote that "translatability became the characteristic mode of Christian expansion through history." On the Wycliffe Global Alliance's estimate in August 2025, some part of the Bible now exists in more than 4,000 of the world's 7,396 languages. A faith that believed its meaning would be lost in translation wouldn't have done that.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument from how translation works to what a reader can know.

1. Modern translations are made directly from the original languages, from editions built on the manuscripts, so each stands one step from the original, and the errors of one version don't pass into the next.
2. What one translation loses or decides, others show: versions differ in the open, and footnotes, comparison between versions and the original languages let a reader find where they differ and why.
3. The meaning of a text can survive translation even where some of its form can't, as the New Testament itself assumes when it gives Jesus' Aramaic words in Greek and quotes the Old Testament in a Greek translation.

So: translation always costs something, and readers should know what. But the meaning of the Bible isn't lost in it. What a careful reader of an English Bible reads is the Bible, seen through a window someone else has cleaned, and he can check the glass.

The weight sits on the second step. It is the one Alter's critique tests most, because his point is that ordinary readers don't compare. The step claims only that they can, and that the differences that matter are few enough, and marked clearly enough, to be found.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply grants all this and says it helps only scholars. Most people read one Bible, trust it, and never see a footnote. The committees that make the main English versions share broad theological commitments. So in practice the ordinary reader gets one tradition's reading presented as the text itself, and his freedom to compare is a freedom he will never use.

That is a fair description of how many people read, and it should worry the church more than the skeptic. But it is an argument for reading more carefully, not evidence that the meaning is lost. Two things count against the stronger conclusion. First, translations made by people with very different convictions agree on the substance. Alter writes as a scholar of Hebrew literature, Hart as a theologian who has argued that all will be saved, the King James translators were Reformation Protestants, and the modern committees range across the churches. On what Jesus did and said, on his death and what his followers proclaimed about it, they agree. They differ at known places. Second, no reader of any ancient text reads without trusting translators, and with the Bible he has more of them to trust, more independent of one another, and more openly argued about, than with any other book. That is the same pattern this area has found with the manuscripts: many witnesses, compared in the open, with the doubtful places known.

## What remains unresolved

Some translation questions are open, and scholars who share the same evidence answer them differently. The meaning of *pistis Christou* is one. How to render *aiōnios*, and what the answer means for judgment, is another, though most translators and most of the church's tradition read it as eternal. Whether formal or functional translation serves a church better has no settled answer, and the best English Bibles borrow from both. Alter's charge, that modern versions too often explain instead of translating, deserves a better hearing than it has had. None of these touches the substance of the gospel. All of them are reasons to read more than one translation and to read the notes.

## Where Christians differ

Faithful Christians differ over which translation to read, and this page takes no side. Some churches read the King James Version alone, out of reverence for its text and its English. Some prefer a version that keeps close to the original's form for study and preaching, and others a version in plainer speech for new readers. Christians also differ on how far a translation should go in making the text clear and how much it should leave as it stands. These are family arguments. On the conviction behind every Bible in a new language, that God speaks to people in their own tongue, the churches agree, and Acts 2 tells the story of Pentecost as a day when each listener heard the apostles speaking in his own language.
