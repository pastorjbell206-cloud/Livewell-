# Who decides what the Greek New Testament says, and was there ever a single original to find?

## The short answer

No one decides it by say-so, and the work is done in the open. An edition of the Greek New Testament, such as the Nestle-Aland text most translators use, is made by gathering the witnesses (Greek manuscripts, early translations, quotations in early Christian writers), comparing them at every place where they differ, and printing the reading that best explains how the others arose, with the alternatives and their witnesses set out underneath for anyone to check. Editions made by different teams, with different methods and convictions, agree on the great bulk of the text. The harder question is the one D. C. Parker and Eldon Epp have pressed: whether "the original text" is even the right name for what an edition recovers. It's a fair question. Editors now speak more modestly of the earliest text the evidence can reach. That text stands very close to what the authors wrote, though not in every place provably identical to it, and where it isn't, the evidence usually shows it.

## The objection at its strongest

The popular form of the objection says that a committee decides what your Bible says, and changes its mind from one edition to the next. That form is too crude to be worth answering as it stands. The serious form comes from inside the discipline.

In *The Living Text of the Gospels* (1997), D. C. Parker, a leading British textual critic, argued that the search for one original text of the Gospels misunderstands the manuscript tradition of the early church. The Gospels, on his account, were living texts. The communities that used them copied, corrected and adapted them, and the variation in the manuscripts is part of that life, not only damage done to a fixed original. He works through cases such as the Lord's Prayer and the sayings on marriage and divorce, where the wording the church used was not one wording. Two years later Eldon Jay Epp, in a long article in the *Harvard Theological Review* (1999) on the "multivalence" of the term "original text", observed that textual critics had rarely said what they meant by the phrase, and that the older handbooks simply assumed it meant the autograph, the text as the author first wrote it. The phrase can mean several things: what an author first drafted, what was first sent out, what a church first read as authoritative.

The skeptic can build on this. If the experts themselves no longer use the word "original" without qualification, then the confident claim that we have what the apostles wrote rests on a word the field has quietly retired. Meanwhile the edition in your Bible is the work of an editorial board, and it is revised. The 28th edition of Nestle-Aland, published in 2012, changed its text in some thirty places in the letters of James, Peter, John and Jude. Every revision shows that the previous edition was wrong somewhere. Why should the current one be trusted?

## What is true in it

Much of it. No autograph of any New Testament book survives, and every printed Greek New Testament is a reconstruction. Editions are revised, and should be, as evidence is found and methods improve. In its Catholic Letters, NA28 adopted the text of the *Editio Critica Maior*, the large edition now being made book by book, and sources count the changes at between thirty and thirty-four. Editors make judgment calls, and good editors disagree.

Parker is also right that the text lived in the church's use, and the Lord's Prayer shows it. Luke gives a shorter form of the prayer than Matthew, and many later copies of Luke filled it out with Matthew's words. The doxology many Christians say at the end of the prayer, "for thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever", is missing from the earliest manuscripts of Matthew, among them Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Bezae, and early commentators on the prayer did not include it. It came into later copies from the church's worship, where a closing word of praise was in use very early. That is a living text.

And the field has changed its language. The *Editio Critica Maior* aims at what its editors call the "initial text" (*Ausgangstext*), the earliest text the evidence allows them to reconstruct, and it doesn't claim more. Christians who talk as if "the original" were a document we could consult have sometimes claimed more than the scholars who make their Bibles.

## The evidence: how an edition is made

The first printed Greek New Testament, Erasmus's of 1516, was set from late medieval manuscripts. The modern method grew out of the realization that this was not enough. In the 1730s Johann Albrecht Bengel taught that witnesses must be weighed and not merely counted. He was among the first to group manuscripts by age and region, and he gave the field one of its lasting rules, that the harder reading is to be preferred to the easier, since copyists tended to smooth a difficulty rather than create one. In 1881 Westcott and Hort published *The New Testament in the Original Greek*, built mainly on Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus. Later critical editions, Nestle-Aland among them, have stayed close to it in character, though they now rest on far more evidence, the early papyri among it.

The work itself has three stages. First the witnesses are gathered: the Greek manuscripts, the early translations into Latin, Syriac, Coptic and other languages, and quotations by early Christian writers, each used with care. Then they are collated, compared word by word, so that every place of difference is recorded. Then each difference is weighed. External evidence asks which witnesses support each reading, how early they are, and whether they come from one region or many. Internal evidence asks what a copyist was likely to do and what the author was likely to write. The guiding question, as an older formula puts it, is which reading best explains how the others arose. In Matthew 24:36 the oldest manuscripts have Jesus say that not even the Son knows the day or hour, and most later ones lack "nor the Son". It is easy to see why a copyist would drop the words and hard to see why one would add them, so editors print them, and Bruce Metzger, an ordained Presbyterian minister, judged them original.

The newest tool, used for the *Editio Critica Maior* and so for NA28's Catholic Letters, is the Coherence-Based Genealogical Method. It uses computers to compare the texts of manuscripts at every place where they differ, to work out which texts stand closest to which, and so to test editors' judgments against the whole tradition at once. Tommy Wasserman and Peter Gurry's *A New Approach to Textual Criticism* (2017) explains it for students.

None of this is hidden. Under its text, NA28 prints an apparatus that lists the main alternatives at each disputed place and the witnesses for each. Metzger's *Textual Commentary* explains, passage by passage, why the committee behind the standard text decided as it did. And there isn't one edition. *The Greek New Testament, Produced at Tyndale House, Cambridge* (2017), edited by Dirk Jongkind with Peter Williams, was made by a separate team over ten years from the most ancient manuscripts available. Michael Holmes edited the SBL Greek New Testament (2010). Maurice Robinson and William Pierpont edited a text (2005) that follows the reading of the majority of manuscripts, on a different theory. A collation of the Tyndale House text against NA28, posted on the Evangelical Textual Criticism blog in 2020, estimated that their differences, apart from spelling, could reach 500 or more, most of them minor, in a book of about 138,000 words.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about a method and what its results can bear.

1. A critical edition prints, at each place, the reading its editors judge earliest by stated criteria, and prints the main alternatives and their witnesses beneath it, so that each decision can be checked by anyone with the evidence.
2. Editions made independently, by different teams with different methods and convictions, agree on the great bulk of the text and disagree at a small and known number of places.
3. The earliest text the evidence reaches is the text circulating in the second century, and where the evidence shows early variation or revision, as in the Lord's Prayer or the ending of Mark, the variation is visible in the witnesses.

So: no one decides what the New Testament says by authority, and the field's caution about the word "original" marks the limit of the method without emptying its results. What the editions print is the earliest text the evidence can reach, and the evidence gives no sign of a different text standing behind it, except in the places where it shows one.

The weight sits on the third step, because it is where Parker's challenge bites. A reader can test it by asking for the cases: Parker's own examples are drawn from variation we can see, which is what the step claims. What the step can't do is reach behind the second century, and the reply below takes that up.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply says this. If the editors themselves now aim at an "initial text" and not an original, then they have admitted that the original is out of reach. The initial text is simply the oldest form the surviving copies point to. Whatever happened between the authors' first drafts and that form is beyond the method by definition. You say the evidence gives no sign of a different text behind it, but the method could never give such a sign.

That is right about the method, and the change of word should be welcomed, not explained away. It is a change of honesty, not of evidence. But the reply needs one more step to become an argument against the text, and the step is missing. To say that the initial text differs from what the authors wrote, in any particular place, needs evidence of the difference. Where there is such evidence, we have it in view: the two endings of Mark, the story of the woman caught in adultery, the Lord's Prayer in its longer and shorter forms, and in the Old Testament the two editions of Jeremiah found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Those cases show what an earlier form leaves behind when it exists, which is traces in some branch of the tradition. For most of the New Testament there are no such traces. A distance that leaves no trace can't be measured, but it can't simply be assumed to be large either.

There is a second answer, and it belongs to theology, not method. Christians confess that God inspired what the authors wrote, and many hold that his providence also covered how the books were composed and kept. Whether a book went through a draft before it was sent out is a question about how it was written. It isn't evidence that what the church received was something other than what the author meant it to receive.

## What remains unresolved

The meaning of "original text" is still argued, and Epp's point stands: the phrase should be used with care. How much the computer-assisted method can show, and how far it can test the editors' own judgments, is debated among the scholars who use it. The first century of copying is not directly visible to anyone. And a set of specific readings remains open, where editions disagree or print their choices with doubt, some of them taken up on other pages in this area. None of these touches the substance of the books. All of them mean that a careful reader should know which edition he is reading and what its brackets mean.

## Where Christians differ

Faithful Christians differ on which Greek text to follow, and this page takes no side. Most scholars, evangelicals among them, follow critical editions like Nestle-Aland or the Tyndale House text. Others hold that the reading of the great majority of manuscripts, the Byzantine text, is usually original, and use editions like Robinson and Pierpont's. Others keep to the printed Greek text behind the King James Version as the text the church received, a position the next pages take up with the respect it deserves. Christians also differ on whether inspiration belongs strictly to the autographs or extends to the text God has kept through its copying, and on how to state Scripture's freedom from error. These are family arguments. On the method described here, its openness and its limits, the traditions can largely agree.
