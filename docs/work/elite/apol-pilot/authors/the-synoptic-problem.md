# If Matthew and Luke copied Mark, aren't the first three Gospels really one witness rather than three?

## The short answer

Partly, and that part should be said first. Most scholars think Matthew and Luke used Mark, and the case for some copying among the first three Gospels is strong: long passages agree in order and in exact Greek wording. So for the stories all three tell, the Synoptic Gospels are not three independent witnesses, and Christians who count them that way overstate the case. But copying isn't the same as having one source. Matthew and Luke each add sayings and stories Mark doesn't have, and historians on the majority view, skeptics included, count Mark, the shared sayings and each one's own material as separate sources; those who think Luke used Matthew count fewer, but still more than one. Paul stands outside the question altogether, and John, whether or not he knew the others, is not one of the three. And a writer who keeps his source, even its awkward sayings, while smoothing its Greek, is telling us he trusted it. This page takes no side on how the three are related, or on whether the lost source called Q existed.

## The objection at its strongest

Set Matthew, Mark and Luke side by side in columns, as a synopsis does, and the problem jumps off the page. They tell many of the same stories, often in the same order and often in the same Greek words. As the Study Bible's guide to reading the Gospels sets out the facts, nearly all of Mark's content appears in Matthew, about ninety percent of it, and a little over half of it in Luke, and where the three share a story, Matthew and Luke seldom agree with each other in order against Mark. The explanation most scholars accept is the one B. H. Streeter gave its classic English form in *The Four Gospels* (1924): Mark was written first, and Matthew and Luke each used it, along with a second, lost source of sayings that scholars call Q.

Then the objection draws the conclusion. If Matthew and Luke copied Mark, then for every story in the triple tradition there is one witness, not three. The healing of the paralytic, the feeding of the five thousand and the trial before the council are not confirmed three times over; they are told once and retold twice. And the retelling was free. Mark says that in Nazareth Jesus "could not perform any miracles there, except to lay His hands on a few of the sick and heal them" (Mark 6:5, BSB); Matthew makes it "He did not do many miracles there, because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58, BSB). In Mark, Jesus answers a man, "Why do you call Me good?" (Mark 10:18, BSB); in Matthew the question becomes "Why do you ask Me about what is good?" (Matthew 19:17, BSB). Writers who changed their source to suit their theology, the objection says, can't be counted as further witnesses to what happened. They are witnesses only to what Mark wrote and to what they preferred.

## What is true in it

The core of it is true. The agreements are too close, across too much material, in reports of speech first given in another language, to be explained without some writers using the written work of others, and most scholars think so. For the stories the three share, the Synoptics are not three independent accounts, and a Christian who says "three Gospels tell us" about such a story is counting copies. It's also true that Matthew and Luke edited Mark. The guide notes that they often smooth his rough Greek and soften statements that might seem to limit Jesus or embarrass the disciples, and Mark 6:5 beside Matthew 13:58 is the standard example.

None of this was discovered by skeptics. Around 400 Augustine, comparing the Gospels line by line in his *Harmony of the Gospels*, wrote that Mark "follows him closely, and looks like his attendant and epitomizer" (1.2, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* translation), meaning Matthew. He had the order differently from most modern scholars, but he saw the literary relationship fifteen centuries ago.

## The evidence

How the three are related is not settled, and the main solutions should be stated fairly, as the guide states them. The Two-Source view, argued in Germany from Karl Lachmann in 1835 to Heinrich Julius Holtzmann in 1863 and given its English form by Streeter, holds that Mark came first and that Matthew and Luke each used Mark and Q. It is the majority view. The Farrer view, proposed by Austin Farrer in "On Dispensing with Q" (1955) and argued most fully by Mark Goodacre in *The Case Against Q* (2002), keeps Mark first but holds that Luke used Matthew, so that Q never existed; it points to the many small places where Matthew and Luke agree against Mark, as in the mockers' question "Who hit You?" (Matthew 26:68 and Luke 22:64, BSB), which Mark's account lacks. Q's defenders answer, as the guide sets out both cases, that such agreements can be explained as coincidence, later copying or overlap with Q, and that if Luke had Matthew, his treatment of it, rearranging and omitting so much, would be very hard to explain. The Two-Gospel view, proposed by Johann Jakob Griesbach in the 1780s and revived by William Farmer in 1964, puts Matthew first and Mark last. Others have defended Augustine's order, and some, such as Eta Linnemann in *Is There a Synoptic Problem?* (1992), have denied literary dependence altogether. Most scholars now hold, the guide reports, that written sources and living oral tradition overlapped for decades.

Now look at what copying shows. Matthew kept about nine-tenths of Mark. Luke kept over half of it and opens his Gospel by saying that many had already written accounts handed down "by the initial eyewitnesses and servants of the word" and that he had "carefully investigated everything from the beginning" (Luke 1:2-3, BSB). Whether or not he meant his book to take its place, a writer who builds on a source that much is vouching for its substance. Papias, early in the second century, passed on the report that Mark had written down Peter's teaching (Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15, in McGiffert's translation), and if the later evangelists believed that, their use of Mark is what we would expect. Their changes are mostly of wording, and they show restraint as well as freedom. Luke kept Mark's awkward "Why do you call Me good?" word for word (Luke 18:19, BSB), and Matthew, who reworded it, didn't drop the scene.

Then look at what copying doesn't show. Matthew and Luke share about two hundred and twenty to two hundred and thirty-five verses that Mark doesn't have, mostly sayings, such as John the Baptist's "You brood of vipers, who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?" (Matthew 3:7 and Luke 3:7, BSB), word for word. Each also has material no other Gospel has: Matthew's Magi, Luke's prodigal son. On the majority view historians count these as distinct sources. Bart Ehrman does: in *How Jesus Became God* (2014), arguing against the historicity of Jesus' divine claims in John, he lists as our numerous earlier sources Paul, Mark, Q, and the material found only in Matthew or only in Luke, as a reading of that section records his words. A skeptical historian who counts five sources in the first three Gospels and Paul is not counting one.

Last, the witnesses outside the problem. Paul's letters are earlier than any Gospel, and he hands on, as tradition he received, the words of the Last Supper (1 Corinthians 11:23-25), which the Study Bible's note on that chapter says closely match Luke 22:19-20, and a list of those who saw the risen Jesus (1 Corinthians 15:3-7). Whether John knew the other Gospels is debated: C. H. Dodd, in *Historical Tradition in the Fourth Gospel* (1963), argued that John drew on an independent tradition, while Frans Neirynck and others argued that he used the Synoptics, as the guide summarizes the debate. Either way, the Synoptic problem concerns three books, not the whole of the evidence.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about sources: what the literary relationship among the first three Gospels shows, and what it leaves standing.

1. The close agreements in order and Greek wording show that the Synoptic writers used one another's work or shared written sources, so they are not independent witnesses to the stories they share.
2. On every major solution, Matthew and Luke each contain much that Mark lacks, and scholars of every view, the skeptical Bart Ehrman among them, treat much of that material as earlier tradition rather than the evangelists' own invention.
3. A writer who keeps most of his source, including sayings awkward for him, while improving its wording, shows that he regarded it as reliable.

So: the first three Gospels are not three independent witnesses to the stories they share, but neither are they one; they preserve more than one early source on any solution, and several on the majority view, their use of Mark is evidence that Mark was trusted by the next writers to tell the story, and Paul remains a witness outside the problem, as John does if he wrote independently of them.

The weight sits on the second step. If the material Matthew and Luke add to Mark came from no source but the evangelists' own invention, the Synoptics would reduce to Mark plus editing, and the objection would stand.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that the second step leans on hypotheses. Q is a reconstructed document no one has ever seen. On the Farrer view, Luke took the shared sayings from Matthew, so the "double tradition" is Matthew's, and Matthew's own material, and Luke's, may be their own composition rather than older sources. Count only what can be proved, the reply says, and the Synoptics give us Mark and two editors.

That reply is right that Q is a hypothesis, and this page doesn't depend on it. But the reply proves less than it needs. If Q never existed, the sayings Matthew and Luke share still came from somewhere, and on the Farrer view the somewhere is Matthew, a Gospel which, on any view, is not simply Mark. To make the sayings and the special material the evangelists' inventions, the reply has to show it, saying by saying, against the scholars on every side who treat them as tradition, Ehrman among them. And the most reductive solution still leaves Mark, written within the lifetime of people who had known Jesus, Paul's received traditions from earlier still, and John, whatever his relation to the others. Independence is also not the only test of a witness. A later writer who corrects his source's grammar but keeps its hard sayings is evidence that the source was received as true by people in a position to know.

## What remains unresolved

Whether Q existed is unresolved, and so is the order of the Gospels; the minor agreements of Matthew and Luke against Mark are explained differently on each view. How much the variation among the Synoptics comes from editing and how much from oral performance is debated. Whether John knew Mark is still argued. And the literary question, however it is answered, doesn't settle whether what the Gospels report happened; that is weighed on the other pages in this area.

## Where Christians differ

Christian scholars stand on every side of the Synoptic problem. Many accept Mark's priority and Q; some, with Farrer and Goodacre, accept Mark's priority without Q; some, among them John Wenham in *Redating Matthew, Mark and Luke* (1991), as the guide reports, have defended Augustine's order; and some, with Linnemann, deny that the Gospels copied each other at all. This page takes no side. Nothing in the church's confession turns on it. Luke says in his first sentence that he used the accounts of others, and the church has never thought that made his Gospel less inspired. What Christians confess together is that God gave the church four Gospels, related as they are, as a true witness to Jesus Christ.
