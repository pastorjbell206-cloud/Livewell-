# Weren't some New Testament letters forged in the names of Paul and Peter?

## The short answer

Some New Testament letters may not have been written by the men whose names they carry, and the case for doubting them is serious, literary, and made by careful scholars of every conviction. Nearly everyone accepts seven of Paul's thirteen letters as his. Ephesians, Colossians and 2 Thessalonians divide scholars; the letters to Timothy and Titus, and 2 Peter, are doubted by most critical scholars and defended by many evangelical ones. Bart Ehrman calls the doubted letters forgeries, and he's right that ancient writers, Christians among them, condemned writing under a false name. But each strand of the case has an answer, the early church received these letters while rejecting others it knew to be false, and what Christians should conclude if a letter was written by a disciple in his teacher's name is itself debated. One thing doesn't depend on the answer. What the creeds confess about Christ is taught in letters almost no one doubts, and in the Gospels.

## The objection at its strongest

Bart Ehrman made the case for a general reader in *Forged* (2011) and for scholars in *Forgery and Counterforgery* (2013). Of the thirteen letters that carry Paul's name, critical scholars agree on seven as Paul's own: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon. The others, Ehrman argues, were written after Paul's death by people who claimed to be Paul, and the two letters under Peter's name were written by people who claimed to be Peter. Acts itself calls Peter an unlettered man, and his native language was Aramaic, yet 1 Peter is written in polished Greek.

The reasons are not prejudice against the supernatural. They are the kind of evidence any literary historian uses. The letters to Timothy and Titus use words that are frequent there and rare or absent in Paul's other letters, among them godliness and sound teaching. They describe a church with overseers, deacons and an enrolled order of widows, more settled than the churches of Paul's undisputed letters, and their travel notes are hard to fit into the story Acts tells. Ephesians is written in long, piled-up sentences unlike Paul's, 1:3-14 a single sentence in Greek; it speaks of salvation as already accomplished and of the church as one universal body, and it calls the apostles and prophets the church's foundation, which reads like a later generation looking back. Colossians says believers have already been raised with Christ, where Romans speaks of resurrection as still to come. Second Thessalonians follows 1 Thessalonians closely in its wording yet seems to teach differently about the end, and it warns against letters seeming to be from Paul while insisting on his signature, just what a later writer claiming his authority might do. Second Peter is in ornate Greek quite unlike 1 Peter, seems to use the letter of Jude, refers to Paul's letters as a collection standing alongside the other Scriptures, and speaks as if the founding generation had died.

And, Ehrman argues, the ancients knew what this was. Pagan, Jewish and Christian writers alike treated a false claim of authorship as deceit. Tertullian, around 200, reports that a presbyter in Asia who wrote the Acts of Paul, and said he did it out of love for Paul, was removed from office. The Muratorian fragment rejects letters to the Laodiceans and to the Alexandrians forged in Paul's name. If the church condemned forgery, then forgeries in its canon were deceptions it failed to catch. The usual Christian reply, that writing in a famous teacher's name was an accepted convention, is on this view a modern excuse the ancients didn't make.

## What is true in it

The arguments are real, and they deserve to be met as arguments. They were not invented by skeptics; many of them go back to the nineteenth century, and some of the doubts are ancient. Eusebius, early in the fourth century, listed 2 Peter among the books disputed by some, and Jerome noted that many rejected it because its style differed from 1 Peter. Marcion's collection of Paul's letters in the second century didn't include the letters to Timothy and Titus. A Christian who answers these arguments by calling them anti-supernatural hasn't read them.

Ehrman is also right about ancient attitudes, more right than some Christian writers have allowed. Armin Baum, reviewing *Forgery and Counterforgery*, accepted that ancient readers condemned false authorship as deceit, though he disputed other parts of the argument. The Tertullian story and the Muratorian fragment are real evidence. Christians who say simply that "everyone wrote in other people's names and no one minded" have overstated a case that is, at best, debated.

## The evidence

Begin with how letters were written. Paul dictated. At the end of Romans the man who wrote it down adds his own greeting: I, Tertius, who wrote down this letter. Several letters name co-senders, Colossians Paul and Timothy, 2 Thessalonians Paul, Silvanus and Timothy, and Paul says in more than one letter that he adds the closing greeting in his own hand. First Peter says it was written through Silvanus. E. Randolph Richards, in *The Secretary in the Letters of Paul* (1991), studied how secretaries worked in the Roman world, drawing on Cicero's letters and his use of secretaries among his evidence, and argued that a secretary could shape a letter's wording a great deal. One critic has answered that the shifts of style Richards documents are far smaller than Ephesians or Colossians would need, and the debate continues in the journals. James Dunn suggested that Timothy composed Colossians in Paul's lifetime and with his approval, which would explain both its Pauline substance and its different style.

Then the size of the sample. The letters to Timothy and Titus are short, and statistics drawn from short texts are fragile. Anthony Kenny's computer study, *A Stylometric Study of the New Testament* (1986), found no reason on his measures to reject the hypothesis that twelve of the thirteen letters under Paul's name share one author. Jermo van Nes, in *Pauline Language and the Pastoral Epistles* (2018), tested the familiar claim that the Pastorals' language is un-Pauline and found that only a minority of their peculiarities of vocabulary and syntax differ significantly from Paul's other letters. Neither study proves that Paul wrote them. Both show that the argument from style is weaker than it once seemed.

Then the setting. A letter to a trusted coworker reads differently from a letter of argument to a congregation. The church order in the Pastorals is simple compared with the second century: elders and overseers, with no single bishop over them, which some date early for that reason. Their travels fit a period after Acts ends, if Paul was released from his first Roman imprisonment, as Clement of Rome near the end of the first century and Eusebius later suggest. The personal details of Colossians match Philemon, which nearly everyone accepts, so closely, from Onesimus to Archippus, that a forger would have had to copy them with unusual care. The overlap of Ephesians with Colossians fits one writer sending two letters with the same messenger.

Then the reception. Polycarp, early in the second century, appears to echo 1 Timothy, and Irenaeus, around 180, quotes it as Paul's. Ephesians is well attested among second-century writers. These letters were received while others under Paul's name were refused. The early church rejected writings it judged forged, as Tertullian's story and the Muratorian fragment show, and it received these. Second Peter shows that care at work: it was received slowly, after real doubt, and Athanasius included it in his list of 367.

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about what the evidence settles and what it doesn't.

1. The case against the disputed letters is cumulative and literary, and for each strand of it, vocabulary, style, theology and setting, there is a reasoned reply; on Ephesians, Colossians and 2 Thessalonians scholars remain divided, and on the Pastorals and 2 Peter a majority of critical scholars doubt them while many others defend them.
2. The early church condemned writings it knew to be falsely ascribed, and it received these letters, most of them early and 2 Peter slowly and after debate.
3. Every doctrine the creeds confess, the Trinity, the deity and humanity of Christ, his death for sins and bodily resurrection, salvation by grace, is taught in letters whose authorship is almost universally accepted and in the Gospels.

So: "the New Testament contains forgeries" is a contested judgment, not a finding, and a reader can weigh it either way without his confession moving. What a decision would affect is how a Christian states the truthfulness of Scripture, and there Christians differ.

The weight sits on the first step. If the literary case were as strong as a fingerprint, the reception of the letters would only show that the church was fooled. A reader can test the step against the Study Bible's introductions to each letter, which set out the arguments on both sides.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

Ehrman's strongest reply is that the argument tries to have it both ways. If the ancients condemned forgery, and if any of these letters is pseudonymous, then its author lied, and the church received a lie. Reception proves only that a forgery succeeded. And a book that deceives its readers about its author can't be trusted when it speaks about God.

That reply is serious, and it should be answered in parts. First, it needs the literary case to succeed, and that is just what remains in dispute. For Ephesians, Colossians and 2 Thessalonians scholarly opinion is divided, and the reply can't treat a disputed judgment as a settled one. Second, it needs every pseudonymous letter to have been deceptive. Some scholars who doubt that Paul wrote the Pastorals deny this. I. Howard Marshall proposed, in his work on these letters, that someone close to Paul may have continued to write as Paul would have, perhaps completing work Paul began, and he called this allonymity, writing in another's name without intent to deceive. Others, Terry Wilder among them, argue that ancient writers, early Christians among them, had scruples about writing under another's name, which tells against it. The ancient evidence is debated, and the reply can't assume its own side of the debate. Third, even if a reader grants Ehrman's whole case, the gospel itself doesn't move. Christianity's case never rested on 2 Peter. It rests on Jesus' death and resurrection, attested in 1 Corinthians and Galatians, letters almost no one doubts, and in the Gospels.

What the reply does show is that the question matters for the doctrine of Scripture, and that Christians who hold that every claim a biblical book makes is true have a real stake in it. That is why many of them argue, with reasons, that Paul and Peter wrote the letters that carry their names.

## What remains unresolved

The authorship of each disputed letter is unresolved, and scholars of every conviction come down in different places. What ancient readers thought of a disciple writing in his teacher's name, and whether every case was deceit, is argued in the scholarly literature and isn't settled. How much freedom a secretary had, and whether statistics on texts this short can decide anything, are open. And 2 Peter remains the hardest case, doubted in the ancient church and by most critical scholars now. None of these touches what the creeds confess. All of them deserve better than a slogan from either side.

## Where Christians differ

Faithful Christians differ on the authorship of the disputed letters, and this page takes no side. Evangelical scholarship largely holds that Paul wrote all thirteen letters under his name and Peter both of his; many other scholars, some of them believers who confess these letters as Scripture, think that some were written by Paul's or Peter's followers. Christians also differ on what a doctrine of Scripture's truthfulness requires here. Many evangelicals hold that a letter's claim about its author is part of what it asserts, so that a false claim would be an error, and they defend the traditional authors for that reason. Others hold that writing in a teacher's name, if it was a recognized practice, would make no false claim at all. These are arguments about how God gave his word, not whether he did. The church has confessed these letters as Scripture for sixteen centuries and more, and what they teach about Christ is taught as plainly in the letters almost no one doubts.
