# Before anything was written, the stories about Jesus were passed by word of mouth for decades. Isn't that a game of telephone?

## The short answer

The stories did change in the telling, and the Gospels show it: the same event told with different details, the same saying in different words. But a game of telephone is the wrong picture of how they were handed on. Telephone is one private whisper passed down a line of people who can't check it against anything. The Jesus tradition was told and retold in public, in communities where people already knew the stories, where some of the first witnesses were still active, and where, as Paul's letters show, teaching was received and passed on as a trust. Scholars still argue about how much control such communities exercised, and some confident claims made on both sides have been rightly challenged. What the Gospels themselves display is the pattern you'd expect from a remembered tradition: a stable core told in flexible words. That doesn't prove every detail. It does mean the telephone game describes neither the process nor the result.

## The objection at its strongest

Everyone has played telephone. A sentence is whispered from one person to the next around a room, and by the end it has turned into something nobody said. Now stretch the room across forty years and a thousand miles, change the language from Aramaic to Greek, and give the players reasons of their own to adjust the story. That, the objection says, is the period between Jesus and the Gospels.

The scholarly form of this argument is a century old. Martin Dibelius in 1919 and Rudolf Bultmann in 1921 founded what came to be called form criticism. They studied the Gospels as collections of short units, each shaped by its use in the life of the early church, its preaching, teaching, worship and controversy. Bultmann in particular judged that a great many of the stories and sayings were created by the communities that used them, so that the Gospels tell us more about the early church than about Jesus. Bart Ehrman, in *Jesus Before the Gospels* (2016), brought the argument up to date with modern research on memory. Memory, he argues, is not a recording but a reconstruction. Eyewitnesses misremember, confident memories can be false, and groups remember the past in ways that serve their present. The stories about Jesus were told by people who weren't there to people who had never met him, and each telling reshaped them.

And the Gospels themselves, the objection says, show the damage. In Mark, Jairus tells Jesus his daughter is near death, and the news that she has died comes later; in Matthew he says at once that she has just died. Mark has one blind man at Jericho, Bartimaeus; Matthew has two. At the cross, Mark's centurion says that Jesus was the Son of God and Luke's says he was a righteous man. Even the charge nailed above Jesus' head is worded four ways in four Gospels. If the writers can't agree on the words of a sign, why trust them on the words of a sermon?

## What is true in it

A great deal. The stories did change. The differences listed above are real, and the church has known them as long as it has read four Gospels side by side. Luke's Lord's Prayer is shorter than Matthew's. Jesus' words over the bread and the cup at the Last Supper come down in two forms, one in Mark and Matthew, another in Luke and Paul. Whoever says the Gospels preserve Jesus' words like a recording has said more than they show.

Memory is reconstructive, and the research Ehrman draws on is real. Richard Bauckham, who argued that the Gospels rest on eyewitness testimony, explained in the same book that eyewitness memory can be very unreliable. And some of the answers Christians have given to the telephone objection have been weaker than they sounded. Birger Gerhardsson's *Memory and Manuscript* (1961) argued that Jesus' disciples memorized his teaching as rabbis' pupils memorized their masters', but his evidence came largely from rabbinic sources written down centuries later, and critics, Ehrman among them, have said that reads later practice back into the first century. Kenneth Bailey's account of how Middle Eastern village communities keep their stories, which many Christian scholars took up, rests on traditions he gathered first-hand in Middle Eastern communities, and some of his examples have been shown to be weaker than he presented them.

## The evidence

Start with what a game of telephone is, because the comparison does a lot of quiet work. The message is heard once, whispered, by one person at a time. No one hears it twice. No one else in the room knows what it was. The players are trying to pass it on, not to keep it, and the fun of the game is the distortion. Oral tradition in a community works almost the opposite way. A story is told many times, publicly, to people who have heard it before. Several people tell it, and the hearers know when a teller has it wrong.

The New Testament shows us something of how the Jesus tradition was actually handed on. Paul, writing in the 50s, twice uses the language of a received tradition: what he received, he passed on. He uses it of the summary of Christ's death, burial, resurrection and appearances in 1 Corinthians 15, and of the Last Supper in 1 Corinthians 11. The Study Bible's note on 1 Corinthians 15 calls these the technical terms for transmitting tradition. Paul's words over the bread, "This is my body," and over the cup, the new covenant in Jesus' blood, are close to Luke's, and both stand alongside the form in Mark and Matthew. So we have, in a letter written within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion, a saying of Jesus already being handed on in a recognizable fixed form, with the differences of wording visible beside it. The Gospels also keep Aramaic words in their Greek: *Talitha koum* to a dead girl, *Abba* in Gethsemane, and Paul's *Marana tha*, "Our Lord, come." Translators don't keep foreign words by accident. These are marks of a tradition that remembered where it came from.

Then consider the scholarly argument about control. In 1991 Kenneth Bailey proposed three models of oral tradition. Bultmann's, he said, was informal and uncontrolled. The Scandinavian school of Riesenfeld and Gerhardsson proposed a formal, controlled tradition of memorization. Bailey argued from the communities he had observed for a third, informal but controlled: a story told at gatherings where the community, and especially its senior members, would correct a teller who changed what mattered, while allowing freedom in the rest. James Dunn, in *Jesus Remembered* (2003), made this model central to his study of Jesus, arguing that the earliest tradition passed predominantly by word of mouth and that some differences among the Gospels reflect oral retelling around stable words and themes, alongside the editing of written sources. Theodore Weeden, in the *Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus* (2009), attacked Bailey's evidence severely. Dunn replied in the same volume that Weeden had misunderstood both Bailey and how oral tradition works, and Craig Keener, weighing the exchange later, granted that some of Bailey's examples were weak while arguing that Weeden had not disproved as much as he thought.

Memory research doesn't speak with one voice either. Robert McIver, in *Memory, Jesus, and the Synoptic Gospels* (2011), surveyed the same psychology Ehrman uses and concluded, according to his reviewers, that memories of events with emotional weight last, that a community forms a shared memory quickly, and that the Gospels are trustworthy in their main contours. And Ehrman himself, reviewers note, grants that the Gospels contain gist memories of Jesus that are almost certainly accurate.

Finally, look again at the differences. Jairus's daughter is dying, or dead; either way a desperate father comes, Jesus goes, and the girl is raised. One blind man or two; either way Jesus heals blindness on the road at Jericho. Son of God or righteous man; either way a Roman soldier at the cross is struck by how Jesus died. Four wordings of the charge; all four say he was crucified as the King of the Jews. Telephone loses the core and keeps nothing reliably. This tradition varied the details and kept the core.

## The argument, laid out

This is an inference to the best explanation, from the shape of what survives to the kind of process that produced it.

1. Across the Gospels and Paul's letters, the Jesus tradition shows a stable core, the events and the substance of the sayings, told in varying words and with varying details.
2. Uncontrolled transmission, the kind the telephone game models, loses and transforms the core itself, while tradition handed on in communities that know the story and correct its tellers keeps the core and varies the details.
3. The Jesus tradition was handed on in such communities, in the lifetime of its first witnesses, and as Paul shows, as something received and passed on deliberately.

So: the stories changed in the telling, in ways we can still see, but not as a game of telephone changes a message. The variation lies in the details. The substance was kept.

The weight sits on the second step. It is the step Weeden's critique of Bailey and Ehrman's appeal to memory research both challenge, and it is why the next section matters.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is this. You say the core is stable because the Gospels agree on it. But Matthew and Luke copied Mark, as most scholars think. Their agreement with him isn't three memories confirming one another. It's one text copied twice. So the oral period before Mark is invisible, and nothing in his Gospel can show how well it was kept. A stable core across copies of the same book proves only that copyists copied.

That is a strong reply, and the first part of it should be granted. Where Matthew and Luke follow Mark, their agreement is literary, not independent. But not all the evidence runs through Mark. Paul wrote before any Gospel, on the usual dating, and his summary of the faith and his account of the Last Supper agree with the Gospels on the core. John's Gospel is so different from the other three that scholars still debate whether its author knew them at all, and yet it tells of John the Baptist, the feeding of a great crowd, Jesus' entry into Jerusalem, his trial before Pilate, his crucifixion and the empty tomb. Matthew and Luke also share material that isn't in Mark, which most scholars think came from another early source. Where independent lines of tradition meet, they meet on the core. That is the test the reply asks for, and the tradition passes it, not in every detail but in substance.

As for memory research, it cuts both ways, and both Ehrman and Bauckham concede more to the other side than their readers sometimes notice. Memory distorts details and keeps the gist of what mattered. The Gospels show exactly that pattern.

## What remains unresolved

How much control the early communities exercised over the tradition can't be measured, and Bailey's model, Dunn's use of it and Weeden's critique are still argued. Whether John knew the other Gospels is unresolved, and so is the existence and shape of the source most scholars call Q. Particular stories and sayings are argued case by case, and scholars who agree about the process still disagree about individual pieces of it. And no study of memory or tradition can settle what the reader most wants to know, whether the remarkable things the core contains, above all the resurrection, happened. That is a historical and philosophical question of its own, taken up elsewhere on the site.

## Where Christians differ

Faithful Christians differ on how to understand the Gospels' differences, and this page takes no side. Some harmonize them, holding that apparent differences reflect different selections from fuller events. Others hold that the evangelists, like other ancient writers, had freedom to retell, compress and paraphrase, and that the Gospels give Jesus' voice faithfully without always giving his exact words. Both views are held by Christians who confess the Gospels as the word of God, and they differ too on how a doctrine of Scripture's truthfulness should be stated in the light of such differences. The church received four Gospels, not one smoothed account, and has read their differences openly for nearly two thousand years. It was never asked to believe a transcript. It was asked to believe the witnesses.
