# The Gospels are anonymous. Weren't the names Matthew, Mark, Luke and John added later?

## The short answer

Partly yes, and the part that's true matters less than it sounds. None of the four Gospels names its author in its own text, and the titles we know, "According to Matthew" and the rest, were almost certainly not written by the authors themselves. In that strict sense the Gospels are anonymous. But anonymous isn't the same as unknown. Every early copy that keeps a title gives the same four names, no copy gives a rival name, and the first writer we can hear on the subject, Papias, early in the second century, already links Mark with Peter and Matthew with a written collection of sayings. Two of the names, Mark and Luke, belong to men who were not among the Twelve, which is a strange choice for anyone inventing authority. The evidence doesn't prove that the four traditional authors wrote the four books. It does make it more likely than not that the names travelled with the books from early on, and it leaves the question open where serious scholars, Christians among them, still disagree.

## The objection at its strongest

Open any of the four Gospels and read it through, and you won't find the author's name. Paul's letters begin with his. The Gospels don't. Luke says in his opening lines that he has investigated what was handed down by those who were eyewitnesses from the beginning, which sounds like a man who was not one himself. John's Gospel ends by pointing to the disciple whom Jesus loved as the witness behind the book, and then adds, in the voice of others, that we know his testimony is true. Even the titles have an odd shape. No author calls his own book "the Gospel according to me." "According to Matthew" sounds like a label added by someone who already had several Gospels on the shelf and needed to tell them apart.

Bart Ehrman, an agnostic New Testament scholar, has made this case to a wide readership, first in *Jesus, Interrupted* (2009) and then in *Forged* (2011). As he tells the story, the Gospels circulated without names for decades. Justin Martyr, writing in Rome in the 150s, quotes them often and calls them the memoirs of the apostles, but never says which apostles wrote which. The first writer to name all four is Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, around 180, about thirty years after Justin. By then the church needed apostolic authors to set its books apart from the rival gospels of its opponents, and names were attached for that purpose.

And the names, Ehrman argues, don't fit the books. Matthew and John were Galilean Jews whose language was Aramaic. Acts itself calls Peter and John unlettered men. The Gospels are written in competent Greek, Luke's in very good Greek, by authors with real education in the language. Critics add a point from the texts themselves: Matthew reproduces most of Mark, often word for word. Why would an apostle who had walked with Jesus copy the account of a man who hadn't? Put together, the objection says, the traditional authors are a second-century answer to a second-century need, and believers who assume them are trusting a label, not evidence.

## What is true in it

Much of it should simply be granted. The texts don't name their authors. The titles, in their "according to" form, were very probably given by others when the Gospels began to circulate together, and the German scholar Martin Hengel, who argued that the names are early, thought exactly that. Justin does call them memoirs of the apostles without naming anyone. Irenaeus is the first surviving writer to name all four, and he had a stake in four and only four: he argued that there could be no more and no fewer, as there are four zones of the world and four winds.

Papias, our earliest witness, is not a simple one either. He wrote early in the second century, perhaps around 110 to 130, and his work survives only in quotations, most of them in Eusebius's *Church History*, written two centuries later. What he says about Mark he reports on the authority of an older teacher he calls the elder. What he says about Matthew, that Matthew arranged the sayings, or oracles, in the Hebrew language and each translated them as he could, is hard to fit to the Greek Matthew we have, which doesn't read like a translation and seems to follow Mark's Greek closely.

And Christians have sometimes argued badly here. "The early church unanimously agreed" is true of these names, but unanimity two or three generations later isn't proof, and apologists who have spoken as if it settled the matter have claimed more than the evidence gives. Nor is doubt about the names a skeptic's eccentricity. W. D. Davies and Dale Allison, a Christian, in their commentary on Matthew, think the Gospel's final author was a Jewish-Christian teacher whose work came to carry the apostle's name because some of its tradition went back to him.

## The evidence

Start with the manuscripts. The earliest copies of the Gospels that preserve a title page come from around 200 on the usual dating, among them P66 for John, P75 for Luke and John, and the papyri known as P4, P64 and P67 for Matthew and Luke, though their dates are argued like those of all early papyri. Michael Kruger observes that the second- and third-century copies that keep a title page all carry the traditional names. There are no early copies of these four Gospels under other names.

That uniformity is the heart of the case Hengel made in *Studies in the Gospel of Mark* (1985) and later in a longer study of the four Gospels. If four anonymous books had been given authors by guesswork in different churches across the Mediterranean in the second century, we should expect some variety: a Gospel credited to Andrew in one place and to Philip in another. We find none. Hengel concluded that the titles were added when churches first held more than one Gospel and needed to tell them apart, probably before the end of the first century. Not everyone accepts this. Helmut Koester held that the full form "the Gospel according to ..." can't be that early, while Graham Stanton supported Hengel from other early uses of "gospel" for a written book. In 2018 Simon Gathercole, in the *Journal of Theological Studies*, argued that the lack of a name inside a text doesn't make it anonymous in the sense the objection needs, and that second-century writers who name the Gospels' authors name the same four.

Then the earliest testimony. Eusebius preserves Papias's report, from the elder, that Mark had been Peter's interpreter and wrote down accurately, though not in order, what he remembered of the things the Lord said and did. That takes the link between Mark and Peter back before Papias, to a teacher of the generation before him. Irenaeus, around 180, names all four and gives an account of each: Matthew among the Hebrews, Mark as Peter's interpreter, Luke as Paul's companion, and John, the disciple who leaned on Jesus' breast, at Ephesus. Irenaeus also tells us that as a boy in Asia he had seen Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, through whom he claimed a link to the apostles' own generation. His chain to the first generation is short, though not unbroken.

Now notice the names themselves. If the second-century church had been reaching for authority, Mark and Luke are poor choices. Neither was one of the Twelve. Mark appears in Acts as the companion who turned back partway through Paul's first mission and caused a sharp quarrel between Paul and Barnabas. Luke is a name in three greetings at the ends of letters. When people did write gospels in an apostle's name, they chose apostles: a gospel circulated under Peter's name around 200, and Serapion, bishop of Antioch, read it and found much in it sound and some things added. The four names look less like a bid for authority than like a memory the church wasn't free to improve.

The texts themselves fit that memory in places. Luke's preface doesn't claim that its author saw Jesus. It claims careful investigation of what was handed down by eyewitnesses, which is just what tradition says of Luke. And John's closing verses name a disciple as the witness behind the book while others speak beside him, which fits a Gospel that rests on one man's testimony and was finished by those around him.

## The argument, laid out

This is an inference to the best explanation, about where the names came from.

1. The four Gospels don't name their authors in their texts, but every early copy that keeps a title gives the same four names, and no early copy gives another.
2. Names attached by guesswork in scattered churches during the second century would be expected to vary, and names chosen to lend authority would be expected to belong to apostles.
3. Instead the names don't vary, two of them belong to men who weren't apostles, and the earliest testimony we have, Papias reporting an elder before him, already ties Mark to Peter and Matthew to a written collection.

So: the names most likely travelled with the Gospels from the time churches first held more than one of them, late in the first century or very early in the second. That makes the tradition early. It doesn't by itself make it right, and on Matthew and John in particular careful readers still differ.

The weight sits on the second step, because it is where the objection must push back. A critic can say that standardization could happen later, and the next section takes that up. But a reader can test the step against the gospels that really were credited to famous names, Peter, Thomas, later Judas, and ask whether the four look like the same kind of thing.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply grants the uniformity and denies that it reaches back far. Our copies with titles date from about 200 at the earliest, and by then Irenaeus's account had spread. A copyist adding a title in 200 would add the one his church used. So the uniform titles tell us what the churches said around 200. They can't tell us what they said in 100, still less in 70. And the one earlier witness, Papias, is a fragment preserved by a later admirer, repeating a secondhand report, and what he says of Matthew doesn't fit our Matthew.

That is a fair point about the manuscripts, and it should be granted. Titles in copies from 200 are evidence for 200. But the reply needs the names to have been settled everywhere at once, in roughly a generation, with no central authority to settle them. By the end of the second century the same four names were in use in Gaul with Irenaeus, in Alexandria with Clement, who passed on a tradition of the elders about the order of the Gospels, and in the list known as the Muratorian fragment, which calls Luke the third Gospel and John the fourth and is traditionally placed in or near Rome late in the second century, though some date it to the fourth. Churches that disagreed sharply about other books, as later pages in this area show, didn't disagree about these four names. A uniform result across that much distance is easier to explain if the names were already attached to the books when they spread than if each church supplied them afterward.

The point about Papias and Matthew is real, and it cuts in an interesting direction. A tradition invented to give Greek Matthew an apostle would hardly say that Matthew wrote in Hebrew and that everyone translated as best he could. That awkwardness is the mark of a report someone received and passed on without smoothing it. It may mean that the apostle's work stands behind our Matthew without being identical to it, which is what several of the scholars named above think. It doesn't mean that the name came from nowhere.

## What remains unresolved

A good deal. Whether the apostle Matthew wrote the Gospel that bears his name, or stands behind it as the source of some of its material, is unresolved, and the Study Bible's introduction to Matthew sets out both views. Who the beloved disciple was, the son of Zebedee, another disciple from Jerusalem, or the John the elder whom Papias mentions, is argued by scholars who agree that the Fourth Gospel rests on a remembered eyewitness. What Papias meant by the "sayings" Matthew arranged, and what language he meant, is disputed. How early the "according to" titles were written into copies can't be shown from manuscripts we don't have. These are real gaps, and an honest reader lives with them.

## Where Christians differ

Faithful Christians differ on the authorship of the Gospels, and this page takes no side. Many evangelical scholars hold that the four traditional authors wrote the four books, and that the early and uniform tradition deserves more trust than modern doubt gives it. Others, in every tradition, hold that some of the names mark the source or guarantor of a Gospel's tradition rather than the hand that wrote its final form. What the creeds bind is not a list of authors. The church received Hebrews as Scripture without knowing who wrote it; Origen thought its thoughts were Paul's and its wording someone else's, and wrote, "But who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows." The Gospels' authority doesn't rest on a title page. Their truth as history rests on evidence that the following pages in this area take up: how early they were written, how the tradition behind them was kept, and why the church received these four.
