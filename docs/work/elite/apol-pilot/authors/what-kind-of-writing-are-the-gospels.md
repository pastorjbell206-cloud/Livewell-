# The Gospels were written by believers to make believers. What kind of writing are they, and does their kind make them history, legend or something in between?

## The short answer

They are ancient biographies, the kind of book Greek and Roman readers called Lives, written by believers and shaped by Israel's Scriptures. That answer cuts both ways, and both edges should be felt. Ancient biography was not modern history. Its writers chose scenes to show character, compressed, arranged material by topic rather than by date, and gave speeches in their own words; Plutarch says outright, "my design is not to write histories, but lives." So the Gospels' differences of order and wording are what the genre led readers to expect, not evidence of fraud. But the genre also tells us what the Gospels claim: that they report what a real man said and did, as Luke says he investigated it and as a second-century reader called them "memoirs of the apostles." The genre doesn't prove those reports true. It does rule out reading them as folk tales that never meant to tell what happened.

## The objection at its strongest

The objection comes in two forms, and they pull in opposite directions. The first was made by the founders of form criticism early in the twentieth century. Karl Ludwig Schmidt, Martin Dibelius and Rudolf Bultmann, whose *The History of the Synoptic Tradition* appeared in 1921, argued, as the Study Bible's guide to reading the Gospels summarizes them, that the Gospels were not biographies at all. They were folk literature, collections of small units of oral tradition strung together by editors, a kind of writing with no real parallel. The units had been shaped by the needs of preaching, worship and controversy in the early churches, and the frame that joins them, the order of events, the settings and the transitions, was the editors' own. On this view the question "Did it happen this way?" is a category mistake. The Gospels were never meant to answer it.

The second form grants that the Gospels are ancient biographies and then asks what that buys. Ancient writers of history and of Lives took liberties no modern historian would allow. Thucydides, the Athenian historian of the war with Sparta, said of the speeches in his history that it was hard to carry them word for word in memory, "so my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions" (1.22, in Crawley's translation). Plutarch filled the opening of his *Life of Alexander* with omens: a thunderbolt in his mother's dream, a serpent found lying beside her as she slept, the rumor that a god was his father. Ancient Lives existed to hold up a character for praise or blame. If the Gospels belong to that kind, the objection says, then their speeches may be the writers' compositions and their wonders the ornaments that every great man's Life acquired. Calling them biographies doesn't rescue them. It places them beside Plutarch's omens.

## What is true in it

A good deal, and it should be granted plainly. Ancient biography was not modern history, and anyone who reads the Gospels as if they were transcripts or police reports will misread them and then be shaken when they disagree. Plutarch says his aim was to "epitomize the most celebrated parts" of a man's story rather than to insist "at large on every particular circumstance," and to attend to "the marks and indications of the souls of men," leaving "great battles to be treated of by others" (*Alexander* 1, in Dryden's translation). Suetonius, after a short summary of Augustus's life, says he will treat its parts "not in order of time, but arranging his acts into distinct classes" (*Augustus* 9, in Thomson's translation). The Gospels do the same kinds of thing. Matthew gathers Jesus' teaching into five long discourses, each closed with a formula like "When Jesus had finished saying these things" (Matthew 7:28, BSB). Luke, as the Study Bible's introduction to his Gospel puts it, arranges "sometimes by theme rather than strict chronology." Papias, early in the second century, passed on an elder's report that Mark wrote down what he remembered "accurately, though not in order" (Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15, in McGiffert's translation). Jesus taught in Aramaic, and the Gospels give his words in Greek, so every saying in them is at least a translation.

It is also true that the Gospels were written to persuade. John says so: "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (John 20:31, BSB). And Christians have sometimes defended them as if every sentence were a stenographer's record, which the Gospels themselves never claim.

## The evidence

Start with what happened to the form critics' view. It held the field for decades and then, as the Study Bible's guide records, largely gave way. Charles Talbert, in *What Is a Gospel?* (1977), and above all Richard Burridge, in *What Are the Gospels?* (1992), set the Gospels beside the ancient Lives: Plutarch's, Tacitus's *Agricola*, Suetonius's *Caesars*, Philostratus's *Apollonius*. As the Study Bible's guide summarizes their work, they found the Gospels sharing the genre's main features: a focus on one person, a narrative framed by his public career, a large share of space for his words and deeds, and long attention to his death as revealing who he was. The guide reports that most scholars now accept that the Gospels are ancient biographies "of a distinctive Jewish and Christian kind," and the site's argued answer on trusting the Bible says the same.

Then listen to the earliest readers. Justin Martyr, writing in the middle of the second century in an apology addressed to the emperor Antoninus Pius, called the Gospels "the memoirs composed by" the apostles, "which are called Gospels," and said they were read on Sundays alongside "the writings of the prophets" (*First Apology* 66 and 67, in the *Ante-Nicene Fathers* translation). He reached for a word that meant recollections of a real person, not for a word for fables. Luke opens with the conventions of a historian: others have written accounts of these events "just as they were handed down to us by the initial eyewitnesses and servants of the word," and he has "carefully investigated everything from the beginning" in order to write "an orderly account" (Luke 1:2-3, BSB). A reader may doubt that he succeeded. But he is plainly claiming to report what happened.

Next, look closely at what the ancient freedoms actually were. Thucydides' sentence about speeches goes on: he gave them "of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said," and for the events themselves he says he did not trust even his own impressions but tested every report "by the most severe and detailed tests possible" (1.22). The freedom was a freedom of wording within a duty to the substance. Plutarch's freedom was selection: he leaves the battles to others because his subject is character, not because he thinks battles can be invented. Suetonius rearranges by topic and says so. None of these three claimed the right to make up a career. When Plutarch reports the omens of Alexander's birth, he reports them as what was said ("Others say," "it is said"), and a reader of his day could tell the difference between a reported rumor and the narrated life.

Finally, notice what the Gospels do and don't do. Matthew and Luke do open with wonders, an angel, dreams and a star, and a reader of Plutarch would recognize the kind; that should be granted. But Mark has no birth story at all, John begins with a theological prologue that no reader could mistake for a report of an event, and the long middle of all four is a public career. They name places and officials a reader could check: Pilate as governor, Caiaphas as high priest, Capernaum, Bethany, the pool by the Sheep Gate. And they preserve sayings that embarrassed their own readers, which a writer composing freely for effect would have had every reason to drop. John even tells his readers what he left out: Jesus did "many other signs ... which are not written in this book" (John 20:30, BSB).

## The argument, laid out

This is an argument about genre: what kind of claims the Gospels make, and what freedoms their first readers allowed them.

1. The Gospels share the main marks of ancient Lives, and their earliest readers called them the apostles' memoirs.
2. The ancient writers of Lives and histories who describe their own method, Thucydides, Plutarch and Suetonius among them, claim the freedom to select, compress, arrange by topic and give speeches in their own words, report what they tell of a life as having happened, and mark rumor as rumor.
3. Luke says he investigated the tradition of eyewitnesses, John's Gospel claims an eyewitness behind it, and Papias says Mark wrote down Peter's teaching accurately though not in order.

So: the Gospels are best read as ancient biographies that claim to report what Jesus said and did, written with freedoms of order and wording that their first readers expected; their differences in order and phrasing are not evidence of fraud, and their genre alone does not show that their reports are accurate.

The weight sits on the second step. If writers of Lives were understood to invent what their subjects did, or their books were not read as claims about real lives at all, then placing the Gospels among them would tell us nothing about their intent.

## The strongest reply, and an answer

The strongest reply is that "Life" was a loose genre. Philostratus's *Life of Apollonius of Tyana*, finished, as its translator F. C. Conybeare reckons, no earlier than 217 and about a century after its hero's death, tells of an apparition of the god Proteus to his mother before he was born and of swans that cried out around her as she gave birth (1.4-5, in Conybeare's translation of 1912), and Plutarch himself passes on the omens of Alexander's birth. A genre that holds both sober and fabulous books can't tell us which a given Gospel is, so the conclusion is either empty or smuggles in more than it shows.

That is a fair objection to anyone who argues "the Gospels are biographies, therefore they are reliable," and that argument should not be made. The genre establishes intent, not accuracy: what the writers meant their readers to take as having happened. Whether they had good information is a separate question, answered by when they wrote, how near their sources were to the events, and how their accounts compare with each other and with what outsiders report, which the other pages in this area take up. But the reply doesn't touch the narrower point, and the narrower point matters. It rules out the form critics' claim that the Gospels never meant to tell what happened. And it puts the question about them where it belongs, as a question historians ask of any ancient Life. Plutarch wrote of Alexander some four centuries after Alexander's death, and historians still read him as a source, critically, sorting reported rumor from narrated fact. The Gospels were written within the lifetime of people who had known Jesus. They deserve at least the same treatment: neither waved through as transcripts nor waved away as fables.

## What remains unresolved

How far the conventions of ancient writing reach into the Gospels is still argued. How freely could a writer move a saying to a new setting, or compress two events into one? Scholars who agree that the Gospels are Lives disagree about that. Whether Mark is better read beside Greek Lives or beside the stories of Israel's prophets is debated, and so is whether John, with its long discourses, fits the genre as the Synoptics do. And no study of genre can say, by itself, whether the miracles happened; that turns on the evidence and on whether there is a God who acts, which other pages in this library weigh.

## Where Christians differ

Christians differ on how to describe the Gospels' truthfulness. The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978) denies that inerrancy "is negated by Biblical phenomena such as ... the topical arrangement of material, variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations" (Article XIII). Others prefer to say that Scripture is without error in all that it teaches, and some argue that the Gospel writers used the wider freedoms of ancient biography. This page takes no side among these. What the church confesses together is that the Gospels are Scripture, a true witness to the Word made flesh, crucified and raised, and that God gave them as they are, four portraits rather than one transcript.
