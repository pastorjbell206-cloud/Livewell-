# The six-lens pass on your first five essays

James, you asked for your six editing prompts to be run over your writing. This is the first run: the first five essays on the shelf, in this order: why-does-god-feel-far-away, doubt-after-church-abuse, excavation-not-demolition, what-post-christian-means, the-rise-of-the-nones. The full record, sentence pairs and all, is in docs/work/elite/voice-ledger.md.

## What changed

Not much, and on purpose. These five had already been through two voice readers held to your own standard (docs/VOICE-JAMES-BELL.md), and most paragraphs already sound like a man talking. The pass did four things again and again: it used contractions where the sentence was already spoken ("didn't", "it's", "I'll"); it turned a colon that sets up a reveal into a plain sentence; it cut "simply" where it was filler; and it folded the one-line paragraphs at the end of three essays into fuller ones. Two sentences were rebuilt because they read as built rather than said. For example, "A sixteenth-century friar refused to spiritualize everything." is now "That's a sixteenth-century friar refusing to make everything spiritual."

Length moved by less than half a percent in each essay (19,047 words to 19,009 in all). The tells your third prompt names, counted by a script, fell from 135 to 112 across the five.

## What did not change

A script compared every essay before and after: every quotation, Scripture reference, year, number, name, heading, link and book title. All five passed with nothing changed. Every help line and safety sentence is word for word, including all of them in the essay on abuse. Nothing about the abuse was softened, and nothing was made harder on the reader. Where an essay takes no side (forgiveness and repentance, the reply to Schellenberg), it still takes none. Your sentence about your father and your five sons is untouched.

## What only you can give

The prompts ask for writing that sounds like someone who went through it. In three places a true detail from you would do more than any edit: what the pull to protect the church's name has looked like for you ("I lead one, and I feel that pull too"); which of your own sermons wouldn't survive your sons' questions ("Which of mine would?"); and whether to say more after the sentence about growing up without a father. And an earlier reader cut a line about your coming to faith out of atheism from the essay on God's silence. That fact is confirmed now. Whether to put it back is your call.

## What was run

Every content gate in the CI workflow (36 commands) passed, as did the essays index check, pnpm check and pnpm test (146 test files passed, 7 skipped). There was no build, because no code changed.

## Second run: Bible background guides

After the first five essays an audit counted where the tells really are, and the worst body of writing on the site was the 41 Bible background guides (the "Reading Scripture in Context" library). Their habit was the two-step you'll recognize, "It was not X. It was Y.", used over and over, often followed by a stack of very short sentences. This run took the fifteen guides with the most tells per thousand words and ran your six prompts over them as a teacher talking to an ordinary adult. Because these are reference pages, nothing became first person and no opinion was added; the few first-person lines some guides already had were left as they were.

What changed: in those fifteen the two-step fell from 82 uses to 25 (each guide keeps one at its real hinge, such as "They aren't chapters. They're mail."), and the runs of three or more very short sentences fell from 34 to 2. The fragments were joined into ordinary sentences that say what the thing was and why the common reading misses it, contractions came in where a teacher would use them, and "honest", "simply" and the colon reveals mostly went. Length grew by 1.3 percent in all, under 3 percent in every guide. For example, "Shame was not a feeling. It was a location, the bottom of the ladder, visible to everyone." now reads "Shame, in that world, was where you stood, at the bottom of the ladder in full view, before it was anything you felt."

What did not change: a script checked every guide before and after, and every quotation, Scripture reference, year, number, name, source, title, heading, kicker and group is exactly as it was in all fifteen. Every caveat about what scholars dispute and every fair statement of a view stayed. In the five political guides among the fifteen, the paired paragraphs addressed to the right and to the left were left untouched or given the same small change, so no side gained or lost anything.

Waiting on you: several headings and subtitles are themselves the two-step ("Not Hygiene. Not Sin. Order.", "Not One Hope. Many."), and they were left because titles were held fixed. And seven guides quote the Berean Standard Bible, which capitalizes He and Him for God, while their own prose uses he and him; one guide (on Proverbs 3:5-6) capitalizes in its prose too. Which you want is your decision. The full record is in docs/work/elite/voice-ledger.md under the same heading.

## Fifth run: the four world portraits

The four long portraits (Jewish life in Jesus's day, the Old Testament's world, the Roman world, and honor and shame) were written recently to a plain standard, with every claim checked against a named source. So this run measured first. Three of the four already had fewer tells than the guides have now: 7.2, 8.5 and 8.7 per thousand words against the guides' 11.1. Honor and shame had 14.4, most of them in its last part, on the cross, and in its notes, where "did not X. They Y." came five times in one part.

What changed: 90 small edits across all four, 15 to 30 a portrait, and length moved by less than half a percent in each. The portraits had no contractions at all, so most edits put them in where a teacher talking would use them. The rest joined short pairs that weren't a part's real hinge, and turned a few reveals in the notes the plain way round. For example, "The New Testament writers did not soften the shame. They used it." now reads "Rather than soften the shame, the New Testament writers used it.", and "The revolt failed. The question it asked did not go away." now reads "The revolt failed, but the question it asked didn't go away." Tells across the four went from 9.7 to 8.9 per thousand words, and in honor and shame from 14.4 to 11.7.

What did not change: a script checked each portrait before and after, and every quotation, Latin and Greek phrase, Bible passage (word for word), reference, year, number, name, place, source, title and heading is exactly as it was. It also counted the words that mark uncertainty ("probably", "about", "is argued", "later" and the rest), and not one was lost. Every place where scholars disagree keeps every side at its full weight. The vivid lines stay word for word, from "A beard and a robe started a war." to "They called it a superstition. The believers called it the gospel."

Was it worth it? For honor and shame, yes, a little: its last part reads less like a drumbeat now. For the other three, hardly; they already sounded like a teacher talking, and the contractions are most of what changed. The full record is in docs/work/elite/voice-ledger.md under the same heading.
