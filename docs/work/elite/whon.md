## Ten things in it most readers will not know

1. The Hebrew word for glory and honor, kavod, comes from a root meaning
   heavy: a person of honor carried weight (Hebrew lexicons; Psalm 4:2).
2. Josephus opens his autobiography with his pedigree, priestly and
   Hasmonean, and says he copied it from the public records (Life 1 to 6).
3. Where the Berean Standard Bible has "envy" in Mark 7:22, the Greek says "an
   evil eye," and the landowner of Matthew 20:15 literally asks, "Is your eye
   evil because I am good?" (Thayer's lexicon; Proverbs 23:6 has the Hebrew
   idiom).
4. Paul's "Who has bewitched you?" (Galatians 3:1) uses baskaino, the ordinary
   verb for casting the evil eye, its only use in the New Testament.
5. A second-century floor mosaic from Antioch shows an eye attacked by a raven,
   a dog, a scorpion and a snake and pierced by a trident, with the words kai
   su, "you too" (House of the Evil Eye; Hatay Archaeology Museum).
6. Seneca ranked the ungrateful man below murderers, tyrants, thieves and
   traitors, because nearly every great crime grows from ingratitude (On
   Benefits 1.10).
7. Seneca also wrote that whoever imitates the gods must give even to the
   ungrateful, for the sun rises on the wicked too (On Benefits 4.26), the same
   picture Jesus uses in Matthew 5:45 and Luke 6:35. Where they part: much of
   Seneca's book is advice on giving wisely, to worthy receivers.
8. John Barclay finds every way of thinking a gift "perfect" in ancient
   writing except one: a gift that expects nothing back (Paul and the Gift,
   2015).
9. The satirist Lucian mocked Christians because their lawgiver persuaded them
   they were all brothers and they worshiped "that crucified sophist"
   (Peregrinus 13, Harmon's translation); Celsus complained that Christian
   cobblers and laundry workers turned children against their fathers (Origen,
   Against Celsus 3.55).
10. A stone found near Corinth carries five decrees from Lycian cities
    honoring a woman, Junia Theodora, for helping Lycians who came to Corinth,
    about the middle of the first century (R. A. Kearsley, Tyndale Bulletin,
    1999). Also: the Didache's rule that a traveling apostle who stays three
    days is a false prophet (Didache 11), and Ruth's request to Boaz using the
    same Hebrew word, kanaph, as his blessing about God's wings (Ruth 2:12;
    3:9).

## Where the page says scholars disagree

- The opening: the frame comes from modern anthropology (Peristiany 1965,
  Malina 1981, the Context Group, deSilva 2000), and its critics are named:
  Herzfeld (1980), Downing (1999), Lawrence (2003), Crook (2009). No culture is
  only shame-based or only guilt-based (Benedict 1946; Dodds 1951 allowed for
  mixtures). Bailey's village customs are marked as modern parallels.
- Part 1: the model "helps, and it has been pressed too far" (Downing; Crook
  on challenges across rank and gender).
- Part 2: Bourdieu's Kabyle study is not ancient evidence; limited good is "a
  lens, not a law"; how literally Paul meant "bewitched" is argued.
- Part 4: Morgan's work does not shrink faith to loyalty alone.
- Part 5: interpreters differ on whether Simon meant a slight; the Didache's
  date.
- Part 6: feminist anthropologists' critique of the gendered model;
  prescriptions written by men set against evidence of women's public honor;
  the evidence is uneven, and nothing describes any living people.
- Part 7: Bailey's reading of the running father is a modern parallel; the
  ancient support offered is Aristotle's slow-stepping great-souled man.
