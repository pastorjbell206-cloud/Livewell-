## 1. BibleProject (bibleproject.com)

### Seen (search results only, no page fetched)

Addresses that appeared in results, with what the snippets showed:

- https://bibleproject.com/ (title: "Study the Story of the Bible With Free Tools"; summary says the home page is organized around Videos, Guides and Classes, with Collections by topic, and separate entries for "How to Read the Bible" and book overview videos)
- https://bibleproject.com/guides/book-of-jonah/ , /guides/book-of-acts/ , /guides/book-of-john/ , /guides/book-of-romans/ , /guides/book-of-genesis/ , /guides/book-of-revelation/ (book guides; Acts and John are titled "Guide to the Book of ...: Key Information and Helpful Resources")
- https://bibleproject.com/videos/collections/old-testament/ and /new-testament/ (overview video collections; each book has a short synopsis and a running time)
- https://bibleproject.com/videos/heaven-and-earth/ (a theme video page)
- https://bibleproject.com/videos/collections/themes/ and /guides/categories/themes/ (theme indexes)
- https://bibleproject.com/classroom/ , /classroom/jonah/ , /classroom/heaven-and-earth/modules/1 (free classes, in numbered modules)
- https://documents.bibleproject.com/classroom/teacher-notes/jonah.pdf (class notes, 163 pages, including a figure titled "The Macro-Design of Jonah")
- https://bibleproject.com/other-resources/posters/ and /downloads/ (free downloads, posters)
- App Store listings for the BibleProject app (third party pages about the app)

What the snippets showed:

- **One guide page per book.** A book guide has an "About" section, a chapter-by-chapter walk through the book under headings, a "More Resources" block (overview poster, study notes or script, video download), a "Big Idea" summary, and "Related Resources" linking to other videos, podcasts and articles. The Jonah guide walks chapters 1 to 4 and ends on God's last question to Jonah.
- **A theme page teaches and then asks.** The Heaven & Earth video page has a "Reflect" section of group questions that send the reader to named passages (Genesis 3, Psalm 65:1-4, Leviticus 4:34-35, Revelation 21), and a Downloads block with the transcript and study notes.
- **Themes are traced across the whole Bible.** There is an index of themes; the app's "Torah Journey" reading plan follows Genesis to Deuteronomy through themes such as Tree of Life, Holy Spirit and Exile.
- **Three depths of the same subject.** A short overview video, a guide page, then a free class in numbered modules with notes of 140 to 160 pages.
- **A one-page picture of each book.** Posters are free to download in full resolution; a reviewer of the printed collection says each one shows the author, characters, location, historical background and major themes of the book laid out graphically. (Reviewer's words, not seen.)
- **Free and crowdfunded.** App Store text calls it a nonprofit, crowdfunded organization producing 100 percent free videos, podcasts, classes and resources. One third-party directory lists the app as ad-free. One pricing aggregator claims paid tiers; it contradicts the App Store text and looks unreliable. There is a store selling printed books (poster collection, about $23 to $25).

Not confirmed by any result: word studies and "visual commentaries" as sections (only BibleProject's own one-line description of its library names them); how the pages look; whether donation asks interrupt reading.

### Best at

Showing the shape of a whole book or a whole theme in one sitting. Every book has an overview, a guide page that walks it section by section, and a one-page picture of its structure, and every theme is followed from Genesis to Revelation; then the same subject is offered again at greater depth for whoever wants it.

### Borrow

1. **A book page built in a fixed order.** LiveWell has an introduction to all 66 books. Give every one the same skeleton so a reader learns it once: the book in one sentence; the book in one drawing; a walk through its sections with a link into the first chapter of each; the hard question people ask about it; where to go next. The chapter notes already hold the material for the section walk.
2. **One drawn structure per book ("the 66 books at a glance").** The brief already lists this. Draw each book as a simple band divided into its sections, by chapter range, in LiveWell's own cream, ink and mustard line style. Each segment is tappable and opens that chapter in the Study Bible. This is LiveWell's own diagram in its own look, not a copy of anyone's poster. It also answers the audit's finding that the site has no drawing anywhere.
3. **Theme paths through the Bible.** LiveWell has 62 doctrine studies and notes on every chapter. Build a small set of "follow this through the Bible" paths (temple, exile, covenant, kingdom, sacrifice): eight to twelve stops, each a passage that opens in the reader with its note, with a one-line reason for the stop. This turns the doctrine studies from shelves into roads.
4. **Three depths, named plainly.** For a book or a doctrine: a two-minute version, the full study, and the long course. LiveWell already has the pieces (book introduction, chapter notes, study guide, eight-week plan); what is missing is the ladder between them on the page. Add "Go deeper" and "Start simpler" links so each piece knows its neighbors.
5. **Reflect questions that point at verses.** End each book page, history lesson and doctrine study with three or four questions, each naming a passage that opens as a live reference. LiveWell already has the live verse reference component; the questions exist in the study guides and chapter notes.
6. **A free printable for every book.** LiveWell already renders PDFs. A one-page sheet per book (the drawing, the outline, the hard question, five questions for a group) gives pastors something to hand out.

### Refuse

- **Video as the front door.** BibleProject's method is animation. LiveWell's is one man's prose; it should not become a video site or commission cartoons to imitate one.
- **A store and a giving ask.** The brief bars a giving page; do not add a shop of printed posters or a donation prompt.
- **An app with accounts.** Keep everything in the browser, on the device, with no sign-up.
- **Their distinctive visual language.** Borrow the idea of a structure diagram, never the illustration style or the layout of the posters.
- **Sprawl across video, podcast, class, app and blog.** Five formats for the same subject is a navigation problem LiveWell already has in a smaller form.

## 2. The Bible Recap (thebiblerecap.com)

### Seen (search results only, no page fetched)

Addresses that appeared in results:

- https://www.thebiblerecap.com/start ("Start The Bible Recap")
- https://www.thebiblerecap.com/prep ("How to Prepare for The Bible Recap")
- https://www.thebiblerecap.com/helptopic/readingplan , /helptopic/startreading , /helptopic/howwhentostart (help pages, each answering one question)
- https://www.thebiblerecap.com/shownotes (links to outside resources named in each day's recap)
- https://thebiblerecap.com/radio ("The God Shot" radio show)
- https://www.thebiblerecap.com/helpministries
- Podcast mirror pages (podbean, Apple Podcasts, transcript sites) for the prep episodes and Day 001

What the snippets showed:

- **One promise with numbers on it.** The Start page says each day's reading takes about 12 minutes and each recap about 8.
- **One start page that walks a newcomer through setup.** Choose a format (book, podcast, video), get the plan (in a Bible app, or printed), and begin. It says what you need (an ordinary Bible; the plan uses the ESV) and what not to buy (a printed chronological Bible, because it will not match the plan).
- **Printable plans in several sizes.** Whole Bible in 365 days, New Testament in three months, and a kids' version of each.
- **A short "before you begin" series.** Six prep episodes with titles such as why read the whole Bible, how to prepare, common mistakes to avoid, and reading in community.
- **Late starters are told what to do.** Episodes begin January 1; a help page explains how to start on another date.
- **Help pages are single questions.** "When to start reading," "How to start," "Overview of the reading plan."
- **A daily closing habit.** The recap ends by naming where God's character showed in the day's reading (the "God Shot"); this came from a third-party summary and the radio page title, so treat the detail as lightly supported.
- **Show notes point outward.** Day 001's notes link to another ministry's overview video for Genesis, with a stated disclaimer that a link is not an endorsement of the whole source.
- **Read here, study there.** A sister ministry of weekly groups is presented as the place for study; the daily plan is for reading.
- **What is sold.** A store with a daily study guide, a journal and a weekly discussion guide (described on the Start page as for groups, churches or families). No result confirmed a membership or patron program.

Not confirmed: how the pages look; how heavy the store and sign-up prompts are on the page.

### Best at

Getting an ordinary person to actually read the whole Bible and not quit. It makes one small daily promise with the minutes stated, gives one start page that removes every excuse, and follows each reading with a short plain explanation of what was just read.

### Borrow

1. **State the minutes.** LiveWell's family devotion already says "about ten minutes" and it is true. Do the same on the reading plan, every chapter page and every history lesson: reading time for the text, reading time for the notes. A number is a promise a tired person can accept.
2. **"What you just read," after the chapter.** LiveWell has notes on all 1,189 chapters. Lift three things to a short visible card at the end of the text, above the drawers: what happened, the one thing people get stuck on (the chapter's hard question already exists for 1,033 chapters), and what this chapter shows about God. That last line is a fair pattern for any Christian teacher; write it in James's words and take it from the existing notes, never from their recaps.
3. **One Start page.** The audit found no "new here" road. Build "New to the Bible? Start here" as a single page of steps, each one tap: what you need, where to begin (one Gospel), how long a day takes, what to do if you miss a day, who to read with.
4. **A short "before you begin" set.** Four or five short pieces at the head of the one-year plan: why read all of it, how to read a chapter, mistakes to avoid, reading with someone. LiveWell's how-to guides likely hold most of this already.
5. **A plan that forgives.** The brief lists "a reading plan that knows today." Add the Bible Recap lesson: when the reader returns after a gap, offer "pick up where you left off" and "jump to today" with equal weight, and never show a count of days missed.
6. **The same plan for the table.** A kids' track beside the adult plan (shorter reading, one question), printable on one sheet, joined to the family devotions LiveWell already has. Print it from the existing PDF builder.

### Refuse

- **Selling the companion.** The study guide, journal and discussion guide are for sale there. On LiveWell the questions, the printable and the group guide are free on the page.
- **Sending the reader to an app or another platform to get the plan.** Keep the plan, the text and the notes in one place.
- **Outbound links as the explanation.** Their notes lean on another ministry's videos. LiveWell has its own introduction to every book; link inward.
- **A calendar that shames.** No January-only framing, no streak counters, no "you are 47 days behind."
- **Personality as the product.** The site should be recommended for what it teaches. This also fits the rule that nothing is stated about James beyond what he has confirmed.

## 3. Bible Odyssey (bibleodyssey.org, Society of Biblical Literature)

### Seen (search results only, no page fetched)

Addresses that appeared in results:

- https://www.bibleodyssey.org/ (home)
- https://www.bibleodyssey.org/places/main-articles/ (index of place articles)
- https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/ (dictionary entries)
- https://www.bibleodyssey.org/about-us and /terms-of-use
- https://bibleodyssey.scholasticahq.com/for-authors (the guidelines given to its writers)
- Reviews and library guides: College & Research Libraries News (crln.acrl.org), a Brandeis news piece from 2015, Larry Hurtado's 2014 blog note, a Michigan State library guide

What the snippets showed:

- **What it is.** A free site from the Society of Biblical Literature, begun with a National Endowment for the Humanities grant, written by named scholars, peer reviewed by a panel, for general readers. It says it does not argue for one reading; it sets out to show the range of interpretations.
- **Three ways in.** People, Places and Passages, plus a search box and an A to Z list. Current menus (from a search summary) include Browse (Most Recent, Collections, All), Reference Tools (Search the Bible, Dictionary, Maps, Timelines), and tools listed as Ask a Scholar, Bible Basics, Glossary, Video Gallery and Timeline Gallery. Topic categories include Beliefs & Practices, Biblical Figures and Places.
- **Everything links to everything.** A library review says nearly all content is cross-linked to related articles, dictionary entries and bibliographies, and that Bible passages open in popup boxes so the reader does not leave the article.
- **Short pieces with a fixed ending.** The author guidelines set essays at 700 or 1,000 words and allow a closing "Did you know?" of two or three one-sentence points, 100 to 150 words in all, giving key points, settled facts, or the open debates.
- **A dictionary under the articles.** Entries drawn largely from a published Bible dictionary.
- **Ask a Scholar.** A reviewer singles it out as what makes the site more than an encyclopedia. No result showed how it works.
- **Scale.** An older library page gives over 300 articles and 60 or more videos, with timelines, maps and three translations; three to four new articles a month. These counts are dated.

Not confirmed: how the timelines and maps behave; what an article page looks like; the current state of the site after any redesign.

### Best at

Making what scholars know about the Bible's world checkable by an ordinary reader. Each short article is signed, reviewed, linked to a dictionary entry and a bibliography, and reached by the three questions people actually ask: who was this, where was this, what does this passage mean.

### Borrow

1. **People and Places as doors, beside Passages.** LiveWell's context is filed by chapter and by essay. Add two plain indexes built from what already exists: People (Abraham, Pilate, Augustine, Perpetua) and Places (Corinth, Nicaea, Babylon). Each entry is a short page: who or where, when, the chapters and history lessons that mention it, and a link into each. The audit says church history already has an entry for each person and council that the essays do not link to; this is the same fix extended to the Bible.
2. **Link every name, everywhere.** In chapter notes, history lessons and doctrine studies, every named person, place, council and term opens a small panel with a two-sentence definition and "read more." LiveWell has the verse panel and a glossary; this is the same panel for names. It is the audit's fix for church history, made site-wide.
3. **A sourced "Did you know?" that is really "What is settled, what is debated."** At the foot of each book introduction and history lesson, three short lines: what nearly everyone agrees on, what is disputed, and where to check. This is the Scholarship Standard made visible, and it suits the skeptic and the serious reader. Write only what a named source supports.
4. **Sources shown, by name.** Signed, reviewed and with a bibliography is why a librarian recommends that site. LiveWell has 22 checked primary texts for church history that readers cannot yet see. Show them under each lesson: the quotation, the work, the section, a link. A pastor deciding whether to hand the site to his people looks for exactly this.
5. **Ask a hard question, answered from the shelf.** The brief lists "ask a hard question" on the home page. Build it LiveWell's way: the box searches the 36 honest answers, the chapter hard questions and the doctrine studies and returns the nearest written answer. Where nothing fits, it says so and offers to send the question to James through the contact form. No machine-written answers, no promise of a reply he has not agreed to give.
6. **A timeline as a reference tool, reached from the Study menu.** Their timelines sit under Reference Tools beside the dictionary and maps. LiveWell's planned church history timeline and "who was alive when" belong in the same place: one tool, linked from every lesson and from every book introduction that has a date.

### Refuse

- **The view from nowhere.** Bible Odyssey deliberately takes no position. LiveWell confesses the creeds and says where James lands. Borrow the fairness (state the other view in its strongest form, which the honest answers already do) and refuse the neutrality.
- **Many voices.** Hundreds of contributors make an encyclopedia. LiveWell's strength is one voice from Genesis to the present.
- **Academic titles and insider words.** The audit already faults LiveWell for "The Constantinian Turn." Keep the scholarship under plain titles.
- **Borrowed reference text.** Their dictionary is licensed from a publisher. LiveWell writes its own glossary entries or leaves them out.
- **A gallery of tools.** Video gallery, timeline gallery, glossary, dictionary and basics as separate menu items is the clutter the brief warns against. One tool per job.

## Across the three: what this group teaches LiveWell

1. **Give every book a shape a reader can see.** One drawing, one fixed page order, sections that open the text. LiveWell has the words for all 66 books and no pictures at all.
2. **Say how long it takes, and explain what was just read.** The chapter notes are the best thing on the site and they sit in closed drawers. A short visible summary after the text does the most good for the least work.
3. **Make context a web, not a shelf.** People, places, terms and sources should each be one tap from wherever they are named.
4. **Offer a road with a first step.** A start page, a short "before you begin," a plan that knows today and forgives a gap.
5. **Stay free, quiet and in one voice.** Each of these sites pays for its reach with something LiveWell has ruled out: a store, an app, a donation model, or no point of view. LiveWell can hold the visual clarity of the first, the daily habit of the second and the checkable sources of the third on one free site, with nothing to buy and one man answering.
