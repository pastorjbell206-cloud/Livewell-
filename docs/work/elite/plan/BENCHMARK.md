# The benchmark: LiveWell beside the best

Written 2026-11-18 for James Bell and for the people building the site. One of four planning documents. This one answers three questions. Who is the best at each thing you asked for? Where does LiveWell stand beside them today? What would put LiveWell level with them, or ahead?

## Read this first: how far to trust this page

**Nobody on the research team could open any of the sites.** The work session was not allowed to reach them. Every fact about another site below comes from search results: page titles, page addresses, the sites' own help pages as a search engine summarised them, and a few outside reviews. That is good evidence for what a site offers and how it is organised. It is no evidence at all for how a site looks.

So this page does two things and refuses a third.

- It reports what each site **does**, as the research files recorded it.
- It reports where LiveWell **stands**, from the stock-takes of the code on the work branch (2026-11-18), the November audit, and the notes from the first wave of work.
- It does **not** judge any other site's design, type, colour or feel. No one saw a page. The design section says so again, and names the second pass that is needed.

Counts quoted from other sites (how many answers, how many plans) come from search summaries and may be out of date. No price is quoted, because the sources disagreed.

What I read for this page is listed at the end, with what I read in full and what I read in part.

---

## The short version

1. Thirty-three sites were studied in eight groups. Each one is the best at **one piece** of what you asked for. On the evidence, none of them holds all the pieces.
2. LiveWell already holds every piece: a study Bible with notes on all 1,189 chapters, church history, hard questions, the family table, and one man's essays. All free, no account, no advertising.
3. LiveWell's weakness is not what it has. It is that the pieces do not know about each other, and that a visitor cannot **see** any of it in the first minute. You said the same thing on the first preview: "It just looks like a lot of words."
4. The plan that follows from the evidence is short. **Join the libraries. Draw them. Put four things to touch on the first screen.** The last section of the main text says which four.

## 2. Goal by goal

For each of your goals: who sets the bar, where LiveWell stands today, and what would put it level or ahead. "Today" means the work branch after the first wave. The November audit scores are quoted where they exist. That audit has not been run again since the first wave, so the scores are a floor, not a current reading.

### 2.1 Study the Bible

**Who sets the bar.** For reading: ESV.org (the reader controls the page), Bible Gateway (translations side by side) and YouVersion (the daily habit). For study: STEP (tap a word and follow it), Blue Letter Bible (one fixed panel per verse), Bible Hub (an address for every verse) and the NET Bible (notes that explain the translation).

**Where LiveWell stands.** The audit scored this 7: "The notes are a 9 and the tool that holds them is a 5."

What LiveWell has that the research did not find on any of those seven sites:

- Notes on every chapter in one voice, about 2.2 million words.
- A hard question, with the objection stated fairly, on 1,033 chapters.
- Greek and Hebrew under every word, with the grammar in English words.
- All of it free, with no account and no advertising. Two of the four reading sites charge for study material and one shows banner ads on its free pages.

What the first wave built: a word and phrase search of the whole Bible, a tap anywhere on a verse to open its notes, poetry set in lines, the hard question and study questions printed straight after the text, and a reading typeface.

What a student still cannot do here that the bar-setters allow:

- Keep a highlight or a note on a verse.
- Read a second translation beside the first.
- Follow a cross-reference with the verse text shown. The data holds about 273,000 of them.
- Follow one Greek or Hebrew word through the Bible. There is no page for a word and no counts that link.
- Share a verse by its own address.
- Put the notes away and simply read.
- Listen to a chapter.

And two gaps of depth: verse-by-verse notes exist for 202 chapters and 41 books have none, and 719 of 1,189 chapters show no related page from anywhere else on the site.

**What would put it level.**

1. One verse panel with fixed tabs, the same on every verse: Notes, Words, Cross-references, Compare, Go deeper. Previous and Next inside it. A tab with nothing in it is greyed, not hidden.
2. Two named views of a chapter, **Read** and **Study**, under one "Text settings" menu.
3. Highlights and a note per verse, kept on the device, listed in My Library, with a file to save so the promise "lives on this device" is not a trap.
4. A second translation in a column. Which one is your decision, and its licence is checked first.
5. Cross-references with the verse printed under each.
6. Word counts that are links, and a page for each Greek and Hebrew word.
7. An address for a verse, and "Copy verse" with the reference and "Berean Standard Bible" attached.

**What would put it ahead.**

1. **"Go deeper" points at your own writing.** From any verse: the doctrine it teaches, the century that fought over it, the question a skeptic asks about it, the devotion a family could read on it tonight. The research on the study tools said it plainly: none of the three can do this, because none has one author.
2. **"Why it is translated this way"** on the hard verses, in a pastor's voice, where the second translation and the word data meet.
3. **A short "what you just read" card** after every chapter, lifted from notes already written: what happened, the thing people get stuck on, and what this chapter shows about God.
4. **Say the promise once, near the text:** notes on every chapter, free, no account, no ads.

### 2.2 Understand the context

**Who sets the bar.** BibleProject (the shape of a whole book, seen at once). Bible Odyssey (people, places and passages, signed and sourced). The NET Bible's background notes.

**Where LiveWell stands.** The material is large: an introduction to all 66 books, 41 Bible background guides, 25 guides on how to read each kind of writing, the story of the Bible in eleven parts, and a history and culture section in every chapter's notes. Three things are missing.

- **Nothing is drawn.** The audit found no drawing, map or diagram on eighteen pages. BibleProject's whole strength is that a reader can see a book.
- **The background guides do not point at the chapters they explain,** and the chapters reach them only by a loose match.
- **There is no list of people or places.** Every name in the notes is plain text.

**Level.** A book page in one fixed order for all 66: the book in one sentence, the book in one drawing, a walk through its sections with a link into each, the hard question people ask about it, and where to go next. Reading time stated on every long page.

**Ahead.**

1. **The 66 books at a glance**: one drawing of the whole Bible, each book a bar sized by its length and shaded by its kind, each one opening its page and showing what you have read. Then one drawn band per book divided into its sections. Both in LiveWell's own cream, ink and mustard line.
2. **People and Places** as two plain indexes, with every name in every note opening a small panel, the way a verse reference already does.
3. **"What is settled, what is debated, where to check"** at the foot of each book introduction. Written only from sources that can be named.
4. **The ten "verses everyone quotes" guides shown on their own verse.**

### 2.3 Church history

**Who sets the bar.** The Christian History Institute (one primary source per lesson, with questions that weigh it). Ligonier (a course in parts and numbered lessons with a study guide). World History Encyclopedia (a timeline for every subject from one set of dates). The Christian Classics Ethereal Library and New Advent (the sources themselves).

**Where LiveWell stands.** The audit scored it 5 and said "a set of essays, not yet a course." The first wave changed that. Today there is one course of fourteen numbered lessons with Previous and Next. Names link to their entries. Seven essays carry thirty checked source cards, held word for word against 22 stored primary texts by a check that fails the build. A stepper moves year by year and shows who was alive.

Still short of the bar:

- Fifteen of the 22 essays have no source cards. The checked sources cover the first four centuries only.
- No questions for any lesson.
- The timeline is a list, not a line. It is thin after 1650: 14 events for 375 years.
- The course is short, about 1,750 words a lesson, and the last three lessons carry 1730 to today.
- Whole regions are missing: the East after 1054, the churches outside the Roman world, the Puritans, most of the twentieth century, the Black church.
- The 36 people end in 1963 and include two women.
- A second timeline (45 events) and two more lists of people (31 names) sit at other addresses, unjoined.
- No history lesson links to a creed, a Bible passage, a doctrine study or a study guide.

**Level.**

1. Every lesson in the same order: read, the source, the slice of the timeline, questions. Questions include one that asks "how do we know this?" and one that states the skeptic's doubt at full strength.
2. Source cards on every lesson, each with author, work, section, translator and edition, and "Read the whole work" linking out.
3. One file of dated events, with a check that requires a source on each. The full timeline, the slice on each lesson and the line on each person page are all drawn from it, so a date cannot differ between two pages.
4. A printable handout per lesson so a pastor can run it as a class. Ligonier sells its study guides. LiveWell gives them away.

**Ahead.**

1. **Bible and church on one line.** The writing of the New Testament books, the councils, the people and "You are here," drawn to scale and moved with a thumb. A general history site has no reason to do this. No church history site in the research has a study Bible to join it to.
2. **Links both ways between a lesson and Scripture.** "John 1 in church history: Nicaea, Athanasius" on the chapter. Two or three Bible readings on each lesson, opening onto notes and Greek, not a bare verse.
3. **The hard record as a layer.** Where the church was silent or wrong, shown on the same line in the same voice, switched on by the reader. This is what your Orthodoxy Guardrails call the same instrument and the same grief.
4. **"Who was alive when"** as one slider, built on the same file.

### 2.4 Hard questions

**Who sets the bar.** GotQuestions (the question is the page). Reasonable Faith (a real question, a named answer, numbered and kept). Christianity Explored (a short bounded way in). Gospel in Life (one voice, with a named series for skeptics).

**Where LiveWell stands.** The audit called the Honest Answers and Test the Case "among the best things on the site." The first wave gave them a door (`/topics`), a find box, and better search.

The weaknesses are of arrangement:

- **Each answer has no page of its own.** All 35 sit on one page of 11,000 words. GotQuestions' whole method is one question, one address.
- **The questions are split across libraries that do not point at each other:** 35 Honest Answers, 112 "questions people ask" (overlapping on about twenty), nine argued cases, 31 compared debates, and the best set of all, **1,033 hard questions inside the chapter notes, listed nowhere and not searchable as questions.**
- **29 of the 35 answers were built on an essay that is now retired,** so the reader gets three paragraphs and no way further.
- **No answer stores a Scripture reference,** so no Bible chapter can point to it.
- **A message sent through the contact form is saved to a list and not emailed to you.** An "ask" box cannot be honest until that is fixed.
- Questions the site does not answer by title include abortion, sexuality and gender, suicide, how Catholics and Protestants differ, and other religions besides Islam. Which of these to answer is yours to say.

**Level.** A page for each answer. One list of every question the site answers, searched from one box. A short answer of two or three sentences at the top, with the full treatment under it. A fixed foot on each: back to the group, the next question, the passage, the full study.

**Ahead.**

1. **An answer that opens into the rest.** The research on this group said none of the four showed an answers site whose answers open straight into a study Bible on the chapter in question, a full doctrine study, and the church's history of the same argument. GotQuestions has breadth without a signed voice. Reasonable Faith has a signed voice without a Bible reader. Christianity Explored has the path without the library. Gospel in Life has the library without the question-first door.
2. **A shape the others were not shown doing:** the short answer, then the strongest form of the objection, then the argument, then "what this does not settle."
3. **The 1,033 chapter questions brought out** as "Hard questions in the Bible, by book." It is a larger and better-sourced set than most answers sites hold, and it is already written.
4. **"Read Mark with the notes"** (or John; your choice) as the bounded next step: seven sittings, a place kept, a leader's version.
5. **Your sentence to the skeptic on the first screen:** "You can walk away unconvinced at any point and I will not chase you." Today it is hidden inside one tool.

### 2.5 Family

**Who sets the bar.** New City Catechism (one question, two depths, one switch). The Village Church (a frame a parent can hold in his head). Truth78 (the whole plan on one chart). The Good Book Company (five honest minutes at a meal). Risen Motherhood (one card to print).

**Where LiveWell stands.** This is the side nearest the bar. Tonight's devotion is one tap from Home and runs in six full screens. "A devotion for tonight" picks one in three taps. A family keeps its own place in the catechism. Reading plans start on a date. Advent, Holy Week and Thanksgiving follow the calendar.

The gaps:

- **Nothing on the nightly path is written for a child of three to six.** The only such writing is in an older tool that quotes the King James.
- **Children meet four different Bible wordings** across the family pages.
- **Five catechism questions are asked twice.** There is no out-loud practice and no child-or-adult switch.
- **The catechism and the reading plans cannot be printed.**
- **No walk through the Bible's story for children.** The 72 devotions are subjects and virtues.
- **No Lent, Easter week or Christmas-to-Epiphany set.**
- The writing itself scored 5 and 4 out of 10 in the voice audit: sound and kind, "one shape seventy-two times."
- The Family page is fifteen phone screens long.

**Level.** The child-or-adult switch on every catechism question, carried in the address. A week number. A five-minute "short night" offered on the first screen. The family year on one printable chart. The catechism as a booklet and as cards. One Bible wording for children.

**Ahead.**

1. **"For you, before the table."** One line on each devotion pointing the parent to the doctrine study or the answer on the same subject. The research on this group ended with this: LiveWell is the only one of these with a full study Bible, a church history library and hard-question answers beside the family table. "The work is joining, not adding."
2. **From a Bible chapter to the table.** "Tonight at the table" on the 58 chapters a devotion uses, and "The catechism asks" on the 50 chapters a question cites. The links exist in the data and are not shown.
3. **A frame in your words,** such as at the table, along the way, on the big days, with the 24 parent how-to guides finally linked.
4. **A "For your church" page:** the chart, the print pack, and one sentence saying a church may copy it. That sentence is yours to give.

### 2.6 Discipleship

**Who sets the bar.** Practicing the Way (one first step, a short list of practices, a tool that ends with a plan in hand). Renovaré (every practice rooted in church history). The Bible Recap (one start page that removes every excuse). Ligonier ("how to use this" by setting). The Gospel Coalition (free courses with no sign-up).

**Where LiveWell stands.** The audit scored this 4, the lowest line on the card: "shelves and no road." The first wave built `/start`, a six-step road for someone new to faith or coming back. That is the right first move.

What remains:

- Beyond the six steps there is no second road. Sixty-nine long pieces on everyday Christian life and 132 how-to guides sit in no menu.
- The rule-of-life builder exists, and a second rule is stored separately by another tool.
- The memory tool uses 40 fixed verses in unchecked wording and never brings a verse back.
- No adult reading plan can start today. The year plan begins in 2027.
- Only two of 68 study guides study a book of the Bible. A group that wants to work through Mark has the richest notes on the site and no guide built from them.
- The study guides are the weakest writing on the site (4 out of 10 in the voice audit).

**Level.** A short fixed list of practices, in your naming, each with the same parts. A rhythm builder that ends on one printed page for the fridge. Reading plans that start any day and forgive a gap, with no streak and no count of days missed. One self-check that ends in a next step, not a score. "How to use this" for one person, a family and a group.

**Ahead.**

1. **Practice with a text and a history.** Each practice page links its passages into the Study Bible and names two or three Christians from different centuries who did it, each linked to the history course. The research said no other family or formation site in the group has a church history library to link to.
2. **No account anywhere on the road.** Practicing the Way puts its library behind a free sign-up. LiveWell keeps a person's place on the device and says so.
3. **Courses built from the notes:** Mark in seven sittings, Romans, Genesis, each with a leader's view and a handout.
4. **A monthly rhythm made from what exists:** one theme, one essay, one answer, one study guide, that month's devotions, and one Bible book read a day at a time.

### 2.7 Essays and books

For books: Stripe Press (a small shelf treated as objects). For the permanent shelf under a magazine: The Gospel Coalition's short theology essays.

**Where LiveWell stands.** The rewritten essays are the best writing on the site. The voice audit scored them 8 to 9 for sounding human and 7.5 to 8.5 for sounding like you, and said what holds them below 10 is that "you are almost never in them." The essay title block is, in the audit's words, "the most publication-like moment on the site." Essays are now set in a reading typeface.

The trouble is the shelf and the frame around it:

- **Forty of the sixty essay addresses answer with a notice,** not an essay. Fifteen more rewrites are finished and not landed.
- **Thirteen of the fifteen live essays exist only in the database,** so nobody working from the code can check or link them.
- **Twelve controls surround an essay.**
- **Books:** the audit scored 5. Three books are shown clearly. Five books taken off the shelf can still be bought at their old addresses. Paid files can be downloaded by anyone who has the address. No opening of any book can be read.

**Level.** Land the fifteen. Let each notice point to the best living page on its subject. One quiet row of tools above an essay. After the last line: one next essay, the sources, one invitation to write to you. The three books in one dark section with large covers, one page each in the same order: what it is, who it is for, the opening pages, where to get it.

**Ahead.**

1. **An essay that is wired in.** Under each: the passages it reads opening in the Study Bible, the history lesson on its century, the answer and the study guide on its subject. No essay site in the research has those shelves to point to.
2. **"James on this period"** on a history lesson, and "James on this passage" on a chapter, whenever a public essay exists.
3. **You in them.** The voice audit lists where a real memory or detail is wanted. Only you can supply it. That is the one thing that moves the essays from very good to unmistakably yours.

### 2.8 Reaching James

**Who sets the bar.** The evidence here is the thinnest in this document. No research group studied author pages, contact pages or how a writer gathers his other homes in one place. What the research did record:

- Reasonable Faith prints a guide to writing a good question beside its ask box, asks readers to check the archive first, and does not promise to answer everyone.
- Plough ends each article with one line: "Let us know what you think."
- The Marginalian says in one sentence whose work the site is and that it is free and without ads.
- Practicing the Way says who pays for it.
- Gospel in Life keeps one teacher's whole work in one place.

**Where LiveWell stands.** The audit scored 5. The first wave brought back `/connect` as "Find James": Substack, Facebook, the newest essays, the contact form, speaking, the books. Every sign-up now says only what happens. The contact form says "saved for James to read," which is true and is also the problem: nothing is emailed.

Still open: the facts the site may state about you, your portrait (still loaded from an outside address), the church's web address, the secure podcast address, and whether ENDS stays. Your Facebook and Substack posts are linked, not shown.

**Level.** Contact messages reach your inbox. One plain sentence on About and in the footer: one author, free, no ads, nothing behind a sign-up. One "Write to James about this" line at the end of every essay, answer and lesson.

**Ahead.** "Ask a hard question" that first shows the three nearest written answers as the person types, then offers to send the question on, with one honest line about what happens to it. And, only if you choose it, "A reader asks": the real question printed, your answer under it, numbered and kept. It cannot be faked and should not be started until you say how often you would answer.

### 2.9 Design

**Who sets the bar.** This cannot be answered from the research, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Not one page of any site was seen. The design group wrote: "The questions this group was most asked to answer (which typefaces, what measure, what scale, how dark mode is handled, how motion is used) are still open."

What the research can support is five habits of arrangement, each seen in search results:

1. Tell the reader what a piece is and what it costs before they start (Plough, Aeon).
2. Carry a large archive with two keys and one hand-picked shelf (The Marginalian).
3. Keep the shelf small and treat each thing as an object (Stripe Press).
4. Put the thing to touch inside the explanation, give it one job, and build few (Ciechanowski).
5. Hold a daily rhythm beside the long reading (Plough).

**Where LiveWell stands.** Three judgments are on record, none from a side-by-side look at other sites.

- The audit scored design 6: "careful and quiet, and it is not yet among the ten best." It named the gaps: no reading typeface, nothing drawn, no single set of parts, nothing to touch on the first screen.
- After the first wave a design reviewer scored the changed pages 8.2. A reading typeface, one heading scale, one button family and one label were set. About a third of headings still carry their own sizes. Buttons come in six heights. Three page-header patterns do one job. Night reading is built and switched off.
- Your own word on the first preview overrides both: "It just looks like a lot of words. There's no icons, no pictures, no imagery. There's no interactive things you can click on to see visuals."

**What would put it level.** Finish the one set of parts: one page header, one heading scale applied everywhere, one button and one control shape. Cut each index page to one screen of clear choices. Offer night reading. Then the second research pass below, so "level" is judged by eye.

**What would put it ahead.** A visual language that only this site could have, because it is drawn from what the site is about: the shape of the 66 books, twenty centuries on one line, the places, the table. One hand, one line weight, cream and ink with mustard as the mark. Original drawings, or public-domain images with the source recorded. This is also how "draw attention" and "unhurried, weighted, grown-up" stop pulling against each other: the attention comes from things worth looking at.

**The second pass that is needed.** Someone with a browser opens the sites named here at phone width and laptop width, takes screenshots, and reports type, measure, spacing, imagery, motion and night mode. Until then, no one should say "as good as" any named site on design.

## 3. Where LiveWell can be the best in the world

### The claim

There are better Bible tools than LiveWell. There are bigger answer sites, longer-running history courses, more polished family programmes and more beautiful magazines. LiveWell will not out-tool Blue Letter Bible or out-publish The Gospel Coalition, and it should not try.

What the research could not find, in thirty-three sites, is one place that holds **a study Bible, church history, hard questions and the family table, free, in one voice, with no account.** Each group of researchers reached that finding separately, without being asked to:

- **The study tools.** None of the three does what LiveWell can: "a next step that is not another reference tool: from a verse to the doctrine it teaches, the century that fought over it, the question a skeptic asks about it, and the devotion a family could read on it tonight."
- **The publishers.** "None of the three, on this evidence, puts the Bible text, the notes, the original languages, the history and the family table in one place in one voice, free with no account."
- **Church history.** "No site here does the whole job... LiveWell's opening is the join."
- **The answers sites.** "LiveWell holds all four pieces and today does not connect them."
- **Family and discipleship.** "LiveWell is the only one of these with a full study Bible, a church history library and hard-question answers beside the family table... The work is joining, not adding."

### Why the others cannot simply copy it

Each strong site pays for its reach with something you have ruled out. The research recorded advertising on two of them, paid study material on four or more, a required account on several, apps on many, and many voices on nearly all. The two that take no side do so by taking no position at all.

LiveWell is built the other way round. One author. The creeds confessed. The other side of a disputed question stated in its strongest form. Nothing sold beside the study. Nothing kept about the reader. That is not a feature another site can add next quarter. It is what the site is.

### Why it is not yet true

The stock-take of the libraries found the same fact from the inside. The church history, the creeds, the background guides, the Bible guides and the chapter notes contain **no link to any other library.** A Bible chapter can show related pages from only four kinds of thing, and 719 chapters show none. A history lesson links to nothing but other history. A creed links to nothing. No Honest Answer can be reached from the chapter it is about. One subject is often written five or six times in places that do not point at each other.

Only two parts of the site are properly joined today: Find Help, and three topic pages. Those three pages already do what the whole site should do.

And a visitor cannot see any of this. On the first preview you saw words. So will a stranger.

So the claim "the best in the world at this combination" rests on two pieces of work, in this order:

1. **Join it.** Every piece carries the same four tags: its passages, its doctrines, its topic, and (for history) its years and people. One generated index reads them. Every page then ends with the same short rail, "Go further," with one true match of each kind and nothing where nothing fits.
2. **Show it.** Four things on the first screens that a person can touch, each of which is the combination made visible.

### The four signature experiences

Each is built from what LiveWell already holds. Each shows, inside a minute, something the research found on no single other site.

**1. The verse opens the library.**
On Home there is one verse a visitor can tap. A panel opens with the same tabs it will have on every verse: Notes, Words, Cross-references, Compare, Go deeper. "Go deeper" is the moment. From John 1:14 it offers the doctrine study on the incarnation, the lesson on Nicaea, the skeptic's question, and a devotion for tonight. Every one of them is by the same author, free. In one tap the visitor has seen the whole site.
*Why it wins:* the Bible tools have the panel and fill it with other people's material. The publishers have the material and no panel.

**2. The Line: twenty centuries, and you are here.**
One drawing, to scale, moved with a thumb. The New Testament being written, the councils, the people, the reader's own century marked at the end. Stop on a year and see who was alive, what was decided, the source in the writer's own words, the lesson, and the Bible chapters to read beside it. The hard record is a layer the reader can switch on.
*Why it wins:* the audit said of this piece, "the one people will remember." The history sites have sources or a timeline or a teacher. None was found with all three and a study Bible joined to them.

**3. One box for a hard question.**
On Home: "Look up a passage or ask a question." Type a question in your own words. One best answer comes back, short, with your name on it, then three ways on: argue it with me, see where Christians differ, read the passage with the notes. Beside the box, your sentence: "You can walk away unconvinced at any point and I will not chase you." Where nothing fits, it says so and offers to send the question to you.
*Why it wins:* it draws on one pooled list that already exists in pieces, including the 1,033 chapter questions no one can find today. No machine-written answers. No promise you have not agreed to keep.

**4. Tonight at the table, in three taps.**
Who is at the table, five minutes or ten, go. A devotion opens in large type with the Bible text, a question for a small child and one for an older one, and a line for the parent: "For you, before the table." Below it, this week's catechism question with the child's answer one switch away.
*Why it wins:* the family sites give the parent a devotion. Only here does the parent's line lead to a doctrine study, an answer and a study Bible by the same pastor.

**And one drawing that holds the first screen together: the 66 books at a glance.** Each book a bar sized by its length, shaded by its kind, each opening its own page. It is the answer to "no pictures, no imagery" that is also a tool. It is the door into the first experience.

**One sentence beside all of it, said once:** *One author. Free. No account. No ads. Nothing sold beside the study.* The research found that sentence, in whole, on no site it studied. A visitor will notice it in the first minute if it is there to notice.

### What this asks LiveWell to refuse

The list is the same in every research file, which is itself evidence. No advertising. No paid tier. No account. No app. No streaks or badges. No video as the front door. No many-voices library. No store beside the study. No machine-written answers. No interactive piece that teaches nothing. Few signature pieces, each finished, before a fifth is begun.
