- Third party: https://learnofchrist.com/resources/got-questions , https://mediabiasfactcheck.com/got-questions-gotquestions-org/ , https://www.smartcustomer.com/reviews/gotquestions.org , https://ministrywatch.com/?p=11903

### What the results showed
- The title of every article is the question, worded the way people type it into a search engine. The ministry says this is deliberate and that it studies search queries to choose the wording. [search, own blog]
- Most visitors arrive from search engines, not the home page. The ministry's own history page says so; visitor figures in the results vary (about 13 million a month in one outside report, about 18 million in the site's own). A 2025 page on the site says Google traffic fell once AI Overviews appeared above the results. [search]
- There is a content index that sorts articles into categories and sub-categories (the spiritual-life category showed sub-headings such as Meaning, Faith, Joy, Trust, Freedom). [search]
- Category pages end with a "Return to" block that links back up to the index, and an invitation to get the Question of the Week by email. [search]
- A 2020 post from the team says articles were regrouped into sections, breadcrumb links were added at the top of each article, and each answer ends with a short summarizing statement so the reader leaves with a direct answer. [search, own blog]
- Answers are short; one outside review puts them at about 700 words. [third party]
- A user review says a search returns several possible questions to choose between. [third party]
- A "Top 20" list of most-asked questions and a way to submit your own question are mentioned by outside blogs, some of them old (2012). [third party, may be out of date]
- The ministry's blog answers complaints about the "recommended resources" at the foot of articles, which tells us there is a recommended-book block under each answer. [search]
- Tone and reliability are disputed in outside reviews: one rating site scores it low and partisan; user reviews are mixed; another Christian blog calls it "mostly good" and to be read with discernment. [third party]
- **[not seen]** Audio on answer pages, a "random question" feature, the languages list, the app, ads, the look of the search box, the home page layout.

### Best at
Meeting a person at the exact sentence they typed. Every page is one question in everyday wording with one short answer, so a stranger arriving from a search engine is on the right page before he knows what site he is on.

### Borrow
1. **The title is the question, in the asker's words.** LiveWell's 36 Honest Answers already do this (the audit passes 33 of 35 titles). Extend the rule to the 1,033 "hard question" blocks in the Bible chapter notes: give each its own plain-question heading and its own address or anchor, and put them in search, so "why did God tell Abraham to kill Isaac" lands on Genesis 22's hard question.
2. **A direct answer first, in two or three sentences, then the full treatment.** Put a short "The short answer" paragraph at the top of each Honest Answer, before the steelman and the argument. It serves the phone reader and is what a search engine or AI assistant will quote. The long form stays underneath; nothing is cut.
3. **Search that offers questions, not pages.** When someone types into the home field ("Look up a passage or ask a question"), return up to five matching questions drawn from one pooled list: Honest Answers, chapter hard questions, doctrine studies, Find Help pages. The audit says search cannot find the site's own main things; a single question index fixes the hard-question half of that.
4. **One browsable index of every question, grouped by plain category.** LiveWell has four hard-question index pages (five after the pending work). Fold them into one, at /topics, with a breadcrumb on every answer so a reader always knows the way back up.
5. **A way up and a way on at the foot of every answer.** A fixed three-line foot: back to the category, the next question in that category, and one deeper thing (the doctrine study or the Bible chapter the answer leans on).
6. **Plan for answer engines.** GotQuestions says its search traffic fell when AI summaries arrived. LiveWell already ships `llms.txt` and JSON-LD; mark the Honest Answers and chapter hard questions up as question-and-answer data and make the short answer quotable with the reference attached.

### Refuse
- Unsigned answers in a house voice. LiveWell's promise is one man's voice with his name on it.
- Answers that close a second-order question in 700 words. CLAUDE.md requires the strongest form of each side and a named open question; keep that even when a short answer sits on top.
- Wording titles for the search engine at the cost of the reader. Plain, yes; bait, no.
- A book-selling block under every answer. Further reading only where a real, checked source helps.
- Volume for its own sake. Thirty-six answers that hold weight beat thousands that do not.
- A weekly email as the main next step. On LiveWell the next step is the next thing to read, free, on the page.

### Seen

### What the results showed
- One long-running series, "Question of the Week": a real reader's question answered by William Lane Craig, each one numbered. The archive showed 987 results in one listing; search results mention #978 in March 2026 and a marked one-thousandth question. [search]
- The archive is paged, searchable, and sortable by title, and each archive page carries a "submit a question" link. [search]
- There is a page telling readers how to write a good question: broad interest is preferred, and readers are asked to check the archive first. Craig says he received over 50 questions that week. [search]
- Popular articles are grouped under a handful of subjects (Jesus of Nazareth, Science and Theology, Christianity and Other Faiths, Practical Issues, Existence and Nature of God, Apologetics), each with its own "view writings" link. [search]
- A set of short animated videos, one per main argument (the kalam, moral and fine-tuning arguments are named), scripted from Craig's books. [search and third party]
- A long teaching class called Defenders, a podcast, and an archive of lectures and debates. [third party; the podcast also re-issues Questions of the Week]
- The site's own search returned 468 results for "kalam", which suggests search gives a long flat list. [search, count only]
- **[not seen]** The home page, the layout of a single question-and-answer page, the popular/scholarly split, the submission form, any next-step block, donation prompts, and the tone on the page. I cannot report tone toward skeptics from sight; the only evidence is that readers' questions, including critical ones, are printed and answered at length.

### Best at
Taking one real reader's question a week, printing it, and answering it at full length under the author's own name, for about twenty years, with every one numbered and kept. The archive itself is the proof that hard questions are welcome.

### Borrow
1. **Print the reader's real question, then answer it.** Add a small, honest "A reader asks" series to Honest Answers: the question as it was sent (first name or none, with permission), James's answer beneath. This cannot be faked: it starts only when real questions arrive and James writes or approves each answer. List it for James as a decision.
2. **"Ask a hard question" with a short guide beside the box.** The brief already lists this for the home page. Borrow the two rules the results showed: check whether it has been answered (show three likely matches as the person types), and say plainly that not every question gets a public answer. Wire it to the contact fix in the audit so the question actually reaches James.
3. **Number the answers.** A plain running number on each Honest Answer and each church history lesson gives a sense of a kept body of work and makes "next" and "previous" natural. The audit already asks for numbers on the history course.
4. **One argument, one short visual summary.** LiveWell's "Test the Case" walks an argument step by step. Give each case a one-screen drawn outline (premises, the objection, what the step does not settle) at the top. This also answers the audit's finding that the site has no drawing or diagram.
5. **Six to eight plain subject groups for the answers.** Group the Honest Answers and doctrine studies under subjects a person would say aloud (God, Jesus, the Bible, suffering, science, other faiths, the church's failures, everyday life), with a count on each.
6. **Two depths, marked.** Where an Honest Answer has a matching doctrine study, label the pair "the short answer" and "the full study" so the serious reader sees the door to the 62 studies and the phone reader is not pushed through it.

### Refuse
- The debate frame. LiveWell's own line is "You can walk away unconvinced at any point and I will not chase you." Arguments are offered, not won.
- A flat list of hundreds of results for one search term. Cap and group results.
- Technical philosophy as the default register. Keep the scholarship; keep it readable by the skeptic and the parent in the brief.
- A wall of media types (podcast, video, class, writings) as the way in. One door per need.
- Any impression that the site exists to raise support. No giving page is a rule in the brief.
- A promise to answer every question sent in. One man cannot, and the site does not state what it cannot keep.

## Across the four: how a person with one hard question finds one good answer

What the evidence supports (from search results, not from sight):

1. **The question is the page.** GotQuestions titles every page as the question in the asker's words, and most of its readers arrive straight from a search engine. The front door for a hard question is the answer page itself, not the home page.
2. **The answer comes first and short, with depth behind it.** GotQuestions closes with a summary; Reasonable Faith goes long; Christianity Explored goes short on video. LiveWell can do what none of the four is shown doing: a two-sentence answer, then the strongest form of the objection, then the argument, then "what this does not settle".
3. **Search should return questions.** One pooled list of every question the site answers (36 Honest Answers plus about 1,033 chapter hard questions plus doctrine studies) is a larger and better-sourced set than LiveWell's search can find today.
4. **A real person asks; a named person answers.** Reasonable Faith's numbered series is the pattern. LiveWell has the named person. It lacks the working inbox.
5. **The next step is a text to read, not a form to fill.** Christianity Explored sends the guest to Mark. LiveWell has notes on every chapter of Mark waiting.
6. **Tell the skeptic how he will be treated, early.** Redeemer's "We value questions" is a title; LiveWell has a better sentence already written and hides it inside one tool.

### Where LiveWell can be better than all four
None of the four results showed an answers site whose answers open straight into a study Bible with notes on the very chapter in question, a doctrine study at full depth, and the church's history of the same argument, all free and in one voice. GotQuestions has breadth without a signed voice. Reasonable Faith has a signed voice without a Bible reader. Christianity Explored has the path without the library. Gospel in Life has the library without the question-first door. LiveWell holds all four pieces and today does not connect them.

### What to build, in order (all from existing content)
1. One question index: Honest Answers plus chapter hard questions plus doctrine studies, searchable from the home field, each with its own address.
2. A short answer at the top of every Honest Answer, and the same fixed foot on each: back to the group, next question, read the passage, the full study.
3. "Ask a hard question" with as-you-type matches, wired to James's inbox, with an honest line about what happens to the question.
4. "Mark in seven sittings" as the first path for a skeptic or a new Christian, with a leader's version.
5. "Also on this chapter" in the Study Bible, and one filterable library by Bible book and topic.
6. A drawn one-screen outline for each Test the Case argument.

### Decisions for James that this research raises
- Will he answer readers' questions in public, and how often? (Without this, "A reader asks" is not built.)
- May a short two-sentence answer sit on top of each Honest Answer? He reads a sample first; the wording is his.
- Is Mark the first Gospel a newcomer is sent to, or John?
