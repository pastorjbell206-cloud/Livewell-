## 1. Christian History Institute (christianhistoryinstitute.org)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched):**
- https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/study (title "Study Church History")
- https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/study/era/early-church and /study/era/medieval
- https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/study/module/polycarp ("#103: Polycarp's Martyrdom") and its PDF, https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/uploaded/50aa53f4962db8.97516591.pdf
- https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/today and /daily/feed ("Today in Christian History")
- https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/magazine/issues/ (back issues by year)
- https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/devotionals, /explore ("Educational Resources"), /related-sites, /store/...

**What the results showed.**
- Study modules are grouped by era (early church defined as roughly the first six hundred years; medieval 600 to 1300) and numbered in sequence within the era: 101 Didache, 102 Pliny's letter to Trajan, 103 Martyrdom of Polycarp, 104 Tertullian, 105 Cyprian, 106 Life of Antony, 107 Eusebius on Constantine, 108 Athanasius On the Incarnation, 109 the Nicaea account from Socrates Scholasticus, 110 an Augustine sermon; 201 Benedict's Rule, and so on.
- Each module is one primary document: a short introduction, an excerpt, then study questions. The early church set is described as "chosen and introduced by church historian Stephen Tomkins." The modules are offered "for self study or as a quarterly introduction to church history for a weekly Sunday school class."
- The Polycarp module's seven questions (as summarised by search) do real historical work: compare the account with the Gospel passion narratives and ask how much is the writer's shaping; did the story grow in the telling; was the yearly commemoration a good development; what would a pagan onlooker have made of it. The PDF version adds Bible readings to pair with it (Daniel 3, Matthew 10, Acts 7).
- A daily page ordered by date with three parts: "It Happened Today," a devotional, and a quote of the day; also sent as a daily email and published as a feed.
- A magazine archive listed by year from 1985 (issue 157 the latest listed for 2025).
- A store sits beside the free material (a book, an archive DVD).
- Mirrors of the module list on other hosts showed duplicate module numbers; I could not check the official numbering.

**Not confirmed:** the look of the site, its timeline pages, people pages, donation prompts, how the home page is laid out.

**Best at.** Teaching church history through one primary document per lesson: a numbered run of modules by era, each a short expert introduction, an excerpt of the source itself, and questions that ask the reader to weigh the source as a historian would.

**Borrow.**
1. **One source per lesson.** LiveWell already holds 22 checked primary texts for church history that readers cannot yet see (audit item 8). Give each course lesson a "Read the source" block: a short excerpt, who wrote it and when, the edition it comes from, and a link to the full text. The existing `verify-sources` gate keeps it honest.
2. **Numbered lessons inside plain-named eras.** Choose one of the two overlapping series as the course, number it (Lesson 1 of N), add previous and next, and group by era with plain titles. This is the audit's fix for "22 essays, not a course," and CHI shows the shape works for a Sunday class.
3. **Questions that weigh the source, not only apply it.** Add four to six questions per lesson in the form LiveWell's 68 study guides already use, with at least one that asks "how do we know this?" and one that states the skeptic's doubt in its strongest form. That is LiveWell's "hard question" pattern from the Bible notes, moved into history.
4. **Bible readings paired with each lesson.** Each lesson names two or three chapters that open in the Study Bible (Polycarp beside Daniel 3 and Acts 7). LiveWell is the one site in this group where that link lands on notes and Greek or Hebrew, not just a verse.
5. **A "use this with a group" line.** One sentence on each lesson and a printable handout, reusing the leader's guide and handout pattern from the study guides, so a pastor can run the course as a quarter of classes.
6. **"On this day" only if it can be sourced.** A dated line on the home or Study page drawn from the course's own checked dates. Build it only from entries that pass the source verifier; a thin, true list beats a full, shaky one.

**Refuse.**
- A store beside the lessons. Nothing for sale on a course page.
- Three daily features at once (event, devotional, quotation). LiveWell already has tonight's devotion; one daily thing per page.
- A quote-of-the-day feed. Unsourced or loosely sourced quotations are the one unforgivable act in LiveWell's own rules; every quotation needs its reference.
- Numbering that drifts. The mirrors showed doubled module numbers; LiveWell freezes lesson ids the way it froze devotion ids.

## 2. Christian Classics Ethereal Library (ccel.org)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched):**
- https://ccel.org/ccel/index.html (home), https://ccel.org/about, https://ccel.org/info/faq
- https://ccel.org/index/genre, https://www.ccel.org/index/subject, /index/language, /index/format
- https://ccel.org/fathers2/ ("Early Church Fathers")
- https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/anf09.xvii.i.html (an "Index of Scripture References" page inside an Ante-Nicene Fathers volume)
- https://worldhistorycommons.org/christian-classics-ethereal-library (an outside review)

**What the results showed.**
- A public-domain library of Christian primary sources, founded by Harry Plantinga in 1993 and supported by Calvin. The outside review says it is especially strong in the church fathers and in Reformation sources and draws more than 20,000 users a day.
- Many ways into one library: all works by author, all by title, and index pages by genre (with counts, for example "Apologetics (5), Bibles (42), Biographies (28)"), subject, language, and format.
- Full-text search and advanced search across every document; works can be read online or downloaded in several formats.
- Home navigation lists Study Bible, Bible Browser, an encyclopedia, and Church Fathers beside the library.
- The Fathers volumes carry an "Index of Scripture References" arranged by book, chapter and verse, so a reader can go from a verse to the places a Father cites it, one volume at a time.

**Not confirmed:** whether readers can annotate or highlight (a 2005 article reported only a plan), whether an account is needed for any feature, how donation appeals appear, and how the reading page looks.

**Best at.** Putting the church's primary sources, whole and free, behind several plain indexes (author, title, genre, subject) with full-text search, and tying those sources back to Scripture through verse indexes.

**Borrow.**
1. **A Scripture index for the history course.** A generated page, and a line in each Study Bible chapter, listing which history lessons and doctrine studies quote that chapter: "John 1 in church history: Nicaea, Athanasius." LiveWell already resolves every reference through its `VerseRef` component, so the index can be built by script like the other manifests. No one in this group joins the Bible and the history in both directions.
2. **Several doors into one shelf, with counts.** The history index browsable by era, by person, by council, and by question, each tab showing its count, in place of the eight screens of open-and-close boxes the audit found.
3. **Search that reads the sources.** Add the history lessons, people, councils and source excerpts to site search by plain name, so "Augustine" returns Augustine (audit item 5).
4. **The whole text one tap away, off-site and credited.** Each excerpt ends with "Read the whole work" linking to a public-domain host, naming translator and edition. LiveWell does not need to become a library; it needs to point to one honestly.
5. **Take-it-with-you formats.** Each lesson with its source and questions as a print-ready PDF through the existing `pnpm pdfs` pipeline.

**Refuse.**
- Becoming a library. Thousands of raw texts with no teacher is the opposite of "one voice." LiveWell excerpts, introduces, and links out.
- Victorian translations left unexplained. Where an excerpt uses a nineteenth-century public-domain translation, say so and gloss the hard words.
- Accounts for study features. If highlights or notes come to history pages, they stay on the device, as everywhere else on the site.
- Index pages as long lists with no way in. Counts and tabs, not a wall.

## 3. New Advent, Fathers of the Church (newadvent.org/fathers)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched):**
- https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/ and /fathers/index.html (the Fathers index)
- https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1101.htm (Augustine, Confessions, contents) and /fathers/110106.htm, 110107.htm, 110108.htm, 110110.htm (individual books of the Confessions)
- Outside descriptions at patristics.org and reformedbooksonline.com

**What the results showed.**
- The Fathers index has an A to Z letter bar at the top and lists authors with their works beneath. An outside guide calls it "a large collection and easy to navigate."
- A long work is split into one page per book, with a breadcrumb such as "Home > Fathers of the Church > The Confessions > Book VI," and chapter headings inside the page ("Chapter 4. That in His Confessions He May Do Good, He Considers Others").
- Addresses are short, stable and numbered (1101 the work, 110106 its sixth book), which is why the whole web cites them.
- The work page carries an edition line saying the text was "revised and edited for New Advent by Kevin Knight."
- The page carries a support appeal offering the whole site (encyclopedia, Fathers, Summa, Bible) as a download for $19.99.
- It is a Catholic site; the Fathers sit beside the Catholic Encyclopedia and the Summa.

**Not confirmed:** the exact wording of the source line and translator credit, whether Scripture references in the text are linked, the amount of advertising, and how it reads on a phone.

**Best at.** The plainest possible way to reach a Father's actual words: one alphabetical page of names, one page per book of each work, a breadcrumb that always says where you are, and addresses that never change.

**Borrow.**
1. **People pages with a letter bar.** One A to Z page of the people the course names, each with dates, one sentence on why they matter, the lessons they appear in, and a link to their own words. The audit found people and councils printed as plain text though entries exist; link every name to its entry.
2. **A breadcrumb on every lesson and source.** "Study > Church history > The early church > Lesson 4 > Source." A reader arriving from Google on a phone knows where he is and what comes next.
3. **Short, permanent addresses.** Lesson and person addresses fixed once and never renamed, held by a test, so pastors and other sites can cite them for years. Old addresses redirect in one hop, as the Decision Log already requires.
4. **Cite the source the way the page shows it.** Under every excerpt: author, work, book and chapter, translator, edition, year. The reader should be able to find the same lines in a printed copy.
5. **Split long things by their own divisions.** Where a lesson quotes at length, break by the work's own books and chapters and keep its numbering, so a citation like "Confessions 8.12" opens the right place.

**Refuse.**
- A paid download of what is free on the page. Everything on LiveWell is free and says so.
- Ads or appeals inside the reading column.
- A confessional frame that decides the reading for the reader. LiveWell names where James lands and states the other positions in their strongest voice.
- Text with no teacher. A page of Augustine with no introduction and no questions informs; the brief asks for a course.

## 4. World History Encyclopedia (worldhistory.org), the timeline site

Chosen as the history-teaching site of any kind with a timeline. Seen only through search results.

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched):**
- https://www.worldhistory.org/timeline/Augustine_of_Hippo/ ("Timeline: Augustine of Hippo")
- https://www.worldhistory.org/Augustine_of_Hippo/ (the definition article)
- https://www.worldhistory.org/carthage/ (shows a "Teaching Materials" count on a subject page)
- https://www.worldhistory.org/lessonplan/22/feudal-japan/ and /lessonplan/54/ancient-greece-teaching-resource-bundle/ (teaching packs)
- https://www.worldhistory.org/collection/325/ap-world-history-unit-1/ (a curated collection)
- https://worldhistory.org/video/2150 (a timeline video page showing a citation box, a Membership link and "Remove Ads")
- https://www.worldhistory.org/llms.txt (lists a "Timeline of History" index and a "World History Maps" index)

**What the results showed.**
- **Every subject has its own timeline at a matching address.** The article is at /Augustine_of_Hippo/ and its timeline at /timeline/Augustine_of_Hippo/, with dated entries (born 354, Confessions about 397, City of God from 413). The timeline is a view of the same data the article draws on, not a separate exhibit.
- A site-wide "Timeline of History" index and a separate maps index.
- Teaching packs per topic that bundle a lesson plan with a timeline, maps, and multiple-choice quiz questions; curated collections that order existing articles into a unit.
- A citation box on content pages offering APA, Chicago and MLA forms.
- The site shows advertising, with a "Remove Ads" control and a membership offer.
- Search surfaced two different end dates for City of God on two pages of the same site (425 and 426): a reminder that a timeline is only as good as its one source of truth.

**Not confirmed:** how the timeline is drawn or moved through, whether it can be filtered by date or keyword, and how it behaves on a phone.

**Best at.** Making the timeline a property of every subject: any person or place has its own dated timeline at a predictable address, built from the same entries as the article, with teaching packs that bundle timeline, map and quiz around one lesson.

**Borrow.**
1. **One list of dated events, many views.** A single committed data file of events (date, title, one sentence, people, place, lesson, source). From it generate the full course timeline, a small timeline on each lesson, and a timeline on each person page. One file means a date cannot differ between two pages, and a validator can require a source on every entry.
2. **A timeline per person and per council.** "/study/history/people/augustine" shows his dated life beside the lessons he appears in. This is also the engine for the "who was alive when" tool the brief lists: pick a year, see who was living, writing, and meeting.
3. **Bible and church on one line.** LiveWell can do what a general history site cannot: put the writing of the New Testament books, the councils, and the reader's own Study Bible chapters on the same line, so the gap between Paul and Nicaea is something a reader can see.
4. **"Cite this page."** A small plain-text citation under each lesson and answer, for the serious reader and the student. It costs little and signals that the page expects to be checked.
5. **A lesson bundle.** Each course lesson offers the same four things in the same order: read, the source, the timeline slice, questions. Add a short self-check of three to five questions using the existing self-check pattern and storage helper, so the tool "remembers its place and leads somewhere next."
6. **Maps drawn once, in the site's own hand.** A few simple line maps in the cream, black and mustard palette (the Mediterranean of the first three centuries, the five ancient sees, Reformation Europe), each referenced from the lessons. The audit found no drawing, map or diagram on eighteen pages.

**Refuse.**
- Ads, a "Remove Ads" control, and a membership tier. None of it.
- Encyclopedia sprawl. LiveWell's course is one ordered path in one voice, not thousands of entries by many hands.
- A timeline that is a spectacle. One of the search results was an educator calling a famous all-of-history timeline "too overwhelming for use with students." LiveWell's timeline should carry dozens of sourced events a person can move through with a thumb, not thousands.
- Quizzes that test trivia. Self-check questions should ask what a thing meant and why it mattered, not the year alone.

## What this group adds up to for LiveWell

No site here does the whole job. CHI teaches from sources with questions but, on this evidence, stands apart from a Bible. CCEL and New Advent hold the sources and leave the reader without a teacher. World History Encyclopedia has the timeline and teaching packs, with ads, and no interest in Scripture. LiveWell's opening is the join: a numbered course where every lesson has the source shown and cited, a slice of one shared timeline, questions that weigh the evidence, and links both ways into a Study Bible with notes on every chapter. Free, no ads, one voice.

The build order this suggests, all from what LiveWell already has:
1. One course: pick the series, number it, previous and next, plain era names.
2. Show the 22 checked primary texts as "Read the source" blocks with full citations.
3. One events data file with a validator, then the timeline views from it.
4. People and council pages with a letter bar, every name in a lesson linked.
5. The Scripture index joining Bible chapters and history lessons in both directions.
6. Questions and a short self-check per lesson; a printable handout.
7. "Who was alive when," built on the events file.
