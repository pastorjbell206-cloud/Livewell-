## 1. Plough (plough.com)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched).** Result text for:
- https://www.plough.com/articles/retooling-the-plough (their own 2022 note on the redesign)
- https://www.plough.com/articles/the-sisters-on-light-row
- https://www.plough.com/articles/a-friend-for-life
- https://www.plough.com/articles/becoming-a-people
- https://www.plough.com/en/subscriptions/daily-dig
- https://www.plough.com/en/subscriptions/daily-prayer
- https://www.plough.com/en/subscriptions/weekly

**What the results showed.**
- An article page opens with a trail showing its section, then the title, then the kind of piece (Essay, Editorial, Review), then a summary line. Author and date sit above the body.
- Pictures carry a caption with a small credit line ("Photograph courtesy of the author").
- Pieces that began in print say which issue they first appeared in (one says a 2014 issue).
- Pages end with "Let us know what you think" and links to other articles, often by the issue's theme.
- The menu lists Magazine, Books, Podcast, Free Newsletters.
- Three free rhythms: Daily Dig (a daily email), Daily Prayer (a prayer for each day of the year with matching Scripture, free by email, Facebook or RSS), and a weekly digest.
- Their redesign note says the logo and typography were refreshed across cover and website together: a calligraphic P drawn from their 1938 edition, paired with a wordmark in a contemporary condensed serif.

**Not seen.** The home page, body typeface, measure, colours, dark mode, motion, illustration style, whether any article is metered or paid.

**Best at.** On the evidence seen: labelling every piece by its kind and giving it a one-line summary before the reader commits, and holding a daily rhythm (a prayer for every day of the year) beside long reading.

**Borrow.**
1. A kind label on every piece. LiveWell has essays, Honest Answers, history lessons, doctrine studies, study guides and devotions. Put one plain word above each title ("Answer," "History lesson," "Study guide," "Devotion") in the existing eyebrow style, so a reader always knows what they are holding.
2. A summary line under every title, site-wide. The essay title block already has an italic summary (the audit calls it the most publication-like moment on the site). Give the same block to history lessons, answers and doctrine studies.
3. A dated daily page. LiveWell has 1,189 chapter notes, a catechism and a year of devotions. "Today" can be one page with its own address: today's reading plan chapter, this week's catechism question, tonight's devotion. No email needed.
4. A picture credit and caption style, decided once, before any drawing or map goes in. The audit found no drawing on eighteen pages; when they come, each gets a caption and a source line.
5. One visual mark carried from books to site. Plough tied its website type to its print cover. LiveWell's three book covers and the site should share one title typeface and one rule.
6. End with one invitation to respond, not six asks. The audit counted six asks after an essay's last line. One line ("Write to James about this") and two or three related pieces is enough.

**Refuse.** Anything paid or metered (not confirmed for Plough either way; LiveWell's rule stands regardless). Email as the only way to get the daily thing. A comment section to moderate.

## 2. Aeon (aeon.co)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched).** Result text for ten essay pages, including:
- https://aeon.co/essays/we-cooperate-to-survive-but-if-no-ones-looking-we-compete
- https://aeon.co/essays/your-brain-does-not-process-information-and-it-is-not-a-computer
- https://aeon.co/essays/how-a-warming-earth-is-changing-our-brains-bodies-and-minds
- and, about Aeon, https://fontsinuse.com/uses/3730/aeon-magazine (a 2012 entry)

**What the results showed.**
- Each essay page states its length in words (about 3,600; 4,100) with a "Save" link beside it.
- Most give a listening time ("24 minute listen"), so essays have audio.
- Each credits its editor by name ("Edited by ...").
- Each carries a "Syndicate this essay" button.
- Essay titles are full plain sentences that state the claim ("Your brain does not process information and it is not a computer").
- Page data names the publisher as a nonprofit.
- The 2012 Fonts In Use entry lists PT Serif, Chivo and Quattrocento, all free fonts. That is fourteen years old and says nothing certain about today.

**Not seen.** The home page, today's typefaces, measure, colours, dark mode, motion, imagery, donation prompts, whether there are ads.

**Best at.** On the evidence seen: telling the reader the cost of a piece before they start (words, listening time) and titling essays as plain claims a stranger understands.

**Borrow.**
1. Length stated honestly on every long page. LiveWell already shows reading time on essays. Add it to chapter notes, history lessons, doctrine studies and answers, from the same word count.
2. "Listen" on long reading. The brief already asks for the catechism read aloud. The same player can serve history lessons and essays later. Only with a real recording or a voice James approves.
3. "Save" beside the length. LiveWell has My Library kept on the device. Put one Save control in the title block of every reading page, in the same place every time.
4. Titles as plain claims or questions. The audit found 7 of 22 history titles pass the plain-language test ("The Constantinian Turn"). Retitle them the way the Honest Answers are titled, as sentences.
5. Credit the checking. Aeon names the editor. LiveWell can state on each history lesson and answer when it was last reviewed and link its sources, which serves the "sources a reader can check" goal.
6. A reuse line. One sentence at the foot of study guides and devotions: "Free to print and hand out in your church." Pastors are the reader this serves.

**Refuse.** Donation prompts (LiveWell has no giving page). An account to save. A feed of many authors and topics: LiveWell is one voice.

## 3. Stripe Press (press.stripe.com)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched).** Result titles and summaries for:
- https://press.stripe.com/ (title: "Stripe Press — Ideas for progress")
- https://press.stripe.com/high-growth-handbook
- https://press.stripe.com/where-is-my-flying-car
- https://press.stripe.com/maintenance-part-one
- and third-party descriptions: https://www.lapa.ninja/post/stripe-press/ , https://tdavis.bitweb1.nwtc.edu/wfed2/website-reviews/stripe-press/index.html , https://framer.university/resources/3d-shiny-book-interaction-in-framer , https://styles.refero.design/style/d2c0ed7b-c649-4d77-91de-1bd69dd10a9e

**What the results showed.**
- Every book has its own page at a short address made of its title. The page holds blurbs, reviews and related titles.
- A student review says the site uses a dark background that makes the book spines stand out. A design catalogue tags the site "book, education, minimal," with black the most common colour.
- A tutorial describes a "shiny 3D book" that reacts when the pointer is over it, built with 3D transforms.
- A style catalogue names Stripe Press as a peer of sites with serif display headlines, generous white space and a cream or off-white ground.
- Podcast episodes offer "Read transcript."
- Stripe Press also publishes films, podcasts and a digital edition of a book.

**Not seen.** The home page itself, its typefaces, exact colours, how the shelf moves, whether any book text can be read online.

**Best at.** On the evidence seen: treating a small shelf of books as objects worth looking at, each with one page and one short address, on a dark ground that makes them the only thing in the room.

**Borrow.**
1. The books as objects. LiveWell shows three books. Give them one dark section (black is already the site's second surface), each cover large, nothing else competing.
2. One touchable thing with real craft. The audit says nothing responds when pressed. A book cover that tilts, or the 66 books of the Bible as a shelf of spines sized by length, each opening its introduction. This is the brief's "66 books at a glance." Must honour reduced-motion settings and still work as a plain list.
3. One page per book, one short address, the same order every time: what it is, who it is for, the opening pages, where to get it. The audit found a "read the opening free" promise with no opening; this page is where that becomes true.
4. Small and finished beats large and thin. The public shelf of three books and a curated essay shelf is the right instinct. Design for the small shelf.
5. A transcript beside anything spoken. If the catechism or a lesson is read aloud, the words are on the page too.

**Refuse.** Heavy 3D that slows a phone (the audit scores a Bible chapter 43 of 100 for speed already). A whole site on black: cream is the room. Motion as decoration with no job.

## 4. The Marginalian (themarginalian.org)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched).** Result text for:
- https://www.themarginalian.org/about/
- https://www.themarginalian.org/donate/
- https://www.themarginalian.org/tag/best-of/
- https://www.themarginalian.org/tag/books/ , /tag/lists/ , /tag/media/
- https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/0/ , /2023/ , /2022/ , /2020/ , /2015/

**What the results showed.**
- The About page says the site is one person's work, and "free, ad-free, and AI-free," kept alive by readers.
- The archive is carried two ways: one page per year, and one page per subject tag.
- A "Favorite Reads" heading, drawn from pieces tagged "best of," appears on the year and tag pages.
- A "midweek newsletter" label appears on archive pages.
- A donate page with monthly tiers from $3 and a one-time gift.

**Not seen.** The home page, an essay page, typefaces, colours, sidebar, how an essay ends, dark mode.

**Best at.** On the evidence seen: carrying a very large one-author archive with two simple keys (year and subject) and one hand-picked "best of" shelf that shows up wherever a reader is browsing.

**Borrow.**
1. Two keys for every library: by subject and in order. Bible notes by book; history by century; answers by question; essays by year. Never a third scheme. The audit found four index pages for hard questions; this is the cure.
2. A "Start with these" shelf on every index. Five to seven hand-picked pieces at the top of Topics, Study, church history and Essays, chosen by James, the same component each time. It answers "where do I begin" (audit item 11).
3. Say plainly whose work this is. One sentence in the footer and on About: one author, free, no ads, nothing behind a sign-up. LiveWell already says "619 files, free, nothing gated"; say the whole promise once, everywhere. Do not claim "AI-free" unless it is true of every page.
4. Link pieces to each other inside the prose. Each chapter note that touches a doctrine links the doctrine study; each history lesson links the people and councils it names (the audit says these are plain text today).
5. One-voice confidence. A large body of work under one name can look like a publication without pretending to have a staff.

**Refuse.** The donation asks and tiers. Any sidebar of promotions around the reading column. An archive so long it needs endless scrolling: the audit already faults index pages 12 to 18 phone screens long.

## 5. Interactive learning: Bartosz Ciechanowski (ciechanow.ski)

**Seen (search results only, no page fetched).** Result text for:
- https://ciechanow.ski/archives/
- https://ciechanow.ski/mechanical-watch/
- https://ciechanow.ski/bicycle/
- https://ciechanow.ski/moon/
- and commentary: https://hackaday.com/2023/05/08/the-bicycle-and-more-explained , https://ericholscher.com/blog/2025/jan/7/everything-bartosz-ciechanowski-makes/ , https://flowingdata.com/?p=77008 , https://medium.com/@Max_Goldstein/exploring-explorable-explanations-92f865c8d6ba

**What the results showed.**
- The archive is a plain list of titles with dates. Titles are one or two words: Moon, Bicycle, Sound, Gears, GPS.
- Each article describes itself in one line ("an interactive article explaining how a mechanical watch works").
- The teaching is done by pictures the reader moves: a slider that opens the watch to look inside; a slider that steers the rider; dragging to change the view; sliders for date and time that move the Moon across the sky.
- Commentators say he publishes only a few a year, and that readers come away feeling they understand the thing.
- One essay on the form notes that such pages work best for a system of cause and effect, and that the prose around the picture does the guiding a teacher would do.

**Not seen.** The pages rendered; how the sliders feel; type; colour; phone behaviour.

**Best at.** On the evidence seen: teaching one thing per page by putting a picture the reader can move directly inside the paragraph that explains it, and doing few of them, each finished.

**Borrow.**
1. The timeline as a slider inside the history course. Drag through the centuries; the lesson, people and councils for that span appear. This is the brief's church history timeline, built from the 22 essays and the existing entries for people and councils.
2. "Who was alive when" as one slider. Move a year; see which named people were living. Uses only dates the history library already holds and can source.
3. The picture sits in the paragraph, not on a tools page. A map in the chapter note where the place is named; the compare-the-views table inside the doctrine study; a Greek word's range of meaning beside the verse. The audit says the tools are "hidden, repeated, and all made of buttons and lists."
4. One job, one plain name. "Timeline." "Compare the views." "Memory verse." The audit found 23 of 36 tool names pass; five tools look up verses by topic. Fold them.
5. Few and finished. Build three interactive pieces to a high standard before a fourth. A plain list of them, like his archive, is enough of an index.
6. Words still carry it. Every interactive piece reads correctly with the picture ignored, for screen readers, print, and slow phones.

**Refuse.** Interactive pieces that teach nothing (motion for show). Anything that needs a fast phone to open a Bible chapter. A drawn diagram of a doctrine that claims more precision than the doctrine has.

## What this group can honestly say across the sites

Five patterns recur in the evidence that was seen, and each meets a named weakness in the audit:

1. Tell the reader what a piece is and what it costs before they start: kind, one-line summary, length, listening time (Plough, Aeon).
2. Carry a large archive with two keys and one hand-picked shelf (The Marginalian).
3. Keep the shelf small and treat each thing as an object (Stripe Press).
4. Put the thing to touch inside the explanation, give it one job, and build few (Ciechanowski).
5. Hold a daily rhythm beside the long reading (Plough).

None of these needs new content. All of them are arrangement and labelling of what LiveWell already holds.

The questions this group was most asked to answer (which typefaces, what measure, what scale, how dark mode is handled, how motion is used) are still open. They should be answered by someone who can open the pages.
