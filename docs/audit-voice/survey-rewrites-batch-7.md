## 32. Christianity After Christendom

`christianity-after-christendom` · devotionals · 2467 words

### Current opening

> On February 27, 380 AD, Emperor Theodosius I issued the Edict of Thessalonica, declaring Nicene Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire. With a single imperial decree, the faith of a persecuted minority became the faith of the state. Churches that had met in catacombs and private homes were suddenly building basilicas with public funds. Bishops who had been imprisoned became advisors to emperors. The cross, which had been the instrument of the movement's founder's execution, was mounted on the standards of the imperial legions. The transformation was so complete and so rapid that within a generation, it was nearly impossible to remember what Christianity had been before it had power.
>
> That transformation (from persecuted sect to established religion, from voluntary community to cultural default) is what historians call "Christendom." And for approximately 1,600 years, it was the dominant mode of Christian existence in the Western world. Whether you were born in medieval France, Reformation Germany, colonial New England, or mid-twentieth-century America, Christianity was the water you swam in, the assumed religious framework of your culture, the default setting of public morality, the invisible architecture of your social world. You did not choose Christianity. It chose you, by virtue of your birth into a civilization shaped by its institutions, assumptions, and calendar.
>
> Christendom is over. The sociological evidence is overwhelming and has been accumulating for decades. Charles Taylor, in his magisterial A Secular Age, traces the shift from a society in which belief in God was the unquestioned default to one in which it is merely one option among many, and not the most culturally prestigious option at that. In 1968, the sociologist Peter Berger predicted that by the twenty-first century, religion would persist only in small, embattled enclaves. He was partially wrong about the global picture (religion has proven far more resilient worldwide than secularization theorists predicted) but he was remarkably prescient about the West. In America, in Europe, in Canada, in Australia, the cultural dominance of Christianity has collapsed within a single lifetime.

### Proposed opening

On February 27, 380, the emperor Theodosius I issued the Edict of Thessalonica and made Nicene Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire. With one decree the faith of a persecuted minority became the faith of the state. Churches that had met in catacombs and private houses began raising basilicas with public money. Bishops who had been in prison became advisers to emperors. The cross, the instrument on which the movement's founder had been executed, went up on the standards of the legions. The change was so complete and so fast that within a generation it was nearly impossible to remember what Christianity had been before it had power.

That change, from persecuted sect to established religion, from voluntary community to cultural default, is what historians call Christendom, and for roughly 1,600 years it was the ordinary mode of Christian existence in the West. Born in medieval France, Reformation Germany, colonial New England, or mid-twentieth-century America, you swam in Christianity as a fish swims in water. It was the assumed framework of the culture, the default setting of public morality, the invisible architecture of the social world. You did not choose it. It chose you, by the accident of being born into a civilization shaped by its institutions, its assumptions, and its calendar.

Christendom is over, and I am not writing that as a lament, though it would be easy to. The sociological evidence is overwhelming and has been piling up for decades. Charles Taylor, in A Secular Age, traces the shift from a society in which belief in God was the unquestioned default to one in which it is one option among many, and not the most prestigious. In 1968 the sociologist Peter Berger predicted that by the twenty-first century religion would survive only in small, embattled enclaves. He was wrong about the world, where religion has proved far tougher than the secularization theorists expected, and remarkably right about the West. In America, in Europe, in Canada, in Australia, the cultural dominance of Christianity collapsed inside a single lifetime. The question is what the church does now, and the answer this essay gives is not the one most churches are trying.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am not writing that as a lament, though it would be easy to"

**What changed and why.** Theodosius and the Edict of Thessalonica (380), the 1,600 years, Taylor's A Secular Age, and Berger's 1968 prediction are the essay's. The rewrite refuses the lament the subject invites and points the opening at the essay's own answer: not reversal.

## 33. The Black Church: The Most Important Institution in American Christianity

`the-black-church-in-america` · prophetic-justice · 2812 words

### Current opening

> There is no single institution in American history that has done more with fewer resources, under greater oppression, with less recognition, than the Black church. It educated a people when education was illegal. It organized a community when organization was punishable by death. It produced a theology of liberation a century before European theologians gave it a name. And it did all of this while white Christianity, with rare exceptions, either ignored it, opposed it, or patronized it.
>
> To understand the Black church is to understand that it was never merely a religious institution. It was the school, the bank, the political caucus, the mutual aid society, the concert hall, and the courtroom of a people systematically excluded from every other institution in American life. The church was the one place where Black Americans could be fully human, fully vocal, and fully free. That this institution was built on a faith given to them by their enslavers, and then transformed into something their enslavers could not recognize, is one of the most remarkable feats of theological creativity in the history of Christianity.
>
> The religious life of enslaved Africans in America began in secrecy and subversion. Slaveholders had a complicated relationship with the Christianization of enslaved people. Some believed, or claimed to believe, that baptism conferred freedom, which made them reluctant to allow it. Others promoted a carefully controlled version of Christianity that emphasized obedience: "Slaves, obey your masters" (Ephesians 6:5) was the most frequently cited verse on the plantation. The Christianity offered to enslaved people was a theology of submission, designed to baptize the existing order and render resistance sinful.

### Proposed opening

No institution in American history has done more with fewer resources, under heavier oppression, and with less recognition than the Black church. It educated a people when their education was illegal. It organized a community when organizing was punishable by death. It produced a theology of liberation a century before European theologians gave one a name. And it did all of it while white Christianity, with rare exceptions, ignored it, opposed it, or patronized it.

This essay is written by someone with more to learn from that institution than to say about it, and it should be read that way. To understand the Black church you have to understand that it was never only a church. It was the school, the bank, the political caucus, the mutual aid society, the concert hall, and the courtroom of a people shut out of every other institution in American life. It was the one place where Black Americans could be fully human, fully vocal, and fully free. That it was built on a faith handed to them by their enslavers, and then remade into something their enslavers could not recognize, is one of the most remarkable acts of theological creativity in the history of Christianity.

It began in secrecy. Slaveholders had a complicated relationship with the Christianizing of the people they owned. Some believed, or said they believed, that baptism conferred freedom, which made them reluctant to allow it. Others promoted a carefully controlled Christianity of obedience: "Slaves, obey your masters" (Ephesians 6:5) was the verse most often preached on the plantation. What was offered to enslaved people was a theology of submission, built to bless the existing order and make resistance a sin. They did not accept it, and what they built instead is the rest of this story.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "This essay is written by someone with more to learn from that institution than to say about it, and it should be read that way."

**What changed and why.** Every claim is the essay's, including Ephesians 6:5 as the plantation's most-preached verse. The rewrite adds one sentence of posture (the writer as learner, not authority) and makes no claim about the writer's own race or tradition, since the repository does not establish either; the essay's section on what the white church has never learned does that work in its own place.

## 34. How American Christianity Became American

`how-american-christianity-became-american` · theology · 3688 words

### Current opening

> American Christianity is not European Christianity with an American accent. It is a fundamentally different thing, a faith remade by the forces of revivalism, democracy, capitalism, and a national mythology so powerful that many American Christians cannot tell where their faith ends and their national identity begins. Understanding how this happened is essential for anyone trying to make sense of the most religiously observant nation in the Western world, and one of the most theologically confused.
>
> The story begins with the Puritans, but it does not end there. At every stage of American history, Christianity has been reshaped by the culture it encountered, absorbing the values of individualism, pragmatism, and optimism that define the American character. The result is a form of Christianity that would be unrecognizable to most Christians throughout history, and yet it is, for better and worse, the dominant expression of the faith in the most powerful nation on earth.
>
> The Puritans who settled Massachusetts Bay in 1630 were not seeking religious freedom in the modern sense. They were seeking the freedom to build a godly commonwealth, a community ordered by covenant theology, governed by the elect, and dedicated to the glory of God. John Winthrop's famous sermon aboard the Arbella, in which he described the colony as "a city upon a hill," was not an expression of American exceptionalism in its later, triumphalist form. It was a warning: because the colony had entered into a covenant with God, its failures would be judged more severely than those of other nations. "The eyes of all people are upon us," Winthrop said, "so that if we shall deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a byword through the world."

### Proposed opening

American Christianity is not European Christianity with an American accent. It is a different thing, a faith remade by revivalism, democracy, capitalism, and a national mythology so strong that many American Christians cannot tell where their faith ends and their national identity begins. I pastor an American church, and I cannot always tell, in my own preaching, where the one ends and the other begins. That is the confession this essay starts from, and it is the reason the history matters: you cannot see the water you swim in until someone shows you where it came from.

The story begins with the Puritans but does not end there. At every stage, Christianity in America has been reshaped by the culture it met, absorbing the individualism, the pragmatism, and the optimism that make up the American character. The result is a form of the faith that would be unrecognizable to most Christians across most of history, and that is, for better and worse, the dominant expression of Christianity in the most powerful nation on earth.

The Puritans who settled Massachusetts Bay in 1630 were not seeking religious freedom in the modern sense. They wanted the freedom to build a godly commonwealth, ordered by covenant, governed by the elect, and given to the glory of God. John Winthrop's sermon aboard the Arbella, the one that called the colony "a city upon a hill," was not American exceptionalism in its later triumphant form. It was a warning. Because the colony had covenanted with God, its failures would be judged more harshly than other nations': "The eyes of all people are upon us," Winthrop said, "so that if we shall deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a byword through the world."

## 35. What Christians Can Learn From Judaism

`what-christians-can-learn-from-judaism` · theology · 2022 words

### Current opening

> Jesus was Jewish. This statement is so obvious that it should not need saying, and so consistently ignored in its implications that it cannot be said often enough. Jesus was circumcised on the eighth day. He was presented at the temple according to the law of Moses. He attended synagogue. He observed the feasts. He wore tzitzit. He debated Torah with rabbis. He quoted the Psalms from the cross. The prayer he taught his disciples (the Lord's Prayer) is structured on Jewish liturgical patterns. The meal he shared on his last night (the Last Supper) was a Passover seder. The faith that Christians call their own was born inside Judaism, shaped by Jewish categories, expressed in Jewish idiom, and proclaimed by Jewish apostles to a world that had no framework for understanding it apart from the Jewish story of God, creation, covenant, and redemption.
>
> And yet, within a few centuries, Christianity had not merely distinguished itself from Judaism but defined itself against it. The synagogue became the enemy. The Torah became the old dispensation, superseded and surpassed. The people of Israel became the people who had rejected their own Messiah, a rejection that, in the hands of Christian theologians from John Chrysostom to Martin Luther, justified centuries of persecution, forced conversion, ghettoization, and, in its most catastrophic expression, the theological soil in which the Holocaust took root.
>
> The recovery of Christianity's Jewish roots is not a matter of interfaith politeness. It is a matter of theological survival. A Christianity severed from Judaism is a Christianity that has lost access to the intellectual, spiritual, and moral resources of its own foundation. And the things that Judaism preserves (things that Christianity has largely forgotten) are precisely the things that the contemporary church most desperately needs.

### Proposed opening

Jesus was Jewish. The sentence is so obvious it should not need saying, and its implications are so consistently ignored that it cannot be said often enough. He was circumcised on the eighth day and presented at the temple according to the law of Moses. He went to synagogue. He kept the feasts. He wore tzitzit. He argued Torah with rabbis and quoted the Psalms from the cross. The prayer he taught his disciples is built on Jewish liturgical patterns, and the meal he shared on his last night was a Passover seder. The faith Christians call their own was born inside Judaism, shaped by Jewish categories, spoken in Jewish idiom, and carried by Jewish apostles into a world that had no way to understand it apart from the Jewish story of God, creation, covenant, and redemption.

I preach every week from a book written almost entirely by Jews, and it is easy to forget that, and I do. The church forgot it early. Within a few centuries Christianity had not only distinguished itself from Judaism but defined itself against it. The synagogue became the enemy. The Torah became the old dispensation, superseded and surpassed. The people of Israel became the people who had rejected their own Messiah, and that charge, in the hands of theologians from John Chrysostom to Martin Luther, underwrote centuries of persecution, forced conversion, and ghettoization, and became, in its most catastrophic form, the theological soil in which the Holocaust took root.

Recovering the Jewish roots of the faith is not a matter of interfaith politeness. It is a matter of theological survival. A Christianity cut off from Judaism has lost access to the intellectual, spiritual, and moral resources of its own foundation, and the things Judaism has kept, things the church has largely let go, are exactly the things the contemporary church most needs. This essay is about what those things are, and about the error that made the church drop them.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I preach every week from a book written almost entirely by Jews, and it is easy to forget that, and I do."

**What changed and why.** Every detail of Jesus's Jewishness, Chrysostom to Luther, and the Holocaust sentence are the essay's. The rewrite has the preacher confess the forgetting before charging the church with it.
