## 11. What Evangelicalism Was Supposed to Be

`what-evangelicalism-was-supposed-to-be` · theology · 2524 words

### Current opening

> The word "evangelical" comes from the Greek euangelion, meaning "good news" or "gospel." In its oldest usage, it simply described anyone who took the gospel message seriously. Martin Luther used the term to describe churches shaped by the Reformation. In much of Europe, "evangelical" remains a synonym for "Protestant." The word carries no political connotation in Germany, Brazil, or South Korea. It is only in the United States that "evangelical" has come to mean something that would be unrecognizable to its founders.
>
> The story of how that happened is a story about good intentions, institutional ambition, cultural anxiety, and the seductive power of political access. It is also a cautionary tale about what happens when a theological identity becomes a tribal one.
>
> The historian David Bebbington, in his landmark 1989 study Evangelicalism in Modern Britain, identified four defining characteristics of evangelical Christianity. These have become the standard scholarly framework, known as the Bebbington Quadrilateral.

### Proposed opening

The word "evangelical" comes from the Greek euangelion, good news, the gospel. In its oldest use it described anyone who took that news seriously. Luther used it for the churches shaped by the Reformation, and across much of Europe it is still a plain synonym for Protestant. In Germany, in Brazil, in South Korea, the word carries no politics at all. Only in the United States has "evangelical" come to mean something its founders would not recognize, and anyone who still uses the word here has to say which one they mean.

How that happened is a story about good intentions, institutional ambition, cultural anxiety, and the pull of political access, and it is a cautionary tale about what happens when a theological identity becomes a tribal one. I have to be careful here, because the easy version of this story is one more piece of ammunition for one side, and this essay is trying not to be that. The people who built the movement were not villains. Most of what they wanted was right. That is what makes the ending hard to read.

Start with what the word was supposed to mean. In 1989 the historian David Bebbington, in Evangelicalism in Modern Britain, named four marks that have become the standard scholarly description of the movement, the Bebbington Quadrilateral. They are worth taking one at a time, because every later chapter of the American story is a chapter about one of them being traded for something else.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have to be careful here, because the easy version of this story is one more piece of ammunition for one side, and this essay is trying not to be that."

**What changed and why.** Euangelion, Luther's usage, the three countries where the word carries no politics, and Bebbington's 1989 study are the essay's. The rewrite names the tribal temptation the essay itself has to resist, concedes that the movement's founders mostly wanted the right things, and sets up the four marks as the measure the rest of the essay uses.

## 12. Colonialism and Missions

`colonialism-and-missions` · prophetic-justice · 1985 words

### Current opening

> On mission Sundays in American churches, the story is always the same. Brave missionaries cross oceans and jungles to bring the gospel to people who have never heard it. The emphasis is on sacrifice, heroism, and the life-altering power of the Christian message. Slides show smiling children. Testimonies recount dramatic conversions. The offering plate circulates with a special appeal for the unreached. It is a stirring narrative. It is also, in its selective telling, a profoundly dishonest one.
>
> The parts we do not tell on mission Sundays are the parts that matter most for understanding why the word "missionary" carries, in much of the non-Western world, connotations not of love but of destruction. The history of Christian missions is inseparable from the history of European colonialism. The gospel traveled in the holds of slave ships, in the baggage trains of conquering armies, in the administrative apparatus of colonial governments that used Christianity as a tool of cultural subjugation. This does not mean that every missionary was a colonialist, or that every conversion was coerced, or that the gospel itself is reducible to its colonial carriers. But it means that any honest reckoning with the history of Christian missions must contend with the entanglement of the gospel with empire, an entanglement that was not incidental but structural, not accidental but theological.
>
> In 1452, Pope Nicholas V issued the papal bull Dum Diversas, authorizing King Alfonso V of Portugal to "invade, search out, capture, vanquish, and subdue" all non-Christians, to reduce them to perpetual slavery, and to take their possessions and lands. In 1493, Pope Alexander VI issued Inter Caetera, granting Spain sovereignty over the lands Columbus had "discovered" in the Western Hemisphere. These documents (collectively known as the Doctrine of Discovery) provided the theological foundation for European colonialism. They established the principle that Christian nations had a divinely sanctioned right to claim sovereignty over non-Christian lands and peoples.

### Proposed opening

On mission Sunday in an American church the story is always the same. Brave people cross oceans and jungles to bring the gospel to those who have never heard it. The slides show smiling children. The testimonies recount dramatic conversions. The offering plate goes around with a special appeal for the unreached. I have stood in front of my own church and told that version, and it is stirring. In its selective telling it is also profoundly dishonest.

The parts we leave out are the parts that explain why, in much of the non-Western world, the word "missionary" means destruction before it means love. The history of Christian missions cannot be separated from the history of European colonialism. The gospel traveled in the holds of slave ships, in the baggage trains of conquering armies, in the paperwork of colonial governments that used Christianity to subdue the people they ruled. That does not make every missionary a colonialist, or every conversion coerced, or the gospel reducible to the people who carried it. It does mean the entanglement was not an accident. It was structural, and it was theological, and an honest reckoning has to begin there.

In 1452, Pope Nicholas V issued the bull Dum Diversas, authorizing King Alfonso V of Portugal to "invade, search out, capture, vanquish, and subdue" all non-Christians, to reduce them to perpetual slavery, and to take their lands and possessions. In 1493, Alexander VI issued Inter Caetera, granting Spain sovereignty over the lands Columbus had "discovered." Together these documents are known as the Doctrine of Discovery, and they supplied the theological foundation for European colonialism: the principle that Christian nations had a God-given right to claim non-Christian lands and peoples. This was not a fringe opinion. It was the teaching of the church.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have stood in front of my own church and told that version, and it is stirring."

**What changed and why.** Mission Sunday, the slides, the slave ships and baggage trains, Dum Diversas (1452), Inter Caetera (1493) and the quoted words of the bull are the essay's. The rewrite puts the pastor in the pulpit on mission Sunday before it says the telling is dishonest, so the indictment starts with him.

## 13. Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant: The Three Families of Christianity

`three-families-of-christianity` · theology · 2473 words

### Current opening

> There is a persistent illusion in the Western world that Christianity is basically one thing with minor variations. Protestants may assume that Catholics are Protestants who added too much. Catholics may assume that Protestants are Catholics who subtracted too much. And both tend to forget that the Orthodox exist at all. This is not merely ignorance. It is the result of a thousand years of geographical, linguistic, and political separation that has produced three distinct civilizations of faith, each with its own intellectual tradition, its own liturgical grammar, its own understanding of how God relates to the world.
>
> To understand Christianity as a single monolithic entity is to misunderstand it completely. The faith that emerged from first-century Palestine splintered (first in 1054, then again in 1517) into three great families, each preserving something essential, each losing something in the separation. The Catholic tradition preserved institutional continuity and sacramental depth. The Orthodox preserved mystical theology and liturgical beauty. The Protestant traditions recovered biblical authority and the radical implications of grace. None of them, on their own, contains the fullness of what the early church possessed.
>
> Before mapping the differences, it is important to establish the common ground, which is far more substantial than most Christians realize. All three families confess the Nicene Creed, the theological statement hammered out in the fourth century that defines the boundaries of Christian orthodoxy. All three affirm the full divinity and full humanity of Jesus Christ, as defined by the Council of Chalcedon in 451. All three recognize the same twenty-seven books of the New Testament as canonical Scripture. All three practice baptism and Eucharist as central rites of the faith. All three confess the Trinity (one God in three persons) as the foundational grammar of Christian theology.

### Proposed opening

There is a persistent illusion in the West that Christianity is basically one thing with minor variations. Protestants tend to assume Catholics are Protestants who added too much. Catholics tend to assume Protestants are Catholics who subtracted too much. Both tend to forget the Orthodox exist. That is not simple ignorance. It is what a thousand years of geographical, linguistic, and political separation produces: three distinct civilizations of faith, each with its own intellectual tradition, its own liturgical grammar, and its own account of how God relates to the world.

I am a Protestant, and I write from inside one of the three, which is the only honest place to write from. To treat Christianity as a single monolith is to misunderstand it completely. The faith that came out of first-century Palestine split, first in 1054 and then again in 1517, into three great families, each keeping something essential and each losing something in the separation. Catholicism kept institutional continuity and sacramental depth. Orthodoxy kept mystical theology and liturgical beauty. The Protestant traditions recovered the authority of Scripture and the radical implications of grace. None of them alone holds everything the early church had, and my own family is no exception.

Before the differences, the common ground, which is larger than most Christians realize. All three families confess the Nicene Creed, the fourth-century statement that marks the boundary of Christian orthodoxy. All three affirm the full divinity and full humanity of Jesus Christ as the Council of Chalcedon defined them in 451. All three receive the same twenty-seven books of the New Testament. All three practice baptism and the Eucharist as the central rites of the faith, and all three confess the Trinity, one God in three persons, as the grammar underneath everything else.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am a Protestant, and I write from inside one of the three, which is the only honest place to write from."; "my own family is no exception"

**What changed and why.** The illusion, the two dates, what each family kept, and the five points of common ground (Nicaea, Chalcedon 451, the twenty-seven books, the two rites, the Trinity) are the essay's. The rewrite has the writer name his own family before describing the others and count it among the ones that lost something.

## 14. The Mystics: Christianity's Best-Kept Secret

`the-christian-mystics` · theology · 2508 words

### Current opening

> Somewhere in the fourteenth century, an anonymous English author sat down and wrote a book about nothing. Not nothing in the nihilistic sense. Nothing in the mystical sense, the disciplined practice of letting go of every thought, image, concept, and idea in order to encounter God in the darkness beyond thought. The book was called The Cloud of Unknowing, and its central instruction was deceptively simple: "Lift up your heart to God with a humble impulse of love, and mean God himself who created you, and not any of his goods." Let go of everything you know about God. Let go of every image you have formed of God. Let go of every theological category, every biblical concept, every spiritual experience. Beneath all of that (beneath the knowing, beneath the images, beneath the concepts) is God himself. And God can be reached not by thinking but by loving. Not by knowing but by unknowing.
>
> This is the mystical tradition of Christianity. It is not a marginal phenomenon, an esoteric curiosity, or a dangerous departure from orthodox faith. It is the tradition's deepest root, the current that runs beneath the surface of doctrine, liturgy, and institutional religion, carrying the faith's most radical claim: that God can be known directly, personally, immediately, in an encounter that exceeds the capacity of language to describe or theology to contain.
>
> And yet, for most Christians in the Western Protestant tradition, the mystics are invisible. They are not taught in Sunday school. They are not quoted from pulpits. They are not included in the canon of voices that shape evangelical faith. The richest, deepest, most experience-saturated dimension of the Christian tradition has been, for most Protestants, a closed book.

### Proposed opening

Somewhere in the fourteenth century an anonymous English author sat down and wrote a book about nothing. Not nothing in the nihilist's sense. Nothing in the mystic's sense: the disciplined practice of setting down every thought, image, and concept in order to meet God in the darkness beyond thought. The book was The Cloud of Unknowing, and its central instruction is almost too simple to take seriously: "Lift up your heart to God with a humble impulse of love, and mean God himself who created you, and not any of his goods." Let go of what you know about God. Let go of the images you have made of him. Beneath the knowing and the images is God himself, and he is reached not by thinking but by loving.

This is the mystical tradition of Christianity, and it is not a curiosity at the edge of the faith or a dangerous departure from it. It is the faith's deepest root, the current that runs under doctrine and liturgy and institution, carrying the tradition's most radical claim: that God can be known directly, personally, immediately, in an encounter that outruns what language can describe or theology can contain.

I am writing as a Protestant pastor, which is to say as a member of the tradition that misplaced them. For most Western Protestants the mystics are invisible. They are not taught in Sunday school. They are not quoted from pulpits, including, for too long, mine. They are not in the canon of voices that forms evangelical faith. The richest and most experience-saturated dimension of the Christian tradition has been, for most of us, a closed book, and this essay is an attempt to open it.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am writing as a Protestant pastor, which is to say as a member of the tradition that misplaced them."; "including, for too long, mine"

**What changed and why.** The Cloud of Unknowing, its century, and its instruction (quoted verbatim) are the essay's, as is the claim that the mystics are invisible to Western Protestants. The rewrite has the Protestant pastor confess that his own pulpit is among the ones that never quoted them.

## 15. A Guide to Every Major Denomination

`guide-to-every-major-denomination` · theology · 3023 words

### Current opening

> The existence of denominations is an embarrassment to many Christians and a stumbling block to many outsiders. "Why can't they just agree?" is the perennial question, and it is not a foolish one. But the assumption behind it (that the differences between denominations are petty or trivial) is almost always wrong. The major denominations exist because they represent genuinely different answers to fundamental questions: What is the church? How does God save? Who has authority to interpret Scripture? How should Christians worship? What is the relationship between faith and works, between individual conviction and communal tradition, between the sovereignty of God and the freedom of the human will?
>
> These are not small questions, and the denominations that have formed around different answers to them are not arbitrary. Each one carries a theological insight that the others need, and each one has blind spots that the others can correct. What follows is a guide to the major Protestant and Protestant-adjacent denominations, not a ranking, not a recommendation, but a map of the terrain, drawn with as much fairness as a single author can manage.
>
> The Baptist tradition is the largest Protestant family in America and one of the most consequential in global Christianity, with roughly 100 million adherents worldwide. Its origins lie in the early seventeenth century, among English Separatists who concluded that the Church of England's practice of baptizing infants was without scriptural warrant. The first identifiable Baptist congregation was established in Amsterdam in 1609 by John Smyth, a former Anglican clergyman, and Thomas Helwys, who returned to England to establish the first Baptist church on English soil.

### Proposed opening
