## 1. Non-Denominational Doesn't Mean No Tradition

`non-denominational-doesnt-mean-no-tradition` · theology · 2003 words

### Current opening

> Walk into the average non-denominational church in America and you will find, with remarkable consistency, the same constellation of elements: a worship band playing contemporary songs projected on screens, a charismatic lead pastor in jeans delivering a topical sermon series, an auditorium designed to feel like a concert venue rather than a sanctuary, a children's ministry modeled on the entertainment industry, and a statement of faith on the website that is broadly evangelical but deliberately vague on the questions that divide denominations. The church will call itself "non-denominational," and it will mean this as a selling point, a declaration of independence from the institutional baggage, the bureaucratic overhead, and the tribal loyalties of denominational life.
>
> But here is what non-denominational churches rarely acknowledge: the constellation of elements described above is itself a tradition. It is a specific, historically situated set of theological commitments, worship practices, and organizational assumptions that can be traced to identifiable sources in the late twentieth century. It is not the absence of tradition. It is a tradition that does not know it is one.
>
> The non-denominational church is a relatively recent phenomenon. As recently as the 1960s, the vast majority of American Protestants belonged to a denomination, and denominational loyalty was a significant part of religious identity. You were a Baptist, a Methodist, a Presbyterian, or a Lutheran, and that affiliation carried theological content, social meaning, and family history.

### Proposed opening

Walk into the average non-denominational church in America and you can predict the room before you find a seat. A band, songs on screens, a lead pastor in jeans working through a topical series, an auditorium built to feel like a venue rather than a sanctuary, a children's wing modeled on the entertainment industry, and a statement of faith on the website that is broadly evangelical and carefully quiet on the questions that divide denominations. The church calls itself non-denominational, and it means the word as a selling point: no institutional baggage, no bureaucratic overhead, no tribal loyalties.

Some of that is true. The old denominational world carried real weight, and plenty of people were glad to set it down. But the room I just described is not the absence of a tradition. It is one. Every element in it has a source you can name, most of them in the last sixty years, and together they form a specific set of theological commitments, worship practices, and organizational assumptions as inherited as anything in a Lutheran hymnal. The difference is that this tradition does not know it is one, and a tradition that does not know it is one cannot examine itself.

I pastor a church with a denomination in its name, so I should say plainly that the sign out front is no guarantee we know our own tradition either. But the non-denominational church is a recent arrival. As late as the 1960s, most American Protestants belonged to a denomination, and the belonging carried theological content, social meaning, and family history. You were a Baptist or a Methodist, a Presbyterian or a Lutheran, and the word told people something about what you believed. What happened to that world, and what replaced it, is the story this essay has to tell before it can ask the harder question: what a church with no name on the door actually teaches.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I pastor a church with a denomination in its name"

**What changed and why.** The essay's facts are untouched. The change is that a Baptist pastor is now in the room, and the mirror turns on his own sign before it turns on anyone else's. The 'sixty years' is the essay's own late-twentieth-century dating.

## 2. Toxic Masculinity in the Pulpit

`toxic-masculinity-in-the-pulpit` · prophetic-justice · 2053 words

### Current opening

> In 2006, Mark Driscoll stood before a conference of pastors and declared that the problem with the American church was that it had become too feminine. The church needed men, he argued, real men, tough men, men who would fight and lead and dominate. He mocked pastors who were gentle, who wept, who preferred diplomacy to confrontation. He described Jesus as a cage fighter, an alpha male, a warrior who would return at the end of days to deliver divine violence. The message was clear: godly manhood was aggressive, confrontational, and unapologetically dominant. Anything else was weakness. Anything else was feminization. Anything else was failure.
>
> Driscoll was not an outlier. He was the loudest voice in a movement that had been building for decades, a movement that defined masculinity in terms of power, authority, and control, and that located these qualities at the center of God's design for men. John Eldredge's Wild at Heart, published in 2001 and eventually selling over four million copies, told men that they were designed to be warriors, adventurers, and rescuers, and that the modern church, with its emphasis on gentleness, servanthood, and emotional vulnerability, had stolen their masculine identity. The Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, founded in 1987, codified a vision of gender in which male leadership and female submission were presented as the eternal, unchangeable order of creation.
>
> The result was a theology of masculinity that bore almost no resemblance to the man at the center of the Christian story. It looked, instead, remarkably like the cultural ideal of the American alpha male (competitive, dominant, emotionally controlled, sexually aggressive) dressed in theological clothing and marketed as God's original design.

### Proposed opening

In 2006, Mark Driscoll told a room full of pastors that the American church had a problem, and the problem was that it had become too feminine. The cure was men: tough men, men who would fight and lead and dominate. He mocked pastors who were gentle, who wept, who would rather talk than fight. He described Jesus as a cage fighter, an alpha male who would return at the end of days to deliver divine violence. The message was not subtle. Godly manhood was aggressive and unapologetically dominant, and anything else was weakness, feminization, failure.

It would be convenient to treat Driscoll as one loud voice, and he was loud. But he was the front man for a movement that had been building for decades. John Eldredge's Wild at Heart, published in 2001, sold more than four million copies telling men they were made to be warriors, adventurers, and rescuers, and that a church preaching gentleness and servanthood had stolen their masculine identity. The Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, founded in 1987, had already codified male leadership and female submission as the unchangeable order of creation. By the time Driscoll stood up, the theology was waiting for him.

I have five sons, and I know how much easier it is to hand a boy a script than to show him a man washing feet. That is the pull of this theology, and we should be honest that many of us felt it. It offered young men a clear part to play in a church that had given them none. What it produced, though, was a picture of manhood that bears almost no resemblance to the man at the center of the Christian story: competitive, dominant, emotionally controlled, sexually aggressive, the American alpha male in theological clothing, marketed as God's original design. This essay is about how that happened, what it cost, and what the man in the Gospels actually looks like.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have five sons, and I know how much easier it is to hand a boy a script than to show him a man washing feet."

**What changed and why.** Driscoll, Eldredge and the Council are the essay's own witnesses, in the essay's own dates. The rewrite concedes what the movement offered young men before it names what it produced, and puts a father of five inside the temptation rather than above it.

## 3. Religious Trauma Is Real

`religious-trauma-is-real` · american-church · 2064 words

### Current opening

> In 2011, a psychologist named Marlene Winell published an article in the magazine of the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies introducing a concept she called Religious Trauma Syndrome (RTS). The term described a set of symptoms she had observed repeatedly in her clinical practice among clients who had been raised in authoritarian religious environments: anxiety, depression, difficulty trusting, cognitive difficulties related to critical thinking and decision-making, a damaged sense of self, problems with social functioning, and struggles with sexuality and emotional expression.
>
> The response from the religious establishment was predictable: dismissal, defensiveness, and hostility. Religious trauma, critics argued, was not a real clinical category. It was an invention of secular psychologists who were hostile to religion. The people who claimed to have been traumatized by their religious upbringing were simply weak, or rebellious, or insufficiently committed. The problem was not the religion. The problem was the person.
>
> This response is itself a textbook example of the dynamic that produces religious trauma: the subordination of individual experience to institutional authority, the silencing of suffering in the name of protecting the institution, and the insistence that any critique of the system is evidence of the critic's spiritual failure. The people who most need to hear that religious trauma is real are often the people most invested in denying it.

### Proposed opening

In 2011, a psychologist named Marlene Winell published an article in the magazine of the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies and gave a name to something she kept meeting in her clinical practice. She called it Religious Trauma Syndrome. The clients had been raised in authoritarian religious environments, and they shared a cluster of symptoms: anxiety, depression, difficulty trusting, trouble with critical thinking and decisions, a damaged sense of self, problems in social life, and struggles with sexuality and emotional expression.

The church's answer was the one you would have predicted. Religious trauma was not a real clinical category. It was an invention of secular psychologists hostile to religion. The people claiming to have been harmed were weak, or rebellious, or never really committed. The problem was not the religion; the problem was the person. Some of that response came from real conviction, and it should be said that a demanding faith does produce ordinary difficulty that is not trauma. But the shape of the reply gave the game away.

Because that reply is the very dynamic that produces the harm: the individual's experience subordinated to the institution's authority, suffering silenced to protect the institution, and any critique of the system treated as proof of the critic's spiritual failure. I came to faith as an adult, from outside, so I did not carry a childhood version of this into the church. But I have pastored people who did, and I have learned that the people who most need to hear that religious trauma is real are often the people most invested in denying it, and that on some days the pastor is one of them.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I came to faith as an adult, from outside, so I did not carry a childhood version of this into the church."; "But I have pastored people who did"

**What changed and why.** Winell, the journal and the symptom list are verbatim from the essay. The rewrite concedes the one true thing in the church's dismissal (a demanding faith produces ordinary difficulty that is not trauma) before showing what the dismissal reveals, and ends with the pastor inside the indictment.

## 4. What Christians Can Learn From Buddhism

`what-christians-can-learn-from-buddhism` · theology · 1852 words

### Current opening

> In 1968, Thomas Merton (Trappist monk, contemplative writer, and one of the most influential spiritual voices of the twentieth century) traveled to Asia to meet with Buddhist monks and teachers. In Bangkok, he delivered a lecture to a conference of monastics. He spoke of the convergences between Christian and Buddhist contemplative practice, the shared commitment to silence, simplicity, and the disciplined emptying of the self. He spoke of what Christians could learn from Buddhism without abandoning their own tradition. Later that day, Merton died, electrocuted by a faulty fan in his hotel room, in circumstances that remain somewhat mysterious. He was fifty-three years old.
>
> Merton's Asian journey was not a departure from his Christian faith. It was a deepening of it. He had spent twenty-seven years in a Trappist monastery, living under a rule of silence, prayer, and manual labor. He had written over sixty books on Christian spirituality, theology, and contemplative practice. He was, by any measure, one of the most deeply rooted Christians of his century. And it was precisely the depth of his rootedness that gave him the freedom to engage Buddhism without fear, to recognize in another tradition the same realities he had encountered in his own, expressed in different language and practiced through different forms.
>
> Merton's example points to a truth that the Western church has been slow to grasp: Christianity and Buddhism, despite their profound metaphysical differences, share a remarkable convergence in their contemplative practices, their diagnosis of the human condition, and their prescriptions for spiritual transformation. The church that dismisses Buddhism as a pagan philosophy or an incompatible worldview is not protecting its faith. It is impoverishing it.

### Proposed opening

In 1968, Thomas Merton traveled to Asia to meet Buddhist monks and teachers. He was a Trappist, a contemplative writer, and one of the most influential spiritual voices of his century. In Bangkok he gave a lecture to a conference of monastics on what Christian and Buddhist contemplatives held in common: silence, simplicity, the disciplined emptying of the self, and what a Christian could learn from Buddhism without leaving his own tradition. Later that day he was dead, electrocuted by a faulty fan in his hotel room, in circumstances that have never been fully explained. He was fifty-three.

It is tempting to read the trip as a departure, a monk drifting east at the end. It was the opposite. Merton had spent twenty-seven years under a rule of silence, prayer, and manual labor, and had written more than sixty books on Christian spirituality and contemplative practice. Few Christians of his century were more deeply rooted, and the rootedness is the point. He could engage Buddhism without fear because he knew what he believed, and he could recognize in another tradition realities he had already met in his own, spoken in a different language and practiced in different forms.

I have to be careful here, because the Western church has two reflexes about Buddhism and both are wrong. One dismisses it as pagan philosophy and locks the door. The other borrows its practices as wellness techniques and never asks what they were for. Merton's example refuses both. Christianity and Buddhism disagree profoundly about God, the self, and what is finally real, and this essay will not pretend otherwise. But they converge in their contemplative practice, in their diagnosis of the human condition, and in their prescriptions for change, and a church that will not look at that convergence is not guarding its faith. It is thinning it.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have to be careful here"

**What changed and why.** Merton's trip, the Bangkok lecture, the twenty-seven years, the sixty books and the manner of his death are all the essay's. The rewrite names the two reflexes the essay later argues against (dismissal, and borrowing without asking) and states the disagreement about God and the self before the convergence, so a skeptic cannot accuse it of blurring the two faiths.

## 5. The Anabaptist Option

`the-anabaptist-option` · theology · 2073 words

### Current opening

> In January 1525, a small group of men gathered in the home of Felix Manz in Zurich. They had been followers of Ulrich Zwingli, the city's reformer, but had grown convinced that Zwingli's reformation had not gone far enough. Zwingli had broken with Rome, but he had not broken with the fundamental assumption that had governed Christianity since Constantine: that the church and the state were bound together, that every citizen was a church member, and that infant baptism was the rite of entry into both civic and spiritual life.
>
> That evening, Conrad Grebel baptized George Blaurock, an adult who had already been baptized as an infant. It was an act of profound theological significance and extraordinary political danger. By rebaptizing an adult, these men were not merely making a statement about the proper subjects of baptism. They were rejecting the entire Christendom model. They were saying that the church was not a territorial institution coextensive with the state but a voluntary community of committed believers. They were saying that faith could not be inherited, imposed, or assumed, it had to be chosen.
>
> For this, they would be hunted, imprisoned, tortured, and killed, not only by Catholics, but by the very Protestants whose reformation they believed they were completing.

### Proposed opening

In January 1525, a handful of men met in Felix Manz's house in Zurich. They had been followers of Ulrich Zwingli, the city's reformer, and had come to believe that his reformation had stopped short. Zwingli had broken with Rome. He had not broken with the assumption that had governed Christianity since Constantine: that church and state were one body, that every citizen was a church member, and that infant baptism was the door into both.

That evening Conrad Grebel baptized George Blaurock, a grown man who had been baptized as an infant. It looks like a small thing, water on an adult, and it was a revolution. To baptize an adult who had already been baptized was to say that the church was not a territory but a people, not everyone born inside a border but those who had chosen, and that faith could not be inherited, imposed, or assumed. For that they would be hunted, imprisoned, tortured, and killed, and not only by Catholics. The Protestants whose reformation they believed they were finishing killed them too.

I am a Baptist pastor. I baptize people who can answer for themselves, and nothing about it feels dangerous. It once was, and the reason it was dangerous is the reason the Anabaptists matter now, when the American church is arguing again about how close to power it should stand. We have mostly kept their baptism and forgotten their politics. This essay is about the people who paid for both, what they got wrong, and why their vision of a church that refuses to be the state is having a renaissance it did not ask for.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am a Baptist pastor. I baptize people who can answer for themselves, and nothing about it feels dangerous."

**What changed and why.** Manz's house, Zwingli, Grebel and Blaurock, January 1525, and the persecution from both sides are the essay's. The rewrite makes believer's baptism the hinge, which it is, and lets a Baptist pastor confess that his tradition kept the baptism and dropped the politics. 'What they got wrong' points at the Munster section the essay already carries.
