## 6. The Rise and Fall of Mainline Protestantism

`the-rise-and-fall-of-mainline-protestantism` · theology · 2619 words

### Current opening

> If you had attended a suburban church in the United States in 1955, there is a strong chance it would have been a mainline Protestant congregation. The minister would have been educated at a reputable seminary. The hymnal would have been vetted by denominational committees. The congregation would have included bankers, schoolteachers, lawyers, and local politicians. The church building itself would have been architecturally substantial, set prominently on a main street. And the theology preached from the pulpit, while perhaps not systematic, would have carried the quiet confidence of a tradition that believed it spoke for the nation.
>
> That world is gone. Not destroyed by persecution or suppressed by the state, but eroded from within and overtaken from without. The story of mainline Protestantism's rise and fall is one of the most consequential narratives in American religious history, and most Americans have never heard it told clearly.
>
> The term "mainline" refers to a cluster of Protestant denominations that dominated American religious and civic life from the colonial era through the mid-twentieth century. The label itself derives from the "Main Line," a stretch of affluent suburbs along the Pennsylvania Railroad west of Philadelphia, where these churches were as much a part of the social furniture as the country clubs and prep schools. The term was not originally theological. It was sociological. These were the churches of the American establishment.

### Proposed opening

If you had gone to church in an American suburb in 1955, the odds are good that you walked into a mainline Protestant congregation. The minister had been trained at a reputable seminary. The hymnal had passed through denominational committees. The pews held bankers, schoolteachers, lawyers, and the town's politicians, and the building stood on a main street, substantial and confident, because the tradition inside it believed it spoke for the nation.

That world is gone. No persecution ended it and no state suppressed it; it eroded from within and was overtaken from without. The rise and fall of mainline Protestantism is one of the most consequential stories in American religious history, and most Americans have never heard it told straight. The temptation is to tell it as a warning about other people, and I have heard it told that way far more often than I have heard it told honestly. This essay tries to tell it honestly, which means the warning will land closer to home than the usual version allows.

The word "mainline" comes from the Main Line, the run of affluent suburbs along the Pennsylvania Railroad west of Philadelphia, where these churches sat beside the country clubs and the prep schools as part of the social furniture. The term was never theological. It was sociological. These were the churches of the American establishment, and you cannot understand what happened to them without holding that fact next to the theology.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have heard it told that way far more often than I have heard it told honestly"

**What changed and why.** The 1955 church, the Main Line suburbs and the Pennsylvania Railroad are the essay's. The rewrite names the way this story is usually told (as a warning about other people) and commits the essay to landing the warning closer to home, which is what its later sections on the conservative critique and the progressive defense do. It does not prejudge why the mainline declined; the essay argues that.

## 7. Faith at Work When Nobody Shares It

`faith-at-work` · devotionals · 1989 words

### Current opening

> The average American will spend roughly 90,000 hours at work over the course of a lifetime. For the Christian who takes vocation seriously, this is not a neutral fact. It is a theological one. The majority of your waking life is spent in a context where your faith is, at best, irrelevant to the people around you and, at worst, a source of suspicion. The question of how to be a Christian at work is not a peripheral concern. It is one of the central questions of the integrated life.
>
> And the church has been remarkably unhelpful in answering it. The dominant models on offer are, roughly, two. The first is the "workplace evangelism" model, which treats the office as a mission field and your coworkers as potential converts. This model produces the person who leaves tracts in the break room, invites colleagues to church every Monday, and manages to reference Jesus in every performance review. The second is the "sacred-secular divide" model, which treats work as a necessary evil, the thing you endure in order to fund the things that actually matter: church, family, ministry. In this model, your work has no intrinsic spiritual significance. It is simply how you pay the bills.
>
> Both models are failures. The first makes you insufferable. The second makes your faith irrelevant. Neither takes seriously the theological claim that lies at the heart of Christian vocation: that all of human life (including the hours spent in meetings, spreadsheets, patient care, construction, teaching, and code) is lived before God and can be lived for God. The integrated life refuses both errors. It insists that the God who cares about your prayer life cares equally about your professional life, and that the two are not separate compartments but a single existence lived under a single lordship.

### Proposed opening

The average American spends about 90,000 hours at work over a lifetime. If you take vocation seriously, that number is not a piece of trivia; it is a theological fact. Most of your waking life happens in a place where your faith is, at best, beside the point to the people around you and, at worst, a reason for suspicion. How to be a Christian at work is not a side question for the integrated life. It is one of the central ones.

My workplace is a church, which makes me the wrong person to write this, and I know it. The building assumes my faith; yours almost certainly does not. That is part of why the church has been so unhelpful here. We have offered two models, and neither survives contact with an actual Monday. The first treats the office as a mission field and every coworker as a prospect: tracts in the break room, an invitation to church every Monday, Jesus worked into a performance review. The second treats work as a necessary evil, the thing you endure to fund what actually matters, church and family and ministry, so that your job has no spiritual weight of its own beyond paying the bills.

The first model makes you insufferable. The second makes your faith irrelevant. Neither takes seriously the claim at the heart of Christian vocation, that all of human life, the meetings and the spreadsheets and the patient care and the construction site and the classroom and the code, is lived before God and can be lived for God. The God who cares about your prayer life cares just as much about your professional one, and they are not two compartments but one existence under one lordship. This essay is about what that looks like on a Tuesday, in a building where nobody else believes it.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "My workplace is a church, which makes me the wrong person to write this, and I know it."; "The building assumes my faith; yours almost certainly does not."

**What changed and why.** The 90,000 hours and the two models (workplace evangelism, the sacred-secular divide) are the essay's. The rewrite starts by admitting the writer's disadvantage, a pastor writing about workplaces that are not churches, which is the honest position and the reason the essay has to earn its advice rather than assume it.

## 8. Spiritual Abuse: How Good Theology Gets Weaponized

`spiritual-abuse-how-good-theology-gets-weaponized` · american-church · 2150 words

### Current opening

> The phrase "spiritual abuse" entered public discourse through the work of David Johnson and Jeff VanVonderen, whose 1991 book The Subtle Power of Spiritual Abuse named and described a phenomenon that millions of Christians had experienced but could not articulate. Johnson and VanVonderen defined spiritual abuse as "the mistreatment of a person who is in need of help, support, or greater spiritual empowerment, with the result of weakening, undermining, or decreasing that person's spiritual empowerment."
>
> The definition is deliberately broad, and its breadth is important. Spiritual abuse is not limited to the obvious extremes, the cult leader who demands total obedience, the pastor who sexually exploits a congregant, the televangelist who defrauds the elderly. These are genuine instances of spiritual abuse, but they are also the cases that the church finds easiest to condemn, precisely because they are so extreme. The more common forms of spiritual abuse are subtler, more pervasive, and more difficult to identify, both for the victims and for the communities in which the abuse occurs.
>
> A pastor who uses the pulpit to shame a member who questioned his authority is engaging in spiritual abuse. An elder board that threatens a family with church discipline for raising concerns about financial transparency is engaging in spiritual abuse. A youth leader who tells a teenager that their doubts are evidence of demonic influence is engaging in spiritual abuse. A worship leader who creates an emotional environment designed to overwhelm critical thinking and produce compliance is engaging in something that, while not typically categorized as abuse, shares its essential features.

### Proposed opening

The phrase "spiritual abuse" reached ordinary Christians through a 1991 book, The Subtle Power of Spiritual Abuse, in which David Johnson and Jeff VanVonderen gave a name to something millions of people had lived through and could not describe. Their definition was deliberately wide: "the mistreatment of a person who is in need of help, support, or greater spiritual empowerment, with the result of weakening, undermining, or decreasing that person's spiritual empowerment." The width matters, and it is the reason the church has found the book easier to admire than to apply.

The abuse we condemn without difficulty is the extreme kind: the cult leader who demands total obedience, the pastor who preys on a congregant, the televangelist who defrauds the elderly. Those are real, and they are also the easy cases, because their distance from us is the point. The common forms are quieter and closer. A pastor who uses a sermon to shame the member who questioned him. An elder board that threatens a family with discipline for asking where the money went. A youth leader who tells a teenager that doubt is a sign of the demonic. A worship set built to overwhelm the mind and produce compliance, which nobody files under abuse and which shares its essential features.

I hold a pulpit, and I know how little effort the first of those takes. That is the uncomfortable thing this essay has to say, and it will say it about my own tribe before anyone else's: spiritual abuse does not require bad theology. It requires only a room in which theology, any theology, including the most orthodox, is used to control people rather than to free them. Good doctrine in the wrong hands is not a safeguard. It is the weapon.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I hold a pulpit, and I know how little effort the first of those takes."

**What changed and why.** Johnson and VanVonderen, 1991, and their definition are quoted verbatim from the essay. The four quiet examples are the essay's own. The rewrite moves the essay's central claim (abuse does not require bad theology) into the opening and has the pastor say it about his own tribe first.

## 9. Digital Discipleship: Spiritual Formation in the Age of Algorithms

`digital-discipleship` · devotionals · 1999 words

### Current opening

> In the year 2007, the average American spent approximately 2.7 hours per day consuming digital media. By 2023, that number had risen to over seven hours. In the space of sixteen years, the screens in our pockets have colonized more than a third of our waking hours. This is not a technological development. It is a spiritual one. Because whatever commands that much of your attention is, functionally, your liturgy. It is forming you. The only question is: into what?
>
> The attention economy, the system in which technology companies compete for the limited resource of human attention in order to sell it to advertisers, is not merely an economic arrangement. It is, in the language of James K.A. Smith, a "liturgy of desire." It shapes what you want, how you see, and who you become. And it does so with a sophistication that would make the most accomplished spiritual director in Christian history weep with envy, because the algorithms that curate your digital experience know more about your desires, fears, and vulnerabilities than your pastor, your spouse, or your therapist.
>
> The church has been remarkably slow to reckon with this. The dominant Christian responses to digital technology have been either uncritical adoption (churches migrating to Instagram, pastors building personal brands on Twitter, youth groups moving to TikTok) or blanket rejection (screen time is bad, social media is evil, delete your accounts). Neither response takes seriously the depth of the challenge. The attention economy is not a tool to be used or a temptation to be resisted. It is an environment that shapes the soul, and the Christian tradition has more resources for understanding that shaping than it has yet deployed. The question is not whether you will be formed by your digital environment. You will be. The question is whether you will be formed intentionally or by default, and the default, as we shall see, is not neutral.

### Proposed opening

In 2007, the average American spent about two hours and forty minutes a day with digital media. By 2023 it was more than seven. In sixteen years the screens in our pockets took more than a third of our waking hours, and it would be a mistake to file that under technology. It is a spiritual fact. Whatever holds that much of your attention is your liturgy, whether or not you would call it one, and a liturgy forms you. The only open question is into what.

The attention economy, in which companies compete for the scarce resource of human attention in order to sell it to advertisers, is not just an arrangement of money. In James K.A. Smith's phrase it is a "liturgy of desire." It shapes what you want, how you see, and who you are becoming, and it does so with a precision the greatest spiritual director in Christian history never had, because the algorithm that curates your feed knows more about your desires, your fears, and your soft places than your pastor, your spouse, or your therapist.

I am not writing this from outside the problem. The phone in my pocket has the same apps as yours, and the church I serve is on the same platforms. That is part of why the church has been so slow here. Our two responses have been adoption without thought, the church on Instagram, the pastor building a brand, the youth group on TikTok, or rejection without thought, screens are bad, delete your accounts. Neither takes the measure of the thing. The attention economy is not a tool to use or a temptation to resist. It is an environment, and it is forming your soul whether you attend to it or not. The Christian tradition knows more about that kind of formation than it has yet put to use, which is what this essay is for.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am not writing this from outside the problem."; "The phone in my pocket has the same apps as yours, and the church I serve is on the same platforms."

**What changed and why.** The 2007 and 2023 figures, the sixteen years, James K.A. Smith's phrase and the two church responses are the essay's. The rewrite keeps the essay's strongest line (whatever holds your attention is your liturgy) and puts the pastor and his church inside the attention economy before it diagnoses anyone else.

## 10. What the Reformation Actually Changed

`what-the-reformation-actually-changed` · theology · 2898 words

### Current opening

> The standard telling of the Reformation begins with a heroic monk nailing his complaints to a church door and ends with the triumph of religious freedom. Like most standard tellings, it is approximately one-third true. Martin Luther did post his 95 Theses (though the nailing-to-the-door part is debated by historians). The Reformation did eventually contribute to the development of religious liberty. But between those two points lies a story of theological genius, political calculation, savage violence, and unintended consequences that reshaped Western civilization in ways the reformers never imagined and would not have endorsed.
>
> To understand what the Reformation actually changed, you have to understand what it was responding to. And to understand that, you have to look squarely at a medieval church that was, by the early sixteenth century, in desperate need of reform, a fact acknowledged not only by the reformers but by many faithful Catholics.
>
> The church of the late Middle Ages was, by almost any honest reckoning, institutionally corrupt. This is not an anti-Catholic statement; it is a historical observation made by Catholic and Protestant historians alike. The papacy had endured the Avignon exile (1309-1377), during which the popes lived in France under the effective control of the French crown. It had survived the Great Western Schism (1378-1417), during which two (and at one point three) men simultaneously claimed to be pope. And by the late fifteenth century, it had produced a series of Renaissance popes whose behavior was difficult to reconcile with any recognizable form of Christian piety.

### Proposed opening

The standard telling of the Reformation begins with a heroic monk nailing his complaints to a church door and ends with the triumph of religious freedom. Like most standard tellings it is about a third true. Luther did post his Ninety-five Theses, though historians still argue about the nailing. The Reformation did, in time, help religious liberty come into being. But between those two points lies a story of theological genius, political calculation, savage violence, and consequences the reformers never intended and would not have endorsed, and it reshaped Western civilization in ways nobody in Wittenberg could have imagined.

I am a Protestant and a pastor, which means I inherited the heroic version and told it from a pulpit before I had checked it. This essay is the checking. To understand what the Reformation actually changed, you have to understand what it was answering, and that means looking squarely at a medieval church that was, by the early sixteenth century, in desperate need of reform. That is not a Protestant slander. The reformers said it, and so did many faithful Catholics.

By almost any honest reckoning the church of the late Middle Ages was institutionally corrupt, and Catholic and Protestant historians say so in nearly the same words. The papacy had lived through the Avignon years, 1309 to 1377, when the popes resided in France under the effective control of the French crown. It had survived the Great Western Schism, 1378 to 1417, when two men, and for a time three, each claimed to be pope. And by the late fifteenth century it had produced a run of Renaissance popes whose conduct was hard to square with any recognizable form of Christian piety. What follows is what happened when a monk in Wittenberg decided that the situation could not stand, and what it cost everyone, including the people who agreed with him.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am a Protestant and a pastor, which means I inherited the heroic version and told it from a pulpit before I had checked it."

**What changed and why.** The standard telling, the debated nailing, Avignon (1309 to 1377), the Great Western Schism (1378 to 1417) and the Renaissance popes are the essay's. The rewrite has a Protestant pastor own the heroic version before correcting it, and frames the corruption of the medieval church the way the essay does: as something the reformers and faithful Catholics both said.
