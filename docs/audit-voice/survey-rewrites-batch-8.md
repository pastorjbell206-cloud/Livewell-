## 36. What Christians Can Learn From Islam

`what-christians-can-learn-from-islam` · theology · 1607 words

### Current opening

> In the popular imagination of Western Christianity, Islam occupies the space reserved for the threat, the civilizational rival, the theological adversary, the religion that replaced Christian persuasion with the sword. The centuries of conflict (the Crusades, the Ottoman wars, the contemporary specter of terrorism) have deposited a residue of fear and hostility so thick that most Western Christians cannot think about Islam without thinking through it. The faith practiced by nearly two billion people, rooted in the same Abrahamic soil as Christianity, oriented toward the same God, honoring many of the same figures (Abraham, Moses, Mary, Jesus) is reduced, in the Christian imagination, to a caricature: violent, oppressive, monolithic, and fundamentally incompatible with Christian truth.
>
> This caricature serves no one. It does not serve Christians, who remain ignorant of a tradition that has much to teach them. It does not serve Muslims, who are perpetually misrepresented by the people who claim to follow a God of truth. And it does not serve the God who, Christians confess, is sovereign over all peoples and all traditions, a God whose work in the world is not confined to the boundaries of Christianity, however uncomfortable that truth may be.
>
> Learning from Islam does not require the abandonment of Christian convictions. It requires the intellectual honesty to recognize that God may have deposited truths in other traditions that Christians have neglected in their own, and the spiritual humility to receive those truths as gifts rather than threats.

### Proposed opening

In the popular imagination of Western Christianity, Islam occupies the place reserved for the threat: the civilizational rival, the theological adversary, the religion that traded persuasion for the sword. Centuries of conflict, the Crusades, the Ottoman wars, the modern specter of terrorism, have left a residue of fear so thick that most Western Christians cannot think about Islam without thinking through it. A faith practiced by nearly two billion people, rooted in the same Abrahamic soil, turned toward the same God, honoring Abraham, Moses, Mary, and Jesus, is reduced to a caricature: violent, oppressive, monolithic, and incompatible with Christian truth.

I have to be careful here, because I preach in a country where most of what people know about Islam they learned from the news, and I learned a good deal of it there too. The caricature serves no one. It does not serve Christians, who stay ignorant of a tradition with much to teach them. It does not serve Muslims, who are misrepresented year after year by people who claim to follow a God of truth. And it does not serve the God who, Christians confess, is sovereign over every people and every tradition, whose work in the world is not fenced by the boundaries of Christianity, however uncomfortable that is to say.

Learning from Islam does not require giving up Christian convictions, and this essay will not pretend the two faiths agree about who Jesus is. It requires the honesty to admit that God may have preserved in another tradition truths Christians have neglected in their own, and the humility to receive those truths as gifts rather than threats. What follows is an attempt at both.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have to be careful here, because I preach in a country where most of what people know about Islam they learned from the news, and I learned a good deal of it there too."

**What changed and why.** The threat image, the three conflicts, the two billion and the four shared figures are the essay's. The rewrite has the preacher admit his own knowledge came from the same place as everyone else's, and names the one disagreement (who Jesus is) the essay's dialogue section keeps in view.

## 37. Science and Faith Are Not at War

`science-and-faith-are-not-at-war` · theology · 2243 words

### Current opening

> The narrative is so deeply embedded in Western culture that it feels like a fact: science and religion have always been at war, and science has been winning. The narrative has its canonical stories (Galileo persecuted by the Inquisition, Darwin demolishing Genesis, Copernicus defying the church) and its implicit moral: as scientific knowledge advances, religious belief retreats, because the two are fundamentally incompatible. One deals in evidence; the other in wishful thinking. One progresses; the other regresses. The choice between them is a choice between reason and superstition.
>
> This narrative is largely false. It is not false because the church has always been friendly to science, it has not. It is false because the relationship between science and faith is far more complex, far more collaborative, and far more interesting than the warfare metaphor allows. The conflict thesis, as historians of science call it, was itself an invention, a nineteenth-century polemical construction that serious historians have spent the last several decades dismantling.
>
> The idea that science and religion are inherently at war was popularized by two books: John William Draper's History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896). Both were enormously influential. Both were also deeply misleading. Draper and White had agendas, Draper was an anti-Catholic polemicist, and White was the president of Cornell University, which he had founded as a secular institution and needed to defend against religious critics. Their books were not dispassionate histories. They were advocacy documents dressed in historical clothing.

### Proposed opening

The story is so deeply set in Western culture that it feels like a fact: science and religion have always been at war, and science has been winning. It has its canonical scenes, Galileo before the Inquisition, Darwin demolishing Genesis, Copernicus defying the church, and its moral is implied in every telling. As knowledge advances, belief retreats, because the two cannot share a room. One deals in evidence, the other in wishing. The choice between them is a choice between reason and superstition.

I believed that story when I was an atheist, and I believed it partly because nobody in the church had given me a reason not to. It is largely false. Not false because the church has always been friendly to science; it has not. False because the actual relationship between science and faith is more complicated, more collaborative, and a great deal more interesting than the warfare picture allows. The conflict thesis, as historians of science call it, was itself an invention, a nineteenth-century piece of polemic that serious historians have spent decades taking apart.

Two books made it. John William Draper's History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896) were enormously influential, and both were deeply misleading. Draper was an anti-Catholic polemicist. White was the president of Cornell, which he had founded as a secular institution and needed to defend against religious critics. These were not dispassionate histories. They were advocacy in historical clothing, and the church has been paying for their success ever since.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I believed that story when I was an atheist, and I believed it partly because nobody in the church had given me a reason not to."

**What changed and why.** The canonical scenes, Draper (1874), White (1896) and Cornell are the essay's. The rewrite lets the convert from atheism say he once believed the warfare story, and puts part of the blame where the essay's later sections put it: on a church that never answered it.

## 38. The Megachurch Model: What Worked, What Didn't, What's Next

`megachurch-model` · prophetic-justice · 1807 words

### Current opening

> In 1975, Bill Hybels rented a movie theater in Palatine, Illinois, and launched a church service designed for people who did not go to church. He had conducted door-to-door surveys in the surrounding suburbs, asking unchurched residents what they disliked about church. Their answers were consistent: it was boring, irrelevant, always asking for money, and made them feel guilty. Hybels built Willow Creek Community Church as the answer to those objections. The music was contemporary. The messages were practical. The atmosphere was welcoming, nonthreatening, and intentionally modeled on the spaces where suburban Americans already felt comfortable, corporate auditoriums, shopping malls, entertainment venues.
>
> It worked. Willow Creek grew to over twenty-five thousand weekly attendees. It became the prototype for a movement. Rick Warren applied similar principles at Saddleback Church in Lake Forest, California, which grew to comparable size and produced The Purpose Driven Life, one of the best-selling nonfiction books in American history. By the early 2000s, the megachurch model (defined as a church with weekly attendance of two thousand or more) had spread across the country. The Hartford Institute for Religion Research counted over seventeen hundred megachurches in the United States by 2020, a number that had more than doubled since 2000.
>
> The megachurch movement was the most significant development in American Protestant Christianity in the second half of the twentieth century. It reshaped the terrain of American religion, the expectations of churchgoers, the economics of ministry, and the culture of pastoral leadership. It did some things remarkably well. It did other things catastrophically badly. And the reckoning that followed has forced a conversation about what the church is actually for, a conversation that is still far from resolved.

### Proposed opening

In 1975 Bill Hybels rented a movie theater in Palatine, Illinois, and started a church service for people who did not go to church. He had gone door to door in the suburbs asking the unchurched what they disliked about church, and the answers were consistent: it was boring, irrelevant, always after money, and it made them feel guilty. Willow Creek Community Church was built as the answer to those objections. Contemporary music, practical messages, a welcoming and unthreatening room modeled on the places suburban Americans already felt at ease, the corporate auditorium, the mall, the entertainment venue.

It worked. Willow Creek grew past twenty-five thousand a week and became the prototype for a movement. Rick Warren applied the same principles at Saddleback in Lake Forest, California, grew to comparable size, and wrote The Purpose Driven Life, one of the best-selling nonfiction books in American history. By the early 2000s the megachurch model, a church of two thousand or more a week, had spread across the country; the Hartford Institute for Religion Research counted more than seventeen hundred of them by 2020, more than double the number in 2000.

I pastor a church that will never be one, and I have borrowed more from the model than I would like to admit, which is the honest place to start. The megachurch movement was the most significant development in American Protestant life in the second half of the twentieth century. It reshaped the terrain of American religion, what churchgoers expect, the economics of ministry, and the culture of pastoral leadership. It did some things remarkably well and other things catastrophically badly, and the reckoning that followed has forced a question the church is still not done answering: what is a church actually for?

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I pastor a church that will never be one, and I have borrowed more from the model than I would like to admit, which is the honest place to start."

**What changed and why.** Hybels, Palatine, the surveys, Willow Creek's numbers, Warren and Saddleback, and the Hartford count are the essay's. The rewrite has the pastor of an ordinary church admit what he borrowed before the essay weighs what the model got right and wrong.

## 39. The Great Schism: When One Church Became Two

`the-great-schism` · theology · 2182 words

### Current opening

> Most Western Christians, if they think about Eastern Orthodoxy at all, think of it as a quaint and peripheral tradition, incense-heavy liturgies, icon-laden churches, bearded priests in unfamiliar vestments. This is a failure of historical imagination on a grand scale. For the first thousand years of Christian history, the center of theological gravity was not Rome but the East: Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. The great creeds were written in Greek. The great councils met in Asia Minor. The great theologians (Athanasius, the Cappadocians, John Chrysostom, Maximus the Confessor) wrote and thought in the intellectual world of the Eastern Mediterranean.
>
> When the church split in 1054, it was not a case of a small Eastern faction breaking away from the main body. It was the fracture of Christendom itself, the severing of two ancient and equally legitimate traditions that had been growing apart for centuries. Understanding how it happened requires attention to theology, politics, language, and culture. And understanding why it matters requires a willingness to take seriously a form of Christianity that the Western world has spent a millennium ignoring.
>
> The early church organized itself around five great centers, each presided over by a patriarch: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. This structure, known as the pentarchy, was not a hierarchy in the modern sense. The bishop of Rome was acknowledged as holding a "primacy of honor" (first among equals (primus inter pares)) but this was understood differently in East and West. Rome interpreted its primacy as jurisdictional: the pope had actual authority over the whole church. The East interpreted it as ceremonial: the pope was honored for his see's apostolic connection to Peter and Paul, but he did not govern the other patriarchates.

### Proposed opening

Most Western Christians, if they think of Eastern Orthodoxy at all, think of something quaint and peripheral: incense, icons, bearded priests in unfamiliar vestments. That is a failure of historical imagination on a grand scale. For the first thousand years of Christian history the center of theological gravity was not Rome but the East, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem. The great creeds were written in Greek. The great councils met in Asia Minor. The great theologians, Athanasius, the Cappadocians, John Chrysostom, Maximus the Confessor, thought and wrote in the world of the Eastern Mediterranean.

I write as a Western Christian, which means I write from the side that did the ignoring. When the church split in 1054 it was not a small Eastern faction breaking off from the main body. It was the fracture of Christendom itself, the severing of two ancient and equally legitimate traditions that had been drifting apart for centuries. How it happened takes theology, politics, language, and culture to explain. Why it matters takes something harder: a willingness to take seriously a form of Christianity the West has spent a thousand years not looking at.

The early church arranged itself around five great centers, each under a patriarch: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. The arrangement, the pentarchy, was not a hierarchy in the modern sense. The bishop of Rome held a "primacy of honor," first among equals, primus inter pares, but East and West understood that phrase differently, and the difference would break the church. Rome read its primacy as jurisdiction: the pope had real authority over the whole church. The East read it as ceremony: the pope was honored for his see's link to Peter and Paul, and he did not govern the other patriarchates.

**What changed and why.** The four Eastern centers, the Greek creeds, the named theologians, 1054, the pentarchy and primus inter pares are the essay's. The rewrite places the writer on the Western side and lets that be the confession.

## 40. Can You Trust the Bible?

`can-you-trust-the-bible` · theology · 2176 words

### Current opening

> The question "Can you trust the Bible?" is asked by two very different groups of people, and they mean two very different things by it. The skeptic means: Is this ancient collection of texts reliable in any meaningful sense, or is it a repository of myths, contradictions, and outdated moral frameworks that no educated person should take seriously? The anxious believer means: Can I still hold on to this book that shaped my faith, given everything I have learned about its composition, its history, its internal tensions, and the uses to which it has been put?
>
> Both questions deserve honest answers. But honest answers require that we first establish what the Bible actually is, not the Bible of popular imagination, whether that imagination is fundamentalist or secularist, but the Bible as it actually exists: a sprawling, multi-voiced, internally diverse collection of texts written across more than a millennium by dozens of authors in multiple languages and genres, compiled and canonized through a centuries-long process of communal discernment, and read in wildly different ways by wildly different communities for two thousand years.
>
> The first step toward trusting the Bible is to stop expecting it to be something it never claimed to be.

### Proposed opening

"Can you trust the Bible?" is asked by two very different kinds of people, and they mean two very different things. The skeptic means: is this ancient pile of texts reliable in any meaningful sense, or is it a warehouse of myths, contradictions, and outdated morals that no educated person should take seriously? The anxious believer means: can I still hold on to the book that made my faith, now that I know what I know about how it was written, how it was assembled, where it argues with itself, and what it has been used to justify?

I asked the first question before I asked the second. I came to the Bible as an atheist, so the skeptic's version was mine first, and the believer's version came later and did not cancel it. Both deserve honest answers. But honest answers require knowing what the Bible actually is, not the Bible of popular imagination, fundamentalist or secularist, but the one that exists: a sprawling, many-voiced, internally diverse collection written across more than a thousand years by dozens of authors in several languages and genres, compiled and canonized through centuries of communal discernment, and read in wildly different ways by wildly different communities for two thousand years.

The first step toward trusting the Bible is to stop expecting it to be something it never claimed to be. Most of the trust that gets lost was placed in that other book, the one that does not exist, and this essay is about the one that does.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I asked the first question before I asked the second."; "I came to the Bible as an atheist, so the skeptic's version was mine first, and the believer's version came later and did not cancel it."

**What changed and why.** The two askers and the description of the Bible as it exists are the essay's. The rewrite has the convert say he asked the skeptic's question first, which is the reason the essay can address both askers without condescending to either.
