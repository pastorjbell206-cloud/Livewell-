## 51. Why the Church Lost the Culture War

`why-the-church-lost-the-culture-war` · prophetic-justice · 1634 words

### Current opening

> In 1979, Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority with an explicit political objective: to mobilize conservative Christians as a voting bloc capable of shaping American law and policy according to biblical principles. The strategy was clear, the coalition was large, and the early results were impressive. Ronald Reagan was elected in 1980 with overwhelming evangelical support. The religious right became a permanent fixture of Republican politics. For a generation, white evangelical Christians wielded disproportionate influence over judicial appointments, social policy, and the terms of public moral discourse.
>
> By any conventional political measure, the movement succeeded. It elected presidents. It shaped a Supreme Court. It passed legislation. It built institutions (think tanks, media networks, lobbying organizations, legal defense funds) that gave conservative Christianity a permanent infrastructure of political power.
>
> And yet. By the third decade of the twenty-first century, the culture war strategy had produced the opposite of what its architects intended. Public support for same-sex marriage went from 27 percent in 1996 to 71 percent in 2023. Church membership fell below 50 percent for the first time in recorded history. The religiously unaffiliated (the "nones") became the fastest-growing religious demographic in America. Among adults under thirty, nearly four in ten claimed no religious affiliation at all. The word "evangelical" became, for a majority of Americans, a political identity rather than a spiritual one. And the generation most alienated from the church was precisely the generation the culture war was supposed to protect.

### Proposed opening

The strategy was clear, the coalition was large, and the early returns were impressive. Ronald Reagan was elected in 1980 with overwhelming evangelical support. The religious right became a permanent part of Republican politics, and for a generation white evangelicals held outsized influence over judicial appointments, social policy, and the terms of public moral argument.

It built the think tanks, media networks, lobbying groups, and legal defense funds that gave conservative Christianity a permanent infrastructure of power. I am part of the tradition that ran this strategy, and its losses are mine to count, which is why what follows is not written from the other side of the aisle.

And yet. By the third decade of the twenty-first century the culture war had produced the opposite of what its architects intended. Public support for same-sex marriage went from 27 percent in 1996 to 71 percent in 2023. Church membership fell below half the population for the first time on record. The religiously unaffiliated became the fastest-growing religious group in America, and among adults under thirty nearly four in ten claimed no affiliation at all. "Evangelical" became, for most Americans, a political word rather than a spiritual one. And the generation most alienated from the church was exactly the generation the war was supposed to protect. The church did not just lose. It lost the one thing it could not afford to lose, the capacity to be heard.

## 53. The Prosperity Gospel Is Not the Gospel

`prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel` · american-church · 2199 words

### Current opening

>
> The investigation produced no legislative action. It is also, by any rigorous theological standard, a heresy. Not a minor doctrinal variation, not a difference of emphasis, not a matter of legitimate theological disagreement, but a fundamental distortion of the Christian message that inverts the gospel's priorities, exploits the vulnerable, and blasphemes the character of God.
>
> The prosperity gospel did not emerge from the mainstream of Christian theological reflection. Its intellectual roots lie in the New Thought movement of the nineteenth century, a loose collection of metaphysical teachings that emphasized the power of the mind to shape material reality. Phineas Quimby, a New England clockmaker and mesmerist, developed a system of "mental healing" that attributed disease and poverty to incorrect thinking. His ideas influenced Mary Baker Eddy (who founded Christian Science) and, through a chain of transmission, the Pentecostal healing evangelists of the twentieth century.

## 54. What Calvinism and Arminianism Actually Argue About

`calvinism-and-arminianism` · theology · 2492 words

### Current opening

> No theological debate in Protestant history has generated more heat, less light, and more caricature than the dispute between Calvinism and Arminianism. On the internet and in church lobbies, the debate is often reduced to bumper-sticker slogans: "God chooses, we don't" versus "God offers, we choose." Calvinists are accused of worshipping a tyrant who damns people arbitrarily. Arminians are accused of worshipping a weak God who depends on human cooperation. Both accusations are wrong, and both reveal how poorly the actual positions are understood.
>
> The real debate is sophisticated, biblical, and (at its best) conducted between people who agree on far more than they disagree. Both sides affirm the authority of Scripture. Both confess the Trinity and the full deity and humanity of Christ. Both believe that salvation is by grace through faith. Both affirm that human beings are sinful and incapable of saving themselves. Both believe in the necessity of the atonement. The disagreement is not about whether God saves, but about the mechanics of how, and specifically, about the relationship between divine sovereignty and human freedom in the process of salvation.
>
> The debate did not begin with John Calvin. It began, in its Protestant form, with Jacob Arminius (1560-1609), a Dutch Reformed theologian who was trained in Calvinist theology at the University of Geneva under Calvin's successor, Theodore Beza. Arminius was assigned to refute the teachings of Dirk Volckertszoon Coornhert, a Dutch layman who objected to the Calvinist doctrine of predestination. But the more Arminius studied the question, the more he found himself agreeing with Coornhert's objections. He came to believe that the Reformed understanding of predestination, which taught that God, before the foundation of the world, unconditionally chose some individuals for salvation and passed over the rest, made God the author of sin and rendered human responsibility meaningless.

## 55. Raising Kids in a Post-Christian Culture

### Current opening

> There is a moment every Christian parent dreads, though few will admit it publicly. >
> For most of American history, Christian parents operated within a supporting ecosystem. The culture reinforced what parents taught at home. Schools observed Christian holidays. Neighbors went to church. Television programming, for all its flaws, operated within broadly Christian moral assumptions. The culture was the water, and Christianity was the current.

Your children's friends, by statistical probability, include a significant number who have no religious framework at all. Their teachers may be agnostic. Their coaches may be atheist. The cultural voices they consume on YouTube, TikTok, and podcasts are overwhelmingly secular. Christianity is no longer the current. It is a tributary, and a shrinking one.

## 56. When Your Family Thinks You've Lost Your Mind

### Current opening

She heard from your sister that you have stopped attending the church you were raised in. Or she noticed that you did not bow your head at Thanksgiving dinner. Or your father found a book on your shelf (Ehrman, or Enns, or Zahnd) that signals a departure from the theological world they inhabit. The conversation that follows is one of the most painful in the human repertoire, because it is a conversation in which the people who love you most become convinced that you are in danger, and the danger they perceive is your own thinking.
>
> Faith transitions (the shifts in belief, practice, affiliation, or theological framework that happen throughout the course of a human life) are among the most common and least discussed sources of family conflict in American culture. The reason they are so volatile is that they touch something deeper than opinion. For the family that has organized its identity around a shared faith, a member's departure from that faith is not merely a change of mind. It is experienced as a betrayal, a death, a rejection of the family itself. The parents who raised you in the faith hear "I no longer believe what you taught me" as "I no longer value what you gave me." The translation is incorrect, but the pain is real.
>
> The most common version of this conflict runs downhill, from parents to adult children. The parents hold a faith they received from their own parents and transmitted, at considerable cost and effort, to the next generation. They chose a church, enrolled their children in Sunday school, said grace before meals, prayed at bedtime, sacrificed vacations for mission trips, and organized their entire family life around a set of convictions they regarded as the most important thing they could pass on. And now their adult child has come home and announced (or more commonly, gradually revealed through a thousand small signals) that those convictions are no longer theirs.
