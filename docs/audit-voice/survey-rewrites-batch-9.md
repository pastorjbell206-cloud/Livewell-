## 41. The Sexual Abuse Crisis: A Reckoning the Church Cannot Avoid

`sexual-abuse-crisis-in-the-church` · prophetic-justice · 1924 words

### Current opening

> In January 2002, the Boston Globe's Spotlight team published the first of a series of investigative reports documenting the systematic sexual abuse of children by Catholic priests in the Archdiocese of Boston. The reporting revealed that Cardinal Bernard Law and his predecessors had known about the abuse for decades. They had not merely failed to stop it. They had actively enabled it, transferring accused priests to new parishes, suppressing complaints, pressuring victims into silence, deploying church lawyers to intimidate families who came forward. The institution had chosen its reputation over its children.
>
> The Boston reporting was not the beginning. Allegations had surfaced for years, in Louisiana in the 1980s, in documents uncovered by attorney Jeff Anderson, in the courageous testimonies of survivors who were dismissed as liars or opportunists. But the Globe's investigation achieved what individual complaints had not: it established a pattern. Not isolated cases. Not a few bad actors. A system. A system designed, at every level, to protect the institution at the expense of the vulnerable.
>
> What followed was a global reckoning. Grand jury reports in Pennsylvania documented over a thousand child victims and three hundred predator priests across six dioceses, spanning seven decades. Investigations in Ireland, Australia, Germany, Chile, and dozens of other countries revealed the same architecture of abuse and concealment. The case of Theodore McCarrick (a cardinal, one of the most powerful figures in American Catholicism) demonstrated that the rot reached the highest levels of church leadership. McCarrick had abused seminarians and minors for decades while ascending to positions of extraordinary influence. People knew. The institution knew. It advanced him anyway.

### Proposed opening

In January 2002 the Boston Globe's Spotlight team published the first of a series of reports documenting the systematic sexual abuse of children by Catholic priests in the Archdiocese of Boston. Cardinal Bernard Law and his predecessors had known for decades. They had not merely failed to stop it. They had enabled it: moving accused priests to new parishes, burying complaints, pressing victims into silence, sending church lawyers after the families who came forward. The institution had chosen its reputation over its children.

Boston was not the beginning. Allegations had surfaced for years, in Louisiana in the 1980s, in documents uncovered by the attorney Jeff Anderson, in the testimony of survivors who were called liars and opportunists. What the Globe did that individual complaints could not was establish a pattern. Not isolated cases, not a few bad men, but a system, built at every level to protect the institution at the expense of the vulnerable. I am a Protestant pastor, and I watched that story the way most of us did, from what felt like a safe distance. The section of this essay I least wanted to write is the one about my own house.

What followed was a global reckoning. Grand jury reports in Pennsylvania documented more than a thousand child victims and three hundred predator priests across six dioceses and seven decades. Investigations in Ireland, Australia, Germany, Chile, and dozens of other countries found the same architecture of abuse and concealment. Theodore McCarrick, a cardinal and one of the most powerful men in American Catholicism, had abused seminarians and minors for decades while rising to positions of extraordinary influence. People knew. The institution knew. It advanced him anyway. And the Protestant churches that watched all this with a mixture of horror and relief were about to learn that the architecture was not Catholic. It was institutional.

## 42. Interfaith Marriage: When You Love Someone Who Doesn't Believe

`interfaith-marriage` · devotionals · 2494 words

### Current opening

> You did not plan for this. Nobody sits down in their twenties and thinks, "I would like to spend my life negotiating the most fundamental questions of existence with someone who disagrees with me about all of them." And yet here you are. You love someone whose understanding of God, the universe, meaning, death, and the moral structure of reality differs from yours at the foundation. Or you are that person, the one who does not believe, married to someone for whom faith is not a preference but an identity. Either way, you are standing on the most intimate fault line in human experience, and there is no marriage manual that adequately prepares you for it.
>
> The prevalence of interfaith marriage in America has increased dramatically. Pew Research Center data from 2015 showed that 39 percent of Americans who married since 2010 had a spouse of a different religious tradition or no tradition at all. Among those who married before 1960, the figure was only 19 percent. The sociologist Robert Putnam, in American Grace, documented the same trend and noted its most significant driver: as religious affiliation has declined, the pool of potential partners who share any given faith tradition has shrunk correspondingly. Interfaith marriage is not a lifestyle choice. It is a demographic inevitability.
>
> The church has traditionally responded to this reality with a single word: don't. Do not marry outside the faith. The end. This counsel is not wrong in its theological instinct, there are genuine, serious challenges to building a life with someone who does not share your deepest convictions. But as pastoral counsel, it is catastrophically insufficient. It does nothing for the millions of Christians who are already in interfaith marriages. It does nothing for the person who fell in love before they came to faith, or who married a believer who later lost their belief, or who simply fell in love with someone they were not supposed to fall in love with. Real pastoral wisdom begins where the clean categories end.

### Proposed opening

You did not plan for this. Nobody sits down in their twenties and decides to spend a life negotiating the deepest questions of existence with someone who disagrees about all of them. And yet here you are. You love someone whose understanding of God, the universe, meaning, death, and the moral shape of reality differs from yours at the foundation. Or you are that person, the one who does not believe, married to someone for whom faith is not a preference but an identity. Either way you are standing on the most intimate fault line in human experience, and no marriage book prepared you for it.

You are not unusual. Pew Research Center data from 2015 found that 39 percent of Americans married since 2010 had a spouse from a different religious tradition or from none; among those married before 1960 the figure was 19 percent. The sociologist Robert Putnam, in American Grace, traced the same trend to its plainest cause: as religious affiliation declines, the pool of partners who share any given faith shrinks with it. Interfaith marriage is not a lifestyle choice. It is a demographic fact.

The church's traditional answer has been one word: don't. Do not marry outside the faith, and that is the end of it. The instinct is not wrong; there are real, serious difficulties in building a life with someone who does not share your deepest convictions. But as pastoral counsel it is catastrophically thin. I have sat with couples on both sides of this line, and the sermon they were given beforehand was rarely any use to them afterward. It does nothing for the millions of Christians already in such marriages, for the person who fell in love before coming to faith, for the one whose believing spouse stopped believing, or for the one who simply fell in love with someone they were not supposed to. Real pastoral wisdom begins where the clean categories end.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have sat with couples on both sides of this line, and the sermon they were given beforehand was rarely any use to them afterward."

**What changed and why.** The second-person address, the Pew figures, Putnam's American Grace and the church's one-word counsel are the essay's. The rewrite adds the pastor who has counseled these couples and found the pre-marriage sermon useless afterward, which is the essay's own judgment on it.

## 43. How the Church Handles Mental Health (Badly)

`church-and-mental-health` · prophetic-justice · 2044 words

### Current opening

> In 2013, Matthew Warren (the twenty-seven-year-old son of Rick Warren, pastor of Saddleback Church and author of The Purpose Driven Life) died by suicide after a lifelong struggle with mental illness. Rick Warren, one of the most influential pastors in America, a man whose book had sold over fifty million copies and whose church had helped shape the theology and practice of a generation of evangelical Christianity, could not save his own son. Not because he lacked faith. Not because he lacked resources. Not because he failed to pray. Because mental illness is an illness, and prayer, however essential to the life of faith, is not a substitute for clinical treatment.
>
> Warren's tragedy forced an unwelcome confrontation with a reality that the American church has spent decades avoiding: the church's dominant framework for understanding mental health is not merely inadequate. It is harmful. It has caused measurable damage to real people. It has driven the suffering away from the one community that claims, as its founding mission, to welcome the broken and bind their wounds.
>
> The church has a mental health problem. Not merely in the sense that its members struggle with mental illness, though they do, at rates comparable to the general population. The church has a mental health problem in the sense that its theology, its culture, and its institutional practices have made it one of the worst places in American life for a person struggling with mental illness to seek help.

### Proposed opening

In 2013 Matthew Warren, the twenty-seven-year-old son of Rick Warren, pastor of Saddleback Church and author of The Purpose Driven Life, died by suicide after a lifelong struggle with mental illness. His father was one of the most influential pastors in America, a man whose book had sold more than fifty million copies and whose church had helped shape a generation of evangelical practice, and he could not save his son. Not for lack of faith. Not for lack of resources. Not for lack of prayer. Because mental illness is an illness, and prayer, essential as it is to the life of faith, is not a substitute for treatment.

I am a pastor, and the framework this essay indicts is one I was handed and, for a while, one I handed on. Warren's grief forced a confrontation the American church had spent decades avoiding: its dominant way of understanding mental health is not merely inadequate. It is harmful. It has done measurable damage to real people, and it has driven the suffering away from the one community that claims, as its founding purpose, to welcome the broken and bind their wounds.

The church has a mental health problem. Not only in the sense that its members struggle with mental illness, though they do, at rates comparable to everyone else. The church has a mental health problem in the sense that its theology, its culture, and its habits have made it one of the worst places in American life for a person in that struggle to look for help. This essay is about how that happened and what a church that took the wounded seriously would do instead.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am a pastor, and the framework this essay indicts is one I was handed and, for a while, one I handed on."

**What changed and why.** Matthew Warren, 2013, Rick Warren and the fifty million copies are the essay's, and the opening keeps the essay's care about how it speaks of his death. The rewrite has the pastor confess he inherited and passed on the framework before the essay names the damage.

## 44. What Christians Can Learn From Indigenous Spirituality

`what-christians-can-learn-from-indigenous-spirituality` · theology · 1859 words

### Current opening

> In the opening chapters of Genesis, God places the first humans in a garden and gives them a task: tend it. The Hebrew word is shamar, to keep, to guard, to watch over. The human relationship to the earth, in the Bible's own framing, is not ownership but stewardship. Not dominion in the sense of domination but care in the sense of guardianship. The earth belongs to God. Humans are its caretakers, charged with preserving what they have been given.
>
> This theology is clear in the text. It is almost entirely absent from the practice of Western Christianity. The Christian West (shaped by the Enlightenment, the Industrial Revolution, and the capitalist logic of extraction and consumption) has treated the earth not as a garden to be tended but as a resource to be exploited. Forests have been clear-cut, rivers poisoned, species driven to extinction, and the climate itself destabilized, all within cultures that claimed the Bible as their foundational text. The disconnect between the theology and the practice is staggering.
>
> Indigenous spiritual traditions, across the globe and across their enormous diversity, have preserved what Western Christianity forgot: that the earth is sacred, that humans are part of the creation rather than above it, that the land has a voice and the creatures have a dignity that human ambition does not override. The church that destroyed these traditions in the name of evangelization silenced the very voices that were saying what the Bible itself says. The irony is painful. The cost is incalculable.

### Proposed opening

In the opening chapters of Genesis, God puts the first humans in a garden and gives them a task: tend it. The Hebrew word is shamar, to keep, to guard, to watch over. In the Bible's own framing the human relationship to the earth is not ownership but stewardship, not dominion as domination but care as guardianship. The earth belongs to God. Humans are its keepers, charged with preserving what they were given.

The theology is plain in the text and almost entirely absent from the practice of Western Christianity. Shaped by the Enlightenment, the Industrial Revolution, and the capitalist logic of extraction, the Christian West has treated the earth not as a garden to tend but as a resource to spend. Forests clear-cut, rivers poisoned, species driven to extinction, the climate itself destabilized, all inside cultures that claimed the Bible as their foundation. The gap between what the book says and what its readers did is staggering, and I write as a member of the church that opened it, which is the only honest place to begin.

Indigenous spiritual traditions, across the world and across their enormous variety, kept what Western Christianity forgot: that the earth is sacred, that humans belong to the creation rather than stand above it, that the land has a voice and the creatures a dignity that human ambition does not override. The church that destroyed those traditions in the name of evangelization silenced the very voices that were saying what its own Scripture says. The irony is painful and the cost is beyond counting, and this essay is an attempt to listen late to what should have been heard first.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I write as a member of the church that opened it, which is the only honest place to begin."

**What changed and why.** Genesis and shamar, the three forces, the four harms and the silenced traditions are the essay's. The rewrite places the writer inside the Western church that opened the gap between text and practice, and makes no claim about his own heritage.
