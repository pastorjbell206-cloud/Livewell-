## 16. Purity Culture and Its Wreckage

`purity-culture-and-its-wreckage` · american-church · 2216 words

### Current opening

> In 1993, the Southern Baptist Convention launched a campaign called "True Love Waits." Teenagers were invited to sign pledge cards committing to sexual abstinence until marriage. The cards were collected, stacked, and displayed in public spectacles designed to demonstrate the movement's scale: 210,000 cards staked on the National Mall in Washington, D.C., 350,000 more displayed at subsequent events. The campaign spread to churches across the evangelical world, spawning an entire industry of conferences, curricula, rings, t-shirts, and branded merchandise built around a single message: your sexual purity is the most important thing about you, and if you lose it, you have lost something that can never be recovered.
>
> Four years later, Joshua Harris, a homeschooled Christian who had written it at twenty-one, published I Kissed Dating Goodbye, a book that extended the purity framework beyond sex to encompass all romantic relationships. Harris argued that the modern dating system was fundamentally broken, that emotional attachments before marriage were spiritually dangerous, and that the godly alternative was "courtship", a parent-supervised process in which romantic relationships were undertaken only with the explicit intention of marriage. The book sold over a million copies and became a foundational text for a generation of young evangelicals.
>
> These two phenomena (True Love Waits and Harris's book) were the most visible expressions of what has come to be called "purity culture," a broad movement within American evangelicalism that placed sexual behavior at the center of Christian identity and made virginity the primary marker of spiritual faithfulness. The movement was enormous in its reach, deeply formative in its effects, and, as a growing body of testimony and research now makes clear, profoundly damaging in its consequences.

### Proposed opening

In 1993 the Southern Baptist Convention launched a campaign called True Love Waits. Teenagers signed pledge cards promising sexual abstinence until marriage, and the cards were collected, stacked, and put on display to prove the movement's size: 210,000 of them staked into the National Mall in Washington, 350,000 more at later events. The campaign spread through the evangelical world and grew an industry of conferences, curricula, rings, and t-shirts around one message: your sexual purity is the most important thing about you, and if you lose it you have lost something that cannot be recovered.

Four years later Joshua Harris, homeschooled and twenty-one when he wrote it, published I Kissed Dating Goodbye, which carried the purity framework past sex into every romantic attachment. Dating was broken, he argued; emotional attachment before marriage was spiritually dangerous; the godly alternative was courtship, supervised by parents and undertaken only with marriage in view. The book sold more than a million copies and became a founding text for a generation of young evangelicals.

I came to the faith as an adult, so the pledge cards were never put in front of me. I am raising five sons inside the church, so whatever replaced them will be. That is why I cannot write about this from a distance. True Love Waits and Harris's book were the visible face of what came to be called purity culture, a movement across American evangelicalism that put sexual behavior at the center of Christian identity and made virginity the chief marker of faithfulness. It was enormous, it was formative, and, as a growing body of testimony and research now shows, it did profound damage. The churches that built it have not finished admitting that, and I am not writing from outside them.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I came to the faith as an adult, so the pledge cards were never put in front of me."; "I am raising five sons inside the church, so whatever replaced them will be."; "I am not writing from outside them"

**What changed and why.** True Love Waits (1993), the card counts, Harris's book and its sales are the essay's. The rewrite puts a father of five who missed purity culture as a teenager inside the churches that built it, and ends the opening where the essay's argument begins: the churches have not finished admitting the damage.

## 17. The Spirituality of Doubt

`the-spirituality-of-doubt` · devotionals · 2067 words

### Current opening

> The assumption that faith and doubt are opposites (that to believe is to be certain, and to question is to fail) is one of the most damaging ideas in the history of Christianity. It has driven millions from the church, not because they lacked faith but because they had been taught that the experience of doubt was evidence that their faith was defective. The equation is simple, devastating, and wrong: if you doubt, you do not truly believe; if you do not truly believe, you are not truly saved; therefore doubt is not merely an intellectual experience but a spiritual emergency. Under this framework, the questioning mind is not a gift. It is a threat. And the church's response to threat has historically been suppression.
>
> But this equation has no support in Scripture, no precedent in the deepest streams of Christian tradition, and no correspondence with the actual experience of the saints. The biblical narrative is, from beginning to end, a record of people who believed and doubted simultaneously, who trusted God and argued with God in the same breath, who obeyed and protested, who worshipped and wept. The flattening of faith into certainty is a modern invention, a product of the Enlightenment's demand that all knowledge conform to the standard of mathematical proof. It is not a Christian idea. It is a secular one, baptized by fundamentalism and passed off as orthodoxy.
>
> Abraham is the paradigmatic figure of faith in the Jewish and Christian traditions, the man who "believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, cited by Paul in Romans 4:3). And yet this same Abraham, having received God's promise of descendants as numerous as the stars, laughed at the absurdity of the promise (Genesis 17:17) and conspired with Sarah to produce an heir through Hagar because he apparently did not trust God to deliver on the original commitment (Genesis 16). Abraham's faith was not the absence of doubt. It was the decision to act on the promise despite the doubt. The believing and the laughing happened in the same person, at the same time.

### Proposed opening

The idea that faith and doubt are opposites, that to believe is to be certain and to question is to fail, is one of the most damaging ideas in the history of Christianity. It has driven millions out of the church, not because they lacked faith but because they had been taught that the experience of doubt proved their faith defective. The equation is simple and devastating: if you doubt, you do not truly believe; if you do not truly believe, you are not truly saved; so doubt is not a thought but an emergency. Under that rule the questioning mind is a threat, and the church's answer to a threat has usually been to suppress it.

I came to faith from atheism, which means I arrived carrying more doubt than most people in the room, and nobody could tell me it disqualified me, because it was the door I came in through. That is not a special case. The equation has no support in Scripture, no precedent in the deepest streams of the tradition, and no match in the actual lives of the saints. From beginning to end the Bible is a record of people who believed and doubted at the same time, who trusted God and argued with him in the same breath, who obeyed and protested, worshipped and wept. The flattening of faith into certainty is a modern invention, born of the Enlightenment's demand that all knowledge look like mathematical proof. It is not a Christian idea. It is a secular one that fundamentalism baptized and passed off as orthodoxy.

Take Abraham, the figure of faith in both the Jewish and the Christian traditions, the man who "believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, the verse Paul reaches for in Romans 4:3). The same Abraham, having been promised descendants as numerous as the stars, laughed at the absurdity of it (Genesis 17:17) and conspired with Sarah to produce an heir through Hagar because he apparently did not trust God to keep the original promise (Genesis 16). His faith was not the absence of doubt. It was the decision to act on the promise in spite of it. The believing and the laughing happened in one man at one time.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I came to faith from atheism, which means I arrived carrying more doubt than most people in the room, and nobody could tell me it disqualified me, because it was the door I came in through."

**What changed and why.** The equation, its Enlightenment origin, and the Abraham material (Genesis 15:6 with Romans 4:3, Genesis 17:17, Genesis 16) are the essay's, with its Scripture quotation kept exactly as the essay has it. The rewrite lets a convert from atheism say that doubt was his way in, then hands the argument back to Scripture rather than to his story.

## 18. Pentecostalism and the Global South

### Current opening

> On April 9, 1906, at a house on Bonnie Brae Street in Los Angeles (from which the revival moved days later into a converted stable at 312 Azusa Street) something happened that would reshape global Christianity more profoundly than any event since the Reformation. A small group of worshippers, led by William J. Seymour, a one-eyed Black preacher and the son of former slaves, began speaking in tongues, a phenomenon they identified as the baptism of the Holy Spirit described in Acts 2. The Azusa Street Revival would continue for three years, drawing visitors from around the world who carried its fire back to their home countries. >
> The irony is worth pausing over. The movement that would become the dominant expression of twenty-first-century Christianity was born not in a cathedral or a university but in a former horse stable. It was led not by a credentialed theologian but by a man denied admission to the seminary of his own denomination because of his race. And it was, in its earliest expression, radically interracial, a fact that would be quickly suppressed as American racial ideology reasserted itself.

> William Joseph Seymour (1870-1922) was born in Centerville, Louisiana, to parents who had been enslaved. He was largely self-educated, having been denied formal theological training by Charles Fox Parham's Bible school in Houston, which allowed him to listen to lectures only from the hallway because of his race. Seymour took Parham's teaching on the baptism of the Holy Spirit and transformed it into something Parham never intended: an interracial, egalitarian, Spirit-filled community that violated every social convention of early twentieth-century America.

## 19. Liturgical vs. Contemporary Worship: What We Gained and What We Lost

`liturgical-vs-contemporary-worship` · theology · 2270 words

### Current opening

> Every Sunday, across tens of thousands of churches, two fundamentally different visions of Christian worship are enacted, often without their participants understanding the assumptions behind either one. In one church, a congregation stands and sits and kneels in choreographed rhythm, following a printed order of service that includes a call to worship, a confession of sin, a declaration of pardon, the reading of three Scripture lessons, a sermon tied to the lectionary, the recitation of the Nicene Creed, the celebration of the Eucharist, and a benediction. In the church down the road, a worship band opens with twenty minutes of singing, the congregation remains standing with hands raised, the pastor delivers a forty-five-minute topical sermon with video clips and personal stories, an altar call is given, and the service ends with a closing song.
>
> Both of these are Christian worship. Both involve the reading of Scripture, prayer, singing, and preaching. But they are built on different foundations, shaped by different histories, and (most importantly) they form different kinds of Christians. The question of liturgical versus contemporary worship is not, at bottom, a question of preference. It is a question of formation: What is worship for, and what does it do to us over time?
>
> The word "liturgy" comes from the Greek leitourgia, which means "the work of the people." In ancient Athens, a liturgy was a public work performed by a wealthy citizen for the benefit of the community, funding a warship or sponsoring a festival. The early church adopted the term for its public worship, and the etymology is revealing: liturgy is not something performed by clergy for a passive audience. It is the common work of the gathered community, in which every person participates.

### Proposed opening

In the church down the road a band opens with twenty minutes of singing while the people stand with hands raised, the pastor preaches a forty-five-minute topical message with video clips and personal stories, an altar call follows, and a closing song ends it.
