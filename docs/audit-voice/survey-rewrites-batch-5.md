## 21. Creeds, Confessions, and Statements of Faith: Why the Church Wrote Them Down

`creeds-confessions-statements-of-faith` · theology · 2594 words

### Current opening

> There is a common assumption in contemporary Christianity (especially in non-denominational and low-church evangelical circles) that creeds are unnecessary impositions on the simplicity of biblical faith. "No creed but Christ" is the popular slogan, and it carries a certain appeal. Why do we need human documents when we have the Bible? Why add to Scripture? Why bind the conscience with the words of dead theologians?
>
> The answer, which the early church learned at extraordinary cost, is that the Bible does not interpret itself. Every heresy in the history of Christianity has claimed biblical support. Arius quoted Scripture to prove that Christ was a creature. The Gnostics quoted Scripture to prove that the material world was evil. The Docetists quoted Scripture to prove that Christ did not really suffer. The creeds were not additions to Scripture. They were the church's authoritative summary of what Scripture teaches, drawn up because false teachers were using Scripture to teach things that Scripture, rightly understood, does not say.
>
> The earliest Christians had no formal creed. They had confessional formulas embedded in their worship ("Jesus is Lord" (Romans 10:9), "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4)) but nothing approaching a systematic statement of faith. For the first two centuries, this was sufficient. The church was small, persecuted, and united by shared experience and apostolic memory.

I am a Baptist, and my own family of churches has asked those questions with a straight face for a long time, so I am not asking them of strangers.

### Current opening

>
> The SBC was not founded despite slavery. It was founded because of it. Its founding documents make this explicit. Its early leaders made it explicit. The theological case for slavery was not an embarrassing footnote to an otherwise noble enterprise. It was the enterprise. The denomination that would become the largest Protestant body in America (claiming over thirteen million members at its peak) came into existence for the express purpose of defending the right of Christians to own other human beings.
>
> This is not ancient history. The SBC did not formally apologize for its slaveholding origins until 1995, one hundred and fifty years after its founding. And the apology, while significant as a symbolic gesture, did not include any concrete measures of restitution, structural reform, or sustained institutional reckoning with the theological traditions that had made the defense of slavery possible.

## 24. Deconstruction Is Not Destruction

`deconstruction-is-not-destruction` · american-church · 2246 words

### Current opening

> In certain corners of American Christianity, "deconstruction" has become the most dangerous word in the vocabulary. It is spoken with the same hushed alarm once reserved for words like "liberal" or "heretical." Pastors preach against it. Conferences are organized to combat it. Books are written to warn young Christians away from it. The assumption, sometimes explicit and sometimes merely implied, is that to deconstruct one's faith is to destroy it, that the person who begins asking hard questions about what they were taught will inevitably end up with nothing at all.
>
> This assumption is wrong. It is wrong historically, wrong theologically, and wrong pastorally. And the church's panicked response to deconstruction is itself a significant cause of the very departures it claims to lament. When a tradition tells its members that honest questioning is the first step toward apostasy, it creates a binary that forces people out: either accept everything without examination, or leave entirely. Many are choosing to leave, not because deconstruction destroyed their faith, but because the church refused to make room for it.
>
> The term "deconstruction" entered popular use through the work of the French philosopher Jacques Derrida, but its current usage in religious contexts bears only a loose relationship to Derridean philosophy. In the context of faith, deconstruction refers to the process of critically examining the beliefs, assumptions, and practices one has inherited, distinguishing between the core of the faith and the cultural packaging in which it arrived, between what one genuinely believes and what one was simply told to believe, between the living God and the institutional structures that claim to represent God.

### Proposed opening

In some corners of American Christianity, "deconstruction" has become the most dangerous word in the vocabulary. It is said with the hushed alarm once reserved for "liberal" or "heretical." Pastors preach against it. Conferences are organized to fight it. Books warn young Christians away from it. The assumption, sometimes stated and sometimes only implied, is that to deconstruct your faith is to destroy it, that the person who starts asking hard questions about what they were taught will end with nothing.

I have to be careful here, because I am a pastor, and pastors are the people this word frightens most. The assumption is wrong all the same. It is wrong historically, wrong theologically, and wrong pastorally, and the church's panic about deconstruction is itself one cause of the departures it laments. When a tradition tells its people that honest questioning is the first step toward apostasy, it leaves them two doors: accept everything unexamined, or leave. Many are leaving, and not because deconstruction destroyed their faith. We refused to make room for it.

The word came into common use through the French philosopher Jacques Derrida, but its current religious use has only a loose connection to his philosophy. In matters of faith, deconstruction means the critical examination of the beliefs, assumptions, and practices you inherited: sorting the core of the faith from the cultural packaging it arrived in, what you actually believe from what you were told to believe, the living God from the institutions that claim to speak for him. That is not destruction. Whether it becomes destruction depends on what the church does next, which is the subject of this essay.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have to be careful here, because I am a pastor, and pastors are the people this word frightens most."

**What changed and why.** The alarm, the conferences and books, Derrida, and the definition are the essay's. The rewrite has the pastor name himself among the frightened before saying the fear is wrong, and turns the essay's 'the church refused' into 'we refused.'

## 25. What Happened to the Historical Jesus

`the-historical-jesus` · theology · 2127 words

### Current opening

> The question of the historical Jesus is not a question about whether Jesus existed. Virtually no serious historian doubts that a Jewish teacher named Jesus of Nazareth lived in first-century Palestine, was baptized by John the Baptist, gathered followers, and was crucified under Pontius Pilate. These facts are attested not only by the New Testament but by external sources (Josephus, Tacitus, Pliny the Younger) and they are accepted by scholars across the theological spectrum, from devout believers to committed atheists. The question of the historical Jesus is a different and more difficult question: What can we know about Jesus through the methods of historical inquiry alone, and what is the relationship between the Jesus of history and the Christ of faith?
>
> This question has occupied some of the finest minds in Western scholarship for over two centuries. The answers have been varied, contentious, and consequential. They have reshaped theology, challenged the church, and illuminated the Gospels in ways that both believers and skeptics ignore at their peril.
>
> The quest for the historical Jesus began in the eighteenth century with Hermann Samuel Reimarus, a professor of Oriental languages in Hamburg, who argued that the real Jesus was a failed Jewish revolutionary whose disciples, unable to accept his death, invented the resurrection and the entire theological framework of Christianity. Reimarus's work was published posthumously by Gotthold Ephraim Lessing between 1774 and 1778, and it ignited a firestorm that has never fully subsided.
