## 26. What Christians Actually Believe About Hell

`what-christians-believe-about-hell` · theology · 2105 words

### Current opening

> Few doctrines have caused more damage to the Christian witness than the popular version of hell, the one preached from revival stages and painted on highway billboards, the one that reduces the gospel to a fire-insurance policy and God to a deity whose primary attribute is wrath. And few doctrines have been more poorly understood, even by the people who preach them most loudly. The Christian tradition has never spoken with one voice about hell. It has harbored a range of views, some of them held by the most revered thinkers in the history of the church, and the honest conversation about hell requires knowing what the options actually are.
>
> This is not an argument for any single position. It is a map of the terrain. What you do with the map is between you and God. But you owe it to yourself (and to the people damaged by bad theology) to at least know what the tradition actually contains.
>
> The view most commonly associated with Christianity (and the one that has dominated Western theology since the fifth century) is eternal conscious torment (ECT). On this view, those who die without saving faith in Christ are consigned to hell, where they experience conscious suffering for all eternity. There is no exit. There is no reprieve. The punishment is infinite in duration, regardless of the finite nature of the sins committed.

### Proposed opening

Few doctrines have done more damage to the Christian witness than the popular version of hell, the one preached from revival stages and painted on highway billboards, the one that shrinks the gospel to a fire-insurance policy and God to a being whose first attribute is wrath. And few doctrines have been more poorly understood, including by the people who preach them loudest. The Christian tradition has never spoken with one voice about hell. It has held a range of views, some of them held by the most revered minds in the history of the church, and an honest conversation requires knowing what the options actually are.

I have to be careful here, because a pastor who writes about hell is writing about people he has buried. So this is not an argument for a single position. It is a map of the terrain, and what you do with the map is between you and God. But you owe it to yourself, and to the people bad theology has damaged, to know what the tradition actually contains before you decide what you believe or what you cannot.

The view most people associate with Christianity, and the one that has dominated Western theology since the fifth century, is eternal conscious torment. On this view, those who die without saving faith in Christ are consigned to hell and suffer consciously there forever. There is no exit and no reprieve, and the punishment is infinite in duration whatever the finite measure of the sins. It is the majority tradition, it has serious defenders, and it deserves to be stated in its strongest form before any of the alternatives are heard.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have to be careful here, because a pastor who writes about hell is writing about people he has buried."

**What changed and why.** The billboards, the fire-insurance policy, the map rather than the argument, and eternal conscious torment as the Western majority view since the fifth century are the essay's. The rewrite gives the pastor's reason for the map (the people he has buried) and commits the essay to stating the majority view in its strongest form before the alternatives, which its first section does.

## 27. When Politics Replaced Theology

`when-politics-replaced-theology` · american-church · 2386 words

### Current opening

> The standard narrative goes something like this: In 1973, the Supreme Court handed down Roe v. Wade, and evangelical Christians, roused by the slaughter of the unborn, organized politically to defend the sanctity of life. Out of this moral conviction, the Religious Right was born, a grassroots movement of people of faith who reluctantly entered the political arena because the stakes were too high for silence.
>
> This narrative is tidy, morally flattering, and almost entirely false.
>
> The actual history of the Religious Right's formation is more complicated, less noble, and far more revealing about the relationship between American evangelicalism and political power. It is a history in which the initial mobilizing issue was not abortion but segregation, in which the key players were not pastors but political operatives, and in which theological conviction was not the driver of political engagement but its casualty. Understanding this history is essential for anyone who wants to understand how a tradition that once prized its distance from worldly power became one of the most potent political forces in American life, and what it lost in the transaction.

### Proposed opening

The standard narrative goes like this. In 1973 the Supreme Court handed down Roe v. Wade, and evangelical Christians, roused by the slaughter of the unborn, organized to defend the sanctity of life. Out of that moral conviction the Religious Right was born, a grassroots movement of people of faith who entered politics reluctantly because the stakes were too high for silence.

The narrative is tidy, flattering, and almost entirely false. I held it myself before I read the history, and I have preached to rooms full of people who hold it still, so I am not correcting strangers. The actual story of the Religious Right's formation is more complicated, less noble, and far more revealing about the relationship between American evangelicalism and power. The first mobilizing issue was not abortion but segregation. The key players were not pastors but political operatives. And theological conviction was not the engine of the political turn. It was the casualty.

Understanding that history is the only way to understand how a tradition that once prized its distance from worldly power became one of the most potent political forces in American life, and what it gave up in the trade. It begins with a myth about the years before the late 1970s, the myth that fundamentalists and evangelicals were apolitical, and it is worth dismantling that myth first, because the true story is stranger than the one either side likes to tell.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I held it myself before I read the history, and I have preached to rooms full of people who hold it still, so I am not correcting strangers."

**What changed and why.** The standard narrative, Roe, the three reversals (segregation not abortion, operatives not pastors, conviction the casualty) and the apolitical myth are the essay's. The rewrite has the pastor admit he believed the tidy version and preached to people who still do, before the essay takes it apart.

## 29. Reconstructing Faith: What Comes After Deconstruction

`reconstructing-faith` · devotionals · 2505 words

### Current opening

> The word "deconstruction" has become, in the last decade, the dominant metaphor for a particular kind of spiritual experience: the process of questioning, dismantling, and often abandoning the theological framework in which one was raised. The term itself migrated from literary theory (where Jacques Derrida used it to describe a method of textual analysis) to popular culture (where it describes a lived experience of faith unraveling), and in the process it acquired a weight that Derrida never intended and that the experience itself both justifies and exceeds.
>
> The deconstruction conversation has been dominated by two voices, neither of which is particularly helpful. The first is the voice of celebration, the ex-evangelical memoirist, the deconversion influencer, the podcast host who treats every abandoned doctrine as a liberation and every departure from orthodoxy as a step toward enlightenment. This voice is understandable. Many people have been genuinely harmed by the religious systems they are deconstructing, and the relief of escape is real. But celebration as a permanent posture is unsustainable. You cannot build a life on what you have left. At some point, the question shifts from "What am I leaving?" to "Where am I going?" And that question is harder.
>
> The second voice is the voice of condemnation, the pastor who treats deconstruction as apostasy, the apologist who treats it as intellectual weakness, the church leader who treats it as a trend that will pass if they just preach loud enough. This voice is also understandable. If you have spent your life building a theological system and a community structured around it, the sight of people walking away is genuinely threatening. But condemnation is not a pastoral response. It is a defensive one. And it ensures that the people who most need the church's wisdom will never hear it, because they have been told that their questions make them enemies.

### Proposed opening

In the last decade "deconstruction" has become the dominant name for a particular kind of spiritual experience: questioning, dismantling, and often abandoning the theological framework you were raised in. The word migrated from literary theory, where Jacques Derrida used it for a way of reading texts, into popular culture, where it describes a faith coming apart, and along the way it picked up a weight Derrida never intended and the experience itself both justifies and exceeds.

The conversation about it has been carried by two voices, and neither is much help. The first is the voice of celebration: the ex-evangelical memoirist, the deconversion influencer, the podcast host who treats every abandoned doctrine as a liberation and every step away from orthodoxy as a step toward the light. The voice is understandable. Many of these people were genuinely harmed by the systems they are leaving, and the relief of getting out is real. But celebration cannot be a permanent address. You cannot build a life on what you have left, and sooner or later the question turns from "What am I leaving?" to "Where am I going?", which is the harder one.

The second is the voice of condemnation: the pastor who calls deconstruction apostasy, the apologist who calls it intellectual weakness, the leader who treats it as a trend that will pass if he preaches loudly enough. I am a pastor, and this is the voice my profession is tempted to; I have heard it in my own mouth. It is understandable too. If you have spent your life building a theological house and a community inside it, people walking out the door is a real threat. But condemnation is not a pastoral response. It is a defensive one, and it guarantees that the people who most need the church's wisdom never hear it, because they have been told their questions make them enemies.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I am a pastor, and this is the voice my profession is tempted to; I have heard it in my own mouth."

**What changed and why.** Derrida, the two voices and their casts are the essay's. The rewrite keeps the essay's even-handedness about both voices and adds the one thing it lacked: the pastor admitting the second voice is his own temptation.

## 30. Exvangelical: When Leaving Isn't Losing Faith

`exvangelical-when-leaving-isnt-losing-faith` · american-church · 1817 words

### Current opening

> The hashtag appeared on Twitter in 2016, coined by Blake Chastain, a podcaster and writer from the American Midwest. #Exvangelical. It was not a theological treatise or a manifesto. It was a naming, an attempt to give language to an experience that millions of people were living but that had no public vocabulary. Within months, it had become a movement, or at least the outline of one: a sprawling, decentralized community of people who had left evangelicalism and were trying to figure out who they were on the other side.
>
> The timing was not accidental. The year 2016, with the election of Donald Trump and the overwhelming support he received from white evangelical voters, was a catalytic event for many who had been quietly uncomfortable with the direction of American evangelicalism. For some, it was the final confirmation of suspicions they had held for years. For others, it was a sudden, disorienting rupture, the moment when the gap between the Jesus they had been taught to follow and the political movement that bore his name became too wide to bridge.
>
> But the exvangelical movement did not begin in 2016. It began in living rooms and therapist's offices and late-night conversations, years and sometimes decades before a hashtag gave it a name. It began every time a person raised in the evangelical world looked at what that world had become and said, quietly or loudly, "I cannot do this anymore."

### Proposed opening

The hashtag appeared on Twitter in 2016. Blake Chastain, a podcaster and writer from the Midwest, coined it: #Exvangelical. It was not a treatise or a manifesto. It was a naming, an attempt to give words to something millions of people were living without a public vocabulary for it. Within months it was a movement, or the outline of one, a sprawling and decentralized company of people who had left evangelicalism and were trying to work out who they were on the other side.

The timing was not an accident. The election of 2016, and the overwhelming white evangelical support for Donald Trump, was a breaking point for many who had been quietly uneasy for years. For some it confirmed what they had long suspected. For others it was a sudden, disorienting rupture, the moment the gap between the Jesus they had been taught to follow and the political movement carrying his name became too wide to cross.

But the movement did not begin in 2016. It began in living rooms and therapists' offices and late-night conversations, years and sometimes decades before a hashtag named it. I have sat in some of those conversations, on the pastor's side of the table, and I know how it sounds when someone raised in the evangelical world looks at what that world has become and says, quietly or loudly, "I cannot do this anymore." The first thing to understand about the people who say it is what they are not.

**First-person sentences to confirm:** "I have sat in some of those conversations, on the pastor's side of the table"

**What changed and why.** Chastain, the 2016 hashtag, the election and the living rooms are the essay's. The rewrite puts the pastor at the table where people say they cannot do this anymore, and hands off to the essay's first section (what exvangelicals are not).
