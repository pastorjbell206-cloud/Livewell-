## 46. How Christianity Became an Empire

## 48. The Rise of the Nones

`the-rise-of-the-nones` · american-church · 2303 words

### Current opening

> When survey researchers ask Americans about their religious affiliation and a respondent checks the box marked "none," the institutional church tends to hear one thing: atheist. This is a profound misunderstanding, and it has led the church to misdiagnose one of the most significant religious shifts in American history. The "nones" (those who claim no religious affiliation) are now approximately 28 to 30 percent of the American population, making them the largest single "religious" category in the country, surpassing Catholics, white evangelicals, and every other identifiable group. And the church, by and large, has no idea who these people are.
>
> The term itself emerged from survey methodology. The General Social Survey, conducted by NORC at the University of Chicago since 1972, asks respondents, "What is your religious preference?" and offers a list of options including Protestant, Catholic, Jewish, and other affiliations. Those who answer "none" are the "nones." When the survey began, they constituted about 5 percent of the population. Their growth since then has been steady, accelerating, and shows no signs of reversing.
>
> But a box on a survey is not an identity, and the assumption that all nones are the same (that they are all secular humanists, or scientific materialists, or village atheists) is flatly contradicted by every study that has examined the category in any depth. The nones are theologically, philosophically, and spiritually diverse. And understanding them requires the church to abandon some of its most comfortable assumptions about what unaffiliation means.

### Proposed opening

When survey researchers ask Americans about their religion and someone checks the box marked "none," the institutional church tends to hear one word: atheist. That is a profound misunderstanding, and it has led the church to misdiagnose one of the largest religious shifts in American history. The nones, the people who claim no religious affiliation, are now roughly 28 to 30 percent of the country, the largest single "religious" category in America, larger than Catholics, larger than white evangelicals, larger than any other group you can name. And the church, by and large, has no idea who they are.

I would have checked that box once, and in my case it did mean atheist, which makes me the exception the church keeps mistaking for the rule. The term itself came out of survey method. The General Social Survey, run by NORC at the University of Chicago since 1972, asks "What is your religious preference?" and offers Protestant, Catholic, Jewish, and other affiliations. The people who answer "none" are the nones. When the survey began they were about 5 percent of the population. Their growth since has been steady, accelerating, and shows no sign of turning around.

The nones are theologically, philosophically, and spiritually diverse, and understanding them requires the church to give up some of its most comfortable assumptions about what walking away means. Most of them did not walk toward atheism. They walked away from us.

**What changed and why.** The box, the 28 to 30 percent, the General Social Survey since 1972 and the 5 percent are the essay's. The rewrite lets the convert from atheism say he was once a none, and the atheist kind, which is exactly the case the essay says the church wrongly generalizes from.

## 49. Miracles: Do They Still Happen?

`do-miracles-still-happen` · theology · 2125 words

### Current opening

> The word "miracle" carries more baggage than almost any other word in the religious vocabulary. For the skeptic, it conjures images of credulous peasants, faith healers, and the suspension of rational thought. For the believer, it conjures images of divine power breaking into the natural order, parted seas, empty tombs, water turned to wine. Both images are distortions, and the honest conversation about miracles requires setting both aside long enough to ask the real question: Is the universe a closed system in which nothing can occur that is not determined by prior natural causes, or is it open to the action of a reality beyond itself?
>
> That is a philosophical question, not an empirical one. No experiment can determine whether the universe is open or closed. No measurement can detect the presence or absence of a God who acts. The question of miracles, at its deepest level, is a question about the nature of reality itself, and answering it requires philosophy and theology, not just science.
>
> The most famous argument against miracles was advanced by David Hume in section 10 of his An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748). Hume defined a miracle as a violation of a law of nature and argued that the evidence against any miracle must always outweigh the evidence in its favor. The reasoning goes like this: a law of nature is established by uniform experience, the sun has always risen, the dead have always stayed dead, water has always flowed downhill. Testimony that a miracle has occurred must be weighed against this uniform experience. Since the regularity of nature is supported by the entire weight of human observation, while testimony for a miracle is always limited and fallible, it is always more rational to reject the testimony than to accept the miracle.
