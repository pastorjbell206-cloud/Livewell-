# The Doubting Christian Track — six weeks

> **Audience:** the subscriber who checked "Christian with questions" — carrying
> real doubt, sometimes church-hurt, still inside the faith or barely.
> **Cadence:** one email a week for six weeks, each carrying one essay, in an
> order that lets the reader keep the question inside the faith instead of
> leaving to keep it.
> **Care posture:** no pressure to attend anything, ever; no timeline; no
> attendance checks; the doubt named more honestly than they have heard it
> named from a pulpit. Weeks 4 and 5 carry the church wound — no return
> pressure, no diagnosing the reader, an easy exit offered. Validate the
> feeling, never the despair.
> **Where it ends:** Week 6 closes the arc at reconstruction and names two
> open doors — *Faith After Deconstruction* (/faith-after-deconstruction) and
> the /answers page — offered, never pushed.
> All quoted lines are verbatim from the essays.

## Week 1 — When God Stops Making Sense

**Subject:** The question is not the enemy

You signed up as a Christian with questions, and this is the first of six letters written for exactly that person. The covenant on my end: no guilt, no attendance checks, no timeline — your questions taken as seriously as you have been carrying them.

I came to faith from atheism, and I expected the universe to make more sense once God was in it. What I found instead was a God who would not be solved. This week's essay is about the equation most of us were handed — faithfulness in, blessing out — and about the book God left in the canon to break it. Job's friends run the math for thirty-some chapters, careful men, orthodox men, defending God's logic against the evidence of their friend's body. God rebukes them. The man who shouted at heaven and demanded an accounting is the man God commends.

And when the answer finally comes out of the whirlwind, it is not an answer. "He did not get the answer. He got the Answerer." Those are not the same thing, and if your faith is currently caught in the gap between them, you are not losing it. You are standing where Job stood, and the book was left there for you on purpose.

## Week 2 — Is It Okay to Be Angry at God?

**Subject:** The prayers with the anger left in

Somewhere along the way you were told, sideways or straight, that the anger was the problem. Be careful with that. Who are you to answer back to God. So you swallowed it, and then you stopped talking to him altogether — because the only thing you had to say was the one thing you were told you were not allowed to say.

Open the Psalms and count. Roughly a third of them are laments, complaints flung directly at God by people who believed he had forgotten them. And God kept them. He put the angry prayers in the book and called them prayer, and his Son sang one of them as he died.

This week's essay is about the difference the church flattened into a single forbidden thing. There is a kind of anger at God that walks out and never addresses him again. And there is a kind that stays in the room and keeps yelling at the one it refuses to stop believing in. "You do not rage at a God you have given up on. You rage at the one you are still holding to his promise."

You do not have to clean it up first. You do not have to make it presentable for church. Pray the thirteenth psalm with your teeth clenched, if clenched teeth are all you have tonight. That is not the failure of your faith. That is your faith.

## Week 3 — What to Do When God Feels Absent

**Subject:** The silence has a name

Maybe there was no crisis. No scandal, no argument you lost. The faith is intact on paper — you would still sign the creed — but the warmth is gone, the sense of being heard is gone, and the worst part is the suspicion that the silence is your fault.

The church handed you a vocabulary for conversion and a vocabulary for sin and almost nothing for this. But the condition has a name, and it has had one since 1577, when John of the Cross wrote from a prison cell about the dark night — not God leaving, but God refusing to be loved merely for his gifts. The warmth was never him. The warmth was the wrapping.

Scripture knew this country long before John mapped it. Psalm 88 is the only psalm that ends in the dark with no turn toward the light, and the church kept it — set it in the middle of the book and prayed it for three thousand years — because a person in the dark needs a prayer that stays in the dark with him. Notice one thing about it, though. Every line is still addressed to God. The man in the pit has lost the answer and kept the address.

This week's essay asks the hard thing of that ache: to "keep walking toward a God you cannot feel". I wrote it for the version of you who reads the page and feels nothing move.

## Week 4 — When the Church Is the Thing That Hurt You

**Subject:** What happened to you was real

If this one is for you, you already know it. There is a face, a room, maybe a smell. And you have spent years being told — directly or by implication — that the wound is your problem. That you are bitter. That you left because you wanted to sin.

I am a pastor, and this week's essay says what my profession has not said to you nearly enough. "What happened to you was real. Your anger is not your faithlessness." And the God you are afraid you have offended by leaving is, on the matter of what was done to you, angrier than you are. Read Ezekiel 34 sometime — God's own words about shepherds who fed on the flock instead of feeding it. It is more violent than anything you have ever said about them.

The essay will not tell you to get over it. It will not give you a timeline, and it will not ask you to walk back into any building. It was written for one reason: so that the people who hurt you do not get to keep the one thing they were never actually standing between you and.

If reading it costs too much this week, delete this email. It will not come up again, and nothing about these letters changes.

## Week 5 — What If You're Done With Church but Not With Jesus?

**Subject:** Done with church, not with Jesus

People say it to me in parking lots, lowered, like admitting a debt: "I still believe. I just can't do the building anymore." They brace for the argument. I am not going to argue with you.

You are not rare. Around forty million American adults have stopped attending church in the last quarter-century, and a large share of them did not stop believing anything. They still pray. They still believe Jesus rose. They drifted, or they moved, or they were burned, and Sunday came and they did not, and the not-going hardened into a life nobody chose on purpose.

This week's essay holds two true things at once, without pushing either. The first: your faith is real, and no one gets to tell you otherwise. The second is a piece of physics I would rather say now than in five years: the faith you are carrying alone is a coal pulled out of the fire. It is still glowing. "A coal alone goes cold. That is not a verdict on the coal."

The essay does not end with an invitation, and neither does this email. No church is named. No Sunday is suggested. It only asks you to look at what the ache is actually for — the one that makes you lower your voice when you say you still believe.

## Week 6 — What Happens After You Deconstruct Your Faith?

**Subject:** What the hammer was for

"The people deconstructing their faith are usually right about why." That is the first sentence of the last essay in this series, and I mean it. If you were handed a Christianity that was equal parts Jesus and a voting bloc, the impulse to tear it down is not rebellion. It is closer to repentance.

I have held the hammer. At nineteen I built an atheism brick by brick, and tearing down the faith I had been handed was the most honest thing I had done up to then. So this essay is not a warning against demolition. It is a question asked while the hammer is still swinging: is there anything you are tearing toward, and not only away from? Jesus deconstructed the Pharisees relentlessly, and the people who loved the old building hated him for it — but "His demolition always had a destination." A field is not a home. Suspicion has no Tuesday.

There is one wall I have swung at for thirty years that has not come down. A man on a cross who refused to defend himself, and an empty tomb I cannot explain away. Everything else I have taken apart and rebuilt, some of it more than once. That wall has held my weight every time.
