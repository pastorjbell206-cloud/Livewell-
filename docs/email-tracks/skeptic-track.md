## Week 1 — The Atheist in the Pulpit

**Subject:** There is an atheist in my pulpit every Sunday

You checked the box that said skeptic. Here is what that gets you: one essay a week, no pressure, and the questions taken seriously.

Before the first one arrives, you should know who is writing. I pastor a church in Michigan. Before that I was an atheist for most of my life, and my unbelief was not a phase or a wound. It was a conviction, argued and defended, and I can still recite its reasons without notes. Most conversion stories kill the earlier man off by the second paragraph — a cartoon, a before picture. I will not do that to him. He was not a fool, and if I treat him like one I will deserve your distrust.

So the first essay puts his reasons on the table at full strength — Russell at nineteen, the crimes on religion's ledger, the fatherless boy underneath the philosophy — before it says a word about what changed. The church's failures are in there, uncorrected. There is no checkmate at the end and no altar call, because nobody was ever loved into anything by a checkmate. The terms of the whole exchange are in one line: "Your arguments may be better than the arguments of the believers around you. Mine were."

## Week 2 — What If Christianity Is Wrong?

**Subject:** I have asked whether Christianity is wrong

The question does not knock. It is already in the room — at two in the morning, halfway through a hymn, in the search bar when the house is asleep. What if the whole thing is a story frightened people told themselves to bear the dark.

I have asked it. I came to faith from atheism, so the question is not a foreign army to me — it is my first language, and it has never fully left. There are mornings the whole edifice looks like wishful thinking for a few seconds, and I have learned not to panic at them. A faith that has never met the question has only been sheltered, and sheltered faith breaks the first time the weather turns.

One thing before you read. The question is not free. It presents the bill for belief and pockets the bill for unbelief. To say the faith is wrong is also to say that the dignity of your children is a fiction, that the love you would die for is chemistry, that the injustice that shakes your hands is a preference. Maybe so. But that is a wager too, staked on what nobody can prove. "Both bets are total. Both stake the whole of a life."

You can finish the essay and still disagree. That was the design.

## Week 3 — Did the Resurrection Actually Happen?

**Subject:** The resurrection is a historical claim. Treat it like one

Christianity is the one religion that cannot survive having its founding claim softened into metaphor, and it never tried to. Paul said it flatly, writing around the year 54: if Christ has not been raised, the faith is worthless and the preachers should be pitied. Everything stands or falls on whether one man was dead on a Friday and alive on a Sunday.

That honesty disarmed me long before I believed any of it. So this week's essay treats the claim as history — the kind you weigh, not the kind you inherit. It starts from facts nearly all scholars grant, skeptical ones included: the crucifixion, the experiences the followers were convinced were encounters with him alive, the conversions of two committed enemies of the movement. It takes the rival explanations seriously — hallucination, fraud, a moved body — because they deserve it, and a case is only as strong as the rivals it survives. And it names its own limit. This is not a proof. History does not deliver proofs; it delivers best explanations, and on those ordinary terms the resurrection competes.

One fact I could never file away: "The tomb was in a city full of people who wanted the movement dead and had only to produce a body. They never did."

## Week 4 — Why Would Anyone Trust the Bible?

**Subject:** The case against the Bible rests on stories about it

For years my unbelief leaned hard on a story about the Bible: copied by hand until it was garbled like the telephone game, assembled by a council with political motives, riddled with contradictions. The story was load-bearing. I never checked it.

When I finally did, the checking did not make me a Christian. It did something more uncomfortable. It showed me I had accepted secular folklore about a book on worse evidence than the book itself offered — that I had been, in the essay's words, "quoting the trailer and calling it the film".

This week's essay walks through what the checking turned up. The telephone game is the wrong picture; thousands of branching manuscripts are how errors get caught, not how they hide. The canon was not voted in by a council; it was recognized, slowly, from below. And the contradictions — the objection I respect most — turn out to be the signature of independent witnesses, who differ at the edges and converge at the center. The harmonized account would be the suspicious one.

None of this proves the book true, and the essay says so. It clears the ground so the real question can be asked. Most people confident the Bible cannot be trusted have done everything with it except open it.

## Week 5 — If God Is Good, Why Is There So Much Suffering?

**Subject:** Suffering is the best reason not to believe

The strongest case against God I ever carried was not built in a seminar. It was a hospital ward full of children who did nothing to deserve what is happening to them. If a good and all-powerful God exists, that ward should not exist. It does. For many thoughtful people that single fact has closed the question, and I do not think they are stupid or shallow for letting it close.

So this week's essay takes the objection at full strength — Ivan Karamazov piling up his cases and returning the ticket — before it says anything in reply, and it does not tie a bow on the reply. "There is no bow." The Bible itself refuses one. Job demands the explanation and God does not give it. Habakkuk shouts the problem straight at heaven and the book never resolves the complaint into a clean answer. The protest is authorized. It sits in the mouths of the heroes.

One turn to watch for. To call a child's suffering evil — not unfortunate, evil — is to appeal to a standard of how things ought to be, and I could never say where my materialism got one. The weapon I aimed at God was borrowing its ammunition from the thing it was firing at.

## Week 6 — Do You Have to Choose Between Faith and Science?

**Subject:** The war between faith and science has a birth date

The story goes that faith and science are at war, that they always have been, and that every honest person eventually picks a side. I built years of my atheism on that story. Then I traced it to its source and found a birth date: two American writers, John William Draper in 1874 and Andrew Dickson White in 1896, who assembled the war as a polemic to free the new professional sciences from clerical oversight. Historians have spent decades quietly taking the picture apart. Science grew up among believers who expected the universe to be lawful because they believed a rational mind had written the laws. Newton wrote more on theology than on physics.

The frictions that are real stay in the essay, named — I am not selling you a frictionless harmony. The point is narrower. Science tells you the mechanism and stays silent on the meaning, and I spent years mistaking that silence for the answer no. "Science hands you a magnificent description of the house. It is constitutionally unable to tell you whether anyone lives there."

This is the sixth essay, and the end of the sequence you signed up for.
