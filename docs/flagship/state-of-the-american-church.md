The word comes to me the way bad news usually does — across a table, from a
pastor who drove a while to say it somewhere his own people would not hear.
Half of his best families have left, and not over doctrine. Over politics. Over
which grievance the church would bless and which it would not. He is not asking
me how to grow. He is asking me whether the thing he gave his life to is coming
apart in his hands.

He is not the only one who suspects it. The skeptic suspects it too, and reads
the same headlines, and reaches a colder conclusion: that the American church
was never about the crucified Christ at all, only about power wearing his name,
and that its decline is simply the mask slipping. I want to say something to
that skeptic before I say anything else. Some of what you see is real. The
church did trade its birthright, in places, for a seat at a table it should have
flipped. If your objection is that we have often preferred the flag to the cross,
you are not wrong, and I will not pretend you are. Grant me only this: that the
disease you have correctly diagnosed is not the whole body, and that the cure you
have written off is the very thing the body has used to heal before.

Begin with what is measurable, taken at its strongest. *[VERIFY: the current
figures.]* The share of Americans who claim no religion has climbed for a
generation and is now among the largest religious groups in the country. Trust
in the church as an institution has fallen with trust in nearly every
institution. The people leaving are disproportionately young, and they are not,
by and large, storming out over the resurrection. They are drifting out over the
sense that the church became one more tribe, louder about its enemies than about
its Lord. Take that account at full weight. It is not the invention of the
church's despisers. It is, in large part, the church's own harvest.

But a symptom named is not a cause found, and here the honest observer and the
comfortable one part ways. The comfortable read is that the culture came for the
church from outside — secularism, the phone, the university — and the church is
simply a victim of a hostile age. There is truth in it. It is not the deepest
truth. The deepest truth is that the church was captured from inside, and
captured twice, by two spirits that despise each other and are, underneath,
the same trade.

The first capture is the one the skeptic sees and the right will not. It is the
fusion of the faith with the nation — what the sociologists Andrew Whitehead and
Samuel Perry, in *Taking America Back for God*, name and measure as Christian
nationalism: not patriotism, not public conviction, but the belief that America
is God's covenant people and that Christian power over the country is the point
of Christian faith. Kristin Kobes Du Mez, in *Jesus and John Wayne*, traces how
a particular militant masculinity was baptized and sold until the strongman
became the model and the Sermon on the Mount became an embarrassment. The trade
is always the same. The cross is a scandal that cannot be turned into power;
so the cross is quietly exchanged for a flag that can, and the exchange is called
courage. It is not courage. It is idolatry, and it is ours — I have felt the pull
of it in my own chest, the wish that the gospel came with an army.

The second capture is the one the right sees clearly and the left will not — and
it is the older of the two. A century ago the Social Gospel set out to press the
kingdom of God into the structures of society, and much of what it saw was real
and neglected. But somewhere the offense was subtracted. The cross became a
symbol of solidarity rather than an atonement; sin became mainly systemic;
salvation became mainly justice. J. Gresham Machen saw where it led and said so
in 1923, in *Christianity and Liberalism* — that what remained was not a variety
of the faith but a different religion entirely, sharing its vocabulary and
little else. H. Richard Niebuhr wrote the epitaph a decade later, describing a
faith in which "a God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom
without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross." That is
the left's trade, and it is the right's trade in a mirror: the offense of the
gospel spent, in one case for power, in the other for relevance, in both for a
seat at a table.

And underneath both, the folk religion of the whole country — what the
sociologist Christian Smith, studying American teenagers, named Moralistic
Therapeutic Deism: a god who wants you to be nice, who stays out of the way
until you need him, whose highest purpose is your happiness. That is the water
most of the American church actually swims in, right and left alike. It is not
that we chose nationalism or its progressive cousin instead of the faith. It is
that both are what you get when the God of Sinai and Golgotha has already been
replaced, quietly, by a therapist who blesses your side.

If this were only our moment, it would be despair. It is not only our moment,
and here is the pastoral word under the prophetic one: the church has been
captured before, and the capture has never been the end of the story. When
Constantine made the faith safe, something was gained and something was lost, and
the desert filled with people fleeing the safety. When the German Christians
folded the church into the Reich, a remnant met at Barmen in 1934 and declared,
against the whole weight of the state, that the church has one Lord and it is not
Caesar — and paid for the sentence. When American pulpits built a careful
biblical case for slavery, the historian Mark Noll has shown it produced a
genuine theological crisis, a moment when the plain reading of the proof-texts
served the oppressor and the deeper reading of the whole canon had to be
recovered against them. Every time, the same pattern. Power takes the church.
A remnant remembers who the church belongs to. The faith survives its friends.

So what is the state of the American church. It is captive, and it is not
finished. It is captive because it keeps trading the one thing that cannot be
weaponized — a crucified God — for the many things that can. And it is not
finished because underneath the captivities there is still the older faith they
are all counterfeits of: creedal, ancient, confessed at Nicaea and Chalcedon
before America was imagined and answerable to no party now. That faith does not
need to win the country. It has outlived empires that were sure they owned it.

I do not know if the numbers will turn. I am not sure that is the right thing to
pray for. I know that the church that comes through this will be smaller and
truer, and that it will be recognizable by a single mark — that it will have
stopped asking the crucified Christ to serve its side, and started, again, to
follow him. That church is already here, in rooms too quiet to trend. The pastor
who drove to see me is building one. So, on the days I am honest, am I.
