## Post 1 — Consider the Birds

- **Title:** The Word for What You Are Feeling at Three in the Morning
- **Subtitle:** What the Bible actually says about anxiety — and the one word in Matthew 6 we keep racing past
- **Preview text:** Worry is not a wiring problem. It is a tell. And it tells the truth about what we are leaning on.
- **Button:** "Read *Consider the Birds*" → https://livewellbyjamesbell.co/consider-the-birds

**Body:**

Most people who struggle with anxiety can name the hour. For a lot of them it sits somewhere around three in the morning. The house is quiet. The people they love are asleep. And they are awake, working through the same short list they worked through last night and will work through again tomorrow night. Nothing gets solved at that hour. They know nothing is getting solved. They cannot stop anyway.

We tend to treat that hour as a malfunction, a glitch in the machine. We come at our own fear the way a mechanic comes at a car that will not start. Find the broken part. Replace it. Move on. The right book, the right breathing exercise, the right verse on the mirror, and we could solder it shut and sleep like the calm people we assume everyone else to be.

But anxiety is not a broken part. Anxiety is a tell.

It tells the truth about what we are actually leaning on. People do not lie awake over the things they have genuinely handed to someone stronger than themselves. They lie awake over the things they believe, at three in the morning, depend entirely on them. Follow your worry down far enough and it leads, nearly every time, to a place where you have quietly concluded, without ever deciding it on purpose, that you are the one holding everything up, and that if you let go, it falls.

The word the New Testament uses for worry is *merimna*. It shows up on the lips of Jesus in the most famous passage ever spoken about anxiety, the sixth chapter of Matthew, and it is older and stranger than our English softens it into. *Merimna* carries the sense of being divided, pulled apart, drawn in two directions at once. The anxious person is not someone with too much on their mind. The anxious person is someone being torn — the heart pulled toward the God it half-trusts and toward the future it cannot control, stretched between them until something begins to tear.

That is what three in the morning is. The sound of a soul being pulled in two.

There is a detail we tend to skim. When Jesus says "do not be anxious" in Matthew 6:25, he opens with a word that should stop us: *Therefore.* It is a conclusion. He is drawing a line from what he said one verse earlier: "you cannot serve God and money." The anxiety teaching is not a fresh subject. It is the second half of the money teaching. Worry, it turns out, is what serving the wrong master feels like from the inside. It is the tax the false master charges, payable nightly.

Then he does something genuinely strange. He does not say try harder. He does not say think positively. He points out the window. *Look at the birds.* The sparrows that never planted a field or filled a barn, and are fed anyway. Then the sentence the whole passage turns on, the one we skim because we have heard it so often: *Are you not of more value than they?*

He is not insulting your intelligence. He is rebuilding your trust from the foundation up. He takes the person who is convinced, at three in the morning, that they are alone and in charge and holding up the sky, and walks them back out into a created world that is fed, every day, by a hand that is not theirs.

I want to be careful here, because the church has done real damage at exactly this point. To tell a person whose chest tightens at a full inbox, whose hands shake before the phone rings, to "just have more faith" is not the gospel. It is a slap with a Bible verse on it. Some people carry anxiety the way a soldier carries shrapnel, lodged deep, from a wound they did not choose. *Consider the Birds* talks plainly about the body, the doctor, and the medication, and treats all three as good. I am not going to stand at a safe distance and call your trembling a failure of belief.

But I will not pretend it is only chemistry, either. Because that is the other lie, the gentler one, and it abandons you just as surely. It tells you your fear means nothing. And in the comfort of that, it takes away the one thing that could heal you — the possibility that your restlessness is pointing somewhere. That it is a hunger. And that hungers can be fed.

That is what *Consider the Birds* is about. It is for the person awake at three in the morning, and it does not promise the hour will never come again. What it promises is that the hour does not have to mean what you have been afraid it means.

The birds are singing this morning. They started before it was light.

## Post 2 — Where Your Treasure Is

- **Title:** The One Rival Jesus Named
- **Subtitle:** Why he called money "mammon," and why your heart follows your treasure and not the other way around
- **Preview text:** The Bible's first concern about money is not how much you have. It is what it is doing to you.
- **Button:** "Read *Where Your Treasure Is*" → https://livewellbyjamesbell.co/where-your-treasure-is

**Body:**

Jesus talked about money more than he talked about heaven, and far more than he talked about hell. By a common reckoning, about one of every ten verses in the Gospels touches it, and roughly a third of his parables turn on it. He came to deal with sin and death, and he spent a striking share of a short public life talking about what people earn, keep, owe, and fear losing.

We have quietly agreed not to notice.

Money is the one subject a congregation will forgive a pastor for nearly anything except raising. We will sit still for hard preaching on pride, on anger, on doubt. Bring up money and the room changes temperature. That reaction is worth paying attention to, because we do not guard what does not matter to us. We guard what we are afraid to lose. The flinch is information.

Here is the question the Bible actually asks, and it is not the one we expect. Its main concern about money is not how much you have. It is not even, first, what you do with it. It is what it is doing to you.

Because money, in the teaching of Jesus, is never only a tool. He says something about it he says about nothing else. He gives it a name and treats it as a rival. "You cannot serve God and money" — and the word he used was *mammon*, an Aramaic word he personifies, a master with a throne and a claim. He never does this with sex. He never does this with power. Only money gets named as a rival god, because money, more than almost anything we handle, has the strange ability to stop being something we own and start being something that owns us.

I want to be careful, because the church has gone wrong here in two opposite directions. One made God a means to money: give, and he will make you rich. That is mammon in a clerical collar. The other made money itself the sin, so that a paid-off house or a good meal becomes a failure of holiness. That is a different lie. The Bible does neither. It does not flatter wealth, and it does not romanticize poverty. It tells the truth about a power, and it offers to set us free.

And it tells that truth to all of us, which is the part we resist. We assume the warnings are for the rich, by which we always mean someone richer than we are. The man with the boat aims the verse at the man with the yacht. Everyone has someone above them to point it at, so it never lands. But mammon does not check your balance before it goes to work on you. The pull is the same in a trailer and a penthouse.

Here is the line that holds everything together: "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Read it slowly, because the order is not what we assume. We think the heart leads and the money follows — that we fund what we already love. Jesus says it runs the other way. The treasure leads, and the heart follows it, the way a compass needle turns north whether or not you meant it to.

That is either very bad news or very good, depending on what you do with it. It means you cannot simply decide to care less about money by trying harder. But it also means there is a door. Move the treasure, and the heart will follow it out.

That is the hope underneath the whole thing, and it is not a technique for getting rich or a guilt trip for being comfortable. It is freedom. The closed fist — gripping, calculating, never quite able to rest because the number is never quite enough — can become an open hand. Not by willpower. Not by shame. By the slow, real work of moving our treasure to a place that cannot be lost, until the heart, that obedient compass, finally turns and follows it home.

You cannot serve both. That is not a threat. It is a mercy.
