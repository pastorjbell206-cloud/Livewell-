## What's going on
This may not look like what you pictured when you heard the word abuse. You may never have been hit, or it happened once and came with tears and promises. What you know is the watching: reading their mood, rehearsing what you'll say, apologizing for things you didn't do. On good days you wonder if you're exaggerating.

Abuse is a pattern in which one person uses fear, force, or control to hold power over another. It can be physical (hitting, shoving, choking, blocking a door, breaking things near you), sexual (forcing or pressuring you into sex, even in marriage), emotional and verbal (insults, threats, humiliation, cutting you off from friends and family), financial (taking your pay, hiding money, keeping you from work), digital (reading your phone, tracking where you go, demanding your passwords), or spiritual (Scripture, prayer, or church used to control or silence you: *a good wife submits*, *God hates divorce, so you can't leave*, *you have to forgive*). It happens to men as well as women, and it can be harder for a man to say so. Sometimes the one reading is young, and it's a parent or another adult doing the hurting.

People will offer explanations: *he's under so much stress*, *she had a hard childhood*, *it's the drinking*, *every couple fights*. Some of that is true. Stress and old wounds are real, and drinking can make a bad night worse. But plenty of people carry all of them without hurting anyone at home, and none of it excuses abuse. Ordinary conflict runs both ways and leaves no one afraid. Christians sometimes add a spiritual one: marriage is hard, so pray more, submit more, forgive. That is true about marriage and silent about danger, and it lays the whole weight on the person being hurt.

What these explanations leave out is fear. The question isn't only whether you've been hit. It's whether you're afraid of someone you live with, and whether you've arranged your life around keeping them calm. That fear is telling you something. And because leaving is often the most dangerous time, safety is something to plan with help, not to improvise alone.

## What Scripture says
Scripture doesn't look away from violence at home, and it keeps the prayers of people hurt by those closest to them. These passages give words to betrayal, show a faithful man escaping, and read in full some lines that have been used to keep people in danger.

> "But it is you, a man like myself, my companion and close friend. ... My companion attacks his friends; he violates his covenant. His speech is smooth as butter, but war is in his heart. His words are softer than oil, yet they are swords unsheathed." (Psalm 55:13, 20-21, BSB)

This psalm bears David's name, and it is the prayer of someone betrayed by a close friend. An enemy he could have endured (Psalm 55:12), but this was “my companion and close friend,” someone he had worshiped beside (Psalm 55:14), soft in speech with war in his heart. That is why the one hurting you can sound so reasonable to others. Earlier the psalmist prays the wish you may feel guilty for: “Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and find rest” (Psalm 55:6). God kept that prayer in his book.

> "Then Saul sent messengers to David’s house to watch him and kill him in the morning. But David’s wife Michal warned him, “If you do not run for your life tonight, tomorrow you will be dead!” So Michal lowered David from the window, and he ran away and escaped." (1 Samuel 19:11-12, BSB)

That night began with a spear thrown while David played the harp, and it wasn't the first (1 Samuel 18:10-11; 19:9-10). Saul, his king and now his father-in-law, had sworn, “As surely as the LORD lives, David will not be put to death” (1 Samuel 19:6), and Jonathan had brought David back (1 Samuel 19:7). This time Saul's own daughter got him out the window. David refused ever to harm Saul (1 Samuel 24:6), but he never went back. Jesus, sending his disciples into danger, told them, “When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next” (Matthew 10:23). Getting out of reach is not a failure of faith.

> "Wives, submit to your husbands, as is fitting in the Lord. Husbands, love your wives and do not be harsh with them. Children, obey your parents in everything, for this is pleasing to the Lord. Fathers, do not provoke your children, so they will not become discouraged." (Colossians 3:18-21, BSB)

If Scripture has been used to keep you quiet, read these lines whole. Each word to the one with less power sits beside a limit on the one with more: husbands must not be harsh, fathers must not provoke. The parallel passage in Ephesians begins, “Submit to one another out of reverence for Christ” (Ephesians 5:21), and measures a husband's love by Christ, who “gave Himself up” (Ephesians 5:25). Christians disagree about what headship means, and this page won't settle that. But harshness is what these verses forbid.

## Why this is so hard
People who love you may ask *why don't you just leave?* It's an honest question that misreads how this works. Fear holds people, and so do love, children, money, shame, and faith. The one hurting you may be the person you married, or the parent you still want to please. Abuse makes staying hard and leaving harder, and it teaches you to doubt what you know.

It also hides well. It can begin long before any blow, with control that looks like care and jealousy that sounds like love, and the one doing it may be admired at church or at work. That is one reason the person being hurt may not be believed.

We've also inherited a long habit of looking away. English and American common law once allowed a husband to *chastise* his wife. When courts gave that up in the nineteenth century, American law went on treating a husband's violence differently from other assaults for about a century, often declining to step in so as to protect family privacy and domestic peace. The instinct outlived the law. It's there whenever a friend says *that's between them*.

The church should have been the first to break that silence, and too often we've kept it. We've prized the intact family so highly that a frightened home looked better to us than a broken one. We've sent people home with a verse about submission or forgiveness and asked the one being hurt to be patient while the other promised to change. Scripture records this failure too. When Tamar was assaulted by her half-brother, her brother Absalom told her, “Be quiet for now, my sister. He is your brother” (2 Samuel 13:20), and though King David, her father, “was furious” (2 Samuel 13:21), the text records nothing he did for her. I'm a pastor, and the reflex to smooth things over runs deep in people like me, so I can't point at this from outside it.

Underneath is a confusion about power. Jesus said the rulers of the nations “lord it over” their people, and then, “But it shall not be this way among you” (Mark 10:42-43). God's charge against Israel's shepherds was that they had “ruled them with violence and cruelty” (Ezekiel 34:4), and a psalm says that God's “soul hates the lover of violence” (Psalm 11:5). Whatever authority anyone holds in a home, Scripture gives it for the good of the ones under it. Abuse turns it against the very people it was given to protect.

So the trouble is not that you haven't loved enough, prayed enough, or tried hard enough. Scripture calls taking cover wisdom: “The prudent see danger and take cover” (Proverbs 22:3). A home was meant to be a shelter. Taking cover doesn't break it. The fear already has.

## What to do this week
If you're in danger right now, the first step isn't on this list: call 911. Otherwise, take the smallest safe step, only what you can do unnoticed. An advocate can help you judge what's safe.

**1. Make a safety plan with an advocate.** When you can talk without being overheard, reach the Domestic Violence Hotline in the help block above. An advocate will help you plan for a bad night, whether you stay or go: where you'd go, who you'd call, what you'd take, how to keep a record. Don't confront the person hurting you or announce that you're leaving.

**2. Tell one safe person.** Choose one person outside your home who won't tell the one hurting you. Say it in a sentence: *I'm afraid at home, and I need someone to know.* Agree on a word or a text that means *call 911* or *come get me*.

**3. Guard your phone.** Phones and accounts can be watched without your knowing. Look for help on a device the other person can't reach, like a friend's phone or a library computer. Changing passwords or deleting apps can alert someone who is watching, so plan that with an advocate.

**4. Tell God the truth.** You don't have to pretend with God. Pray Psalm 55, or one honest sentence: *Lord, I'm afraid in my own home.* The psalmist cried out “morning, noon, and night,” and God heard (Psalm 55:17). Praying doesn't replace the call, and the call isn't a lack of faith.

## When to get more help
This is more than a pastoral matter from the start. If you're in danger, call 911; police and courts can do what no pastor can. A local domestic violence program, which the Hotline can connect you with, offers advocates, shelter, support groups, and help seeking a protective order, which works alongside a safety plan, never in place of one. If you've been hurt, see a doctor.

For counseling, see a licensed counselor on your own, one experienced with abuse; your doctor, your insurer's directory, or a pastor who knows local counselors can point you to one. Couples counseling isn't the answer: the Hotline doesn't recommend it with an abusive partner, because it can't fix the imbalance of power, and what you say there can be used against you at home. If you want a Christian counselor, ask about training as well as faith, and confirm the license with your state's licensing board.

Signs:
- You or a child are in danger, or someone has threatened to kill you, the children, or themselves. Call 911.
- You've been choked or strangled, even once. See a doctor, even if you feel fine.
- You've been forced or pressured into sex. RAINN, in the help block, is for you.
- You've had thoughts of ending your life. Call or text 988 now.

**The first call.** You don't need proof or the right words. You can say, *I don't know if this counts, but I'm afraid of my husband*, or wife, partner, or parent. You can hang up whenever you need to and needn't give your name. With a counselor, say you're coming alone and ask whether they've helped people living with abuse.

**What it costs.** The Hotline is free, and federal law bars fees for domestic violence services paid for through its family violence program. Legal aid offices help people with low incomes seek protective orders. For counseling, ask about sliding scales and community mental health centers, and if your insurance is in the other person's name, ask how statements are sent.

## If you're helping someone
If someone tells you they're afraid at home, believe them. You don't need proof; your part is to help them get safe. Don't confront the person hurting them or try to talk that person around. Jonathan pleaded with his father and won an oath that lasted until the next spear, and when he pleaded again, the spear came at him (1 Samuel 19:6-10; 20:33). Ask how it's safe to reach them, and let them make their own decisions. Hotline advocates talk with friends and family too.

If you're a pastor or church leader, don't investigate or referee; that is work for police and courts. Never counsel the couple together or send anyone home to work it out. Don't share what you've heard with the other spouse, the prayer chain, or anyone the person hasn't agreed to tell, except where the law requires a report. If a child is being harmed, you may be a mandated reporter; those laws vary by state, so learn yours. This isn't legal advice.

Things you can say:
- Thank you for telling me. I believe you.
- This isn't your fault.
- Are you safe right now?
- What's the safest way to reach you?

Things not to say:
- Why don't you just leave?
- What did you do to set them off?
- You two need to sit down and talk it out.
- Submit, and God will change their heart.

Keep checking in on their terms, and keep coming back even if they return. Offer something concrete: a ride, a phone, a safe place to keep copies of important papers. If a child is at risk or anyone is in immediate danger, call 911.

## Questions people ask
### How do I know if it's abuse?
Start with fear. If you're afraid of someone you live with, or you've arranged your life around keeping them calm, take that seriously. Abuse is a pattern of using fear, force, or control to hold power over someone. It needn't leave bruises; threats, insults, control of money or your phone, pressured sex, and isolation all count, whether you're a man or a woman. You don't have to be sure to call the Domestic Violence Hotline.

### Does the Bible say I have to stay?
No faithful reading of Scripture requires you to stay where you'll be harmed. David escaped through a window (1 Samuel 19:12), Paul in a basket through a window in the city wall (2 Corinthians 11:33), Joseph by night with Mary and the child (Matthew 2:14). Even the Catholic Church, which teaches that a consummated marriage between baptized Christians can never be dissolved, lets a spouse leave when the other puts them or the children in grave danger. Whether abuse can end a marriage is debated. Whether you may get safe is not.

### Can a Christian divorce an abuser?
Faithful Christians answer this differently; it divides churches, not the faith. One view holds that Scripture allows divorce only for sexual immorality or desertion (Matthew 19:9; 1 Corinthians 7:15), or not at all, since “what God has joined together, let man not separate” (Matthew 19:6). On this view abuse justifies separation and protection but not divorce, and the vow, with the hope of the abuser's repentance, still stands. Another view holds that the one who makes a spouse afraid has broken the covenant and deserted the marriage in all but address, so that the one harmed is “not bound in such cases. God has called you to live in peace” (1 Corinthians 7:15). Both views agree you may get safe now.

### If I forgive my abuser, do I have to go back?
No. Safety comes first. Forgiveness hands the debt to God: “Do not avenge yourselves, beloved, but leave room for God's wrath” (Romans 12:19). Trust is different; it's rebuilt by changed behavior over time. Saul once told David, “I have sinned. Come back, David my son. I will never harm you again” (1 Samuel 26:21). David refused to harm him, and he went on his way. You can forgive from a safe distance.

### What if it's my mom or dad who hurts me?
It isn't your fault, and telling isn't betraying your family. Tell a trusted adult, like a teacher, a school counselor, a coach, or a relative, and if the first one doesn't help, tell another. If you're in danger, call 911. Honoring your parents (Exodus 20:12) never meant keeping a secret that is hurting you. “Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me” (Psalm 27:10).
