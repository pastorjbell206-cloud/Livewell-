## What's going on
If you haven't talked to anyone yet, I'd rather you did that before you read on. This page will still be here in the morning.

Thoughts of not wanting to be alive don't all sound the same. For some people it's a quiet wish to fall asleep and not wake up, or to disappear, or for everything to stop. For others the thoughts turn to how and when, and that can be frightening. Some have lived with this for years, and some never imagined they would feel it. If yours are the quiet kind, they still deserve to be taken seriously, and you don't have to wait for them to get louder before you tell someone. It's easy to carry this in silence, afraid that saying it will make people panic or treat you differently, or will make it more real. If you're a Christian, you may carry one more weight: the fear that a believer shouldn't be thinking this at all.

Two thoughts are worth naming. One says the pain will never end. The other says you've become a burden, and the people you love would be better off without you. That second thought can feel like clear thinking, even like love, and it keeps you from asking for help, because asking feels like one more weight to put on someone. It's a lie, and a convincing one. You don't have to win an argument with it tonight. Say it out loud to someone who loves you, and let them answer it for you.

Each of the common explanations holds something true. Doctors know these thoughts can come with depression and other illnesses that respond to treatment, and with long pain, heavy stress, or a crushing loss. Counselors know how pain narrows what a person can see, until it's hard to imagine anything ever changing. The church has long treated despair as a matter of the soul as well as the body. You may need all three kinds of help, and there's no shame in needing them. But an explanation can't sit up with you through the night. A person can.

## What Scripture says
Scripture doesn't look away from the wish to die. It keeps the prayers of faithful people who reached that place, and it shows God meeting one of them with bread and sleep before anything else. Read these slowly, one at a time.

> "He sat down under a broom tree and prayed that he might die. “I have had enough, LORD,” he said. “Take my life, for I am no better than my fathers.” Then he lay down under the broom tree and fell asleep. Suddenly an angel touched him and said, “Get up and eat.”" (1 Kings 19:4-5, BSB)

Elijah had just seen God answer with fire on Mount Carmel, yet one threat from Queen Jezebel sent him running, alone, asking to die. God didn't grant that prayer or scold it. He let Elijah sleep, set bread and water by his head, and woke him a second time to eat again (1 Kings 19:5-8). Later he gave him Elisha as a companion and told him he wasn't the only faithful one left (1 Kings 19:16-21). God met a man who asked to die with food, rest, and company.

> "O LORD, the God of my salvation, day and night I cry out before You. May my prayer come before You; incline Your ear to my cry. For my soul is full of troubles, and my life draws near to Sheol." (Psalm 88:1-3, BSB)

Psalm 88 is the darkest prayer in the Psalms. Its heading names Heman the Ezrahite, who says plainly that his life has come near the grave, and it never turns the corner into praise; it ends with "darkness is my closest companion" (Psalm 88:18). Yet it's a prayer from the first line to the last, spoken to "the God of my salvation," and it keeps coming back: "in the morning my prayer comes before You" (Psalm 88:13). If all you can do tonight is tell God the truth, that is still prayer.

> "We were under a burden far beyond our ability to endure, so that we despaired even of life. Indeed, we felt we were under the sentence of death, in order that we would not trust in ourselves, but in God, who raises the dead." (2 Corinthians 1:8-9, BSB)

Paul never says exactly what happened to him in the province of Asia, only that it was a deadly peril (2 Corinthians 1:10) that felt like a death sentence. He wasn't describing a wish to die. He was describing a weight so far beyond his strength that he expected it to kill him, and it taught him to rely on "God, who raises the dead." He hopes God will deliver him again "as you help us by your prayers" (2 Corinthians 1:11). An apostle who despaired of life leaned on other people's prayers, and he wasn't ashamed to say so.

## Why this is so hard
Part of what makes this so hard is what long pain does to time. When you've hurt enough for long enough, the present starts to feel permanent, and tomorrow looks exactly like tonight. It happens to people of deep faith and sharp minds alike, and it's part of why the same thoughts can feel so certain at two in the morning and less certain after sleep, a meal, and an honest conversation.

Some of the reasons are older than any of us. In England, suicide was a crime as well as a sin for centuries, and it stayed a crime until 1961. The Church of England's Book of Common Prayer of 1662 forbade its burial service for people who had taken their own lives, and other churches refused Christian burial too. Families learned to hide how someone they loved had died, and people carrying these thoughts learned to hide them as well. It's why this page says a person died by suicide, and leaves aside the older verb that belongs to crimes and sins.

That hard line came from a serious conviction, and it deserves a fair hearing. Christians confess that life is God's gift, not our possession, and many feared that a person who ended their own life left no time to repent. They meant to protect life. What they built, too often, was a silence, and the silence did its own harm. I'm a pastor, so I write this from inside that failure. Those of us who preach have sometimes spoken of suicide only as a warning, and never as something a person in the pew might be carrying that very morning.

The silence was never faithful to the church's own Lord. On the night before he was crucified, Jesus told his closest friends, "My soul is consumed with sorrow to the point of death. Stay here and keep watch" (Mark 14:34). He wasn't wishing to die; he was facing the cross. But he knew anguish that reaches toward death, he said so out loud, and he asked his friends to stay. The next day he cried out the first line of Psalm 22: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Mark 15:34). You aren't praying to a God who has never been in the dark himself.

If you've told yourself that asking for help would make you a burden, look at what he did in the worst hours of his life. He asked his friends for company. And the church's instruction about burdens is to share them: "Carry one another's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). The weight you're afraid to hand anyone was never meant for one person to carry.

## What to do this week
These are for the days after the worst of the night. If you haven't talked to anyone yet, start with the lines at the top of this page, then come back.

**1. Tell one person, in plain words.** Pick one person you trust and tell them plainly: *I've been having thoughts of ending my life.* Softer words, like *I've been down lately*, let people miss what you mean. If saying it out loud is too hard, text it, or write it down and hand it over.

**2. See a doctor or a licensed counselor.** Make the appointment this week, and use the same plain words when you get there. A doctor can look at your body as well as your mood, and there are treatments that help. Going isn't a lack of faith. It's caring for the life God gave you.

**3. Write a plan for the hard nights.** While you're steady, write a short plan, ideally with a counselor: what tends to come before a hard night, what helps you through the next hour, who you'll call, and how you'll stay safe, including keeping distance from anything you've thought about using. A plan made in daylight thinks for you when the night won't.

**4. Know the thoughts can come and go.** A quiet week doesn't mean the thoughts are gone for good, and a loud night doesn't mean they'll always be this loud. If they come back, use your plan and call your person. When they're quiet, keep your appointments anyway. With treatment, support, and time, they can grow less intense.

## When to get more help
Thoughts of ending your life are always more than a pastoral matter. I'm a pastor, and I want you to have one, but you also need a doctor and a licensed counselor, and you don't have to wait for the thoughts to become a plan before you get them. Start with your doctor, or a clinic if you don't have one. To find a counselor, ask your doctor for a referral, check your insurance company's directory, or ask a pastor which counselors they trust. If you want a Christian counselor, look for one who is also licensed, and confirm the license with your state's licensing board. A counselor can also help you write the plan described above. If you ever feel you might act on the thoughts, don't wait for an appointment: call 911, go to the nearest emergency room, or reach the lines at the top of this page.

Signs:
- You've thought about how or when, or started getting ready. Call or text 988 now, or call 911.
- You feel you might act on the thoughts soon. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.
- The thoughts are coming more often or are harder to push away.
- You've been giving things away, saying goodbye, or putting your affairs in order.

**The first call.** You can say it in one sentence: *I've been having thoughts of ending my life, and I need to be seen soon.* Saying it plainly tells them how quickly to help. Ask whether they're licensed in your state and take your insurance. If your faith matters to you, say so and ask whether it will be welcome.

**What it costs.** Cost shouldn't keep you from help, and the lines at the top of this page are free. Ask about sliding-scale fees, community mental health centers, university training clinics, an employee assistance program at work, or a church benevolence fund. If you're in danger, don't let cost keep you from an emergency room.

## If you're helping someone
If someone has told you they want to die, or you're afraid they might, you don't need perfect words. Take any talk of wanting to die or of being a burden seriously, even when it comes out as a joke. Ask directly: *Are you thinking about suicide?* Asking plainly won't put the idea in their head; it tells them they can finally say it. Then listen, without arguing or rushing to fix it. If they say yes, stay with them, or make sure someone else can. Gently ask whether they've thought about how, since that tells you how urgent this is, and help put distance between them and anything they might use. Call or text 988 together, or call 911 if they're in immediate danger. Don't promise to keep it secret; promise to stay. And if you're a pastor, save the sermon. Sit down, ask, and make the call together.

Things you can say:
- Are you thinking about suicide?
- Thank you for telling me. I'm staying right here.
- You're not a burden to me.
- Let's call 988 together, right now.

Things not to say:
- Think of what this would do to your family.
- You have so much to live for.
- Suicide is selfish.
- Just pray about it.
- I promise I won't tell anyone.

Follow up the next day and the days after; the National Institute of Mental Health notes that staying in touch after a crisis can make a difference. Offer to drive them to their appointment, and bring others in so you don't carry this alone. If you're a teenager worried about a friend, tell a parent, a teacher, or a school counselor, even if your friend asked you not to.

## Questions people ask
### What does the Bible say about suicide?
Scripture treats every life as God's gift (Acts 17:25), which is why Christians have long taught that a life is not ours to end. It also shows God meeting Elijah, who asked to die, with food and sleep instead of a rebuke (1 Kings 19:4-8). When a jailer in Philippi was about to take his own life, Paul called out, "Do not harm yourself! We are all here!" (Acts 16:28). Before daylight, that man was washing his prisoners' wounds and setting a meal before them (Acts 16:33-34).

### Is suicide an unforgivable sin?
Suicide is not the unforgivable sin Jesus warned about; he named that sin (Matthew 12:31-32) when religious leaders watched him heal a man and said he did it by the devil's power. The church's historic views on suicide are serious, and they have changed. For centuries many Christians taught that it left no time to repent, and churches refused Christian burial to people who died by suicide. The Catholic Church's 1983 code of church law dropped that rule, and its Catechism teaches that we should not despair of their salvation; the Church of England's General Synod voted in 2017 to lift its own restriction. Christians still differ over any one person's eternal state, and that judgment belongs to God. If you're asking about your own life, hear this first: God's answer to your pain is not your death. Please call or text 988 now, or call 911 if you're in danger.

### Is it a sin to have suicidal thoughts?
No. A thought you didn't ask for is not a choice you made, and it doesn't mean your faith is fake. Job cursed the day he was born (Job 3:1), Jeremiah wished he had never been born (Jeremiah 20:14-18), and Scripture keeps their words instead of hiding them. What matters is what you do with the thoughts: bring them into the open, to God and to someone who can help.

### What if my family would be better off without me?
That thought can feel like the most loving one you've had, which is what makes it so dangerous. The National Institute of Mental Health lists talk of being a burden among the warning signs of suicide. The thought is the pain speaking, not a clear view of your life. Don't try to settle it alone at night. Tell someone who loves you, in those exact words, and let them answer it.

### Will asking someone about suicide put the idea in their head?
No. The National Institute of Mental Health says studies show that asking people whether they're suicidal does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior, and that talking about it may even reduce those thoughts. Asking plainly, *Are you thinking about suicide?*, tells them you can hear the answer. If they say yes, stay with them, call or text 988 together, and call 911 if they're in immediate danger.
