## Chapter 1: The Part of the Letter We Never Read

You've seen the verse. You just haven't seen where it lives.

"For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope." It's on the coffee mug and the graduation card. Somebody probably read it over you at a commencement. It might be the most quoted verse in the American church, and we've turned it into a private promise — that my dreams are about to land, that God is lining up good things with my name on them, on a schedule I'll live to see.

We pulled that off with a little quiet surgery. We cut the verse loose from the sentences around it, the ones nobody reads. And those sentences were written to people whose whole world had just burned to the ground.

So go back and read them. In 597 BC the armies of Babylon came for Jerusalem. The boy king Jehoiachin gave up after three months, and the winners did what winners did back then. They marched the best of the nation a thousand miles east — the king, the metalworkers, the soldiers, the families that mattered, ten thousand of them by the book of Kings — and left a puppet named Zedekiah behind to keep the peace. Ten years later the puppet got brave, bet on Egypt, and Babylon came back to finish the job. They starved the city until people ate things I won't write down. They burned the temple, the one place on earth God was supposed to live. They made the king watch them kill his sons, and then they put his eyes out, so that was the last thing he ever saw.

Understand who these people were. They'd been told their whole lives that God would never let this happen. The temple was the guarantee. Zion couldn't fall. Now they're sitting in the ash of every promise they thought they understood, in a country that isn't theirs, surrounded by people who pray to other gods and have no reason to be kind.

That's who got the letter. The first wave, already hauled off in 597, grieving and lost and desperate to go home. That's who Jeremiah sat down and wrote to.

The plans verse is the eleventh line of that letter. We treat it as the headline. In the letter it's closer to a footnote, a bit of reassurance bolted onto the end of a much harder word. And the harder word is the one we've spent twenty-five centuries refusing to read. Because the first thing the letter does is take away the only hope the exiles wanted. There were prophets among them, men like Hananiah, telling the people exactly what their grief was begging to hear — that the nightmare was short, that inside two years God would break the yoke of Babylon and bring everyone home and set the furniture back where it stood. Jeremiah, speaking for the same God, called that a lie. He handed them a number instead. A number so cruel nobody selling comfort has ever wanted to say it out loud.

Seventy years. A lifetime. Most of you will die here. Your grandchildren will see Jerusalem. You won't. Settle in. This isn't a weekend and it isn't a detour. It's the rest of your life, and your children's lives, and you'd better learn how to live it.

And then, having taken away the fantasy of rescue, the letter does the shocking thing. It doesn't tell the exiles to fight. It doesn't tell them to disappear. It tells them to live. Here's what God actually said, with none of the sanding we've done to it: "Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease." And then the line that should stop the heart of anyone who's watched their country slide out from under them: "But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare."

Build. Plant. Marry. Multiply. Seek. Pray. And behind the last two, holding them up, a command so total it doesn't need its own verse because it runs under all the rest: do not bow. Do not worship what they worship. Be the most present, most generous people in Babylon, and belong, all the way, to Someone else.

Read them together and count them, because the count matters. Six commands handed to a beaten people, and every one of them refuses the two things grief reaches for first. They aren't told to keep their bags packed and wait it out in protest. They aren't told to melt into Babylon and forget who they are. They're told to do the third and stranger thing: put down roots in the soil of the empire that ruined them, seek its good, and bow to none of its gods. That's the letter. That's the whole letter. And it's written, I'm going to spend this book arguing, to us.

I didn't always see it that way. For a long time I read Jeremiah 29 the way everybody reads it, as a nice private promise with an awkward history attached, and I preached it that way too, God help me, at more than one graduation. What changed came slow and came hard. It was the growing sense that the letter isn't describing my feelings. It's describing my address. It's written to a people who've lost a home they thought was permanent, who are grieving a world that felt like the water they swam in, who are tempted by exactly two bad answers and are handed a third. That is not a metaphor for the American church. That's a description of it, close enough to be uncomfortable, and the discomfort is where wisdom starts.

Let me say it plainly, because the whole book depends on getting it right, and most of the American church still can't make itself say it out loud. For sixteen hundred years, and in this country for the better part of four centuries, Christianity had an arrangement we've come to call Christendom. The faith was the assumed water everyone swam in. Culturally established. Socially respectable. Woven so far into the civic furniture that you didn't have to believe much of anything to get carried along by it. That arrangement is over. It's not coming back in our lifetime or our children's. And what died with it was never Christianity. It was Christianity's scaffolding — the borrowed power, the easy respect, the vague civic warmth. The church has been weeping at the wrong funeral. We stood over the body of cultural Christianity as if it were the body of the church, and mistook the loss of our privileges for the loss of our God. We're exiles now, a moral and spiritual minority in a country that no longer shares our memory. And the sooner we quit waiting for the water to come rushing back, the sooner we can do the one thing the exiles were actually told to do, which is live, and live well, here.

> We've been weeping at the wrong funeral. What died was not Christianity but Christendom, the scaffolding and not the building. We mistook the one for the other because we were told, for so long, that they were the same.

I can say this with a freedom I didn't earn and won't pretend to, because I never swam in the water in the first place. I didn't grow up Christian. I came to Christ out of atheism, as a grown man, in the full daylight of a secular age, as exactly the kind of skeptic the hardest chapters of this book are written to reach. I've never once needed the culture to assume the faith on my behalf, because the culture I lived in assumed the opposite, and Jesus turned out to be enough anyway. That's not a small thing to say to a church in a panic. But I won't overplay the hand, because I've watched something happen in my own chest that I didn't expect. The longer I've pastored inside Christendom's ruins, the more I've caught myself missing a world I never actually lived in. Aching for a deference I was never owed. Wanting the room on my side the way it was for the generation before me. So I'm not writing this from above the grief. I'm writing it from inside a grief I have less right to than most people reading it, which is its own kind of rebuke, and I feel it.

Let me be honest about what a book like this can and can't do. A diagnosis, once you have it, is strangely useless. It tells you what happened. The water drained, the arrangement collapsed, the country moved on and isn't coming back to apologize. It does not tell you how to get out of bed on a Tuesday and be a Christian in the world that's left. And we've had plenty of diagnosis. The books, the conferences, the laments, the graphs about decline and the hand-wringing about the rising tide of the religiously unaffiliated. Much of it is true. Almost none of it helps, because a man who knows only that he's in exile knows only that he's lost. The letter is the cure for that particular paralysis, because the letter doesn't stop at you are in exile. It keeps going, out of the indicative and into the imperative. Into verbs. Into the unglamorous, load-bearing business of how an exile actually builds a life. This book is those verbs. It spends most of its length not proving we're in Babylon, which takes only a few chapters, but learning, slow and concrete, how a faithful people lives there.

And the verbs turn on a single word, which is why the platform I write from carries the name it does. Seek the welfare of the city, God says, and in its welfare you'll find your welfare. The word translated welfare, both times, is the Hebrew shalom. And shalom is not the thin thing we've cut it down to. It's not quiet. It's not the absence of conflict. It's not a private calm you buy with a meditation app. It's wholeness. Flourishing. The way things are meant to be when they're finally right, a world stitched back together at every seam. Shalom is the city where the hungry are fed and the stranger is safe and the work is honest and the weak aren't crushed and the streets are at peace after dark. It's the shape of the world God intended before it broke, and the shape he's promised to put back. And God tells the exiles to seek that — not for Jerusalem, which is gone, but for Babylon, the empire that burned his temple and blinded their king. He tells them to work for the flourishing of the place that ruined them.

Then he does something that ought to take the breath away. He ties their own flourishing to it, so tight the two can't be pulled apart. In its welfare, he says, you'll find your welfare. In its shalom, your shalom. You won't find your wholeness by hoarding it behind a wall while the city outside rots. You won't find it by conquering the city, or fleeing it, or walling it out. You'll find it the only place it's ever been found, by spending it on the good of a city that isn't your home and never will be. This runs against every instinct a frightened minority has. The instinct says protect what's yours, circle the wagons, secure the remnant, and shalom will follow. God says the opposite. Pour yourselves out for the good of Babylon, and your own good is hidden inside Babylon's, and there's no other door to it. To live well, in the sense that verse means, is to seek the life of a city that doesn't know you. That's why the platform is called what it is. Not live comfortably. Not live safely. Live well, in the full weight of shalom, which turns out to be inseparable from the welfare of everyone around you.

That sentence is the whole argument of this book in miniature, and it cuts against the two answers the church keeps reaching for — the same two answers the exiles were tempted by and Jeremiah forbade. There's the answer that wants to fight its way home, take the country back by force, grab the levers of culture and law and the state and crank the old Christendom back into place. And there's the answer that wants to stop being different at all, to file down every offensive edge of the faith until it dissolves into the surrounding culture and the ache of being a stranger finally goes away. One wants to conquer Babylon. The other wants to move in and change its name to home. I'm going to make the strongest case I can for both before I refuse either, because the pull of them is enormous, I feel both in myself, and a church that only sneers at the people caught in them hasn't understood the grief underneath.

Take the first answer, the one that wants the country back. It isn't held only by cynics and opportunists, whatever the caricature says. Underneath the worst of it there's very often a real love. A love of a country and an inheritance that genuinely gave people good things — schools that taught the children something true, neighborhoods where a door could be left open at night, a shared moral vocabulary that made a common life possible. And there's real grief at watching it dissolve, and real fear for the grandchildren who'll grow up in the ruins with none of it. Those loves are not nothing. A pastor who can't feel their weight has no business speaking to the people who carry them. But name the thing at the root, because the root is rotten even where the grief is honest. The take-it-back answer believes the kingdom of God can be advanced by the sword of Caesar. And the moment it believes that, it's lost the ability to tell the two cities apart. It mistakes the nation for the kingdom. It baptizes a tribe. It produces a Christianity finally indistinguishable from a voting bloc with a worship band. You can win every election ever held with that faith and lose the one thing the church was ever sent into the world to keep.

Now take the second answer, the one that wants to stop being strange. It deserves better than a sneer too, because it almost always shows up as compassion and sometimes sincerely is. It's a desire to remove needless offense, to quit defending things that were never worth defending, to love the very people the church has too often wounded and driven off, to be done with a cruelty that was always a betrayal of the gospel it claimed to protect. Those instincts aren't all wrong. Some of what the accommodating church wants to shed genuinely deserved shedding. But name the root here too, with the same honesty. The give-it-up answer believes the church survives by agreeing with the world. And a church that agrees with the world about everything has nothing left to say to it and no reason on earth to exist. It'll be applauded all the way to its own empty buildings, because the world has never once needed a slightly more spiritual version of what it already believes. A faith that exists only to bless the going consensus dies the moment the consensus stops needing its blessing. And the consensus always stops needing it.

Look at what the two answers have in common, because it's the most important thing on these pages, and it's the thing neither can see about itself. Both are driven, underneath the bluster on the one side and the compassion on the other, by the same fear. Strip them down and neither actually believes the faith can survive without the culture's backing. The one who reaches for power can't imagine a faithful church that isn't also a powerful one. The one who reaches for approval can't imagine a faithful church the culture despises. Different reaches. Identical terror. Both looked at the drained water and concluded, in their bones, that the church can't live without it. And both are wrong. The back half of this book is the proof, because there was a church before there was ever any water — a church born powerless and despised in the heart of a pagan empire, and it didn't merely survive there. It came alive there. It always has. The exiles were never offered either of the two answers. They were handed a third and harder thing, and this book is about that third thing: stay, and build, and bless, and stay entirely distinct, in a city they'd never control and were told to love anyway.

I won't pretend I stand outside any of this, because I don't, and the man who's felt only one of these pulls hasn't been honest with himself. There's a version of me that wants the old deference back, that misses being able to assume the room, that would gladly crank the water on again if someone showed me the valve. There's a version of me that wants the clever world's approval, that would love to be the kind of Christian the people who matter find reasonable and unembarrassing at a dinner party. And there's a third version, on the tired days, that wants only to pull my wife and my five sons behind a wall and let the whole burning city fend for itself. All three of those wants are the flesh dressed up as faithfulness. All three are the cultural Christian in me grieving his lost water. And the man who met Christ in the open sea, with no water under him and no culture holding him up, knows better than all three, because he knows the church was born with none of the things the three answers are frantic to get back, and it converted an empire anyway, from the bottom of a prison, with nothing but its life.

Let me map the road, so you know where this goes. There are three movements. The first is diagnosis, and it's short, because it has to be, because diagnosis is the part the church has already overdosed on. We'll make the water visible — the sixteen hundred years of Christendom that ran from a Roman emperor's battlefield conversion to a few decades ago in America — so you can finally see the thing you've been swimming in. We'll watch the scaffolding come down in the actual numbers, and in the deeper change underneath them, the quiet move of the throne from God to the self that the sociologist Robert Bellah saw coming as early as 1985. We'll let the philosopher Charles Taylor name our condition for what it is, a secular age, not a world emptied of belief but a world where belief has become one contested option among many. And we'll look hard at the two, or really the three, bad answers, and see that they're secretly the same answer, and refuse them.

The second and longest movement is the letter itself, taken command by command, verb by verb, because the letter isn't a mood. It's a set of instructions, and instructions are meant to be obeyed, not admired. We'll sit with the seventy years until the number stops being cruel and starts being merciful, until we can tell the true prophet, who hands you a shovel, from the false one, who promises your old country by the weekend. Then we'll build houses and learn what it costs to put your weight down in a place, with the writer Wendell Berry as our witness against a nation of people who can't stay anywhere. We'll learn to live in the houses we build, to work in Babylon as people who know there's no such thing as secular labor, and to rest in Babylon as people who refuse to let the empire measure them by their output. We'll plant gardens, which is to say we'll learn the long obedience Eugene Peterson wrote about, the willingness to plant slow things whose fruit we'll never taste. We'll eat the produce, because the God who sent his people into exile didn't send them into a joyless one. We'll take wives and raise children, and find that the home is the first and most durable outpost of the faith in any foreign land, the place where the next generation of exiles is either formed or forfeited, one kitchen table at a time. We'll seek the welfare of the city, and pray for it, and hold the hard line under all of it, which is that we do not bow.

The third movement is the great cloud of witnesses, the proof that this has been done before and can be done again. We'll stand with the church before the empire, the powerless and hunted minority that grew at something near forty percent a decade for three centuries, and ask with the sociologist Rodney Stark the only question that matters, which is why. The answer wasn't strategy and it wasn't cultural power, because they had none. They lived in a way the empire couldn't explain. They stayed to nurse the sick through plagues everyone else fled. They gathered up the infants the empire threw away. They sat slave and master at one table and called them brothers. We'll sit beside Augustine as he watches Rome fall in 410 and writes the book that should be the constitution of every Christian living after Christendom, the book that pries the City of God apart from the city of man with both hands and shows a panicked church that the burning empire was never the indestructible kingdom in the first place. And we'll end where the whole thing has been heading, with the recognition that the future of the American church doesn't belong to stronger tribes but to deeper disciples, and that these are not two flavors of one project but two roads running opposite directions, and that we are early, very early, in something long.

That last word is the one to carry out of this chapter, because everything going wrong in the church right now goes wrong because we're in a hurry, and the people of God were never meant to be in a hurry. You and I are not going to see the end of this. The recovery of a deep, faithful, post-Christendom church is not a project we'll complete or a problem we'll solve inside a program cycle or a campaign or a single lifetime. We're the early exiles. The early exiles planted gardens whose fruit fed people they'd never meet, and copied Scripture by candlelight through centuries whose dawn they didn't live to see. If we can't make our peace with that, we'll keep grabbing for the fast and ruinous answers, because the fast answers are finally just the answers of people who can't bear to wait. The man who learns to live well in exile is not a man who never felt the pull to go home. He's a man who heard the prophet say seventy years, and grieved it, and then picked up a shovel and planted a garden in the Babylonian dirt anyway, because that was what faithfulness looked like in the world as it actually was and not the world he wished he lived in. That's the whole vocation. And it's available to a church with no power at all, which is the only kind of church that has ever changed anything.

So let me hand you the letter, the way it was actually written — the hard word, the strange commands, and the buried promise that your welfare is hidden inside the welfare of a city you didn't choose. But we can't start with the commands, because the exiles couldn't start there either. We have to start where they started, with the grief, and with the honest work of admitting what's actually happened to the world that used to feel like home. And the first and hardest part of that work is the part the two young fish couldn't manage in the old story, the part about the water. You were swimming in something so long you stopped being able to see it. So long it stopped feeling like a culture at all and simply felt like reality. Like air. Like the way things are. You can't learn to live after Christendom until you can see the Christendom you're living after. So before we build a single house, we have to make the water visible, and admit it's already drained away into the sand.
