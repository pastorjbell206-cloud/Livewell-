## Introduction: The Part of the Letter We Never Read

There is a verse from the prophet Jeremiah that you have seen on a coffee mug, a graduation card, a wall hanging in a Christian bookstore. For I know the plans I have for you, declares the Lord, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope. We have made it the most personal verse in the Bible, a private promise that things are going to work out for me, and we did it by tearing it loose from the four verses that come before it, which almost no one reads, and which were written to people whose whole world had just ended.

In 597 before Christ the army of Babylon took the first wave of Jerusalem's people a thousand miles east into captivity, and a decade later they came back and burned the temple to the ground. To the exiles already in Babylon, sitting in the wreckage of everything that had told them who they were, the prophet Jeremiah sent a letter. The plans verse is the eleventh line of that letter. The first lines are not a comfort. They are a set of instructions, and they are the most surprising instructions in the Old Testament, because they tell a defeated and homesick people not to do the two things every defeated and homesick people want to do.

I have argued the diagnosis at length in another book, so I will not relitigate it here. The short version is the one most of the American church still cannot bring itself to accept. The long arrangement we call Christendom, in which the culture assumed the faith and carried it for free, is over, and it is not coming back, and what died with it was never Christianity but only Christianity's cultural scaffolding. We are exiles now. We are a minority in a place that no longer shares our memory. That is the diagnosis, and a diagnosis, once you have it, is strangely useless. It tells you what happened. It does not tell you how to live on Tuesday.

The letter does. And it does not stop at you are in exile. If it did, it would be a death certificate. It keeps going, into the imperative mood, into verbs. Build. Plant. Marry. Multiply. Seek. Pray. Six commands to people who expected to be told either to fight their way home or to lie down and disappear. God told them to do neither. He told them to live, fully and fruitfully and on purpose, in the one place they were sure no real life could be lived. He told them to make Babylon bloom.

This book is those verbs. It is not another diagnosis of how bad things are, because you have had enough of those and so have I. It is the part of the letter we never read, worked patiently into an ordinary life, into your work and your house and your marriage and your children and your street and your prayers. It is an attempt to answer the only question that finally matters once the grief has had its say. Not how do we get our country back, and not how do we make peace with losing it, but the older and harder question the exiles were handed first. How do we live, and live well, here.

The whole letter turns on a single word, and that word is the reason this book has the title it does. Seek the welfare of the city, God says, and in its welfare you will find your welfare. The Hebrew word under welfare is shalom, and shalom does not mean what we have shrunk it to. It is not the absence of conflict or the calm of a private heart. It is wholeness, flourishing, the way things are supposed to be, a city where the hungry are fed and the stranger is safe and the work is good and the weak are not crushed. God tells the exiles to seek that, for Babylon, the empire that burned their temple. And He tells them that their own flourishing is hidden inside it. You will not find your shalom by guarding it. You will find it by spending it on the good of a city that is not your home.

That is what it means to live well in exile. Not to wait out the captivity with your bags packed. Not to forget you are a captive and melt into the crowd. To build, and plant, and bless, and stay strange, in a place you did not choose, for the sake of a God the place does not know. The exile is not the interruption of the good life. For the people of God it has nearly always been the address where the good life is learned.

So let me hand you the verbs, one at a time.

## Build Houses

The first thing God tells the exiles to do is the most ordinary thing imaginable, and that is exactly its power. Build houses and live in them. Not tents. Not anything you can strike and carry when the call to march home finally comes. Houses, with foundations, the kind of thing a man builds when he has decided he is staying. To a people straining toward the horizon for the first sign of rescue, God says the one thing they did not want to hear. Stay. You live here now. Build something you cannot take with you.

There are two ways to hold a place you did not choose, and the instruction to build houses kills them both. The first is the suitcase. The suitcase Christian keeps his bags by the door. He is only passing through, he reminds you, this world is not his home, and he means it as piety but it functions as an excuse. Why invest in a city you are leaving. Why pour yourself into the schools or the streets or the long civic work of a place that is under judgment anyway. The suitcase looks spiritual and is really just a refusal to love what God has told you to love. The second way is the bunker. The bunker Christian also knows he does not belong, but his answer is to wall himself in, to build a parallel everything, to pull his family and his money and his attention back behind the fortifications and wait for the barbarians to lose interest. The suitcase will not invest in Babylon. The bunker will not touch it. God forbids them both with four words. Build houses and live in them.

A built house is a confession. It says I am staying, I am exposed, I have decided that this particular patch of a pagan empire is worth the labor of permanence. And permanence, it turns out, is the precondition of every good thing the rest of the letter asks for. You cannot seek the welfare of a city you are about to abandon. You cannot love a neighbor you have walled out. Wendell Berry has spent a lifetime making this single point against an American economy built to move us, that there are two kinds of people, the ones he calls boomers, who treat every place as a resource to use up and leave, and the ones he calls stickers, who settle, who stay, who learn a place well enough to love it and tend it across a lifetime. The exiles are commanded to become stickers in Babylon. You cannot love a place in general. You can only love it in particular, this street, these neighbors, this work, this long unglamorous staying.

And staying means working, because a house has to be paid for, and the exiles took their place in the ordinary economy of Babylon. They did not retreat into a holy enclave funded by nostalgia. They learned trades, kept books, built things, served a pagan city's needs and were paid for it, and in doing so they discovered what the church keeps having to rediscover, that there is no such thing as secular work for a person who belongs to God. The Reformers recovered this and gave it a name. Vocation. The conviction that the milkmaid at her pail and the magistrate at his bench serve God as truly as the priest at his altar, that God is not waiting for you in the religious corner of your life but is already present in the work of your hands, the spreadsheet, the classroom, the loading dock, the exam room. Tim Keller spent a book arguing that the gospel transforms how and why and for whom we work, that the Christian in Babylon does not clock in to fund the real Christian life that happens on Sunday but offers the work itself, done with excellence and integrity, as a way of seeking the city's good. Your cubicle is one of the houses Jeremiah told you to build.

> A built house is a bet on staying. And almost nothing the gospel asks of you is possible until you have made that bet, because you cannot bless a place you are standing on with one foot out the door.

But notice the second half of the command, because we are prone to hear only the first. Build houses, and live in them. Not just build. Live. A house is not a worksite, it is a place you are meant to inhabit, to rest in, to be a human being and not a machine in. The exile who builds and builds and never lives has not actually obeyed, he has only relocated his striving. This is why the people of God have always carried Sabbath into exile with them, the weekly refusal to work that declares with the whole body that the world is held by God and not by my effort. Abraham Joshua Heschel called the Sabbath a palace in time, a sanctuary you build not out of stone but out of hours, and he understood that a people can be enslaved in a free land if they never stop, and free in a hostile one if they know how to rest. To live in the house you have built, to actually dwell there and not merely maintain it, is its own quiet resistance to a Babylon that measures you by your output and would gladly consume every hour you have. Build the house. Then have the nerve to live in it.
