## Introduction: The Funeral That Told the Truth

I have stood at the front of a lot of funerals. Fifteen years of it now. I have watched the same casket wheeled up the same aisle under the same fluorescent light, and I have said, more times than I can count, the words a pastor is supposed to say. But there is one funeral I keep returning to, because it was the day I heard the church tell the truth about what it actually believes, and the truth was smaller than the Bible.

The man in the box was named Walter. He had farmed the same ground outside Fenton for fifty-one years. He had calloused hands and a slow way of talking and a habit of showing up at the church before anyone else on a Sunday to run the vacuum, because he did not think a man should sit while a thing needed doing. When Walter died, his daughter asked me to say something at the graveside, and then his brother stood up first, uninvited, and said the thing I have heard a thousand times.

He said, Walter is not here. This is just the shell. He is up in heaven now, looking down on us, and one day we will all be together with him there, far from all this.

Everyone nodded. It was a comfort. I do not blame the man for saying it, and I do not doubt he loved his brother. But I stood at the edge of that grave, next to that farmer's body, on the ground that farmer had loved, and I felt the whole weight of a lie the church has been telling itself so long it does not know it is a lie anymore. Not a wicked lie. A comfortable one. The most comfortable lie there is.

The lie is that the point of the whole thing is to leave. To get the soul out of the body, out of the dirt, out of the farm and the calloused hands and the slow talking, and up into a location called heaven where none of it follows you. The lie is that the body in the box is a shell, and the earth we buried it in is a waiting room, and the entire story of God is an evacuation plan.

I believed that once. I want to be careful here, because I came to all of this late and hard. I was an atheist until I was a grown man. I did not grow up in the church absorbing its comforts. I came to Christ as an adult who had spent years deciding the whole thing was a fairy tale for people who could not face the dark. And when I finally could not resist it any longer, when the resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth broke over me as something that had actually happened in actual history, I walked into the church expecting to find people who believed that. People whose whole life was built on the fact that a dead man got up.

What I found instead, mostly, was people waiting to die so they could go somewhere else. Sincere people. Kind people. People who would give you the coat off their back. But their hope, when you pressed on it, was not resurrection. It was escape. It was the shell in the box and the soul in the clouds. And I remember thinking, even then, even new, that this was not the thing that had knocked me down. The thing that had knocked me down was a tomb that was empty on a specific Sunday morning outside a specific city. Not a rumor of heaven. A body that was gone.

I want to tell you in this book that the church made a trade. Somewhere along the way, and I will show you where, we swapped the Bible's actual hope for a thinner one. We traded the new heaven and the new earth for a disembodied heaven only. We traded resurrection for immortality of the soul. We traded God's promise to make all things new for a promise to take us away from the things that break. And because it was a comfort, and because it was easy, and because it let us stop caring about a great many things it is hard to care about, we did not notice the price.

But there is a price. When your hope is escape, the body becomes disposable, because the body is what you are escaping. When your hope is escape, the earth becomes disposable, because the earth is the thing you are leaving behind. When your hope is escape, justice becomes optional, because why would you scrub the deck of a ship that is going down. When your hope is escape, grief has no floor under it, because you are pretending the body in the box was never really the person at all. I have watched all four of these prices get paid, in real people, in my own congregation, in my own heart. This is not a book I am writing at anyone. I am inside every indictment in it.

Let me say the thing plainly, because the rest of the book is the argument for it. The New Testament does not promise you a disembodied heaven as your final home. It promises resurrection. It promises that God is not going to abandon the material world He called good in the first chapter of the Bible, but redeem it. It promises a new heaven and a new earth, which is to say a renewed creation, and a city that comes down out of heaven to the earth rather than a people who float up out of the earth to heaven. It promises, as the scholar N.T. Wright put it in his 2007 book Surprised by Hope, life after life after death. There is a life immediately after you die, with Christ, and it is real and it is good. But it is not the end of the story. It is the waiting room. The end of the story is your body, raised, on a healed earth, under the reign of the God who made it.

That is a bigger hope than escape. It is also a harder one, because it will not let you off the hook about anything. If God is going to redeem this world and not junk it, then this world matters now. If God is going to raise your body and not discard it, then your body matters now. If the poor and the grieving and the wronged are not being written off as passengers on a doomed ship, then justice is not a distraction from the gospel but a rehearsal for the world that is coming. Escape lets you relax. Resurrection puts you to work.

I want to steelman the escape before I take it apart, because the people who hold it are not fools and are not, most of them, being lazy. The going-to-heaven language does something real. It says the dead are safe. It says God has not lost them. It says there is a place of comfort beyond the reach of cancer and car accidents and the long humiliation of old age. All of that is true, and I will defend it in these pages, and I will not let the recovery of resurrection cost us the genuine comfort inside the older way of speaking. When Walter's brother said his brother was with the Lord, he said something the Bible affirms. The problem was not what he affirmed. The problem was what he assumed it meant, and what he assumed it ruled out, and how small he had let the story become without ever choosing to.

So here is what this book is going to do. I am going to take you back to that funeral, and then further back, to the third and fourth centuries, to show you where the clouds-and-harps picture came from and why it is not actually the Bible's picture. I am going to walk you through what the New Testament actually promises, and I am going to plant the whole thing on the one fact everything else hangs on, which is that the tomb of Jesus was empty and His body was raised. Then I am going to follow the consequences out, all the way out, into your body and the dirt under your feet and the injustice in your city and the grave you have already visited and the one you have not yet had to.

I am not writing this to win an argument about eschatology. I could not care less about winning that argument. I am writing it because I have buried too many people under a hope that was too small, and I have watched too many people live small lives because they thought the real thing started somewhere else, and I have felt the pull of the same escape in my own chest at the funerals of people I loved. I have five sons. I was raised without a father, and I have spent my whole life since deciding what kind of world I was going to hand them. I refuse to hand them an escape hatch. I want to hand them a hope big enough to make them love the world God made and grieve it honestly and work in it like it matters, because it does.

Heaven is not your reward. I know that sentence sounds like a demolition, and to a certain picture it is. But hold it a moment before you flinch. What if the reason heaven is not your reward is that heaven, the disembodied waiting room, was never the point. What if the point was always bigger. What if the point was a raised body on a remade earth, God dwelling with His people, every tear wiped away by a hand you can feel, and Walter, calloused hands and all, standing on ground that does not die.

That is the hope I am asking you to want. Let me show you it was there the whole time.

## Chapter One: The Shell in the Box

I want to stay at the graveside a little longer, because the sentence Walter's brother said deserves to be taken seriously and not just used. This is just the shell. He is not here. I have heard some version of it at nearly every funeral I have ever conducted, and I have said versions of it myself, and I need you to understand that I am not sneering at it. I am grieving what it costs.

Start with what is true in it. When we say the dead person is not here, we are reaching for something real. The thing that made Walter Walter, the personality, the memory, the love, the will, is not lying in that box in any way we can reach. Whatever a human being is, it is not simply a quantity of meat, and when the meat stops, something has gone that the meat did not contain. The materialist, the version of me that argued against all of this for years, has a genuine problem here and knows it. You can weigh the body before and after death and the number does not change and yet everything has changed. Something is absent. The brother at the graveside is not wrong to feel it. He is naming a real absence with the only words his church ever gave him.

But watch what the words do next. This is just the shell. The word just is doing an enormous amount of work, and all of it is destructive. It says the body was a container, and the container is empty now, and the container was never the point. It says that what we are burying is packaging. And once you have said that, once you have called the body a shell, you have quietly agreed to a picture of the human person that the Bible does not teach and that Christianity spent its first centuries fighting.

The picture is this. A human being is a soul, and the soul is the real you, and the body is a temporary suit the soul wears. On this picture, death is not an enemy. Death is a graduation. Death is the moment the soul finally gets free of the suit. And salvation, on this picture, is essentially death perfected, the permanent liberation of the soul from the body, the shell discarded for good so the real you can float off unencumbered.

I want to say this as clearly as I can. That is not the Christian hope. That is a hope, and it is an old and respectable one, but it is the hope of Plato, not the hope of the empty tomb. It came into the church from outside and made itself so much at home that most Christians now assume it is the gospel. It is not. The Christian hope is not that the shell gets discarded. The Christian hope is that the shell gets raised.

Consider what the New Testament actually does with a dead body. When Jesus stands outside the tomb of Lazarus in John 11, He does not comfort Martha by telling her that Lazarus is a soul now, free at last, better off. He weeps. He is, the text says, deeply moved, and the word underneath the English is closer to angry, a snorting, indignant fury. Jesus stands in front of death and He is enraged. Why would He be enraged at a graduation. Why would the Son of God stand at a doorway of pure liberation and shake with anger. He is angry because death is not a graduation. Death is an enemy. Death is a violation of the world His Father made. And then He does the thing that gives the game away entirely. He does not release Lazarus into a better disembodied existence. He calls the body back out of the tomb. Lazarus, come out. The dead man walks out in his grave clothes, and the crowd that had come to comfort a soul gets a body instead.

That scene is a preview. It is not the main event, because Lazarus will die again, and the raising of Lazarus is a sign pointing forward to a raising that will not have to be repeated. But the direction of the sign is unmistakable. When God addresses death in the pages of the New Testament, He does not tidy up around it by relocating souls. He reverses it. He brings the body back.

Now hold that next to the funeral. This is just the shell. Do you feel the collision. At the tomb of Lazarus, God treats the body as the thing to be recovered. At the graveside of Walter, the church treats the body as the thing to be discarded. Somewhere between the Gospel of John and the funeral home, we changed sides. We started agreeing with death that the body does not matter. We started calling the enemy's verdict a comfort.

I felt this collision in my own body before I could name it. My grandmother died when I was still an unbeliever, still convinced the whole apparatus of faith was wishful thinking, and I remember standing at her open casket and being furious in a way I could not explain and did not want. Everyone around me was calm, murmuring the shell and the better place. And the atheist in me, the one who supposedly did not believe in any of it, wanted to grab someone by the collar and say, that is her. That is her hand. That is the hand that made the pie and held mine when I crossed the street. Do not tell me it is a shell. The hardest materialist and the most orthodox Christian ought to be able to stand together in that fury, because both of them, if they are honest, know that the body is not packaging. It is the person. It was always the person.

The word the Bible uses for the human being is not soul, floating free. In the second chapter of Genesis, God forms the man from the dust of the ground and breathes into him, and the man becomes a living creature, a living soul, and the Hebrew there does not mean God installed a soul into a body. It means the dust plus the breath together became a living being. You are not a soul that has a body. You are, in the biblical picture, an embodied soul, a besouled body, a unity so complete that to tear it apart is precisely the horror that death is. That is why Jesus is angry at the tomb. Death is not the soul being freed. Death is the human being being ripped in half. And the whole promise of the gospel is not that the tearing is fine because only the disposable half was lost. The promise is that God is going to put the halves back together, and more, He is going to do it in a body that can never be torn again.

I know the objection, because I have made it. Someone will say, Pastor, this is a distinction without a difference. Whether we float off as souls or get raised as bodies, either way we are with God and either way we are comforted, so why press so hard on it. Let people have their shell.

Here is why I press. Because the picture you hold of the end quietly governs how you live in the middle. A man who believes the body is a disposable shell will treat his body as disposable. He will treat other people's bodies as disposable. He will find it very easy to believe that what happens to bodies, to hungry bodies, to imprisoned bodies, to the bodies of the poor and the trafficked and the bombed, is a secondary matter, a temporary matter, packaging trouble, because in the end the bodies do not count. And a man who believes God is going to raise the body will find that he cannot look away from bodies at all. The doctrine is not academic. The doctrine walks into the food pantry and the hospital room and the voting booth. What you think happens to Walter's body is going to show up in how you treat the living.

So no, I will not let people have the shell. Not because I want to take a comfort away, but because I want to give a bigger one. I want to tell the brother at the graveside something better than his church told him. I want to say, you are right that Walter is not fully here, and you are right that he is safe with the Lord, and you are more right than you know, because this ground you are standing on is not his final defeat. This body you are burying is a seed, and the God who made it is not in the habit of losing what He has made. What goes into the ground is not garbage. It is planting. And planting is the most hopeful thing a farmer ever does.

Walter would have understood that. He put things in the ground his whole life on the faith that they would come up changed and greater. He would not have called a buried seed a shell. He would have called it the beginning.

That is where I want to take you. But first I have to show you how the shell got into the box in the first place. Because it was not always there. There was a time when the church stood at its gravesides and did not call the body a shell, but a seed. Something happened between then and now. Someone smuggled a different hope in through the back door, and the church, tired and frightened and wanting comfort, let it stay. Let me show you who, and when, and why it worked.
