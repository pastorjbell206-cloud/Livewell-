## Introduction: The Prayers That Hit the Ceiling

I have prayed prayers that went nowhere. I need you to know that before we go any further, because most books on prayer are written by people who seem to have never had the experience, or who have decided not to mention it. They begin at the destination. They describe a room I have stood outside of, cold, with my hand on a door that would not open. If that is where you are, I am not going to pretend I have never been there. I have prayed in the dark. I am writing this from inside it, not from the far side.

There is a particular sound a prayer makes when it stops at the ceiling. You will know it if you have heard it. It is not a sound, really. It is the absence of one. You say the words, out loud or under your breath, and they rise about as far as the light fixture and then they are just words in a room, and the room is quiet, and you are aware, suddenly and completely, of how quiet it is. You wonder if anyone is there. You wonder if you have been talking to yourself this whole time, the way a man in an empty house talks to a dog that died last spring out of a habit his mouth has not yet unlearned. I have had that thought at two in the morning. I have had it standing over a hospital bed. I have had it, God help me, in the pulpit, mid-sentence, while leading three hundred people in a prayer I was no longer sure I believed anyone was receiving.

I came to God late and from the outside. I was an atheist, a real one, not the polite agnostic who keeps a pew warm out of nostalgia. I believed there was no one there, and I had good company, and I was not unhappy about it. So when I say I know the suspicion that prayer is a man talking to the ceiling, I am not performing a doubt I overcame long ago as a rhetorical warm-up. I lived years inside that suspicion. And here is the thing I did not expect, the thing no one warned me about when I crossed over. Coming to faith did not end the suspicion. It relocated it. Before, I was sure no one was listening and it did not trouble me. After, I hoped someone was listening, and the silence, when it came, troubled me to the bone. Faith did not remove the possibility that I was talking to no one. It raised the stakes of the possibility.

I was also raised without a father. I mention this not to invite you into my griefs but because it is load-bearing for what follows. A boy who grows up calling out and getting no answer learns something in his body about calling out. He learns to expect the silence before he opens his mouth. When Jesus taught his followers to begin their praying with the word Father, he was not, for me, handing me a comfortable image. He was reaching straight into the wound. Some of you will find that the tenderest word in the language. Some of you will find it the hardest. I have found it both, on different days, and I no longer trust anyone who has found it only easy.

So let me say what this book is not, because you have been burned before and I want to spare you the slow disappointment of discovering it three chapters in. This is not a book that will teach you to pray so that it works. It is not a technique. It is not a set of steps that, correctly performed, produce the result you are after. There are books like that, whole shelves of them, and some of them are written by sincere people, and I think, on the whole, they do more harm than good, because they leave the reader who prays correctly and gets nothing convinced that the failure is his. He did the steps. The steps did not work. Therefore he is the broken part. That is a cruelty dressed as encouragement, and I will not do it to you.

What this book is, is honest. It takes aim at the hard parts and does not flinch. Unanswered prayer, the real kind, the prayer you prayed for a decade that heaven never granted. The silence of God, not explained away with a tidy reason but sat with, the way you sit with a friend in a hospital corridor when there is nothing left to say. Prayer when you are numb, when you feel nothing, when the words are dust in your mouth. Prayer when you are angry at God, actually angry, angry enough to stop, and the strange grace that this too is prayer. I am going to walk you into these rooms because you are already in them and it does you no good to be told they do not exist.

And I am going to try to recover something for you that I think has been stolen, quietly, by a whole culture of religion that should have known better. Prayer is not a vending machine. It is not the mechanism by which you insert the correct devotion and receive the desired outcome. When the Psalmists prayed, and they prayed more honestly than anyone before or since, they did not pray like that. They complained. They accused. They spread their rage out in front of God like a man emptying his pockets onto a table, and God, astonishingly, kept those prayers, canonized them, handed them down to us as Scripture, so that three thousand years later a raging, doubting man in Michigan could open the book and find his own worst thoughts already there, already prayed, already received. That is what I want to give back to you. Not a method. A conversation. One you are allowed to stay in even when you cannot see the face of the one you are talking to.

Eugene Peterson, who died in 2018 after a lifetime of teaching the church to pray the Psalms, wrote that we do not learn to pray by getting good at it. We learn to pray by praying, badly, honestly, in the dark, using the words God already gave us for exactly this. I believe that. I have staked a ministry on it. This book is my attempt to say it to you slowly, over many pages, in the hope that somewhere in here you find permission to open your mouth again. Not because it will work. Because staying in the conversation, even the silent one, even the furious one, even the one you are not sure anyone hears, is itself the thing. It is not the means to the life with God. It is the life with God. And it can be had in the dark. That is the whole book. The rest is just me trying to convince you it is true.

## Chapter One: When You Are Not Sure Anyone Is There

> The worst thing that can happen to a man who prays is not that God says no. It is that a man decides, without ever quite deciding, that there was never anyone to say it.

Let me tell you about the first prayer I ever prayed as a believer, because it did not go the way the testimonies go. In the testimonies, the new convert falls to his knees and is flooded with peace, and the room fills with a warmth he has no words for, and he rises a changed man. That is a real experience. I have met people who had it. I did not have it. I prayed my first prayer sitting on the edge of a bed in a rented room, and what I felt was mostly embarrassment, the specific embarrassment of a grown man doing something he has spent his whole adult life mocking. I felt like a fraud. I felt like I was talking to the furniture. And underneath both of those I felt a third thing, small and stubborn, which was the reason I kept going. I felt that if there was anyone there, I wanted to be found by him more than I wanted to protect my dignity. So I kept talking to the furniture, on the outside chance the furniture was God.

I want to sit with the not-being-sure, because it is where most of you live and almost no one will name it from a stage. The stage version of prayer assumes the connection. It assumes you know, in your bones, that God is present and attentive, the way you know your spouse is in the next room. From that assumption, prayer is easy to describe. You simply talk to the person you know is there. But that is not the situation of the honest praying person. The honest praying person is often not sure. Not intellectually unsure, necessarily, though that too. Unsure in the gut. He affirms in the creed that God hears, and he means it, and then he goes into his room and shuts the door and the room is just a room, and the affirmation and the experience do not match, and he does not know what to do with the gap.

Here is the first thing I want you to hear, and I want you to hear it before I have earned it, because you may not make it to the chapter where I earn it. The gap is not evidence against your faith. The gap is not a sign that you are doing it wrong. The gap is the ordinary condition of prayer for most of the people who have ever prayed seriously, and the tradition knew this, and named it, and did not panic about it. The mystics called it the absence. The Psalmists called it the hiding of God's face. Teresa of Avila, writing in Spain in the 1500s, spent whole books on the aridity of prayer, the dryness, the sense of drawing water from a dry well. She did not treat this as a problem to be solved by better technique. She treated it as a stage of the life with God, and in some cases the deepest stage. You are not below the tradition. You are inside it.

But I do not want to spiritualize the doubt too quickly, because that is its own kind of dodge. Sometimes the not-being-sure is not the dark night of the soul. Sometimes it is just the plain, cold possibility that there is no one there. I refuse to write a book about prayer that does not admit this out loud. When you pray and hear nothing, one available explanation is that nothing is what there is to hear. I held that explanation for years. I do not hold it now, but I hold my not holding it with a certain humility, because I know how reasonable it seemed from the inside, and I will not insult the version of me who believed it, and I will not insult you if you believe it now. If you are reading this and you are not sure God exists at all, you are welcome here. This book is not going to threaten you or shame you into certainty. It is going to invite you to keep the conversation open a little longer than your logic says you should, and to see what happens.

Because something did happen to me, and I can only report it, not prove it. What happened was not that I got an answer that settled the question. What happened was that the talking itself began to change me, over years, in a direction I had not chosen and would not have chosen. I became less afraid. I became, slowly, more honest, first with God and then with everyone else. I found that the parts of me I had kept hidden even from myself came up out of the dark when I prayed, and I had to look at them, and looking at them in what I hoped was the presence of God turned out to be different from looking at them alone. I am not saying this proves God was there. I am saying that the man who prayed to the furniture on the outside chance became, over time, a man who could not honestly account for himself without reference to someone on the other end. The evidence was not a voice. The evidence was a life bent by a conversation.

I think of the father in Mark's Gospel who brings his sick son to Jesus and says the truest sentence in the New Testament. I believe. Help my unbelief. He does not resolve the contradiction. He prays it. He holds both halves of himself out to Jesus at once, the faith and the doubt, and Jesus does not send him away to sort himself out first. That is the posture I am asking you to consider. Not certainty. Not the pretense of certainty. The honest holding out of both halves. Lord, I am not sure you are there. I am talking to you anyway. If that is the most faith you can muster, it is enough to begin, because it was enough for that father, and his son was healed, and the man went home carrying a faith with a hole in it, which is the only kind most of us ever carry.

So here is what I would ask of you as we begin. Do not wait until you are sure to pray. You will wait forever, and the waiting is itself a decision, a slow closing of the door that you will not even notice you have made until one day you realize you have not spoken to God in a year and you do not miss it, and that not missing it is the most dangerous thing of all. The worst outcome is not the anguished prayer into apparent silence. The anguished prayer is alive. The worst outcome is the quiet settling into a life without prayer, the drift, the gradual conviction that there was never anyone to talk to, arrived at not by argument but by neglect. Stay in the conversation. Talk to the furniture on the outside chance it is God. I did, and I am telling you, from inside a life I did not expect to have, that the outside chance was worth everything I have.
