## Introduction: After the Water Receded

For most of American history a Christian and a citizen were swimming in the same water, and almost nobody noticed the water was there.

You could assume things. You could assume your neighbor shared your basic moral vocabulary even if he never darkened a church door, because the vocabulary was in the air he breathed too. You could assume the stores would be closed on Sunday morning, that the President would end his speeches asking God to bless the country and mean roughly the God you meant, that a pastor was a respected man in town and not a suspicious one. None of it required anyone to actually believe very much. That was the point. It was the water. To be a decent American and to be a vaguely Christian person were close enough to the same thing that you could go your whole life without telling them apart.

That world is gone, and the striking thing is how fast it went. In the span of a single generation the share of Americans who claim no religion at all went from a rounding error to something near a third of the country. Belief did not just decline. It changed categories. The philosopher Charles Taylor spent eight hundred pages making this one point, that we have moved from an age in which faith was the assumed default to an age in which it is one option among many, and a contested one, held by people who know their neighbors have looked at the same evidence and chosen otherwise. That is a different kind of believing. It is belief without the water.

And here is the thing the American church has mostly refused to accept. The water is not coming back.

We are, a great many of us, flopping on the sand. Furious, grieving, certain that the faith itself is in its death throes, casting around for someone to blame, the schools or the courts or the other party or the young. I understand the grief, and I am not going to mock it, because something real did die. But I want to say as plainly as I know how what actually died, because almost everything depends on getting this right. What is dying is not Christianity. What is dying is Christendom. They are not the same thing, and the church's failure to tell them apart is the single most expensive mistake we are making right now.

Christendom is the arrangement. It is the long stretch of history, running roughly from a Roman emperor's conversion to a few decades ago in America, in which Christianity was assumed, established, respectable, and backed by the power of the culture. Christianity is the thing itself. A crucified and risen Lord, a gospel, a people, a way of life that was already centuries old before any emperor ever heard of it. For a long time we got to have both at once, and we forgot they could be separated, and so when the arrangement started to fall we assumed the faith was falling with it. It is not. The scaffolding is coming down. The building is fine. We have just been told for so long that the scaffolding was the building that we can no longer tell the difference, and we are weeping at the wrong funeral.

I can say this with a certain freedom, because I never swam in the water. I did not grow up Christian. I came to Christ out of atheism, as a grown man, in the full daylight of the secular age, as exactly the kind of skeptical outsider this book is partly written to reach. I have never once needed the culture to assume the faith on my behalf, because the culture I lived in assumed the opposite, and Jesus turned out to be enough anyway. That is not a small thing to say to a church in a panic. The faith does not need the scaffolding. It never did. If anything the scaffolding needed the faith, and borrowed its prestige, and gave very little back.

Lesslie Newbigin saw this before most of us. He spent the better part of forty years as a missionary in India, and when he finally came home to Britain he found a country that had become, by his own account, a far harder mission field than the one he had left, and a church that did not even know it had become one. He had crossed the world to bring the gospel to people who had never heard it, and come back to people who had heard it and moved on, which is a different and lonelier kind of soil to work. The West, he understood, is no longer pre-Christian. It is post-Christian. It has the church in its memory the way a man has a marriage he walked out of, and that is not nothing, and it is not the same as never having married at all.

This book is about that country, and how to follow Jesus inside it without losing your nerve or your soul. I am going to argue something that will sound at first like bad news and turn out to be the opposite. The church has lived after Christendom before. It lived before Christendom for three hundred years, as a small and frequently hated minority with no power to speak of, and it was never more alive, never more clear about what it was, never more dangerous to the empire that despised it. We are not being marched toward extinction. We are being marched back to the beginning, which is the one place the church has always known how to thrive.

I am not going to tell you how to take the country back. That is one of the two bad answers, and I will spend a chapter on why. I am not going to tell you how to make peace with the age by quietly handing it everything it asks for, which is the other one. I am going to try to do something harder and older. I am going to try to help us be the church again. Deep, strange, unafraid, and free. The way we were before we had anything to lose.

## Chapter One: The Water We Forgot Was There

There is a small story the writer David Foster Wallace told a room of graduates, and it has outlived him because it is true about more than he meant. Two young fish are swimming along and an older fish passes them going the other way and says, morning, boys, how is the water. The two young fish swim on a while, and finally one of them looks at the other and says, what is water.

That is Christendom. It was the water, and the whole problem with water is that the fish cannot see it. American Christians have spent so long inside the assumption that the culture was basically on their side that they stopped experiencing it as an assumption at all. It just felt like reality. It felt like the way things are. And a thing you cannot see is a thing you cannot examine, which means you also cannot tell where it ends and your actual faith begins.

So let me try to make the water visible, because you cannot follow Jesus after Christendom until you can see the Christendom you are after. It began, as a historical matter, with a Roman emperor. For its first three centuries the church had no political standing of any kind. Then Constantine won a battle in 312, issued an edict of toleration the next year, called the bishops to Nicaea in 325, and within a lifetime, under Theodosius in 380, the faith that Rome had fed to lions became the official religion of Rome. That is the hinge. That is the day the church stopped being a persecuted minority and started being the establishment, and it stayed the establishment, in one form or another, for sixteen hundred years.

America was supposed to be the exception, and in the narrow legal sense it was. There was no state church, no bishop on the public payroll, no establishment of religion, and thank God for that. But what we built instead was sneakier and in some ways stronger. We built a cultural establishment without a legal one. The faith was woven into the civic furniture so thoroughly that you did not need a law to enforce it. The schools opened in prayer. The politicians quoted the King James whether they had read it or not. The calendar bent around the Christian year. A man could be entirely faithless and still be carried along by a current that ran, unmistakably, in a Christian direction. That current was the water.

And here is what the water did to us, the thing we are only now able to see. It taught us, without ever saying so, that being a Christian meant being central. Normal. Respectable. The home team. It formed in us a set of expectations we mistook for the faith itself, the expectation that the culture would back us, that the institutions would defer to us, that to be a believer was to be a member of the majority that set the terms. None of that is in the New Testament. All of it felt, to us, like Christianity. We took the privileges of the arrangement and quietly folded them into the gospel, until losing the privileges felt exactly like losing the gospel.

> We mistook the water for the faith, and so when the water drained we were sure we were watching the faith die. We were watching the water drain.

Charles Taylor calls the new condition a secular age, and he is careful to say he does not mean a world emptied of belief. He means a world in which belief has become contestable, in which the faith that was once the unspoken floor under everyone's feet is now one position a person takes, knowing full well that the thoughtful people across the table have taken another. That shift is the end of the water. It is not the end of God. It is the end of God being assumed. And a church that only knew how to believe in the shallows of assumption is going to have to learn, quickly and at cost, how to believe in the open sea.

I find I am not afraid of this, and I have had to ask myself why, and the answer is in the introduction. I met Christ in the open sea. I never had the water. So when I watch the culture withdraw its old deference I do not feel the ground give way, because that ground was never under my faith to begin with. I want that for the frightened church. Not the fear. The freedom on the other side of it, which is the freedom of a faith that has finally found out it was standing on the Rock the whole time and not on the water at all.
