## Introduction: The Two Words We Lost

I came to faith from atheism, and the first thing I noticed when I started reading the Bible as a grown man was how much of it I had been taught to ignore. I had assumed the Christians I knew were reading a book about getting to heaven. Instead I found a book that would not stop talking about the poor. It talked about wages held back overnight. It talked about widows and the courts that cheated them. It talked about boundary stones moved in the dark to steal a neighbor's field. It talked about a God who heard the cry of slaves and came down. None of this was in the brochure I had been handed at the door. The brochure was about my private soul and its private destination. The book was about the world and who had been crushed in it.

So I want to begin by naming the gap, because the gap is where this whole book lives. There is a distance between the Bible the prophets wrote and the Bible the modern church has agreed to read. We did not lose the text. We lost two words inside it. And losing those two words has cost us more than we know, because those two words carry the weight of who God is and what God demands, and without them we are left holding a gospel that fits comfortably in a private life and never once threatens the arrangements of the public one. That is not the gospel of the prophets. That is a domesticated thing we made because the real one asked too much.

The two words are mishpat and tsedaqah. They run through the Hebrew Scriptures like two rivers, sometimes apart and often together, and where they meet they describe the kind of community God intends. Mishpat is usually translated justice. It means giving people what they are due, treating cases rightly, protecting those who cannot protect themselves, and it always has teeth, because in the Hebrew it is not an abstraction floating above the courtroom but the actual setting right of an actual wrong. Tsedaqah is usually translated righteousness, and the English ruins it instantly, because we hear righteousness and we think of a man's private moral record, his clean hands and his clean mouth, when the Hebrew means something closer to right relationship lived out in the open, a life so ordered toward others that the community is made whole by the way you hold your possessions and your power. Tim Keller used to point out that tsedaqah is a fundamentally social word. It is not about your inner ledger. It is about whether the people around you can flourish because of how you live.

> We did not lose the Bible. We lost two words inside it, and with them the God those words describe.

Hold those two words together and you get the biblical vision, and the vision is not what most of us were handed. Mishpat is the rectifying justice that stops the predatory practice and punishes the cheat. Tsedaqah is the primary justice, the daily ordering of life so that the predatory practice never gets its grip in the first place. One stops the bleeding. The other refuses to let the wound open. And the prophets, when they wanted to describe a society that pleased God, did not describe a society of correct private beliefs. They described a society where mishpat and tsedaqah flowed like water through every transaction, every court, every field, every gate. Take those words out and you can have full churches and a rotten city, and the prophets would have called that exactly what it is.

I am writing this as a pastor, which means I am writing this from inside the failure. I have preached the privatized gospel. I have stood in a pulpit and talked about a man's heart while saying nothing about the wages he paid the people who cleaned his office. I have organized a food drive in December and called it our witness to the neighborhood while never once asking why the neighborhood was hungry in the first place, never asking who profited from the hunger, never asking whether my comfortable distance from the answer was itself a form of guilt. I am not standing outside this indictment. I helped build the thing I am indicting. When I say the church privatized righteousness and outsourced justice, I am describing my own ministry before I am describing anyone else's.

Here is what the privatizing did. It took tsedaqah, which the Bible means as a social and relational way of holding your life open to your neighbor, and it shrank it down to personal piety, so that a righteous man became a man who did not curse, did not drink, and read his Bible in the morning, while the question of what his money did in the world went entirely unexamined. And it took mishpat, which the Bible means as the active setting right of public wrong, and it handed the whole category over to the government and the courts and the activists, as though justice were a political program that serious Christians could take or leave depending on their temperament. We split the two words from each other and then we hollowed out each half. The result is a church that is personally moral and publicly silent, and the prophets had a name for that too.

This book is an argument that biblical justice is not a political add-on to the gospel. It is not the liberal wing's hobby or the social-justice fad that the careful believer must guard against. It is the character of God, revealed from the Exodus forward, written into the law, sung in the Psalms, thundered by the prophets, and embodied by Jesus when he stood up in his hometown synagogue and read from Isaiah and announced that this is what he had come to do. You cannot remove justice from the Bible and have the Bible left. You can only have the brochure.

And I want to be honest about the danger from the other side, because this is not a book that will fit neatly into anyone's politics. There is a way of talking about justice that has cut itself loose from God entirely, that has made justice into an ideology, a weapon, a tribe, and that version is also under the prophets' judgment, because justice without the living God behind it becomes one more way for the strong to organize against their enemies while calling it righteousness. I am going to indict the church that privatized the gospel. I am also going to indict the movement that secularized justice until it became a religion of grievance with no mercy in it and no humility under God. The prophets struck in every direction. So will this book. If you finish it comfortable, I will have failed.

What I am after is recovery. The recovery of the Exodus as the founding act of a justice-God who comes down to liberate slaves before he gives anyone a single commandment. The recovery of the gleaning laws, where God wrote poverty relief directly into the agricultural code and gave the poor a legal right to the corners of the field. The recovery of the Jubilee, the most radical economic idea in the ancient world, where every fifty years debts were cancelled and land returned and the slate was wiped so that no family could be permanently locked out of the means of life. The recovery of the Sabbath as resistance to an economy that wants to work you and your animals and your land to death. The recovery of the prophets' fury at worship that runs smoothly while the oppressed go unheard. The recovery of Jesus in Nazareth, unrolling the scroll and reading good news to the poor as his platform, his manifesto, the thing he came to do. That is the tradition I am trying to put back in your hands.

So consider this an invitation and a warning at the same time, which is how the prophets always spoke. The invitation is to read the Bible again as if the two words were still in it, because they are, and to let them do to your life and your church what they were always meant to do. The warning is that this will cost you, because justice always costs the people who benefited from its absence, and most of us reading this book have benefited from its absence more than we have ever admitted. I am not going to leave you comfortable. I am going to try to leave you with weight. The prophet Amos said let justice roll down like waters. He did not say let it trickle when convenient. He said roll down, like a river that takes out everything not anchored to something true. That is what I am praying happens here. That is the only reason to write a book like this. Let it roll.

## Chapter One: Mishpat and Tsedaqah, and Why English Flattens Them

Every translation is a small surrender, and the two words at the center of this book have surrendered more than most. When the Hebrew prophets wrote mishpat and tsedaqah, they were not reaching for vague spiritual ideals. They were naming concrete realities that a farmer and a judge and a widow all understood, realities you could see in a courtroom and a field and a marketplace, and when those words crossed into English they lost their edges, their teeth, their specific demand. We ended up with justice, which sounds like a courthouse statue, and righteousness, which sounds like a man being good in private. Both English words point away from the neighbor. Both Hebrew words point straight at him. That is the flattening, and we have to feel the full weight of it before anything else in this book will land.

Start with mishpat. The word appears more than four hundred times in the Hebrew Bible, and it carries a range that English cannot hold in a single term. It means the rendering of a judgment, the verdict of a court. It means the rights that a person is owed, the legal claims that belong to them by virtue of being a member of the community. It means the ongoing practice of treating people equitably, week after week, case after case. When Scripture says God loves mishpat, it is not saying God enjoys an abstract principle. It is saying God is the kind of judge who does not take bribes, who does not favor the powerful, who hears the case of the orphan that no human court would bother to hear, and who acts. Mishpat is justice with a body. It does something. It changes the outcome for the person who was being crushed. And critically, in the Hebrew, mishpat is most often described in terms of its objects, and the objects are almost always the same four figures, repeated until you cannot miss them: the widow, the orphan, the immigrant, and the poor. These are the people who in the ancient world had no standing, no clan to defend them, no power to enforce their rights. Mishpat is what God demands precisely for the people the system was built to ignore.

> Justice in Hebrew is not blind. It has its eyes wide open, and it is looking at the widow, the orphan, the stranger, and the poor.

Then there is tsedaqah, and here the flattening is even worse, because the English word righteousness has been hijacked by centuries of individualized piety until it now means almost the opposite of what the Hebrew intended. We hear righteous and we picture a solitary saint, a person of clean private morals, the man who does not swear and does not cheat on his wife and pays his taxes. That is not wrong, exactly, but it is so partial that it becomes misleading. Tsedaqah in the Hebrew Bible is overwhelmingly relational and social. A tzaddik, a righteous person, is someone whose life is so rightly ordered toward the good of the community that the weak are stronger because that person exists among them. Tsedaqah is the kind of righteousness you can only have in relationship, because it is measured by what your existence does to the people around you. The Hebrew has no concept of a righteousness that benefits no one but yourself. To be righteous in the biblical sense is to live in such a way that justice is being done through your ordinary daily conduct, your business dealings, your generosity, your refusal to exploit an advantage you legally possess. Keller put it sharply once: tsedaqah is day-to-day living in which a person conducts all relationships in family and society with fairness, generosity, and equity. It is not a private virtue. It is a public one.

Now watch what happens when these two words appear together, because in Hebrew they very often do, paired as a hendiadys, two words working as one idea. Mishpat u-tsedaqah. Justice and righteousness. When the prophets put them side by side they are describing the entire moral fabric of a community that pleases God, and the pairing tells you something English cannot. Tsedaqah is the proactive, daily, generous, relationship-ordering righteousness that prevents injustice from taking root, and mishpat is the corrective, judicial, rectifying justice that intervenes when injustice has already done its damage. One is the ounce of prevention woven into the texture of daily life. The other is the pound of cure applied at the gate where cases are heard. You need both. A society that only has mishpat without tsedaqah is constantly putting out fires it keeps starting. A society that imagines it has tsedaqah without mishpat is fooling itself, because a righteousness that never lifts a finger when the courts are corrupt is not righteousness at all. The prophets demanded the pair. We inherited neither word with its meaning intact.

I have watched the flattening do its quiet damage in real churches with real people, and I want to name it concretely because abstractions let us off the hook. I have known good men, generous men, men who would give you the coat off their back if you stood in front of them cold, who understood righteousness entirely as their private record before God and could not for the life of them see why the structures their generosity left untouched were any of their concern. They had tsedaqah cut in half. They had the personal warmth and not the social vision, and so their goodness, real as it was, terminated at the edge of the individual encounter and never reached the system that kept producing cold men with no coats. And I have known the opposite, people consumed with structural critique, fluent in the language of justice, who had no personal righteousness left in them at all, no mercy for the actual human being in front of them, no humility, only the cause. They had mishpat torn loose from tsedaqah and from God, and it had curdled into something hard. The Hebrew refuses both halves. It insists on the whole.

Here is why this matters for everything that follows. If you do not recover the meaning of these two words, you will read the rest of the Bible through the flattened English and you will keep producing the flattened church. You will read the prophets and think they are being emotional or political. You will read the law and think the social legislation is quaint ancient housekeeping irrelevant to the gospel. You will read Jesus in Nazareth and spiritualize every word so that good news to the poor means good news to the spiritually poor and release to the captives means release from the captivity of sin and nothing, nothing, touches the ground where actual poor people stand and actual captives wait. The flattening is not a translation footnote. It is a theology. It is the theology that let the church keep its private piety and surrender the public square, and it did not happen by accident. Languages flatten in the direction their speakers want them to flatten. We flattened these words because the full meaning was inconvenient, because a God who demands mishpat and tsedaqah is a God who will not stay in the private compartment we built for him.

So this chapter is a request to relearn two words, and relearning a word is harder than learning one for the first time, because you have to unlearn the worn-down version first. When you read justice in your English Bible, train yourself to hear mishpat, with its widow and orphan and stranger and poor standing right there in the meaning, with its insistence that something actually change for them. When you read righteousness, train yourself to hear tsedaqah, that socially generous, relationship-ordering, community-healing way of living that has nothing to do with private religious bookkeeping and everything to do with whether your neighbor flourishes. Hold the two together the way the prophets held them, the prevention and the cure, the daily generosity and the corrective intervention. Do that, and the rest of the Bible begins to read like a single sustained argument, an argument I had completely missed when I first picked it up as a skeptic, an argument the modern church has largely agreed to mute. The argument is that God is a God of justice and righteousness, that these are not optional themes but the very grain of his character, and that a people who claim him and ignore them have not understood the first thing about who they claim. The words are still in the book. We only have to read them again.
