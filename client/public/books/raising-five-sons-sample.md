## Introduction: The Room I Had Never Seen

The night my first son was born, my wife handed him to me, and the room went quiet, and the first clear thought I had was that I did not know what I was doing.

I want to tell you where that came from, because it explains the whole book. I came to faith out of atheism as a grown man, and I was raised without a father, and those two facts are closer together than they look. I arrived in that delivery room with no working picture of the thing I was about to spend the rest of my life doing. I had never watched a father, up close and over years, do the ordinary work of fathering a son. I had seen the absence of it. I had assembled some idea of it out of films and older boys and the men I half watched from a distance. But I had not seen it, and now there was a person in my arms who needed me to be it, and I knew in my body, before I had any words for it, that I would have to build the whole thing from scratch and that I would fail at it more than I would succeed.

That happened five times. Five sons, over the better part of two decades, and I am still in the middle of it, and I am writing this not as a man who figured fathering out but as a man who was changed by it, slowly, in the failing, in the staying, in the ten thousand unremarkable days that no one will ever see. This is a book about those days. It is a book about what it actually costs to be there, every day, for boys who are watching you more closely than you will ever know, and about the strange thing that happened to me along the way, which is that in learning to be a father I finally met the Father, the one in the Trinity, the one I had never had and had stopped believing existed.

I need to say at the start what this book is not. It is not a manual. I am suspicious of parenting manuals, and most of all of the ones written by men who make fathering sound like a system you can run correctly if you just have the right information. Fathering is not a system. It is a forming, and the one being formed is not only the son. It is the father. The man I was the night my first son was born could not have been the father those boys needed, because he was too young and too unfinished and too false, and the only thing that made him into something closer to what they needed was the long work of fathering them, which broke him open and put him back together more times than he can count.

So I am not going to give you steps. I am going to tell you the truth about being there, and about the vacancy that fathering exposed in me, and about the God I kept running into in the room where my sons were. I have come to believe that the most important thing a father does is also the thing our culture counts for nothing, and that almost everything we are told to chase as fathers is the wrong thing, and that the boys in the next room are being formed by what we are far more than by what we provide. I learned that the hard way, five times. Let me show you what they taught me.

## Chapter One: The Vacancy

You do not know what you have lost, growing up without a father, until you are well into your thirties, and some men never know it at all. The loss does not announce itself. It is not the absence of a particular man you remember and miss. It is the absence of a whole category, the category that was supposed to tell you what a man does, what a man holds, what a man carries without flinching, and a boy without a father has to invent all of that, usually badly, out of whatever scraps the world hands him.

I invented mine out of movies. I invented them out of the older boys at the apartment complex who seemed to know things I did not. I invented them out of books, in the same library where, years later, I found the other books that would end my atheism. The inventing was always incomplete, and I carried into adulthood a particular vacancy, a shape cut out of me that nothing in my twenties could fill, and most of what I tried to fill it with left the hole worse than it found it. I am not going to overstate this. There are fatherless men who have grown into magnificent fathers, and there are men with present fathers who never recovered from them. But there is a specific emptiness the father-absent boy carries, and I have come to think the church has mostly been interested in it only as a problem to be solved with a program, when it is older and deeper than any program could reach.

It is so old that the Bible keeps returning to it. The God of Israel is named, again and again, as a Father to the fatherless, a defender of the widow, and most of the people those words were first written for would have heard them and wept, because they knew the vacancy from the inside. We have domesticated those verses into greeting-card sentiment. They were not sentiment. They were a word spoken directly into the emptiest place a human being can carry, the place I carried, the promise that the category I had been forced to invent badly was real after all, and filled, and held by Someone who would not leave.

> The loss is not the absence of a man you remember. It is the absence of the whole category that man was supposed to fill, and the boy without a father has to invent it, badly, from scratch.

I tell you this so you will understand that I did not come to fathering from a position of strength. I came to it from the vacancy. And here is the thing I could not have predicted. The vacancy did not disqualify me. It turned out to be the very place the work would happen, because a man who knows the shape of what he did not have is a man who knows, with terrible precision, what his sons must not be made to carry. I could not give my boys a model I had never seen. But I could refuse to pass on the absence. I could be there. And it turned out that being there, simply staying in the room I had never seen anyone stay in, was most of the thing I had spent my whole life believing I was incapable of.
