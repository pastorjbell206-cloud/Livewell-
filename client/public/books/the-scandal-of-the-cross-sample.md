## Introduction: The Center We Cannot Face

I wore a cross around my neck for two years before I believed a word of what it meant. It was a gift. Silver, small, tasteful. It sat against my chest while I argued that God was a fiction invented by frightened people, and no one who saw it thought anything of it, because by then the cross had become the most familiar object in the Western world and therefore the least examined. That is the first thing I want you to sit with. We have taken the instrument of a tortured death and made it into something you can buy at a checkout counter. We hung it in sanctuaries and tattooed it on forearms and printed it on the covers of books that promise your best life. And somewhere in all that familiarity, we stopped seeing it.

The cross is not a logo. It was a means of execution designed by an empire to be as slow, as public, and as humiliating as human cruelty could arrange. Cicero, writing in the century before Christ, called it the most cruel and disgusting penalty, and said the very word cross should be far removed not only from the body of a Roman citizen but from his thoughts, his eyes, his ears. That is the object. That is what we made into jewelry. And the central claim of the Christian faith, the claim that either founds everything or founds nothing, is that on one of those crosses, on a spring afternoon under Pontius Pilate, God died.

Not a good man died. Not a teacher, not a martyr, not a symbol of the human capacity for love. God. The maker of the galaxies, hanging from a beam, suffocating slowly the way the condemned did, mocked by soldiers and abandoned by nearly everyone who had followed him. This is the scandal. The Greek word Paul uses is skandalon, the thing you trip over, the offense that will not smooth out. He does not apologize for it. He plants it in the center of the message and dares the world to get past it.

I came to faith from atheism as a grown man, and I did not come because someone made Christianity comfortable. I came because someone made it dangerous again. Someone took the jewelry off the cross and showed me the wood. And what I discovered is that everything I most needed and most feared, everything true about guilt and shame and suffering and death and violence and the ache for justice, all of it converges on that one afternoon. The cross is not a detail of the story. It is the hinge of history. It is the center of everything. And the church that raised most of us has spent a generation smoothing it into something we can live alongside without being disturbed.

This book is an attempt to be disturbed again. I want to recover the crucified God, the phrase Jurgen Moltmann gave us in 1972 when he wrote that a God who cannot suffer is poorer than any human being. I want to ask the questions the checkout-counter cross never makes us ask. Why would a good God die. What does the death actually do, and can we say what it does without shrinking it to a slogan. Why does Martin Luther insist, all the way back at Heidelberg in 1518, that a theologian of the cross calls the thing what it actually is, while the theologian of glory calls evil good and good evil. And why is it that the death of God, which sounds like the end of everything, turns out to be the deepest answer we have to suffering, to guilt, to shame, to violence, and to death itself.

I am not writing this from a safe distance. I am inside the indictment. I have preached the cross in ways that were too clean, too resolved, too eager to get to the resurrection before anyone had to sit in the dark of Friday. I have handled the blood of Christ like a man embarrassed to mention it in polite company. I have five sons, and I have taught them to sing about a cross I sometimes could not bear to look at directly, because to look at it directly is to be undone. This is not a book I wrote from above the material. It is a book I wrote from inside it.

Here is what I will not do. I will not pick one theory of the atonement and beat the others with it, as though the death of God were a puzzle with a single tidy solution. The tradition has spoken of the cross as a substitution, as a victory over the powers, as the supreme demonstration of love, as the place where violence exhausts itself against a God who will not return it. Each of these sees something real. Each becomes a distortion when it swallows the others. Fleming Rutledge, in her long and patient book on the crucifixion published in 2015, refused to reduce it, and I will follow her in that. The cross is large enough to hold every true thing said about it and to break every attempt to make it small.

So I ask you to take the jewelry off. Feel the weight of the wood. The chapters ahead move from the scandal the first Christians could not soften, through the reasons a good God would die, into what the death accomplishes and what it exposes, and finally into what it means for the particular darkness each of us carries. If you are a skeptic, I am not going to pretend the offense away to keep you reading. I am going to show you that the offense is the point, and that a faith without it was never worth your objection. If you are a Christian who has grown numb to the story, I want to make it strange to you again. And if you are simply tired, carrying more than you can name on an ordinary Tuesday, I want to tell you that the center of everything is a place where God bled. That is not a small comfort. It is the only one that has ever held.

Let us go to the wood.

## Chapter One: The Cross We Made Into Jewelry

Walk into almost any church in America and count the crosses. They are on the walls, on the pulpit, on the offering plates, embroidered on the paraments, printed in the bulletin, hanging from the necks of half the congregation. We are surrounded by them. And I want to propose that this saturation is not a sign of devotion but a symptom of forgetting. You do not decorate with an electric chair. You do not wear a tiny golden gas chamber to signal your good taste. The reason we can hang the cross everywhere is that we have stopped hearing what it was.

I understand how this happened, and I am not writing to sneer at the woman who wears her grandmother's cross and prays with it between her fingers. There is real faith in that. But something happens to a symbol over two thousand years. It softens. The blood dries and the wood is sanded smooth and the screaming stops, and what remains is a shape, a pleasant geometry, two lines meeting at right angles, easy to reproduce, easy to sell. The offense drains out of it drop by drop until you can print it on a coffee mug next to the words but first, coffee, and no one flinches. We did this slowly, and we did it to ourselves, and the doing of it cost us the thing itself.

Consider what a first-century person would have felt at the sight. The cross was not a religious symbol to them. It was a current event. Rome crucified rebels and slaves and the lowest criminals along the roads leading into its cities, deliberately, so that travelers would see the rotting bodies and understand what happened to those who defied the order of things. After the revolt of Spartacus in 71 before Christ, the general Crassus lined the Appian Way with six thousand crucified men, mile after mile of them, and left them there. When Paul stood in a Greek marketplace and said we preach Christ crucified, his hearers did not picture a piece of jewelry. They pictured that road. They pictured the smell.

This is the distance we have to recover, and it is a real distance, because the church has an interest in not recovering it. A softened cross asks nothing of us. It is a comfort, a brand, a reassurance that we are on the right side. But the cross as the first Christians knew it is an accusation before it is a comfort. It says that the world was so wrong, so deep in its wrongness, that when God himself entered it, the world killed him, and did so with the full machinery of religion and law and empire all working together and all convinced they were doing right. The men who crucified Jesus were not cartoon villains. The priests believed they were protecting the faith. Pilate believed he was keeping the peace. The soldiers were doing their jobs. That is the horror. The cross is what respectable people do to God when he shows up and refuses to fit their arrangements.

I said I stand inside this indictment, and I do. I have preached sermons that treated the cross as a transaction completed long ago, a debt cleared, a receipt filed, nothing to see, move along to your victorious Christian living. I have let the cross become a doctrine I affirmed rather than a death I stood beneath. It is easier that way. A doctrine can be shelved. A death demands that you look at it and ask what it says about you. And what it says about me is not flattering. The distance between me and the men who drove the nails is smaller than I would like. Given the right crowd, the right fear, the right conviction that I was protecting something important, I do not trust myself to have stood with Jesus rather than against him. Neither should you trust yourself. That distrust is the beginning of understanding the cross at all.

There is a particular version of the softened cross I want to name, because it is the one most likely to be doing damage in your life without your noticing. It is the cross as decoration for a religion of self-improvement. In this version, Jesus died mainly to affirm your worth, to remind you that you matter, to prove that you are loved just as you are. There is a true fragment buried in that. You are loved. But the cross does not tell you that you are fine. It tells you that your condition was so grave that nothing less than the death of God could address it, and that the same death is the measure of how far love was willing to go into the wreckage to reach you. Take away the gravity of the condition and the love becomes sentiment. A God who dies to affirm what is already fine is a God who overpaid catastrophically. The cross only makes sense against the black backdrop of a real emergency.

John Stott, in his great book on the cross published in 1986, wrote that the concept of substitution lies at the heart of both sin and salvation, and that we sin by putting ourselves where only God deserves to be, and God saves by putting himself where only we deserve to be. Hold that sentence. It is the exchange the jewelry hides. We climbed onto the throne. God climbed onto the cross. Every softened, decorative, affirming version of the cross obscures that trade, because the trade is not flattering to us. It requires us to admit we were on a throne that was never ours. And a culture that has made the self the last sacred thing cannot easily hear that it was the self, precisely the self, that had to be dealt with at Golgotha.

> We do not decorate with an electric chair. That we can wear the cross as ornament is not the measure of our reverence. It is the measure of our forgetting.

So this is where the book begins, with a subtraction. Before I can show you what the cross gives, I have to take back what the culture gave you, which is a cross with the danger removed. I am asking you to un-remember the pleasant shape and remember the wood. To hear the word crucified the way a Roman heard it, with the road and the smell and the shame all present. Because only then does the Christian claim land with its full and terrible weight. The center of our faith is not a symbol of triumph borrowed from a comfortable religion. It is a corpse on a Roman gibbet who three days later was not in the tomb. If that is true, nothing is the same. If it is not true, we should stop wearing it. What we cannot keep doing is the thing we have done, which is to wear it and not mean it, to hang it everywhere and see it nowhere. Take it off the neck. Put it back in the ground where it stood. Look at what actually happened there. That is where we are going.
