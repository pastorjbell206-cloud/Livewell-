## Introduction: The Marriage That Ministry Nearly Cost Me

I almost lost her to the church.

Not to another man. Not to bitterness she nursed in private until it hardened into a wall I could not climb. Not to any of the failures that get named from pulpits when pastors talk about marriage, the failures we are comfortable confessing because they belong to other men. I almost lost Susanna to the very thing I had given my life to serve. I almost lost my wife to the work of God. And for years I could not see it, because the thing taking her from me wore the face of obedience, and a man does not easily suspect his obedience of theft.

There is a particular cruelty in the marriages that fail inside ministry, and the cruelty is this. The thing pulling the husband away from the wife is not shameful. It is praised. When a businessman neglects his wife for his career, the church has language for that. We call it idolatry, we call it misplaced priorities, we say he loved his work more than his family and we are right to say it. But when a pastor neglects his wife for his congregation, the church has no such language ready, because the congregation is the work of God, and who will stand up in a deacon meeting and say the pastor loves God too much. The pressure that takes the pastor's marriage is sanctified pressure. It comes dressed for Sunday. And a man can spend fifteen years giving his wife the leftovers of his attention and call it sacrifice, call it laying down his life, call it the cost of the call, and never once suspect that the cost was being charged to an account that was never his to spend.

I want to tell you about a Tuesday afternoon, because Tuesdays are where marriages actually live or die, not Sundays. It was early in my time at First Baptist Church of Fenton. Susanna had asked me, not for the first time, to be home by six. There was nothing dramatic in the request. A child needed help with something. She had been alone with the boys all day and she was at the end of what one body can carry. She did not want a date. She did not want romance. She wanted my presence in the house at the hour I had promised it. And I was not home by six. I was at the church, in a meeting that mattered, with people who needed me, doing work that was real and good and necessary, and when I finally came through the door at half past eight I did not even feel guilty, because I had been about the Lord's business. That was the line I used. The Lord's business. As though the Lord had business that required me to break my word to my wife. As though the God who instituted the covenant of marriage in the garden, before there was a temple, before there was a single congregation to pastor, would be honored by a man who tended the gathered people of God while letting the first congregation of his own house go hungry.

I have come to believe that the marriages of pastors are under a pressure that no ordinary marriage manual names, and that this pressure is so woven into the texture of the call that most of us cannot see it any more than a fish can see water. We have shelves of books on marriage. Good books, many of them. Tim and Kathy Keller wrote one in 2011 called The Meaning of Marriage that I have given to a hundred couples and will give to a hundred more, because they understand that marriage is a covenant before it is a contract and a sanctification before it is a satisfaction. But even the best of those books assumes a husband who comes home from a job, and the pastor does not come home from a job. The pastor comes home from a calling, and a calling does not punch a clock, and a calling does not stay at the office, and a calling will follow you through the front door and sit down at your dinner table and put its hand on your shoulder while your wife is telling you about her day, and you will be nodding at her and you will not have heard a word.

This is the pressure I mean. The church can become the other lover. I am going to say that plainly at the start because the whole book depends on it, and because I have watched pastors flinch from the phrase as though it were irreverent. It is not irreverent. It is Hosea. The prophet Hosea, in the eighth century before Christ, was commanded by God to marry a woman who would be unfaithful to him, and the whole brutal point of that marriage was to show Israel what their idolatry felt like from the inside of a covenant. God spoke of himself as a betrayed husband. The language of the other lover is biblical language, and what I am telling you is that a pastor can commit a kind of marital infidelity in which the rival is not a woman but a building full of people who need him, and that this infidelity is harder to repent of than the other kind, precisely because it never feels like sin. It feels like faithfulness. The man giving his best hours, his sharpest attention, his most tender emotional energy to the congregation, and bringing home to his wife a husband who is used up, distracted, and already thinking about Sunday, is committing something the marriage feels as abandonment even when the theology cannot name it as such.

I did this. I want to be clear that this is not a book written by a man who watched other pastors fail and took notes. I am inside the indictment. There were years when Susanna carried our home, our sons, our finances, the endless invisible labor of being a pastor's wife in a small town where everyone knew her name and no one knew her exhaustion, and she carried all of it while married to a man who was technically present and functionally absent, a man whose body sat at the table while his mind sat in the sanctuary. I gave the church my first fruits and gave her the gleanings. And the most damning part, the part I can still barely write, is that I thought I was being a good and faithful servant the entire time. I had a category for adultery. I had no category for what I was actually doing.

So this is a book of confession before it is a book of instruction, and it has to be, because the instruction will not land on a man who still believes he is innocent. I am going to name the pressures that take the parsonage marriage, and I am going to name them one at a time, slowly, because they hide. The fishbowl of congregational expectation, where your marriage is a public exhibit and your wife is a volunteer no one hired. The invisible load she carries that no one sees and no one thanks her for. The reason she cannot say what she really feels, because her honesty could cost you your job and she knows it. The mind that never leaves the church. The slow displacement of intimacy by activity, until the marriage is a logistics company that occasionally remembers it was once a romance. The criticism that follows you home and poisons the one place that was supposed to be safe. The temptation, the subtle and disgusting temptation, to use your own marriage as a sermon illustration, to brand your wife, to make even your intimacy a ministry asset. And underneath all of it, the question that the rest of the book exists to answer. How do you protect a covenant the church is quietly trying to take.

I do not write any of this to make ministry sound like a curse. It is not a curse. It is a high and holy calling and I would not trade it. But Eugene Peterson, who pastored one congregation in Maryland for twenty-nine years and who understood the pastoral vocation more honestly than almost any man of his century, wrote in his memoir The Pastor in 2011 that the great temptation of the pastor is to become a busy and important religious professional and to lose, in the busyness and the importance, the very souls closest to him. He meant his own soul. He also meant, I think, the soul of his wife Jan, who was beside him for all twenty-nine years and most of whose labor no congregation ever recorded. Peterson knew that the pastor's first parish is his own household, and that a man who saves the congregation and loses his marriage has not succeeded at ministry. He has failed at it in the one place the failure does not show up in the attendance numbers.

The church will demand everything. That is not a flaw in the church. A living congregation is a hungry thing, and it will eat as much of you as you set in front of it, and it will always be hungry again by morning. The demand is not the problem. The problem is the man who has never decided in advance what he will not give, and who therefore gives it all, and discovers too late that the part he gave away without thinking was the part that belonged to his wife. This book is about deciding in advance. It is about building a wall around the covenant, not a wall of resentment toward the people I serve, but a wall of fidelity to the woman I promised, so that when the church demands everything, as it will, there is one thing it cannot have, because that thing was given away first, at an altar, before any of these people knew my name.

> The church will eat as much of you as you set in front of it, and it will always be hungry again by morning.

Susanna and I are still married. I tell you that now so you do not read the coming chapters as a tragedy. By the mercy of God and by the slow, costly work of repentance that is not finished and never will be, the marriage that ministry nearly cost me did not die. But it nearly did, and the nearness is the point, and I will not sand the nearness down to make either of us comfortable. If you are a pastor reading this, or married to one, I am not going to tell you that loving your spouse and serving your church are the same act flowing from the same love, as the conference speakers like to say. Sometimes they are at war. Sometimes you have to choose. And the whole of this book is my attempt to tell you, from inside my own failure and from inside the grace that met me there, how to choose her, and why choosing her is not a betrayal of the call but the proof that you ever understood it.

## Chapter One: The Other Woman Who Takes Your Best Hours

A man who is having an affair gives himself away in his calendar before he gives himself away in his confession.

You can see it in the hours. The hours go somewhere, and wherever a man's best hours go, that is where his heart has gone, whatever his mouth is saying. So when I tell you that the church can become the other lover, I am not making a poetic flourish. I am talking about hours. I am talking about the specific, accountable, irrecoverable hours of a man's one life, and where they actually went, as opposed to where he would say they went if you asked him at a deacons retreat what his priorities were. He would say God first, family second, ministry third. Every pastor says it. I said it for years. And my calendar said something different, and the calendar does not lie, and Susanna could read the calendar better than I could because she was the one living in the hours the church did not want.

Here is the cruelty of it again, because we have to keep returning to the cruelty or the whole thing softens into something forgettable. The other woman, in the ordinary affair, gives the man pleasure, and the pleasure is what the family cannot compete with. But the church gives the pastor something more dangerous than pleasure. The church gives the pastor meaning. It gives him significance, the sense that his life counts, that his hours are spent on something eternal. And a man can walk away from pleasure when he is convicted, because pleasure does not defend itself with theology. But meaning defends itself. When I gave the church my best hours, I was not chasing a thrill I could be ashamed of. I was chasing the feeling of being useful to God, and that feeling is a narcotic stronger than any affair, because no one will ever tell you that you took too much of it. The congregation will only ever ask for more, and they will praise you for giving it, and your wife, who is asking for less of you so that she can have more of you, will look in that economy like the selfish one. Think about how upside down that is. The institution asking for all of you looks holy. The woman asking for some of you looks needy. And the pastor in the middle, who genuinely wants to do right, almost always sides with the institution, because the institution speaks the language of calling and the wife speaks the language of need, and we have been trained to distrust need and to obey calling.

I remember a season at First Baptist when we were in the middle of something genuinely good. The church was growing. People were coming to faith. I was preaching better than I had ever preached, studying late, available to anyone who called, and I want to be honest that those were some of the most spiritually alive months of my ministry. And they were some of the worst months of my marriage, and the two facts sat right next to each other and I could not connect them, because I had a theology that told me a flourishing ministry and a flourishing marriage rose and fell together, and my actual life was disproving that theology every single day. The ministry was flourishing precisely because I was starving the marriage to feed it. I was taking the hours that belonged to Susanna and laying them on the altar of the congregation, and the congregation was growing fat on the very hours that were leaving my wife hungry. There is a grim accounting in that. The church was full because the parsonage was empty.

Let me describe the best hours, because the phrase is too abstract until you name what the best hours actually are. The best hours are not the most hours. A man can spend a great quantity of time at home and still give his home nothing, because he is physically present and emotionally elsewhere, and we will come to that, the absence inside the presence, in its own chapter, because it deserves one. No, the best hours are the hours of a man's peak energy and peak attention, the hours when his mind is sharp and his heart is open and he is capable of the kind of presence that another human being can actually feel. For most of us those hours are not many. We get a handful of them a day. And the question that decides a marriage is not how many total hours you spend at home but where your handful of best hours goes. Mine went to the church. By the time I came home, the sharp hours were spent. I gave Susanna a tired man, a man running on fumes, a man whose capacity for attention had been drained dry by ten hours of giving himself to other people. She did not get the husband. She got the husk of the husband. The church got the husband. And no one in the church knew that the warm, attentive, fully-present pastor they enjoyed on a Wednesday evening was a man whose wife had not seen that version of him in weeks.

This is the displacement I want you to feel before we go further, because everything else in the book grows out of it. There is a finite amount of a particular kind of attention in a human being, a relational, emotional, present-tense attention, and it does not regenerate quickly. A pastor spends his whole working day spending it. He spends it on the grieving widow, on the couple in crisis, on the elder who is angry, on the new believer full of questions, on the staff member who needs encouragement, on the three hundred faces on Sunday morning each of whom believes, not wrongly, that the sermon was for them. By the time the pastor gets home he has been emotionally present to dozens of people, and the well is dry, and his wife, who has been waiting all day for her turn, gets the bottom of the bucket. And here is what makes it so hard to repent of. He was being kind all day. He was being patient and attentive and loving all day. He just spent all of it before he got to the one person to whom he had made a vow. He was generous with everyone who had not promised to spend their life with him, and stingy with the one who had.

> The church got the husband. Susanna got the husk.

Kathy Keller, in The Meaning of Marriage, makes a point that took me years to understand and that I think every pastor needs to have driven into him with a hammer. She says that marriage is the most fundamental relationship, the one that all others must serve and not undermine. Not the most important compared to God, but the most fundamental among human relationships, the relationship from which a man's other relationships should flow and to which they should defer. And what I had done, without ever deciding to do it, was invert that order. I had made the congregation the fundamental relationship and the marriage the one that had to flex around it. Susanna's needs were negotiable. The church's needs were not. If the church called during dinner, I took the call. If Susanna needed to talk during my study time, I asked her to wait. The pattern of a thousand small decisions, none of them dramatic, none of them adulterous in any way a court or a congregation would recognize, added up to a marriage in which my wife had learned that she came second to several hundred people she had not married and could not divorce.

She never said it that starkly. That is part of the trap, and we will give the whole chapter to it later, the reason the pastor's spouse cannot say what she really feels. But she said it in smaller ways, the ways a person uses when the direct way is closed to them. She stopped asking me to be home by six, because asking and being disappointed was worse than not asking. She stopped telling me the smaller things about her day, the things that are the actual substance of intimacy, because a man who is clearly somewhere else is not a safe place to put the small and tender things. She became efficient with me. Our conversations became logistics. Who was picking up which son, what was happening on Saturday, whether I had remembered the thing I had promised to remember and usually had not. The romance did not end in a fight. It ended in a series of administrative exchanges between two people running an organization called a household, and I did not even notice the romance was gone, because I was busy, and busyness is the perfect anesthetic. You cannot feel the wound if you never stop moving long enough to feel anything.

I want to say something now to the pastor who is reading this and feeling defensive, because I know the defensiveness from the inside. You are thinking that I do not understand the demands of your particular church, that your situation is different, that you have a board that expects sixty hours, a congregation in crisis, a building campaign, a denomination breathing down your neck, that you do not have the luxury of giving your best hours to your wife because if you did the whole thing would collapse. I understand that thought because I had that thought, and I want to tell you gently that the thought is the affair talking. The other woman always has a reason you cannot leave her tonight. The reason is always urgent and always real. There is always one more meeting, one more soul, one more fire that only you can put out. And if you wait for a season when the church does not demand your best hours, you will wait until your children are grown and your wife is a stranger and the marriage is a corpse that the two of you keep dressed for Sunday. The church will never voluntarily release your best hours. You have to take them back. You have to decide, in advance, in cold blood, which hours belong to her and defend those hours against the church the way you would defend them against another woman, because functionally that is exactly what the church has become.

Bonhoeffer, writing from a prison cell in 1943, in a letter to his niece on the occasion of her wedding, said something that has hung over my study for years. He told the young couple that their love was their own private possession, but that marriage was more than something personal, that it was a status, an office, a post in the world that held the two of them together even when their love faltered. I have thought about that word, office, in connection with the pastorate, because the pastor of all people understands what an office is. He holds one. And what Bonhoeffer was saying is that the husband holds an office too, the office of husband, and that this office has its own duties that do not bend to the duties of his other offices. The man is not only a pastor who happens to be married. He is a husband who happens to pastor, and the husband came first, at the altar, before the ordination, and the husband will remain when the ordination is gone, and the great error of the ministry marriage is to let the office that came second swallow the office that came first. I let it. For years I let the pastor swallow the husband, and I called the swallowing devotion, and Susanna sat across the table from a man who had given his best hours to a rival she could never name and could never beat, because the rival was the work of God, and how do you compete with God for your husband's heart when your husband cannot tell the difference between God and the church that is consuming him.

That is the question this whole book is trying to answer, and I do not pretend to have answered it cleanly, because it does not answer cleanly. But it begins with the hours. Look at your hours. Not your intentions, not your stated priorities, not the nice things you say about your family from the pulpit. Your hours. Where do the best ones go. And if the honest answer is that they go to the church and your wife gets the husk, then you have found the other woman, and she lives in the building with the cross on top, and the first step toward fidelity is to stop pretending the affair is devotion.
