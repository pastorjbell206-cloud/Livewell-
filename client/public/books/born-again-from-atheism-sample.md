## Introduction: The Man I Have to Introduce First

I need to tell you who was standing in the room before I tell you what happened in the room. This matters more than it seems. Most books about coming to faith skip the part where the writer was a real unbeliever, or they keep a cartoon on hand, a straw man wearing the writer's old clothes, easy to knock down because he was never quite alive. I will not do that to the man I was. He deserves better, and so do you, because you may be standing where he stood, and you can smell condescension from a long way off.

I was an atheist. Not the soft kind, not the man who had drifted out of a childhood faith and kept a Bible on a shelf out of sentiment. I did not have a childhood faith to drift out of. God was never a live option in my house, and by the time I was old enough to think about it on my own, I had reasons, and I could name them, and I thought the reasons were good. I still think some of them are good. That is one of the things I want to say to you early, so you keep reading. I do not think you are stupid. I was not stupid when I stood where you may be standing. The arguments against God are not the arguments of foolish people. They are, some of them, the best thoughts of some of the best minds who ever lived, and if a man tells you otherwise, he is selling you something, and you should not buy it.

Here is what I believed, plainly. I believed the universe was what it was and did not owe anyone an explanation beyond its own workings. I believed that the appeal to God was the appeal a frightened animal makes to the dark, a projection of the father a child wants onto a sky that has no father in it. I believed that religion had done a great deal of harm, that it had blessed wars and burned women and shielded men who hurt children, and I believed that the people who kept defending it had to keep their eyes half closed to do so. I believed that when I died I would stop, the way a candle stops, and that the honest thing was to make the candle worth something while it burned. I did not think these beliefs were a wound. I thought they were maturity. I thought I had grown up, and that the people around me in the pews I did not sit in had not.

And underneath all of that, which I did not see for a long time, was a smaller and harder fact. I was raised without a father. He was not dead. He was simply not there, which is a particular kind of absence, the kind that is a decision someone made and kept making every morning by not coming back. I want to be careful here, because it would be too easy, too neat, to say that I did not believe in God the Father because I had no father, that the whole edifice of my unbelief was a boy's grief wearing a philosopher's coat. That is too easy, and it insults the arguments, and it insults the boy. But it would also be a lie to leave it out. The absence was in the room. It was in the room when I read Bertrand Russell at nineteen and felt the clean cold relief of a man who owed the sky nothing. Something in me had already decided that fathers do not come, and a doctrine that promised one arriving felt, at the level below argument, like a cruelty dressed as a comfort.

So this book is going to do two things at once, and I am going to try to keep them honest with each other. It is going to tell you what happened to me, the actual sequence, the years and the reading and the resistance and the night it finally gave way. And it is going to take the arguments seriously, at their strongest, the way I once held them, because if I only tell you my story you can dismiss it as one man's weather, a mood that passed, and if I only give you arguments you can feel me reaching past your objections to grab your collar. I do not want to grab your collar. I spent a long time on the other side of that grip, and I hated it, and I will not do it to you.

What I will do is stay inside the room. That is the only place I know how to write from. Not from a safe distance where the answers are clean and the doubts are somebody else's. From inside, where a marriage is failing while a man argues about the ontological proof, where a diagnosis lands in the same week a sermon claims God is good, where the smart objection and the raw wound are the same size and will not separate. I came to faith in that room, not in a library, though the library mattered. The arguments did not save me. They cleared the ground. Something else walked across the cleared ground, and I will try to tell you what it was, though I have never been fully able to, and I have stopped being embarrassed about that.

Let me say what this book is not, so you are not braced for the wrong thing. It is not a book that will make you feel foolish for doubting. If anything it will take your doubts more seriously than the atheist writers I read, because I think they gave up on the hardest questions too early, satisfied to have wounded religion when they could have pressed all the way through to whether anything at all is there. It is not a book of tricks, the little rhetorical traps where a believer sets you up two moves ahead and springs the checkmate. I do not believe anyone was ever loved into the kingdom by a checkmate. And it is not triumphant. I did not win. I lost, in the specific sense that the thing I had spent years defending was taken from me, and I have never been sorry, and I still remember what it cost.

There is a line in Lewis, in the book I put at the front of this one, where he calls himself the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England, brought in kicking and struggling and resentful, eyes darting for an escape. When I first read that I thought it was a pose, a bit of English understatement. Then it happened to me, and I understood that it was not a pose at all. It was a report. He was describing the thing accurately. A man who wants to believe and talks himself into it has done something psychological. A man who does not want to believe, who has arranged his whole intellectual life around not believing, and who finds himself believing anyway, against his preference, against his pride, against the boy who decided fathers do not come, has run into something outside himself. That is what I am claiming happened. You are right to be suspicious of the claim. I was suspicious of it while it was happening to me.

So here is the man I have to introduce. A boy with no father, grown into a man with reasons, sitting alone with his books and his relief, certain he had seen through the whole thing, unaware that the whole thing had not yet finished looking at him. Picture him clearly. Do not let me turn him into a fool for the sake of a tidy conversion. He was me, and I owe him the truth, and the truth is that he was closer to the door than he ever knew, and that the door was not where he was looking, and that when it opened it did not open the way he would have designed it, and that he went through it at last not because he was argued across the threshold but because he was, in the oldest and most embarrassing word, found.

## Chapter One: The Atheist I Actually Was

I want to give you the man in detail, because a portrait done from a distance is worthless and I have read too many of them. When a believer describes his atheist past in three sentences on the way to the good part, I stop trusting him. The atheist years were not a hallway I hurried through. They were most of my life, and they were not empty, and a great deal of what I now believe was formed by the honesty I learned when I believed nothing. So let me slow down and show you the room I actually lived in.

I was a serious young man, more serious than I had any right to be, and my seriousness took the shape it takes in a certain kind of fatherless boy, which is that I decided very early I would not be fooled. Being fooled was the great danger. Somewhere a man had made promises to a woman and to a child and the promises were not kept, and I do not think I ever formed this into a sentence, but I moved through the world as if the first duty of a mind was to refuse the comforting lie. I read early and I read to arm myself. By the time I found the atheist writers I was already halfway to them, and they did not so much convert me as confirm what I had suspected, which is that the whole apparatus of religion was a beautiful, well-organized way of not looking at the truth.

And the truth, as I held it, was clean, and I loved its cleanness. The universe requires no author. Matter and energy behave according to laws we can write down, and where we cannot yet write them down we will be able to someday, and the gaps are getting smaller every year, and into those shrinking gaps the frightened have always stuffed their gods. Life is chemistry that learned to copy itself. Mind is what the brain does, the way digestion is what the stomach does, and when the brain stops the mind stops, and there is no ghost that steps out and floats away. Morality is a set of behaviors that helped our ancestors survive in groups, dressed up over the centuries in the language of commandment. And the sense that any of it means anything, that there is a purpose stitched into the fabric, is the last and most stubborn illusion, the one even clever people cannot shake, the projection of a small warm animal onto a cold enormous sky. I found this bracing. I want you to hear that I found it bracing and not bleak. There is a real dignity in facing what you take to be the truth without flinching, and I had it, and I do not disown it now.

I read Russell, and I loved him, the essay where he says that man is the product of causes that had no prevision of the end they were achieving, that his origin, his growth, his hopes and fears, his loves and beliefs, are but the outcome of accidental collocations of atoms. I read that the way another young man reads scripture, as a thing that ordered the world. I read the newer men later, the ones who came out swinging around the turn of the century, and I was thrilled by their nerve, by the way they said in public what I felt was obviously true and socially forbidden. Religion poisons everything. God is a delusion. The evidence is absent and the demand for evidence is the only honest posture. I underlined and I nodded and I felt myself part of a company of the clear-eyed, and it was a good feeling, the feeling of belonging to the people who had grown up.

But I have to tell you the other thing that was in the room, because if I only give you the arguments I will have done what the conversion writers do in reverse. I will have made the atheist too clean. He was not clean. He was a young man who lay awake. The arguments were real and I meant them, but they did not fill the hours, and in the hours that they did not fill there was a specific ache that had no name in my vocabulary because my vocabulary had ruled out the things that might have named it. I would come to the end of a good day, a day where I had worked well and been with people I liked, and there would be a residue, a sense of something owed that no one was paying, an appetite with no object. I called it loneliness when I called it anything. I told myself it was the ordinary friction of being a mind in a world that did not care, and that the mature response was to stop expecting the world to care. That is a coherent answer. I gave it to myself for years. It did not touch the ache. It named the ache and filed it and the ache went on.

I want to be fair to my younger self about how he handled this, because he was not a fool and he was not in denial in the cheap sense. He knew the ache was there. He simply had a theory of it, and the theory was that evolution had built into us a hunger for meaning because the hunger was useful, and that the hunger firing with no meaning to feed on was exactly what you would predict on the atheist account, a vestigial appetite, like the craving for sugar in a world that no longer requires us to hoard calories. This is a good answer. I still think it is the best answer available to the position, and I do not wave it away now that I am on the other side. But I notice, looking back, that the answer explained the ache without ever once reducing it, and that a hunger which cannot be reduced by being explained is a strange kind of hunger, and that I did not press on that strangeness because pressing on it frightened me in a way I could not have justified.

There was also, and I have to say this plainly, a great deal of pride in it. I do not mean the ordinary vanity everyone has. I mean that my unbelief had become load-bearing for my sense of who I was. I was the one who saw clearly. I was the one who would not be fooled. If you had shown me, in those years, an argument for God that I could not immediately answer, I would not have felt curiosity. I would have felt threat, and I would have gone looking for the answer not to find the truth but to restore my footing, and I know this because I did it more than once. That is not a philosophical posture. That is an emotional one wearing a philosophical coat, and the honest thing, years later, is to admit that my unbelief was never purely a conclusion. It was also a home. It was the place I had built where the father who did not come could not hurt me, because in that place there was no father anywhere, and an absence that is universal does not single you out. A cold that falls on everyone is not a rejection. I had made the whole sky cold so that my particular patch of cold would stop meaning what it meant.

I did not see that then. I want to be clear that I am not now claiming I secretly believed and was hiding from it. That is the move believers make that I most distrust, the smug little theory that all atheists really know God exists and are just suppressing it, which manages to call you a liar and a coward in one breath while pretending to be generous. I did not secretly believe. I genuinely did not believe, all the way down, with my whole mind. What I am saying is subtler and I think truer, which is that a mind is not a sealed chamber, that our conclusions have neighbors, and that some of my conclusions had grief for a neighbor and pride for a neighbor and fear for a neighbor, and that this is true of every human position including the religious ones, and that noticing it does not refute the position but it does keep you honest about how much of your certainty is reasoning and how much is the shape of the room you happen to be standing in.

So that was the atheist I actually was. Bright, serious, well-read, genuinely convinced, genuinely unfooled, and carrying under all of it an ache he had explained and a wound he had universalized and a pride he mistook for clarity. If you had met me you would have found me good company and hard to argue with. I would not have seemed like a man on the edge of anything. I did not feel like a man on the edge of anything. And that is exactly the point I most want to leave with you at the end of this first chapter, the thing I did not know and could not have known. The approach Lewis describes, the steady unrelenting approach of the One he desired not to meet, does not feel like an approach from the inside. It feels like ordinary life. It feels like reading and working and lying awake. The man being approached is the last to know. I was being approached for years, and I called it loneliness, and I filed it, and I went on being the one who would not be fooled, right up until the fooling turned out to be the thing I had been doing to myself all along.
