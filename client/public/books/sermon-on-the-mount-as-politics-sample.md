## Introduction: The Most Political Sermon Ever Preached

I spent years preaching the Sermon on the Mount as if it were about my inner life, and I want to tell you why that was a mistake, because it was the same mistake almost everyone makes, and it is the mistake this whole book exists to undo.

I would take the Beatitudes and turn them into a mood. Blessed are the poor in spirit became a feeling I was supposed to have about myself, a kind of recommended humility, a posture for my private devotions. Blessed are the peacemakers became a word about not holding grudges. Turn the other cheek became a lesson in personal patience, something to apply when a coworker annoyed me. I took the most public address Jesus ever gave, a sermon delivered on a hillside to crowds who were living under military occupation, and I shrank it down until it fit inside my own chest, and I called that faithfulness. It was not faithfulness. It was a way of keeping the sermon from doing what it was sent to do.

Here is what I missed for too long. When Jesus climbed that hill and sat down and began to teach, he was not offering tips for a calmer interior life. He was announcing a government. He was describing, in concrete and unmistakable terms, what life looks like when God is actually king, and he was doing it in a world that already had a king, a Caesar, a temple establishment, a whole arrangement of power that ran on violence and money and fear. Every line of the sermon lands differently the moment you remember that. Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth, is not a private sentiment. It is a claim about who ends up owning the world, and it is a direct contradiction of how the world believed ownership worked, which was that the earth belongs to the people with the chariots. Jesus stood in an occupied territory and told a crowd of nobodies that the future belonged to them and not to Rome, and we have managed to turn it into a verse for refrigerator magnets.

The Sermon on the Mount is political. I do not mean it is partisan, and I am going to spend a good deal of this book making sure you do not hear me saying that, because the first thing the modern church does with the word political is sort it into left and right, and the sermon will not be sorted that way. It indicts both sides of every argument we are currently having. What I mean by political is older and larger than our elections. I mean that the sermon is about how a community orders its common life, how power is held and used, how the poor are treated, how enemies are dealt with, how money is regarded, how truth is told, how justice is done. Those are political questions in the deepest sense, the sense the ancient world understood, and Jesus answers every one of them, and his answers are so far outside the range of what any nation has ever attempted that we have mostly decided he could not have meant them and have quietly filed them under spirituality instead.

I am writing this book because I think the spiritualizing has cost us almost everything. We took a sermon that was meant to create a visible, peculiar, public people, a city on a hill that the watching world could not help but see, and we used it to manage our private feelings, and the result is a church that looks, in its actual political behavior, exactly like the nation around it. We fight the way our neighbors fight. We hoard the way our neighbors hoard. We fear the way our neighbors fear. We hate our enemies with the same fluency as everyone else and quote a different book while we do it. And the world looks at us and sees nothing it could not get anywhere else, no alternative, no contrast, no city on a hill, just one more interest group scrambling for the same power by the same means. The sermon was supposed to make us unmistakable. We have made it invisible.

So this book is an attempt to read the Sermon on the Mount the way I now believe it was meant to be read, as the founding document of a new kind of community, a constitution for a different kingdom, and to refuse the long habit of shrinking it down until it stops being dangerous. I am going to walk through it slowly, Matthew chapter five through chapter seven, and at every point I am going to ask the question we have trained ourselves not to ask, which is not what does this mean for my private spiritual life but what does this mean for how we actually live together, with each other and with our enemies and with our money and with the people the world has thrown away. That is the question the sermon answers. It is a more demanding question than the one I used to ask. It is also the only one that does the text justice.

I should say plainly where I am writing from, because it shapes everything. I came to faith out of atheism as a grown man, and one of the things that broke through my unbelief was the Sermon on the Mount, read for the first time without anyone telling me what it was supposed to mean. I read it cold, and I remember thinking that no one actually lived like this, that if anyone did the world would be unable to ignore them, and that the very fact that I had never met such a community was itself a kind of argument against the church. I was right about the first part and wrong about the conclusion. The sermon really does describe a life almost no one is living. But that is not because it cannot be lived. It is because we decided, somewhere along the way, that it was not really meant to be. This book is my attempt to take it back, to read it as the marching orders it is, and to count the cost of actually obeying it, which is high, and which is the point.

Come up the mountain with me. Sit down where the crowd sat. And hear the sermon as the people who first heard it heard it, not as a guide to feeling better, but as the announcement that everything they thought they knew about power was about to be turned upside down by a carpenter with no army, who was, it would turn out, the King.

## Chapter One: Up the Mountain

Before Jesus says a single word, Matthew tells us where he is standing, and the location is the first thing the crowd would have understood and the first thing we miss.

He went up on the mountain. That is the whole sentence, and to us it is scenery, a detail about geography, the kind of thing we read past on the way to the important material. To a Jewish audience in the first century it was a thunderclap. There was one mountain that mattered in the memory of that people, one mountain where a man had gone up to receive the law that made a wandering crowd of former slaves into a nation, and that was Sinai, where Moses climbed into the cloud and came down with the commandments that would order the common life of Israel for the next fifteen hundred years. Matthew has spent his first four chapters quietly painting Jesus as a new Moses, brought out of Egypt, passing through the waters, tested in the wilderness for a symbolic forty, and now, deliberately, unmistakably, he sends him up a mountain to deliver a law to a people. No one in that crowd would have missed it. The man on the hill is doing again the thing that founded the nation. He is giving a constitution.

But notice what is different, because the differences are the message. Moses went up the mountain alone and the people were warned to stay back, not to touch the holy mountain on pain of death, kept at a trembling distance from a God too dangerous to approach. Jesus goes up the mountain and the people come with him. He sits down, which is what a teacher did, what a king did, what someone with settled authority did, and the crowd gathers close enough to hear an ordinary speaking voice. The new lawgiver does not thunder from a cloud behind a barricade. He sits on the grass and lets the nobodies come near. The form of the thing already tells you the content. The kingdom Jesus is about to describe is one in which the distance between God and the people has collapsed, in which the law is not handed down from a terrifying height but spoken at eye level by one who has come to live among them. That is not a small adjustment. That is the gospel built into the staging.

And who is in the crowd. Matthew has just told us, at the end of chapter four. They came from Galilee and the Decapolis and Jerusalem and Judea and beyond the Jordan, and they brought the sick, the demon-possessed, the epileptics, the paralyzed. This is not an audience of the settled and the comfortable. This is a crowd of the desperate, the occupied, the poor, the people for whom the existing arrangement of power had produced mostly suffering. They live under Rome, which taxes them into poverty and crucifies their troublemakers along the roads as a warning. They live under a temple aristocracy that has largely made its peace with Rome. They are, most of them, one bad harvest from ruin. And it is to exactly these people that Jesus is about to say, blessed are you, the kingdom is yours. The sermon is addressed, from its first word, to the bottom of the social order, and any reading of it that forgets who is sitting on that hillside will go wrong on the very first line.

> The new lawgiver does not thunder from a cloud behind a barricade. He sits down on the grass and lets the nobodies come near. The form already tells you the content.

I labor this because the setting controls the meaning, and we have been trained to ignore the setting. We come to the Sermon on the Mount as comfortable people in a comfortable church looking for personal enrichment, and we read words that were spoken to occupied peasants by a man announcing the overthrow of everything that was crushing them, and we wonder why it feels a little flat. It feels flat because we have removed it from the only place it makes sense. Put it back. Imagine the chariots on the roads and the crosses on the hills and the tax collectors at their tables and the desperate sick laid out on the grass, and then hear a poor man with no army and no office sit down on a mountain and begin to describe a kingdom in which all of that is reversed. That is the sermon. It was never calm. It was an act of breathtaking nerve, an announcement of regime change delivered to the regime's victims, and the only reason it sounds tame to us is that we have spent centuries sanding off everything sharp about it.

There is one more thing about the mountain. Jesus goes up, but the whole arc of the gospel is that he is going to come down, all the way down, lower than the crowd, lower than anyone, down to a different hill outside the city where the empire will nail him up as one more troublemaker. The lawgiver who climbs this mountain to describe the kingdom will descend to a cross to establish it, and that order matters. He does not impose the kingdom from the height. He inaugurates it from the bottom, by dying, which is itself the deepest political statement in the sermon, made not in words but in his own body. Keep that ending in view as we climb. The man describing this kingdom is going to prove he means it by refusing every shortcut to power and letting the powers kill him instead. That is the only reason we can trust a word he says about meekness and enemies and the cross we are each told to take up. He went first.
