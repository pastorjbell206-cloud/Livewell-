## Introduction: The Marriage I Never Saw

I had never once watched a marriage be kept before I tried to keep my own.

I should say plainly where I come from, because it explains why I had to learn all of this from the ground up, the way a man learns a language he was never raised speaking. I grew up without a father. I came to faith out of atheism as a grown man. So I arrived at my own wedding with no working model of the thing I was promising. I had seen marriages end. I had seen marriages endured. I had not seen, up close and over years, a covenant kept on purpose by two people who knew what they were doing, and so I did what most people in my position do without realizing it. I built my marriage out of the only blueprint the culture had handed me, and the culture's blueprint, I have come to believe, is the single biggest reason so many good people cannot make a marriage last.

The blueprint is a contract. It does not announce itself that way. It comes dressed as romance, as compatibility, as finding the one who completes you. But underneath the wedding language, the actual operating assumption of marriage in our time is that it is a deal between two individuals for their mutual benefit, entered freely, sustained by ongoing satisfaction, and dissolvable when the satisfaction runs out. We do not say this at the altar. We say richer and poorer, sickness and health, till death. And then we go home and run the marriage on entirely different terms, the terms of the contract, where the real vow, the one nobody speaks, is for as long as this keeps working for me.

This is a book about the difference between those two things, the contract and the covenant, because I have become convinced that almost everything that goes wrong in a Christian marriage goes wrong right there, at the foundation, long before it shows up in the fighting or the drifting or the lawyer's office. We are trying to build covenant marriages on contract foundations, and then we are baffled when they crack.

So this is not a book of tips. I want to say that clearly at the start, because the shelf is already groaning with marriage tips and most of them are fine and almost none of them are enough. I am not going to give you communication techniques or a date-night schedule or a quiz that sorts your spouse into a category. Those things have their place and their place is small. A technique laid over a contract is still a contract. You cannot communicate your way into a covenant you do not believe you made. The problem in most struggling marriages is not a skills problem. It is a story problem. It is that we have believed the wrong story about what marriage actually is, and the only repair deep enough to matter is to recover the true one.

The true story is older and stranger and far more demanding than the deal we were sold. It says that marriage is a covenant, a binding promise that welds two people into one flesh and holds them there, modeled on the God who binds himself to people who do not deserve it and will not let go. That kind of marriage is not sustained by love, not in the way we mean the word. It sustains love. It is the riverbed, and love is the water, and a culture that has torn out every riverbed it could find cannot understand why the water keeps running dry.

I am still learning to keep my covenant. I have five sons, and they are watching their father love their mother, which is the one thing I most wanted to give them and the one thing I was never given. I am writing the book I needed and did not have. If you are trying to keep a promise you are not sure you fully understood when you made it, this is for you.

## Chapter One: More Than Tips

Walk into any bookstore and find the marriage section and you will notice something strange about it. Almost all of the help on offer is technique. Here are the words to use in a conflict. Here is how to schedule intimacy and protect your evenings. Here are the five categories of affection and the seven habits of couples who last. It is a whole industry of skill, and I do not want to mock it, because some of it is genuinely useful and I have recommended a fair amount of it over the years. But I have also sat with enough couples to tell you a hard thing about all of it. The skills almost never save a marriage that is actually dying, and we should ask ourselves why.

The reason is that a struggling marriage is rarely failing for lack of technique. The couple in my office who cannot stand to be in the same room did not get there because nobody taught them to use I-statements. They got there because somewhere underneath the symptoms, one or both of them stopped believing they were permanently bound to the other person, and once that belief goes, no amount of skill can hold the building up, because the skill was never the foundation. You can teach a man to communicate beautifully with someone he has already decided he is allowed to leave. He will simply communicate his way out with great clarity.

This is the part the tips cannot touch. Every communication strategy, every conflict tool, every act of service assumes a question has already been settled, the question of whether you are staying. The tools are for people who have answered yes and now want to live well inside the yes. They do nothing for people who have left the yes open, because an open exit changes the meaning of every hard conversation. When leaving is on the table, every fight is an audition for whether the marriage deserves to continue. When leaving is off the table, the same fight is just two bound people working out how to live, which is an entirely different and far less terrifying thing.

> You cannot communicate your way into a covenant you do not believe you made. The skill is not the foundation. The promise is.

I think this is why so much Christian marriage help quietly fails the people it most wants to serve. It takes the culture's contract and tries to make it run better. It does not challenge the foundation, it just polishes the machinery sitting on top of it, and so it ends up offering people a more efficient version of the very thing that is killing them. It teaches contract marriages to function more smoothly without ever telling them they are contract marriages, without ever naming that the thing they actually need is not a better technique but a different and deeper kind of bond.

So I am asking you to set the tips down for a while, not because they are wrong but because they are not the level the real trouble lives on. The marriage that needs saving does not first need a skill. It needs a story strong enough to stand on, a true account of what it is doing and why it cannot simply be undone when the satisfaction runs thin. It needs to know it is a covenant and not a deal. Everything else, including the tips, only works once that is settled, and almost nothing works until it is.
