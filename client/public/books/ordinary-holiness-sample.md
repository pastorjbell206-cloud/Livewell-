## Introduction: The God I Kept Missing

I found God in a jail cell, which is exactly the kind of story I used to want. It has drama. It has a before and an after with a clean line drawn between them. It has a grown man who was an atheist on a Tuesday and a believer by the weekend, and it makes for a testimony that holds a room. I told it that way for years. I am not ashamed of it. It happened, and it was real, and God met me in a place I had no business expecting him. But I want to tell you what I learned in the twenty years after that story ended, because that is the part nobody asks about, and it is the part that saved my faith.

The dramatic conversion was easy. What came after was the hard thing. I walked out of that season expecting the rest of my life to feel like the moment I met God. I expected the mountaintop to be a place you could move to and live. And so for a long stretch of years I chased the feeling. I chased the conference high and the worship set that broke me open and the answered prayer that arrived like a lightning strike. I read my Bible looking for the burning bush. I prayed the way a man plays a slot machine, waiting for the bells and the falling coins. And when the ordinary days came, as ordinary days always do, one after the other after the other, I read them as failure. I thought God had gone quiet. I thought I had lost something. I did not understand that the ordinary day was not the absence of God. It was the address where he actually lived.

I am a pastor now, which means I have spent fifteen years watching people make the same mistake I made, and I have watched myself keep making it long after I should have known better. I stand in the pulpit and I say beautiful things about the presence of God, and then I go home and I cannot find him in a sink full of dishes. I have five sons. I have a wife named Susanna who has watched me look for God in the dramatic and miss him in the dishes for the better part of two decades. There is a particular look she gives me, and it means: he was right here, and you were somewhere else. She is usually right. I keep scanning the horizon for the spectacular arrival of God and I keep tripping over him in the hallway. This is not a book I am writing from above the problem. It is a book I am writing from inside it.

Here is what I have come to believe, and I believe it with the whole weight of a man who resisted it as long as he could. We have been taught, by our culture and often by our churches, to look for God in the exceptional. In the mountaintop. In the miracle, the movement, the ministry with our name on it, the moment that feels different from all the other moments. We have built an entire spiritual imagination around the idea that God shows up when life stops being ordinary. And so we treat the ordinary as the waiting room. We treat Monday as the thing to get through until something real happens. We treat the commute and the meeting and the diaper and the marriage on a Tuesday as the static between the songs. And in doing so we miss him, because the ordinary is not the waiting room. It is the sanctuary. It is where he mostly comes.

Consider the arithmetic of the life of Jesus, which is the arithmetic I keep coming back to. He lived for something like thirty-three years. We have detailed accounts of maybe three of them. The other thirty are almost entirely hidden, and what little we know of them is stunningly ordinary. He grew. He learned a trade. He worked with his hands in a shop in a town so unremarkable that a man once asked whether anything good could come out of it. For thirty years the Son of God swept sawdust off a floor. Thirty years. If the ordinary life were beneath God, if it were merely the thing to escape, then God himself made an incomprehensible mistake, spending nine tenths of his earthly existence in the very place we are all so desperate to get out of. He did not make a mistake. He was telling us something. He was telling us where to look.

I want to say a hard thing about the church, and I say it as a man standing inside it, not throwing rocks from the parking lot. We have often made the problem worse. We have built a spirituality of the event. We have taught people that the real Christian life happens at the retreat, at the altar, at the summer camp, at the conference with the good lighting, and then we send them home to Tuesday with no theology for it. We hand them a mountaintop and no map for the valley, which is where they will spend ninety-five percent of their lives. And then we wonder why they burn out, why they drift, why the faith that felt so alive under the stage lights cannot survive the fluorescent hum of an ordinary week. We did this. I did this. I preached the mountaintop for years and gave people nothing for the plain.

So this is a book about the plain. It is a theology of the ordinary, if that phrase can hold. It is about work as worship and not as the distraction from worship. It is about the body and sleep and food and the rhythms God built into us on purpose. It is about the table and the shared meal, about the dishes and the laundry, about the marriage that is mostly Tuesdays, about parenting as a slow formation in the mundane, about the neighbor you did not choose. It is about the long faithfulness that no one applauds and no one photographs. Eugene Peterson, borrowing a phrase from Nietzsche of all people, called the Christian life a long obedience in the same direction, and I have never found a better description of the thing. Not a series of spectacular sprints. A long obedience in the same direction. A walk.

I am going to lean on some witnesses in these pages, and I want you to meet them now. Brother Lawrence, a seventeenth-century monk who found God among the pots and pans of a monastery kitchen and left us a small book about it that has outlived nearly everything published in his century. Gerard Manley Hopkins, the poet whose line sits at the front of this book, who saw the grandeur of God flaming out of shook foil and gathering to a greatness in the most ordinary things. Wendell Berry, who has spent a lifetime insisting that the sacred is located, that it has an address, that it is found in a particular field and a particular marriage and a particular town. Kathleen Norris, who wrote about laundry and prayer in the same breath and was not being cute about it. James K. A. Smith, who has argued that we are formed less by what we believe than by what we repeatedly do, that our loves are shaped by our liturgies, and that this is true whether we notice it or not. These are my company for this book. They saw what I keep failing to see.

I will make you one promise, and it is the only one I can keep. This book will not hand you a mountaintop. It will not tell you how to feel God the way you felt him at the retreat. It will do something harder and, I think, better. It will try to teach you to find him where you already are, in the life you actually have and not the more spiritual one you keep thinking you are supposed to be living. Because you are not going to get a different life. You are going to get this one. The dishes will be there tomorrow. The commute will be there Monday. The people in your house will need you in a hundred unremarkable ways before you go to bed tonight. The question is not whether you can escape the ordinary into something holier. The question is whether you can learn to see that the ordinary was holy the whole time, and that the God you kept missing was never far off. He was right here. You were somewhere else. Let us go and find him where he actually is.

## Chapter One: The Mountaintop Addiction

> We are a people trained to wait for the exceptional, and so we sleep through the arrival of God.

There is a mountain in the Gospels, and three of the disciples got to go up it. Peter, James, and John climbed with Jesus and watched him change in front of them, his face and his clothing lit from the inside with a light that did not come from the sun. Moses appeared, and Elijah, the two great figures of the law and the prophets, standing there in conversation with him. It was the single most spectacular thing any of them would ever see. And Peter, being Peter, being all of us, immediately wanted to build something and stay. Let us put up three shelters, he said, one for you and one for Moses and one for Elijah. Let us make this permanent. Let us live here on the mountain in the light. He did not know what he was saying, the text tells us, which is a gracious way of putting it. He wanted to move to the mountaintop and take up residence. And within a few hours they were all back down in the valley, where a desperate father was waiting with a sick boy and a crowd was arguing and the ordinary broken world was grinding on exactly as they had left it.

I understand Peter completely, because I am Peter. When something transcendent happens, when God feels close and real and undeniable, the instinct is to build a shelter and stay. To make it permanent. To live there. And the deep spiritual disappointment of the Christian life, the one nobody warns you about, is that you cannot. You always come back down. The retreat ends. The feeling fades. The Monday comes. And if you have built your faith on the mountaintop, if you have taught yourself that the mountaintop is where God is, then coming down feels like losing him. So you start to chase the next mountain. You become an addict of the exceptional. And an addict of the exceptional is, by definition, a person who cannot bear the ordinary, which is a terrible thing to be, because the ordinary is where you live.

I want to name this precisely, because I think it is one of the great unexamined sicknesses of the modern Christian, and I have had it for most of my life. We have confused the presence of God with the feeling of the presence of God. These are not the same thing. The feeling comes and goes with a thousand variables that have nothing to do with God, with your sleep and your blood sugar and the music and the lighting and whether you had a fight in the car on the way. But the presence does not come and go. The presence is constant. The God who was overwhelmingly real on the mountaintop is exactly as present in the valley, in the kitchen, in the traffic, in the ordinary Tuesday. He has not moved. Your feelings have moved. And when we build a spirituality that depends on the feeling, we build a spirituality that will collapse the moment ordinary life resumes, which will be by Monday at the latest.

The culture we live in is not going to help you with this, and I want to be honest about the depth of the problem, because it is downstream of everything. We are formed, all of us, by a machine that runs on the exceptional. The whole economy of the screen in your pocket is built to make the ordinary unbearable. Every feed you scroll is a highlight reel of other people's mountaintops, their weddings and their vacations and their achievements and their perfect meals, curated to make your own ordinary Tuesday feel like a failure by comparison. The technology theorists have a phrase for what this does to us, and it is bleak enough to be true: it manufactures a permanent low-grade dissatisfaction with the actual conditions of your actual life. You have been trained, hour by hour, scroll by scroll, to believe that the real life is elsewhere, that it is more exciting, that it is happening to someone else. This is a lie, and it is a lie with a business model.

And the church, God help us, has too often baptized the same machine. We have Christian versions of the highlight reel. We have the conference speakers with their extraordinary stories, the testimonies of dramatic deliverance, the ministries that grew from nothing to thousands, and we hold these up as the picture of what a real spiritual life looks like. There is nothing wrong with any of it in itself. God does dramatic things and we should tell about them. But when the dramatic becomes the standard, when the exceptional becomes the expectation, we do violence to the ninety-five percent of the people in the room whose lives are and will remain gloriously, stubbornly ordinary. We tell the faithful mother of three that her life is a waiting room until God does something real. We tell the man who has worked the same honest job for thirty years that he is missing out on the move of God happening somewhere with better lighting. We did this. I did this. I gave people a hunger for the exceptional and no theology for the plain, and then I watched the plain crush them.

Here is what the addiction to the exceptional actually costs you, and I have paid this cost, so I am not speculating. It costs you the present. It costs you the only moment you will ever actually be given, which is this one, the ordinary one, the one you are in right now. Because if God is only in the exceptional, then the ordinary present is empty, and you will spend it the way an addict spends the time between hits, which is to say restlessly, distractedly, waiting for the real thing to start. I have missed years of my sons' childhoods this way. Not physically absent. I was in the room. But I was scanning for the significant moment, the teachable moment, the spiritual breakthrough, and while I was scanning for the exceptional the ordinary childhood was happening right in front of me and I was not in it. The bedtime I hurried through because it was not significant was the significant thing. I could not see it because I was looking for a mountain.

Brother Lawrence, the seventeenth-century monk I will keep returning to in this book, understood something about this that took me forty years to begin to grasp. He worked in the kitchen of his monastery, and he was not good at it, and he did not like it, and it was about as far from the mountaintop as a religious life can get. And he wrote that he had come to a place where it was all the same to him. The time of business, he said, did not differ from the time of prayer. In the noise and clatter of his kitchen, with several persons at the same time calling for different things, he possessed God in as great tranquility as if he had been upon his knees at the blessed sacrament. Read that again. He found no difference between the kitchen and the altar. He was not waiting for the kitchen to end so the real spiritual life could begin. The kitchen was the real spiritual life. He had been cured of the addiction to the exceptional, and what he got in exchange was every ordinary moment, which is to say he got his whole life back.

I do not want you to hear me saying that God never does anything dramatic, that the mountaintop is a lie, that you should never go to the retreat or feel the feeling. That is not it. The mountaintop is real. Peter did see the light. God does break in, and when he does you should let it wreck you. The problem is not the mountaintop. The problem is moving there. The problem is treating the exceptional as the norm and the ordinary as the exile. Even in the Gospel account, the whole point of the mountaintop was to send them back down. The vision was fuel for the valley, not an escape from it. Jesus did not let Peter build his shelters. He walked them straight back down into the crowd and the sickness and the argument, because that is where the work was, and the work is almost always in the valley.

So here is the invitation, and it is going to sound like a loss before it sounds like a gift. I am asking you to give up the addiction. I am asking you to stop waiting for the exceptional and to stop reading the ordinary as the absence of God. I am asking you to come down off the mountain you keep trying to move to, and to look for him in the valley where you actually live, in the kitchen with several persons calling for different things, in the ordinary Tuesday that you have been treating as a waiting room your whole life. It will feel, at first, like settling. It is the opposite of settling. It is waking up. Because the God you have been chasing across the mountaintops was never only on the mountaintops. He was in the valley the whole time, in the dishes, in the diaper, in the drive, waiting to be noticed by a people too addicted to the spectacular to see what was right in front of them. He is right here. You have been somewhere else. Come down. He is closer than the mountain ever was.
