Chapter Twenty-Two

The Bible and Science — Where the Conflict Is Real and Where It Isn't

The real conflict is narrower than the culture claims — and most of it concerns the interpretation of one chapter.

Real Conflict, Overstated Conflict

The conflict between the Bible and science is one of the most persistent features of contemporary culture's engagement with Christianity. It is also, in many respects, one of the most overstated.

The perceived conflict runs something like this: science has established a picture of the universe — its age, its origins, the mechanisms by which life developed — that contradicts the Bible's account. Cosmology says the universe is 13.8 billion years old; geology says the earth is 4.5 billion years old; evolutionary biology says that the diversity of life on earth developed through a process of variation and natural selection over hundreds of millions of years. And the Bible says God made everything in six days. The conflict, on this telling, is irreducible.

This conflict is real — but it is much smaller, and much more focused, than the cultural narrative suggests.

Where the Tension Genuinely Lies: The Age of the Earth

The conflict is real in one specific location: the age of the earth and the timing of creation. Young-earth creationism — the position that Genesis 1 requires a creation approximately six to ten thousand years ago, and that geological and cosmological science is wrong in its dating — is a genuinely held theological position with sincere advocates. And it does conflict with the scientific consensus on the age of the earth and the universe, which rests on converging lines of independent evidence that reinforce each other to a degree most scientists find compelling.

I am not going to adjudicate this dispute in detail, because it is not primarily a question about the reliability of Scripture. It is a question about the interpretation of Genesis 1 — about what the text is claiming, and whether it makes specific claims about timing and mechanism or whether it makes claims only about the who and the why of creation.

What I will say is this: the genre and literary context of Genesis 1, which we addressed in chapter eleven, does not obviously require the young-earth reading. The text's ancient Near Eastern context, its structural and poetic features, and its theological concerns can be interpreted in ways consistent with an old earth — old-earth creationism, day-age theory, the framework interpretation, evolutionary creationism — without requiring that the text be adjusted to fit science. These interpretive options have been developed by serious biblical scholars who are committed to Scripture's authority and who have done the exegetical work.

Different Questions: Mechanism Versus Meaning

The conflict between the Bible and science is not as sharp as it is often portrayed, and the reason is this: science answers questions about mechanism and history, while Scripture answers questions about origin and meaning. These are not the same questions. "How did life's diversity develop?" is a scientific question; "Why does life exist at all?" is not. "How old is the universe?" is a cosmological question; "Why is there something rather than nothing?" is not. "By what mechanism did human beings develop their cognitive capacities?" is a biological question; "What does it mean to be human, and what are we responsible for?" is not.

The conflict arises when either science or Scripture is forced to answer questions that belong to the other domain. When science claims that "evolution disproves God," it is making a metaphysical claim beyond its competence; evolution is a description of mechanism, and it has nothing to say about whether the mechanism was intended, guided, or created by a divine being. When religion claims that "the Bible teaches a young earth and therefore science is wrong about the age of the universe," it is making a scientific claim that may not be required by the text.

The relationship between the Bible and science is, at its best, a complementary one. Science describes the mechanism. Scripture addresses the meaning. Both are necessary for a complete account of reality, and neither alone is sufficient.

The Historical Adam and Remaining Tensions

This does not mean there are no genuine tensions. There are. The historical Adam question — whether the biblical account of Adam and Eve requires the existence of a single ancestral pair from whom all of humanity descended, and how this relates to the population genetics evidence — is a genuine question that careful theologians and scientists are working through. The question of how to understand death and suffering in the natural world before the fall is a genuine theological puzzle, one that physical evidence of predation going back hundreds of millions of years makes more complex.

These tensions are real, and they must be engaged honestly. What they do not establish is that the Bible's core claims are contradicted by science.

What Science Has Not Contradicted

The existence of God, the creation of the universe from nothing, the special status of human beings as image-bearers, the reality of human moral responsibility, the historical life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ — none of these claims are contradicted by any finding of physics, chemistry, biology, or cosmology. Some of them are supported by scientific findings. The cosmological argument for creation — the universe had a beginning, and beginnings require a cause — is reinforced by the big bang cosmology that once made scientists nervous about its theological implications.

The Bible is reliable. Part of that reliability is knowing what it is claiming and what it is not. When we read it as the kind of document it is — a theological account of who God is, what God has done, and what human beings are — rather than imposing categories on it from outside, we find that its claims are coherent, substantial, and well-supported by the evidence from multiple domains.
