Chapter 37

Letters Written Under Pressure — Paul and the Corinthians

Paul stakes everything on the resurrection and invites his readers to interrogate the living witnesses.

You do not invite your readers to interrogate your sources unless you believe your sources will hold up.

The Mess at Corinth and the Letter That Answered It

There is no theological document in the ancient world quite like 1 Corinthians. Paul wrote it from Ephesus, probably in the mid-50s AD, to a church he had founded in the city of Corinth roughly three years earlier — and the church he was writing to was a mess. There were factions. There was sexual immorality of a kind that would have shocked the pagan neighbors. There were lawsuits between members, disorder in worship, and theological disputes about resurrection, about spiritual gifts, about whether it was permissible to eat meat sacrificed to idols.

Paul addressed all of it, directly and at length, with the authority of someone who believed he was writing under divine mandate. The letter is argumentative, pastoral, prophetic, theological, and entirely specific to a historical moment in a historical community. And the reason this matters for the question of biblical reliability is simple: 1 Corinthians 15.

1 Corinthians 15 as the Load-Bearing Wall

In chapter fifteen, Paul makes the central argument of his theology. The resurrection of Jesus is not a peripheral claim, he writes; it is the load-bearing wall. If Christ has not been raised, he says, then your faith is futile and you are still in your sins and those who have died in Christ have perished. He is not hedging. He is not offering a spiritual metaphor. He is making a historical claim and telling you that if the historical claim is false, the entire enterprise collapses.

Then he does something that secular historians have noted as remarkable. He lists witnesses.

The List of Witnesses Still Alive

He writes that Jesus appeared to Cephas — Peter — then to the twelve; then to more than five hundred brothers at once, most of whom, Paul says parenthetically, are still alive, though some have died; then to James; then to all the apostles; and last of all to Paul himself, in what he describes as an abnormal birth, a late appearance granted to the one who persecuted the church before his conversion.

That parenthetical — "most of whom are still alive" — is extraordinary. Paul is writing this letter within approximately twenty-five years of the crucifixion, and he is telling the Corinthians, in effect: the witnesses are available, you can ask them. This is not a claim about an event in the remote past; the people who were there are still here. That is the behavior of someone making a publicly verifiable claim, not someone manufacturing a private religious narrative. You do not invite your readers to interrogate your sources unless you believe your sources will hold up.

Why Even Skeptics Date the Creed Early

Scholars across the theological spectrum have noted the significance of 1 Corinthians 15. Bart Ehrman, who does not accept the resurrection, acknowledges that Paul's creedal formula in the opening verses of the chapter — "that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day" — is among the earliest Christian texts we possess, probably formulated within a few years of the crucifixion and certainly within the lifetimes of eyewitnesses; Ehrman dates Paul's reception of this formula to within two to five years of the event itself.

Think about what that means. The core claim of Christianity — death, burial, resurrection — is attested in a document written by a man who knew the eyewitnesses, within a generation of the events, in a culture where those events were recent enough that public refutation was possible. If the claim were fabricated, the fabrication was made in full view of people who could have said otherwise.

The critics of the resurrection have never been able to account adequately for 1 Corinthians 15. They can argue that Paul misunderstood the appearances, that the witnesses were mistaken, or for various psychological or sociological explanations. What they cannot argue is that the claim is late, or legendary, or without connection to eyewitness testimony. The evidence of the letter itself refutes those arguments.

The Structure of History, Not Legend

Paul was writing under pressure — to a community that was fracturing, that was questioning his authority, that was in doctrinal confusion. He was not managing a mythology. He was defending a historical claim in front of people who had every reason to challenge it if it were false. And yet the letter survived; the community in Corinth survived; the church grew; the witnesses Paul cited were known to the Christian communities of the first century.

The argument stands: the most important claim Paul makes, in his most important letter, in the most historically accessible window we have, is grounded in eyewitness testimony that Paul explicitly invites his readers to verify. That is not the structure of legend. It is the structure of history.
