Chapter 32

When Luke Was Wrong — and Then He Wasn't

A skeptic set out to disprove Luke with the spade and ended by calling him a first-rank historian.

The Nineteenth-Century Charge Against Luke

For most of the nineteenth century, one of the standard criticisms of the New Testament was that Luke was a careless historian. The charge was specific, and it was not a vague complaint about tone or theology; critics held that Luke made verifiable errors, that he named officials who never existed, described titles that were historically incorrect, and referenced events in the wrong sequence. This was the consensus among critical scholars.

He planned to prove Luke unreliable through archaeology — and proved the opposite of what he intended.

Ramsay Sets Out to Prove Luke Wrong

William Ramsay accepted that consensus. He was a Scottish archaeologist, trained at Oxford and Göttingen, operating in the late nineteenth century when historical skepticism about the New Testament was at its peak; he had no theological agenda. He planned to prove Luke unreliable through archaeology: to dig up the evidence, examine it against Luke's claims, and demonstrate conclusively that Luke was not a first-rate historian. He spent years doing exactly that.

And then he stopped. Not because he gave up. Because he had proved the opposite of what he intended: the details he had expected to refute turned out, on examination, to be precisely correct.

The Politarchs, the Proconsul, and the Pool

Consider the title "politarch," meaning ruler of the city, which Luke uses in Acts 17:6 for the officials in Thessalonica, a title critics said Luke had invented. It turned out to be confirmed by nineteen inscriptions, seventeen of them in Macedonia, exactly the region Luke describes. The precise sequence of Roman administrative titles Luke uses across Acts (proconsul, propraetor, praetor, tetrarch) matched the administrative structures of the specific provinces he described at the specific times he describes them, in a period when those structures were in constant flux. Luke got every one right.

The more Ramsay dug, the more the ground confirmed Luke. He excavated in Asia Minor for years and found confirmation after confirmation; he wrote books documenting his findings; and after this extended investigation he declared publicly that Luke was one of the greatest historians of antiquity. That is not a small claim from a rigorous archaeologist who had begun as a skeptic and followed the evidence to a different conclusion; Ramsay was not a credulous believer looking to confirm his faith.

The Gallio inscription is one example. Luke identifies Gallio as "proconsul of Achaia" in Acts 18:12, and the title was historically specific: Achaia was a senatorial province from 27 BC to about 15 AD, then an imperial province, then returned to senatorial status in 44 AD, and the title "proconsul" applied only during its senatorial periods. Luke uses it for a period when it applied. The inscription found at Delphi confirms both the title and the man, and allows scholars to date Paul's Corinthian ministry with unusual precision. Luke did not guess. He knew.

The same holds beyond Luke. The Pool of Bethesda, described in John 5:2 with five colonnades, was dismissed by nineteenth-century critics as a literary invention, since no one had found a five-colonnaded pool in Jerusalem; then excavations under the Church of St. Anne, conducted in the twentieth century, revealed exactly that pool with exactly five colonnades. The detail dismissed as evidence of John's unreliability turned out to be evidence of his precision.

The Pattern of Accumulated Confirmation

There is a pattern here. It is not universal: archaeology does not confirm every detail of every biblical account, and honest scholars acknowledge the complexity; there are questions that remain open, sites that have not been excavated, records that have not survived. History does not yield a complete accounting of itself. But the pattern, accumulated across more than a century of archaeology, runs consistently in one direction: when the evidence arrives, Luke tends to look right, John tends to look right, and the details that skeptics cited as proof of invention tend, on examination, to turn out to be proof of knowledge.

The skeptical consensus of the nineteenth century was built on the absence of evidence. When the evidence arrived, the consensus collapsed.

When Coincidence Stops Being a Sufficient Explanation

You can dismiss one confirmed detail as coincidence. You can dismiss two as lucky guesses. But at some point the accumulation of confirmations requires a different explanation: the author knew what he was writing about, he was there or he spoke with people who were, and he had access to information that has since been confirmed by sources he could not have known about.

That is what the evidence shows. Not that Luke was divinely inspired; that is a theological claim. But that he was a careful, accurate, knowledgeable historian whose account of the first-century world has been confirmed repeatedly by independent archaeological and historical evidence. That is worth something. In fact, it is worth a great deal.
