Chapter Thirty

The Reader in the Story — Why This Matters for Your Life

The Bible's claim is that you are not only its reader but a character written into its unfinished story.

I have saved this chapter for last, before the conclusion, because I want to say something that all the manuscript evidence and archaeology and canon history has been building toward but does not by itself reach. You are not just a reader of this text. According to the text's own claim, you are a character in it.

You are not just a reader of this text. According to the text's own claim, you are a character in it.

You Are a Character, Not Only a Reader

This is the unusual, perhaps unique, feature of the biblical story. It is not a completed narrative that you read from outside; it is an unfinished narrative into which you are invited. The story that begins in Genesis and runs through Revelation is still being written, and the chapter being written now is the one you are living.

Paul describes the people of God as letters written with the Spirit of the living God on tablets of human hearts.

You are our letter, written on our hearts, known and read by everyone. You show that you are a letter from Christ, the result of our ministry, written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts.

2 Corinthians 3:2-3

The church is described as the body of Christ, a living, embodied continuation of his presence in the world. Every person who has received the word and believed it has become, in the New Testament's own imagery, part of the story the word tells.

An Unfinished Narrative You Are Invited Into

This is not metaphor in the weak sense: nice language that conveys an abstract truth. It is a claim about what actually happens when the word is received; the person who hears and trusts is incorporated into the story, their life becomes part of the argument, and their faithfulness, or their faithlessness, is evidence in the ongoing case about whether the God of the Bible is real and whether his word is trustworthy.

This is why the reliability question has stakes that are not just intellectual. If the Bible is reliable, if the transmission is trustworthy, the history confirmed, the resurrection real, then the life it calls for is not optional. You are not a spectator reading a story about someone else. You are a participant whose response to the word is part of the story itself.

The New Testament Language of Witness

The New Testament's language for this is witness. The disciples were appointed as witnesses, people whose testimony about what they had seen and heard was to be the mechanism by which the story spread. And then, in the striking move, Jesus tells his disciples that the Holy Spirit will come upon them and they will be his witnesses.

But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes on you; and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.

Acts 1:8

Witnesses: not representatives of an institution, not spokespeople for a theological system, but witnesses, people whose testimony includes their lives as well as their words. The person who has received the word and been transformed by it is, by that transformation, a piece of evidence for the word's truth. Not the only evidence, not the most important evidence, but genuine evidence.

Transformed Lives as Genuine Evidence

This is why the reliability of Scripture, which we have argued for at length on historical and textual grounds, also has a phenomenological dimension: the dimension of lived testimony. The community of people who have trusted the word across twenty centuries is, in aggregate, a remarkable body of evidence. Not because Christians are always right or always good (they are not, and the record of the church's failures is part of the evidence too, evidence that the word had to contend with human sinfulness even in its most committed adherents). But because the transformations are real, the community is persistent across enormous cultural and historical diversity, and the effects of trusting the word (the lives produced, the communities formed, the justice pursued, the suffering endured) are not easily explained by any hypothesis other than that the word is true.

The manuscript tradition is reliable. The history is confirmed. The canon is defensible. The resurrection is attested. And the community of people whose lives have been shaped by trusting the word extends from the first century to the present and includes members on every continent in every language.

What You Are Joining When You Receive the Word

This is what you are joining when you receive the word. Not an institution. Not a tradition. A story: the ongoing story of a God who made the world and will not give it up, who entered it in human flesh and changed everything, and who continues to call people into the story by speaking through the text that has survived every century's attempts to silence it.

The text is reliable. The story is true. The invitation is yours. Take it up and read.
