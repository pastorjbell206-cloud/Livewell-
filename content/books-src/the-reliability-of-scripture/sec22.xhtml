Chapter Twenty-One

The Septuagint, the Apocrypha, and the Boundaries of the Canon

Why Catholic and Protestant Bibles differ — and why the disagreement does not touch the shared core.

That this process was messy does not mean it was arbitrary.

The Question With an Edge to It

There is a question that surfaces in almost every serious investigation of the biblical canon, and it usually arrives with an edge to it: why do Catholic Bibles have more books than Protestant Bibles, and does the difference mean that somebody made an error — either in including the extra books or in excluding them?

The books in question are the deuterocanonical books, called the Apocrypha in Protestant tradition; they include 1 and 2 Maccabees, Tobit, Judith, Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus), Wisdom of Solomon, Baruch, and additions to the books of Daniel and Esther. Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Bibles include these books as Scripture. Protestant Bibles do not, though many print them in a separate section. The historical background is necessary to understand the disagreement.

The Septuagint and the Greek-Speaking Church

The Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, designated LXX — was produced in Alexandria, Egypt, beginning in the third century BC and continuing into the second. According to the Letter of Aristeas, seventy-two Jewish scholars were commissioned to translate the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek for the great library at Alexandria; the legend developed that these seventy-two, working independently, produced identical translations, the divine origin of the work asserted through the miraculous unanimity. This legend gave the translation its name: Septuagint, from the Latin for seventy.

Whether or not the legend is accurate, the Septuagint became the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews throughout the Mediterranean diaspora. And the Septuagint included books that were not in the Hebrew canon recognized by Palestinian Judaism — the deuterocanonical books named above. These were composed in the late Second Temple period, and they were received into the reading of the wider Greek-speaking Jewish community even while Palestinian Jewish authorities did not ultimately include them in the formal canon.

The early church, overwhelmingly Greek-speaking in its first expansion, used the Septuagint as its Old Testament. Most New Testament quotations of the Old Testament follow the Septuagint text rather than the Hebrew — which tells us that Greek was the language of the missionary church and the Septuagint was its Scripture. From the beginning, then, the early church was reading a Greek Old Testament that included books Palestinian Judaism did not ultimately receive as canonical.

Why the Reformation Returned to the Hebrew Canon

When the Protestant Reformation in the sixteenth century returned to the Hebrew canon — the canon recognized by Palestinian Judaism and formalized at Jamnia around 90 AD — it excluded the deuterocanonical books from Scripture proper while often retaining them in a separate section for edification. Luther's reasoning was partly theological; he objected to 2 Maccabees's support for prayers for the dead, which he saw as undergirding the Roman Catholic doctrine of purgatory. His argument for the Hebrew canon was also historical: he believed the Hebrew canon represented the genuine Jewish tradition, while the broader Septuagint canon reflected the expansion of that tradition in the Greek diaspora.

Which canon is correct is not a question this book will settle. It is a genuine theological disagreement between serious Christians, debated for five centuries and certain to be debated still. What I want to establish is narrower and more durable: this disagreement does not undermine the reliability of either the common canon — the books accepted by both Catholics and Protestants — or the additional books.

The Apocrypha Is Ancient, Not Forged

The disputed books are not forgeries. They are genuinely ancient texts. First Maccabees is an important historical source for the Maccabean period; Sirach is a sophisticated wisdom text with real value for understanding second-temple Jewish piety; Tobit is a moving story about faith in the context of suffering and exile. These books did not enter the canon through political conspiracy. They were read in many communities because they were useful and edifying.

What they lack, in the view of those who hold to the Hebrew canon, is the marks that distinguish Scripture proper from other excellent and useful literature: explicit prophetic origin, recognition by the authoritative Jewish community, and coherence with the established revelation. Jerome, who produced the Latin Vulgate and was the greatest biblical scholar of the early church, shared these doubts about the deuterocanonical books and expressed them explicitly in his prefaces — even as the church required him to translate them.

Why the Disagreement Leaves the Shared Canon Intact

For Protestant readers of this book, the question of whether the Apocrypha belongs in the canon does not affect the reliability of the sixty-six books. The manuscript tradition, the archaeological evidence, the early dating, the canon history for the common canon — all of it stands regardless of how you resolve the Apocrypha question.

For Catholic and Orthodox readers, your canon includes the sixty-six books plus the deuterocanonicals, and nothing in this book's argument undermines the common core both traditions share. The reliability case applies with equal force to the books we hold together.

What the Apocrypha question finally illustrates is that the canonical process was not a single event but a long, sometimes contentious discernment across communities with different traditions. That this process was messy does not mean it was arbitrary. Communities were asking real questions about real books, applying real criteria. The outcome — the common canon shared by virtually all Christian traditions — represents the settled conviction of an enormous, diverse, multi-century community that these books bear the marks of divine origin in a way that no other texts do.
