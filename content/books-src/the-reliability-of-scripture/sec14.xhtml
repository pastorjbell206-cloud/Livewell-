Chapter Thirteen

Why It Still Matters — Living from the Authority of Scripture

The reliability question is finally a question about whether you can build a life on this text when everything is falling apart.

You can build an interesting philosophy on an interesting book. You cannot build a life on it when everything is falling apart.

From a Library Question to a Life Question

Let me come back to the nineteen-year-old in the front row. She is older now, and the sharp clarity of that college classroom moment — the excitement of having found the questions nobody in her church could answer — has been replaced by something more complicated. The questions are still there; they are always there. But they have changed texture. They are no longer primarily intellectual puzzles to be solved. They have become existential questions about whether the ground holds.

She is thirty-five now. She has a marriage that is harder than she expected, and children who are watching her — not to see whether she can defend the historicity of the Exodus, but to see whether her faith actually costs her anything and whether it actually helps her. She is trying to make decisions in a professional context that penalizes the ethics the Bible teaches; she is watching people she loves make choices she believes are destructive; and she is trying to figure out whether to say anything, and what to say.

The question she is asking is not a library question. It is a functional question. Can I actually build my life on this? Not hypothetically. In the specific. Can I trust this enough to order my marriage by it when the easier path would be to cut and run? To raise my children by it in a culture that treats its teachings as optional at best and harmful at worst? To hold to moral positions that cost me professionally, socially, relationally, because this book says those positions are true? To speak hard truths to people I love because the text says love requires it? The evidence we have examined for the past twelve chapters has been building toward this question — not toward a library project, but toward a life.

What the Evidence Gives You: Warrant, Not Mere Certainty

The manuscript evidence establishes that the text on our pages is the text originally written. The canon history establishes that the collection we call the Bible is the collection recognized by the church as bearing the marks of genuine apostolic testimony. The archaeological confirmation establishes that the historical claims the Bible makes about the ancient world have been substantially verified by independent evidence. And the early dating establishes that the New Testament was produced within living memory of the events it describes.

What this evidence does together is give us warrant. Not certainty that requires no faith — that is not available in this life for anything that matters — but warrant, the kind of reasonable ground on which reasonable people, evaluating the evidence honestly, can stand. Warrant means that trust is not irrational; it means that when you build your life on this text, you are not doing something intellectually reckless. You are doing something the evidence supports, something that reasonable people across the centuries have done, something that has produced lives of coherence and integrity and service and love that the world has consistently been unable to explain by any other means.

I am a pastor. I have spent more than two decades sitting with people in the hardest moments of their lives — in hospital rooms, in marriages that were falling apart, in conversations about the deaths of children, in the aftermath of moral failure that destroyed careers and families and communities. I have watched what people had when everything else was stripped away. The people who had something were the people who had something the circumstances could not take: something that was true before the crisis began and would be true after it ended, something that gave the suffering meaning without requiring that the suffering be pretended away.

The Bible's reliability is not an abstract academic question. It is the question that determines whether you have that something. If the text is unreliable — if it is a human document produced by ordinary processes subject to ordinary errors and ordinary corruption — then it is a book among books, useful perhaps, interesting certainly, but not the kind of foundation on which a life can actually be built. You can build an interesting philosophy on an interesting book. You cannot build a life on it when everything is falling apart. If the text is reliable — if the manuscript evidence, the early dating, the archaeological confirmation, and the canon history all point to a document that is what it claims to be — then it is not a book among books. It is the word of the living God, transmitted with extraordinary care across twenty centuries, attested by the blood of people who chose death rather than deny it, confirmed by the evidence of independent scholarship, and available to you right now.

The Lives Built on the Text: Wilberforce and King

I want to say something about the people who built their lives on this book, because their testimony matters. William Wilberforce spent twenty years of his political life fighting the British slave trade, at cost to his health, his political capital, and his social standing. His case was built on the biblical teaching that every human being bears the image of God and therefore possesses an inalienable dignity that no institution of man can legitimately strip. He was right about the slave trade. He was right because the Bible is right. The image of God is not a poetic figure; it is a claim about what human beings actually are.

The civil rights movement in America drew from the same wells. Martin Luther King Jr. did not build his moral case on secular liberal philosophy, though he knew it well; he built it on the prophetic texts of the Old Testament and the liberating gospel of the New.

Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream

Amos 5:24

When he stood at the Lincoln Memorial and described his dream, he was not deploying metaphor. He was preaching Scripture. He was calling America to account on the basis of a text whose moral vision exceeded any political philosophy his opponents could produce.

When the Same Text Was Used to Oppress

None of this means that everyone who appeals to Scripture is right about everything. The slaveholder appealed to Scripture. The segregationist appealed to Scripture. The heretic always appeals to Scripture. This is the argument of The Monster in the Mirror, and it is true and important and must be taken seriously: the same text that liberated was used to oppress. This demands humility and rigor in interpretation.

But here is the critical point. The abolitionist's interpretation was better. The civil rights advocate's interpretation was better — better not by some external standard but by the internal standard of the text itself, read carefully, read honestly, read with attention to the whole rather than the convenient part. The slaveholder was reading the text against itself. The abolitionist was reading the text as it demands to be read.

Reading the Whole Text Against Our Convenient Readings

This is exactly what the doctrine of biblical reliability requires us to do. Not to wave the Bible around as a club. Not to extract proof texts for predetermined conclusions. It requires us to read the whole text, carefully, in context, allowing it to challenge what we bring to it rather than simply confirming what we already believe. That is hard. It is the hardest thing the Bible asks of us — not to let it say what we want it to say, but to let it say what it actually says.

And what it says is this: God made the world. God made you in his image, which is why you are not dispensable and cannot be treated as if you are. You have turned from God in a way that has broken you — not in a metaphorical sense, but in an actual sense that you can observe in the gap between who you are and who you know you should be. You cannot fix this yourself. God has fixed it, at cost to himself, in a historical event that the evidence suggests happened. You are invited to receive what was done and to live in response to it.

These claims are demanding. They ask you to relinquish the position of sovereign over your own life and to live under a different authority. They ask you to treat other people as image-bearers of God, which is inconvenient and often costly. They ask you to tell the truth when lying would be easier, to forgive when you have been genuinely wronged, to hold to a sexual ethic that runs against the cultural grain, and to give with a generosity that is not comfortable. All of this is costly. The Bible never promised it would not be. The cross does not suggest a theology of comfort.

But what the Bible also says — and what the lives of people who have built on it have demonstrated, across twenty centuries, in every culture and every language — is that this costly life is the only life that corresponds to the truth of what you are. You are not an accident. You are not the product of random forces. You are a creature made in the image of God, for a relationship with God, to live in a world that is God's; and when you live that way, as difficult as it is, you are living in alignment with what you actually are.

The Bible is reliable. The manuscript tradition establishes it. The archaeology confirms it. The early dating attests it. The canon history supports it. The text that survived every attack from every century's critics is available to you. The question is what you will do with it.
