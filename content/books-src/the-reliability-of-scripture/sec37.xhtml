Chapter 36

What You Do with What You Now Know

The cumulative case does not compel belief, but it does rule out the casual dismissal.

None of these, standing alone, requires you to bow your knee. But they do not stand alone. They stand together.

Evidence That Stands Together, Not Alone

The evidence in this book is cumulative. No single piece of it is decisive in isolation; the manuscript comparison with other ancient documents, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the archaeological confirmations, the eyewitness testimony structure, the early dating of the documents, the prophecy and fulfillment pattern, the campaign to destroy it — none of these, standing alone, requires you to bow your knee. But they do not stand alone. They stand together. And together they form an argument that deserves a serious response.

What the Evidence Rules Out

The serious response is not automatic belief. The evidence can be examined, challenged, weighed, doubted, and engaged with over years of honest inquiry, and that is not only permitted — it is encouraged. The tradition has always welcomed inquiry because the tradition has always believed that careful inquiry leads toward the truth rather than away from it.

What the evidence does not support is the casual dismissal. It asks something of you instead: that you take the Bible seriously enough to read it the way you would read any important historical document — with attention to authorship, dating, sources, genre, and the question of what the author was trying to say, to whom, and why. It asks you to hold a question you may have closed prematurely. Not the question of whether Christianity is true — that is a bigger question, involving the resurrection and the person of Jesus and the claims of experience that lie beyond the scope of this book — but the preliminary one: is this a document that comes to us with sufficient historical integrity that its content deserves serious engagement?

The answer this book has argued is yes. Not a naive yes; a studied yes — a yes that has looked at the manuscript tradition, the archaeological confirmations, the historical corroboration, the structure of eyewitness testimony, and the extraordinary survival of the text against sustained campaigns of destruction, and concluded that the evidence, honestly assessed, supports the reliability of the document we hold in our hands. What you do with that conclusion is up to you.

The Dismissals That Rest on Ignorance

The person who says "the Bible is just a bunch of myths" has not engaged the evidence; the person who says "we don't really know what the original texts said" has not read the textual critics; the person who says "archaeology has discredited the Bible" has not spoken with archaeologists. These positions, confidently held, rest on ignorance. Not stupidity — ignorance. The information exists, and it has not been hidden; it lives in books that are publicly available, in universities that teach these disciplines, in scholarly journals that have been publishing findings for two centuries.

I am not asking you to kneel on the basis of this book. I am asking you to recognize that the dismissals you may have relied on are not proportionate to the evidence. The Bible has not been discredited. The manuscript tradition is robust, the archaeological confirmations are real, the eyewitness structure is genuine, and the text we hold is substantially what was written in the first century.

The Question Behind the Obstacles

I want to be honest about what I believe is at stake. This is not a book about a book. It is a book about the claims the book makes — about God, about human beings, about the nature of our condition and the nature of our rescue. If the book is reliable, then those claims deserve engagement that most of us have never given them; if the book is reliable, then the question of what it says about Jesus cannot be answered with a shrug.

The stakes are not academic. The claims of Scripture, if they are what Scripture says they are — if this is the living word of the God who made the world — then the appropriate response is not a seminar. It is kneeling. What was written in the first century makes claims about Jesus that demand a verdict, and that verdict is yours to reach. This book has tried to clear away the obstacles that might have stood between you and the actual question. The obstacles — the manuscript myths, the archaeology dismissals, the conspiracy theories about canon — were real, and they deserved real answers. The question behind the obstacles is realer still.

Read Mark and Ask the Question It Dares You to Ask

Pick up the Gospel of Mark and read it in one sitting. Ask the question a historian asks: what kind of document is this? Who wrote it, and why? What is it claiming? Is the claim coherent? Then ask the question that goes deeper, the one Mark is daring you to ask.

Who do you say that I am?

That question has been waiting for two thousand years, in a text preserved with extraordinary care, through extraordinary pressure, against extraordinary odds. It is still waiting.
