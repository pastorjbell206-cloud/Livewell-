Chapter Eighteen

When Faith and Doubt Inhabit the Same Room

A seminary professor's honest answer models a faith that can hold real questions without flinching.

Doubt is not the enemy of faith.

The Professor Who Did Not Deflect

I want to tell you about a conversation I had with a seminary professor in my first year of graduate work. I had gone to his office carrying a question I had held for months, not a textbook question but a real one, the kind that does not let you sleep. I had been reading the critical scholarship seriously, not the popular versions but the actual academic work, the journals, the monographs, and somewhere in that reading I had run into an argument I could not immediately answer: a technical question about the relationship between two Gospel accounts that, in the form I had encountered it, seemed to open a genuine historical problem.

I expected one of three things. I expected him to dismiss the question, or to hand me a pre-packaged answer, or to tell me to take it by faith. I had been given each of those responses before, and each time they had left me a little more unsettled than they found me. He did none of them.

He sat back in his chair. "That is a real question," he said. "I have thought about it for twenty years and I am not satisfied with any of the standard answers. Here is what I hold, and here is why I hold it, and here is what I still do not know." He talked for forty-five minutes, showing me the strongest version of the objection, walking through the serious scholarly responses, naming the places he genuinely did not have an answer. And at the end he said something I have never forgotten: the alternative is that everything else he had found, the manuscript evidence, the archaeology, the testimony of the community of faith across twenty centuries, is explained by something less than the truth of the gospel, and he found that explanation less credible than the alternative, which is that he is working with incomplete information.

That was not a dismissal of the question. It was an honest answer to the question, from a man who had spent his life with the question and had reached a considered conclusion. It is the posture I want to commend: not the posture of the person who refuses to ask because the asking threatens faith, and not the posture of the person who mistakes doubt for wisdom and settles into a permanent suspension of judgment to avoid the cost of commitment, but the posture of one who takes the questions seriously, follows the evidence honestly, and arrives, with eyes open, at a conclusion that can be held with integrity even in the presence of unresolved difficulties.

Doubt as the Furnace of Faith, Not Its Enemy

Doubt is not the enemy of faith. This needs to be said with some force, because the church has a long and unfortunate tradition of treating doubt as sin, of policing the question rather than answering it, of mistaking a member's honest uncertainty for an early symptom of unbelief. That tradition has done more damage than the doubts it feared.

What Jesus Actually Did With Thomas

Consider how the church has handled the disciple Thomas, made into a byword for faithlessness, "doubting Thomas," when what Thomas actually said was that he would not believe without evidence. And what Jesus did was not rebuke Thomas for his epistemological standards. Jesus provided the evidence Thomas asked for; he showed him the wounds in his hands and his side. The standard the church learned to scorn is the standard Jesus chose to meet.

A Church That Trusts Its Own Case

A church that panics when its members doubt is a church that does not trust the case. A church that trusts the case can engage the questions with the equanimity of the professor in that office, naming the difficulties honestly, working through the evidence carefully, acknowledging what is not known, and arriving at the considered conclusion that the evidence supports trust.

The young woman in the front row who blew up the college ministry meeting was not wrong to ask her questions. She was wrong, or the community was wrong, to read the silence that followed as proof that the questions were unanswerable. They were not unanswerable. They were questions the community had not been prepared to answer, because the community had not done the work.

And the work is available. It has been done by serious scholars across centuries: the manuscript evidence assembled and analyzed, the archaeology evaluated, the canon history reconstructed, the early dating arguments made, the internal evidence examined. The case is strong: not airtight, because nothing historical is airtight, but strong in the way that warrants trust. What I have tried to do in this book is show you the work, not summarize it superficially but show you enough of the actual content that you can evaluate it, follow up on it, dig deeper where you need to, and arrive at your own considered conclusion.

The Considered Conclusion You Can Stand On

The conclusion I have arrived at, after decades of pastoral ministry and decades of engaging this scholarship seriously, is this: the Bible is reliable. The text we have corresponds to what was originally written. The history it describes has been substantially confirmed. The canon was recognized through a coherent, multi-criteria process. The New Testament is early enough to bear eyewitness testimony. The resurrection claim is supported by evidence that requires explaining.

I hold this conclusion with confidence, not with a certainty that requires no faith, because there is no certainty in this domain that requires no faith, but with the confidence that comes from having followed the argument honestly, having engaged the strongest objections, having sat with the genuine difficulties, and having found that the ground holds.

The silence that met the nineteen-year-old's questions was a failure of the community, not a defeat of the case. The case was there all along. It was just not being made. This book is my attempt to make it.
