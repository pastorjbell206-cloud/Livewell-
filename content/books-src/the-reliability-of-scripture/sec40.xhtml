Standing at the Edge of the Argument

There is a moment in serious intellectual honesty when you have followed the argument as far as evidence can take you, and you are standing at the edge of something that only decision can cross. I have been there. More than once.

The first time was at eighteen, when a Christian roommate sat with my atheist questions and did not flinch, when he answered them rather than deflecting them, when the case he made was stronger than I had assumed any case for Christianity could be. But the moment I found the intellectual case compelling was not the moment I became a Christian. The argument got me to the edge. The decision to cross was something the argument alone could not make for me.

I crossed. And what I found on the other side was not certainty without doubt; I have had doubts at forty that I did not have at eighteen: subtler, deeper, less amenable to quick resolution. What I found on the other side was a world that matched the description in the text. Not in the cheap sense (life is hard, prayer is sometimes silent, faithfulness does not guarantee comfortable outcomes), but in the sense that the text's account of what human beings are, what they need, what restores them, what breaks them, and what holds them in the worst moments proved more accurate than any alternative account I encountered.

What This Book Established and What It Did Not

I want to be clear about what this book has done and what it has not done. It has established that the text we call the Bible bears extraordinary textual reliability: that what we read on the page corresponds to what was originally written; that the historical claims of the biblical narrative have been substantially confirmed by independent archaeological and historical evidence; that the canon was formed through a recognizable process of discernment over time, not by political conspiracy; and that the New Testament documents are early enough to bear eyewitness or near-eyewitness testimony to the events they describe.

What it has not done is prove that God exists. It has not proven that Jesus rose from the dead in a way that requires no faith to accept. It has not removed the element of personal decision from the encounter with Scripture. These things cannot be proven in the way that mathematical propositions can be proven or that chemical reactions can be observed; they belong to a different domain of human knowing: the domain in which the evidence supports a conclusion that only a willed response can fully appropriate.

Faith That Comes After the Thinking, Not Instead of It

This is what the Christian tradition has always said about faith. Not blind faith: faith that ignores the questions, dismisses the evidence, refuses to think. Faith that comes at the end of the thinking, not instead of it; faith that says: I have followed the argument, the evidence is stronger than I expected, the case is more compelling than the culture suggested, and now I am going to do what evidence alone cannot do for me: I am going to trust.

That trust is not irrational. It is built on the best available evidence, and it is the same kind of trust that allows you to board an airplane without understanding aerodynamics, to take medication without understanding biochemistry, to make a vow to another person without being able to guarantee the future. You act on warrant, not certainty. You extend trust where the evidence supports trust.

The Bible has earned that trust. The manuscript tradition is extraordinary; the archaeological record confirms rather than contradicts; the canon was recognized through coherent criteria applied consistently; the documents are early enough to bear genuine testimony; and the internal testimony of the text (its claim to divine origin, its consistency across centuries, its theological coherence) is coherent with the external evidence. The vessel holds.

The Question on the Beach in John 21

There is a story in the Gospel of John, in the twenty-first chapter, that sits at the very end of the Gospel. Jesus has been crucified and risen. Peter has denied him three times. And Jesus meets Peter on the beach, and he asks him three times: do you love me? Three times, matching the three denials. Three times, Peter says yes. And three times, Jesus says: feed my sheep, tend my lambs, follow me.

The question is not a theological question. It is not an intellectual question. It is the only question that the reliability of Scripture was always building toward.

Do you love me?

John 21

What you do with the text finally comes down to this. Not whether you can defend its manuscript tradition. Not whether you can answer every critical scholar. Not whether you have resolved every difficulty. But whether, at the end of the argument, standing at the edge of what the evidence can establish, you are willing to say yes: I love you, I trust you, I will follow.

The Vessel Holds

The text that has survived every century's critics is still asking the question. The ground holds.

Take it up and read.
