Dating, Authorship, and the Documentary Hypothesis

Wellhausen and the Four-Source Theory

The Documentary Hypothesis deserves serious engagement rather than dismissal or anxious defense. Julius Wellhausen presented its classic form in his 1878 work Prolegomena to the History of Israel, and the argument was as ambitious as it was tidy: the Pentateuch — the first five books of the Old Testament — was not written by Moses but compiled from four separate literary sources, designated J, E, D, and P, each composed at a different period in Israelite history and stitched together by later editors into the final document.

The sources were dated in sequence. The J source, named for its use of the divine name Jahweh, and the E source, named for its use of Elohim, were the earliest, belonging to the tenth and ninth centuries BC respectively. The D source, associated with the book found in the Temple during Josiah's reform in 2 Kings 22, was placed in the seventh century BC. The P source — the Priestly strand concerned with tabernacle, sacrifice, and ritual — was reckoned the latest, composed during or after the Babylonian Exile in the sixth century BC. Wellhausen's work was enormously influential; for most of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, source analysis of the Pentateuch along roughly Wellhausenian lines dominated critical Old Testament scholarship.

How the Critics Took the Hypothesis Apart

What has happened to the Documentary Hypothesis since Wellhausen is not what either its proponents or its critics expected. The hypothesis has not been confirmed in its details. It has been progressively dismantled by the very critical scholars who built their careers on it.

By the 1970s, scholars like Rolf Rendtorff and Erhard Blum were arguing that the classic four-source division was not supportable, because the criteria Wellhausen used to identify the sources — the divine names, doublets in the narrative, vocabulary differences — turned out to be unreliable indicators. Different uses of the divine names reflect literary and theological conventions that do not require separate sources; narrative doublets appear throughout ancient Near Eastern literature that has nothing to do with multiple authorship; and the vocabulary differences between supposed sources are no greater than the differences found within any single author's work.

By the late twentieth and early twenty-first century, the field of Pentateuchal criticism had entered what scholars candidly described as a state of crisis — no consensus on how many sources existed, when they were composed, how they were combined, or even whether the source hypothesis was the right framework at all. John Van Seters, David Carr, Jean-Louis Ska, and others have produced dramatically different reconstructions of Pentateuchal composition that agree with one another only in rejecting the classic Wellhausen model.

The Case for Mosaic Authorship Reconsidered

What this means for the conservative reader of the Pentateuch is not vindication in every detail. The questions about composition history are real and will continue to be debated. But the confident assertion that scholarship has definitively established the Pentateuch's late composition and non-Mosaic authorship misrepresents the actual state of the field; that confident assertion has not survived the field's own internal criticism.

The conservative position — that Moses composed the substantive core of the Pentateuch, with possible later editorial additions — is not maintained by ignoring the scholarship. It is maintained by taking the scholarship seriously enough to note that the alternative hypotheses have not achieved the stability of established fact. The Pentateuch presents itself as containing Mosaic material; Jesus treats it as Mosaic; the church has consistently affirmed it; and the critical alternative remains a hypothesis in flux.

One Isaiah or Two? The Question of Predictive Prophecy

The authorship of Isaiah is a separate question, one with somewhat more persistent scholarly support for a divided authorship. The classic argument holds that chapters 1-39 reflect the eighth-century context of the historical Isaiah, while chapters 40-66 presuppose the Babylonian exile as a present reality rather than a future prediction, and are therefore assigned to an anonymous "Deutero-Isaiah" writing in the sixth century BC or later.

The argument deserves a direct answer, and the most direct answer is this: it depends on the prior assumption that predictive prophecy of the kind found in Isaiah 40-66 is impossible. If you exclude supernatural prediction as a category, then you must explain the second half of Isaiah as composed after the events it appears to foretell — because it names Cyrus of Persia specifically. But the assumption that such prediction is impossible is not a historical argument. It is a philosophical presupposition imported into historical analysis.

The manuscript evidence, meanwhile, does not support the division. The Great Isaiah Scroll from the Dead Sea Scrolls — one of our most ancient witnesses to the text — presents the book as a single unified document with no textual break between chapters 39 and 40; the New Testament cites both sections and attributes both to "Isaiah the prophet" without distinction; and the internal thematic coherence between the two halves, the consistency of vocabulary, imagery, and theological vision, has been argued persuasively by scholars like Gleason Archer and John Oswalt. The divided authorship hypothesis remains influential in critical circles, but it is not the settled conclusion that popularizers often present.

The Disputed Pauline Letters

The Pauline authorship questions — regarding 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus, Ephesians, Colossians, and 2 Thessalonians — involve genuine scholarly debate about vocabulary statistics, theological emphases, and historical setting. The arguments for pseudonymous authorship are not trivial. Neither are the responses from conservative scholars, who note that vocabulary differences between letters addressing different audiences and different problems are expected in any writer, and that the notion of pseudonymous authorship as a legitimate ancient practice has been overstated.

What matters for the reliability question is this: even if the pseudonymity hypothesis for some Pauline letters were correct, it would not mean the New Testament was fabricated. Ancient pseudonymous composition operated by conventions understood within the culture; it does not imply deception in the modern sense. And the theological content of the disputed letters coheres with the undisputed letters in ways that make any massive theological departure difficult to sustain. The authorship debates are worth engaging, and they are not resolved in every case. But they are not evidence that the Bible we have is unreliable.
