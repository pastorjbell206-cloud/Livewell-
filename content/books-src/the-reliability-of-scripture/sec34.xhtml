Chapter 33

The Problem of Alleged Contradictions

The famous list of Bible contradictions sorts into four kinds — and none requires abandoning the text.

The List You Have Encountered

This chapter is for the reader who has met the list. You know the one — it circulates online, in skeptical forums, in books written to dismantle faith, sometimes fifty items long, sometimes a hundred, sometimes three hundred, all of it building toward a single devastating implication: a book this internally inconsistent cannot be trusted. If it cannot get its own details right, how can it be the word of God?

That deserves honest engagement — not dismissal, and not capitulation. So I want to address it the way it asks to be addressed, by sorting the list into the kinds of things it actually contains. There are four of them, and the difference between them is everything.

The difficulty of a passage does not mean the document is false. It means the reader has more work to do.

Complementary Accounts, Not Contradictions

Some of the alleged contradictions are not contradictions at all; they are complementary accounts. When Matthew and Luke give different genealogies for Jesus, they are tracing different lines — Matthew the legal lineage through Joseph, Luke the biological lineage through Mary, or Matthew Joseph's line and Luke a collateral one. When Mark says the disciples found a colt and Matthew says they found a donkey and her colt, Matthew is simply including a detail Mark omits. This is not contradiction. It is what you find when multiple independent witnesses describe the same events from different vantage points, with different emphases.

The Gospel accounts are not stenographic transcripts of identical events rendered identically; they are four distinct witnesses, writing for distinct audiences, emphasizing distinct aspects of what they saw and knew. The minor variations between them are exactly what you would expect from honest, independent witnesses. Accounts that agreed on every detail and differed in no way would actually be more suspicious, not less. They would look coached.

Copyist Errors Versus the Inspired Autograph

A second category reflects a misunderstanding of genre. The numbers in Chronicles and Kings sometimes differ from one another — and this is real. The question is why, and Old Testament scholars have spent considerable energy on it; the most credible explanations involve differences in counting systems (were co-regencies counted, were partial years counted?), differences in the sources each author used, and copyist errors in the transmission of numerical data. Such errors are among the most common in manuscript transmission precisely because numbers lack the self-correcting quality of narrative text: a scribe copying a story will notice if a word makes no sense in context, but a scribe copying "3,700 horsemen" has no contextual check against copying "37,000 horsemen."

The existence of copyist transmission errors is not a theological crisis. It is what the textual critics have been working on for two centuries, and it is why we have critical editions of the text. The inspired original — what scholars call the autograph — is what the doctrine of inerrancy describes; the transmission is what the discipline of textual criticism addresses. These are different things, and conflating them produces unnecessary confusion.

Genuine Tensions With Genuine Solutions: Paul and James

A third category contains genuine tensions that require careful exegesis and that have genuine solutions. The apparent conflict between Paul's teaching that we are justified by faith and James's teaching that faith without works is dead is not a contradiction; it is two writers addressing two different errors — antinomianism and a dead orthodoxy — using the same vocabulary to mean different but compatible things. When Paul says Abraham was justified by faith and James says Abraham was justified by works, they are making different points about the same man: Paul points to the moment of justification, Genesis 15, where faith is credited as righteousness, and James points to the demonstration of that faith, Genesis 22, the near-sacrifice of Isaac. Read both carefully, in context, for the audience each is addressing, and the tension resolves.

The Genuinely Difficult Passages, Honestly Named

A fourth category is harder, and honesty requires naming it. Some of the alleged contradictions are genuinely difficult. There are harmonization problems in the passion narratives that careful scholars have wrestled with for centuries without full resolution, and there are chronological puzzles in the sequence of events that do not yield easy answers. An honest account of biblical reliability does not pretend these do not exist.

What an honest account says is this: the existence of difficult passages is not an argument against the Bible's reliability. It is an argument for careful reading. Every ancient document of any complexity has difficult passages, and the difficulty of a passage does not mean the document is false — it means the reader has more work to do. The question is not whether the Bible is without interpretive difficulty; of course it is not. The question is whether the difficulties are of a kind that discredits the overall reliability of the text, and the answer, after two thousand years of careful engagement with those difficulties, is no.

The list of alleged contradictions is real. Some of the items on it have good answers; some have better answers than most people have heard; some remain genuinely difficult. None of them, on examination, require the conclusion that the Bible is fundamentally unreliable. That does not mean they require no examination. It means the examination is worth doing.
