Chapter Twenty-Nine

What Kind of Book Is This? — The Unity of Scripture

Forty authors, fifteen hundred years, three languages — and a single coherent story.

It is the story that emerges from the library when the library is read as a whole.

A Library of Sixty-Six Books and Forty Authors

There is a feature of the Bible that is easy to miss until someone points it out, and then it becomes impossible to miss. The Bible is a library of sixty-six books written by approximately forty different human authors over a period of roughly fifteen hundred years, a span no other text in human possession can claim. The authors include a shepherd-king, a tent-maker, a physician, a tax collector, a fisherman, a priest, a general, a prophet who had a vision of divine chariots, and a prisoner on a Greek island writing about the end of all things.

They wrote in three languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek) across three continents, and the latest of them had no contact with most of the earliest, separated as they were by a thousand years of history. And the book they produced is coherent.

The Single Story From Garden to City

Coherent: not in the sense that everything in it is easy to harmonize, or that there are no tensions within the canon, for we have discussed the genuine tensions at length. Coherent in the sense that the library has a single story running through it; a story that begins in a garden and ends in a city, that starts with a creation and ends with a new creation, that introduces a problem in chapter three of the first book and resolves it in the last chapters of the last book.

The story is this. God made the world good. Human beings, given the gift of freedom, chose to use that freedom against the one who gave it, and the consequences of that choice infected the created order: relationships broken, the ground cursed, death introduced, the image of God in humanity twisted but not destroyed. God did not abandon the creation. He began, immediately, the long work of restoration: promising a seed who would crush the serpent, selecting a particular people through whom the restoration would come, giving them law and prophet and priest and king as anticipatory structures pointing toward the final resolution. And then, in the fullness of time, he himself entered the creation as one of its creatures, lived the human life that no human being had lived before, absorbed the consequences of the human choice in his own body, and walked out of the tomb to demonstrate that death is not the final word.

The church, formed by this event, carries the message of the event into every corner of the world. The story ends (provisionally, because the ending is still being written in human history) with the promise of a final resolution in which everything broken is restored, everything lost is found, every tear wiped away, and the creation finally becomes what it was always meant to be: the dwelling of God with his people.

This is not a story assembled by committee. It is not the product of editorial decisions made by councils trying to harmonize incompatible traditions. It is the story that emerges from the library when the library is read as a whole, when the typological connections, the thematic threads, the narrative progressions are allowed to speak.

Typology: Adam and Christ, Passover and the Lamb

The typological connections are particularly striking. Adam in Genesis and Jesus in the Gospels are paired in Paul's argument in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15: two representative figures, one bringing death into the human condition and one reversing it. There is the Passover lamb whose blood protected Israel from judgment, and there is Jesus, the "Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world".

The tabernacle in the wilderness, the mobile dwelling of God's presence among the people, finds its answer in the incarnation, which John 1:14 describes using the Greek word for "tabernacling": the Word "tabernacled" among us. The Exodus from Egypt anticipates the deliverance from sin; the conquest of the land anticipates the eschatological restoration of all things.

Connections Invited by the Text, Not Imposed on It

These connections are not imposed on the text from the outside. They are invited by the text itself. The later authors are reading the earlier authors and finding in them patterns that illuminate what they are experiencing. Paul is not cherry-picking the Old Testament for useful illustrations; he is reading the Old Testament as a document that was, from the beginning, pointing toward the Christ event, and finding that the pointing is precise.

Coherence as Internal Evidence of One Author

The canonical coherence of Scripture is, in the judgment of many scholars who have studied the question carefully, one of the strongest internal evidences for divine authorship. It is the kind of coherence that emerges when one author superintends many authors, when the diversity of human perspectives is unified by a single divine purpose working through the entire canonical process.

This does not prove divine authorship. Human editors could, over time, have created the appearance of coherence by selecting, arranging, and harmonizing a disparate collection. But the scale of the coherence, and the way it runs through every level of the text (from individual word choices to broad narrative structures to typological patterns) is not easily explained by editorial activity alone. It is the kind of coherence that a single Author's intention would produce.
