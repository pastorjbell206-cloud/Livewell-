Chapter Five

The New Testament Under Fire — Evidence, Eyewitnesses, and Early Dating

The earliest creed and the dating of Acts place the New Testament within living memory of the events it reports.

The case against the New Testament's historical reliability has been made in many forms across two centuries of critical scholarship, but its most recent popular form — Bart Ehrman's Misquoting Jesus and the broader cultural narrative it represents — has been enormously influential. Millions of people who have never opened a textual criticism monograph have absorbed its conclusion: that the New Testament we hold is not the New Testament originally written, because the copying process introduced so many errors and changes that we cannot trust what we are reading.

We have already addressed the manuscript evidence directly. The variations are well-documented, they are mostly minor, and none of them jeopardizes any significant doctrine. But the reliability question is not only about manuscripts. It is also about dating. When were the New Testament documents written, and how close to the events they describe? Were they written by eyewitnesses, by people reporting what eyewitnesses said, or by people far removed in time and place from anything they claimed to record? These questions have concrete answers.

We are not in the territory of legend. We are in the territory of recent history reported by living witnesses.

Paul's Letters as the Earliest Evidence

Let us start with Paul, because Paul's letters are the earliest New Testament documents we have, and they contain material that takes us even further back than the letters themselves. Paul wrote his first letter to the Corinthians approximately 53 to 55 AD, and in its fifteenth chapter he preserves something older — a fragment of tradition he did not compose but inherited, set down in a form already fixed before he ever put pen to papyrus.

The Pre-Pauline Creed in 1 Corinthians 15

What Paul includes in chapter fifteen is what scholars across the spectrum recognize as a pre-Pauline creed — a formulaic statement of the core gospel that existed before he wrote it down:

For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep. Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me.

1 Corinthians 15:3-8

Several elements of this creed carry real weight. The language "I delivered... I received" translates technical Jewish vocabulary for the formal transmission of tradition — the language of a rabbi handing on a teaching that has been carefully preserved. Paul is reporting that he received this material and passed it on; it pre-exists his writing of the letter. So when did Paul receive it? His letter to the Galatians tells us that he visited Jerusalem approximately three years after his conversion and spent fifteen days with Peter and James, the same people named as resurrection witnesses in the creed (Galatians 1:18-19). Paul's conversion is generally dated to 32 to 35 AD, which places that Jerusalem visit around 35 to 38 AD. The creed was already in circulation by then.

This means we possess a formalized statement of the resurrection — naming specific witnesses, making specific claims about the sequence of appearances — within three to eight years of the crucifixion. We are not in the territory of legend. We are in the territory of recent history reported by living witnesses.

Why the Ending of Acts Argues for Early Dating

The formal dating of the Gospels has been debated extensively, yet several considerations push the scholarly consensus earlier than the popular account suggests, and the clearest of them is the strange way the book of Acts ends. The Acts of the Apostles closes with Paul under house arrest in Rome, awaiting trial — and it contains no account of Paul's death, which historical sources date to roughly 64 to 67 AD, nor any account of the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 AD, a catastrophic event of enormous significance for the Jewish-Christian community that would be inexplicable for any writer interested in that relationship to omit. These omissions are most naturally explained by a composition date before the events occurred — that is, before 64 AD.

This matters enormously for the Gospel of Luke, because Acts 1:1 explicitly refers to "the first book I wrote" — the Gospel of Luke — as preceding Acts. If Acts was completed before 64 AD, Luke must be earlier still; and if Luke is earlier, the Gospels' broad sequence pushes back further. Most scholars hold that Mark was the first Gospel written, used as a source by both Matthew and Luke. A date for Luke in the late 50s or early 60s therefore drives Mark back to the early or mid-50s — within twenty years of the crucifixion.

Robinson's Case That Everything Was Written Before 70 AD

John A.T. Robinson, a New Testament scholar of the mid-twentieth century, was decidedly not a conservative apologist; he wrote Honest to God, the controversial Anglican bishop's attempt to reimagine Christian theology. Yet when he examined the dating of the New Testament books systematically in Redating the New Testament (1976), his conclusion startled the scholarly world: every book of the New Testament was written before 70 AD. Robinson was not arguing this from theological conviction. He was following the historical and literary evidence wherever it led.

The early church fathers confirm how soon these documents were circulating. The Apostolic Fathers — Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp, writing between 95 and 120 AD — quote from and allude to New Testament documents extensively. Ignatius of Antioch, writing letters to seven churches while traveling under armed guard to his execution in Rome around 108 AD, cites Paul's letters at length and shows clear knowledge of the Gospel of John. This is not a man with access to some obscure private library; these were documents in wide circulation, already recognized as authoritative. The quotations are themselves a form of manuscript evidence — if we lost every New Testament manuscript, we could reconstruct most of the New Testament from the church fathers alone. We have no need to do so, because we have the manuscripts; but it illustrates the breadth of the early community's engagement with these texts.

Early Dating and the Limits of Legend

The significance of early dating is not merely chronological. It is epistemological. What early dating means is that the New Testament documents were produced while eyewitnesses to the events they describe were still alive — and that is not a circumstance in which legendary embellishment flourishes. When you write about a person within twenty to thirty years of his death, while hundreds who knew him personally are still alive and able to confirm or contradict you, you are not in a position to invent freely. Paul's reference to more than five hundred witnesses who saw the risen Jesus, "most of whom are still alive," is not the language of someone making things up. It is the language of someone confident in the reality of the event, because the witnesses could be consulted.

What the early dating and the manuscript evidence together establish is this: the New Testament documents were produced by people in contact with the events they describe, within living memory of those events, and circulated widely in a community that had direct means of checking their accuracy. They were then transmitted with extraordinary care and in extraordinary quantity, giving us a body of manuscript evidence that allows us to reconstruct the original text with high confidence. This is not the portrait of a corrupted, legendarily inflated, politically constructed text. It is the portrait of a carefully preserved record of remarkable events.
