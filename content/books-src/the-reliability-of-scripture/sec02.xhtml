Chapter One

Three Questions We Confuse for One

"Can I trust the Bible?" is really three different questions — and answering the wrong one is why the conversation stalls.

The worst thing that can happen to a hard question is being answered with a different question. When someone asks whether the Bible is reliable, they rarely get the question they actually asked answered; they get a sermon about trusting God, or a history lecture about textual criticism that never connects to anything they actually believe, or a philosophical argument about the nature of truth — or, worst of all, a dismissal dressed up as piety: "I just take it by faith," as if faith were a substitute for thought rather than an activity that requires it.

The worst thing that can happen to a hard question is being answered with a different question.

None of these are entirely bad responses, exactly. But none of them answer the question being asked, and the reason they miss is that the person responding is answering a different question than the one being asked. There are at least three separate questions that get collapsed into the single phrase "can I trust the Bible" — and until they are pulled apart, the conversation cannot move.

The Textual Question: Is the Bible We Have the Bible First Written?

The first question is historical and textual: is the Bible we have now the same as the Bible originally written? This is the question of textual transmission and manuscript reliability — a question about copying, about whether the scribes who copied the text over twenty centuries preserved what was written or altered it, accidentally or deliberately. At bottom it asks whether the text on the page bears a credible relationship to the text originally produced.

The Historical Question: Did These People and Events Exist?

The second question is archaeological and factual: does the Bible accurately describe real people, real places, real events? This is the question of whether Abraham existed, whether the Exodus happened, whether David was a real king with a real kingdom, whether the census described in Luke's gospel can be corroborated by other historical sources. It asks, in short, whether the Bible's historical claims hold up under examination.

The Theological Question: Is This More Than a Human Book?

The third question is theological: is the Bible more than a collection of human documents? Is it inspired? Is it authoritative in a way that differs from the authority of other excellent books — the word of God in a sense that requires a specific kind of response from those who read it?

These three questions are related, but they are not the same, and answering one does not automatically answer the others. You can establish extraordinary textual reliability — demonstrating that the text we have matches the text originally written — without yet settling the inspiration question; you can confirm archaeological accuracy, showing that the people and places described are historically real, without resolving whether the miraculous claims are true. The questions build on each other, and a responsible case for biblical authority needs to engage all three, but they require different kinds of evidence and different kinds of arguments.

The confusion matters for a practical reason: critics tend to attack question three while arguing as if they are answering question one. They say, in effect, "the Bible is just a human document produced by people with biases and agendas" — which is a theological claim — and present it as if it were a conclusion drawn from textual criticism or archaeology. And defenders of Scripture often respond to attacks on questions one and two by retreating to question three: "I believe the Bible because the Holy Spirit witnesses to its truth in my heart," which may well be true but which does not answer the historical questions. The result is a conversation in which nobody is actually answering anybody else's argument. The skeptic scores points with rhetorical flourish; the believer retreats to an experience that cannot be shared; both go home convinced of what they already thought.

This book is primarily about questions one and two. Not because question three is less important — it is the most important of the three. But you cannot build a doctrine of inspiration on a foundation that has not yet established textual reliability; you cannot claim divine authority for a document you cannot demonstrate to be genuine. The order matters. The questions follow a logical sequence: first, is what we are reading the same as what was originally written; second, are the historical claims it makes substantially accurate; third, given the answers to one and two, what do we make of the theological claims? That third question is a different kind of question, because it involves not just the evaluation of evidence but a response to evidence. It is where faith lives. But faith that skips questions one and two is not stronger for having skipped them. It is more fragile.

So before any discussion of inspiration or authority, we need to settle the prior questions. Does the document we call the Bible bear a credible relationship to the documents originally produced? Has transmission corrupted, amended, or invented content the original authors did not write? Are the words on the page ancient, or are they medieval additions disguised as ancient testimony? This is exactly the right place to start, because if the answer to the textual question were "no, the transmission is unreliable, and the Bible we have is substantially different from the Bible originally written," then no amount of theological argument would save the enterprise. Faith built on a corrupted text would be faith in something nobody actually said.

What "Reliable" Actually Means

The answer is not "no." The answer is the opposite of "no." And the evidence for that answer is one of the most compelling bodies of scholarship in the history of literary study. But before we get there, we need to linger on one word: "reliable."

Reliable does not mean perfect. It does not mean that every scribal copyist never made a mistake — we know they made mistakes, and we can see them. Textual criticism, the scholarly discipline of comparing manuscripts and identifying variations, exists precisely because there are variations; the project of textual scholarship is to trace those variations back to their sources and identify the original readings. Reliability means something more precise and more important than "mistake-free." It means that the process of transmission has preserved the original content with sufficient accuracy that we can be confident we are reading what was originally written — that variations exist at the margins, not the center, that copyists preserved rather than invented, that the text has a coherent identity across centuries and languages and cultures.

Why More Copies Means Greater Accuracy, Not Less

Think of it this way. If I ask ten different people to copy a letter, give them each a copy to work from, and then compare the ten copies, I will find differences — spelling errors, transposed words, a phrase dropped here, a word repeated there. These differences do not mean I cannot recover what the original letter said. In fact, having ten copies gives me more ability to reconstruct the original than having one copy would, because each error is unique to the person who made it, and comparing the ten copies lets me identify the errors and correct for them.

That is, in simplified form, the logic of textual criticism applied to the Bible: more manuscripts means more ability to identify and correct errors, not less reliability. The goal is not to find a perfect chain of transmission — no ancient document has that. The goal is to assess whether, after critical analysis of all available manuscripts, we can reconstruct the original text with high confidence. By that standard, the Bible is not just reliable. It is the most reliably transmitted document from the ancient world — not by a small margin, but by a margin that makes every other comparison look thin.

That is the argument this book will make, and we will make it with evidence. Not with assertion, not with appeals to authority, but with the data that scholars across the ideological spectrum — believing and skeptical, Christian and secular — have produced by doing the actual work of manuscript study and archaeology and historical reconstruction. You do not have to take my word for it. You just have to follow the argument.
