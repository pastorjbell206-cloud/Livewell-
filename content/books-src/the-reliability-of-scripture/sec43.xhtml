Appendix A

The Major Manuscript Families — A Primer for Careful Readers

A working primer on the Alexandrian, Byzantine, and Western text-types and what their differences mean.

We know not just what the text says but how it got to us.

What Text-Types Are

This appendix is for readers who want to go deeper on the manuscript question, who would rather know the terminology than be intimidated by it when they meet it in serious study of textual criticism. New Testament manuscripts are not catalogued at random; they are grouped into text-types (also called manuscript families) based on the patterns of similarity they display. Scholars have identified three primary families: the Alexandrian, the Byzantine, and the Western.

The Alexandrian Text-Type

The Alexandrian text-type is generally considered the most reliable for reconstructing the original New Testament text, and the reasons are not arbitrary; its manuscripts tend to be earlier, and they are characterized by a relatively short, unadorned text that does not appear to have been harmonized with parallel accounts or expanded for liturgical purposes. The two great fourth-century manuscripts, Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, are its primary representatives. Most modern critical editions of the Greek New Testament (and the translations built on them, including the ESV, NIV, and NASB) follow the Alexandrian tradition in their primary text decisions.

The Byzantine Text-Type and the Textus Receptus

The Byzantine text-type is represented by the vast majority of existing New Testament manuscripts, approximately 80 to 85 percent of the total, a figure that reflects not superior antiquity but the dominance of the Byzantine church in medieval manuscript production. These manuscripts are generally later than the Alexandrian ones, and scholars have observed in them a tendency toward expansion, harmonization, and clarification: where two accounts differ, the Byzantine tradition often preserves a harmonized form that incorporates elements of both, and where a passage is difficult, it sometimes preserves a simpler reading that may reflect a scribe's effort to clarify.

This matters for the Bible many readers grew up with. The King James Bible of 1611 was translated from manuscripts in the Byzantine tradition, the body of text known as the Textus Receptus, or Received Text. That lineage explains why the KJV includes certain passages absent from newer translations based on Alexandrian manuscripts: the longer ending of Mark (16:9-20) and the story of the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53-8:11) among them, both present in Byzantine manuscripts but absent in the earliest Alexandrian ones.

The Western Text-Type

The Western text-type is found primarily in manuscripts from the Latin-speaking West, which is how it earned its name. It is characterized by paraphrase, by an expansion of narratives (most strikingly in Acts, where the Western text runs about 10 percent longer than the Alexandrian), and by occasional conflation of readings drawn from other traditions. Codex Bezae is its most important representative.

Why Distinct Families Argue for Reliability

The existence of distinct text-types raises an interesting question: are the differences between them evidence of corruption, or evidence of the reliability of the overall transmission? The answer is the latter. The fact that scholars can identify distinct text-type families, and trace the history of each family's development, means the text has been preserved so carefully that we can reconstruct not just the original but the history of the tradition's transmission. This is extraordinary. We know not just what the text says but how it got to us.

For practical purposes, the variations between text-types are documented in the footnotes of any responsible modern translation. When your Bible's footnote says "some manuscripts do not include this verse" or "other ancient authorities read...," that is the apparatus of textual criticism making itself available to the ordinary reader, and the transparency is itself evidence of the tradition's integrity. The scholarly community has not hidden the variants. It has published them.
