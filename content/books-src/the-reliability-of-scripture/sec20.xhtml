Chapter Nineteen

The Bible in the Life of the Church — Two Thousand Years of Evidence

The text's two-thousand-year track record of forming communities is a kind of evidence in its own right.

Evidence of a Different Kind: The Text's Effects

There is a kind of evidence for the reliability of Scripture that is different from the manuscript evidence, the archaeology, and the early dating. It is the evidence of the text's effects.

I am not suggesting that pragmatic evidence (this works, therefore it is true) is sufficient. It is not. False things can produce apparent effects, and the history of human religion is full of powerful texts that transformed communities in ways their adherents counted as evidence of divine origin, and which did not thereby become true.

The text did what the text claimed it would do.

But the evidence of Scripture's effects on human communities is not merely anecdotal; it is a two-thousand-year record that is available for examination, and what that record shows is significant.

Why Rome Executed Christians and Tolerated Everyone Else

The New Testament community, from its beginning, was characterized by something that observers, friendly and hostile alike, found difficult to explain, and the willingness to die rather than recant is the most dramatic expression of it. Rome executed Christians not because Rome was uniformly hostile to new religions; Rome was remarkably tolerant of religious diversity. Rome executed Christians because they refused to do what citizens of every other religion in the empire were willing to do: burn a pinch of incense before the image of the emperor and acknowledge him as divine. Every other religion in the empire found a way to accommodate this minor civic requirement. The Christians did not, and they were tortured and executed by the thousands for this refusal.

This is not the behavior of people who knew, in their hearts, that their texts were fabricated. People die for things they believe are true. They do not voluntarily submit to torture and death for things they know are false.

An Expansion Without State Power

The expansion of Christianity across the Roman world was itself a remarkable phenomenon. Within three hundred years of the crucifixion of a Jewish carpenter in a minor province of the empire, Christianity had spread through every stratum of Roman society, from slaves to senators, and had produced a literary output (the New Testament, the Apostolic Fathers, the Apologists, the great theological works of the third century) that shaped the intellectual history of the next two thousand years.

This expansion happened without state support for the first three centuries; it happened under conditions of periodic violent persecution; and it happened in a culture that had no particular reason to welcome a religion centered on a crucified Jewish messiah. Crucifixion was the Roman execution method reserved for the lowest classes (to Roman sensibility, the most shameful possible death), and so the proclamation that God had become a human being and had been executed as a criminal was not an appealing message in the Roman world. Paul acknowledged it himself.

We preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles

1 Corinthians 1:23

And yet it spread, through the Mediterranean world, through North Africa, through Persia, through the European continent, through Asia Minor, through Ethiopia and India and Armenia. Not through coercion; the church did not have state power until Constantine in the fourth century. It spread through proclamation and persuasion and, frequently, through the witness of people who were willing to die rather than deny what they had received.

What sustained this expansion was not a charismatic personality, for Jesus was not present after the ascension to charm converts; nor was it a simple message easily assimilated into existing frameworks, for the resurrection claim required a cognitive revolution. What sustained it was a text: copies of Paul's letters, moving through trade networks; copies of the Gospels, carried by missionaries and merchants; the Old Testament in Greek, already in circulation throughout the Mediterranean Jewish diaspora, reread through the lens of the resurrection.

The text did what the text claimed it would do: it functioned as the word of God, producing faith where it was proclaimed, forming communities that modeled a different kind of human existence, sustaining people through suffering with a hope that ran deeper than circumstance. This two-thousand-year track record is not proof of divine inspiration in a strict logical sense, but it is evidence: evidence that the text is the kind of thing it claims to be, not merely a literary achievement, not merely a historical document, but a living word that does something when it is proclaimed and received.

The Worst and the Best the Church Has Produced

The communities that have built their life on this text have produced, across their history, both the best and the worst of what humans are capable of. The worst is real and must be owned: the Crusades, the Inquisition, the Christian justification of slavery, the violence done in the name of a crucified God. This book does not minimize any of it. It is part of the record, and the honest believer must reckon with it.

But the best is also real. The abolition of slavery in the British Empire, driven by men who believed in the image of God in every human person. The hospital, which was a Christian invention: the systematic care of the sick and dying as a religious obligation. The university, which grew out of the cathedral school and the monastery. The music of Johann Sebastian Bach, who signed every manuscript Soli Deo Gloria, to God alone the glory. The poetry of Gerard Manley Hopkins. The service of William Wilberforce. The courage of Dietrich Bonhoeffer. The prophetic witness of Martin Luther King Jr. The care of lepers by Francis of Assisi. The homes for the dying built by Mother Teresa.

These are the products of a text that has been trusted. They are not the inevitable result of trusting the text (the history shows that the text can be misused as easily as used), but they are evidence that when the text is received as it asks to be received, as the living word of the God who is love, requiring a response of love toward God and neighbor, it produces something that the world, at its most honest, recognizes as genuinely good.
