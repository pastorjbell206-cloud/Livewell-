Chapter Six

What the Archaeologist's Spade Found

From Hezekiah's Tunnel to the House of David inscription, the test of the spade tends to confirm the text.

When the Bible makes claims that can be tested archaeologically, the test tends to confirm the claim.

The Siloam Tunnel Inscription and Hezekiah's Tunnel

There is a room in the Istanbul Archaeology Museum that most visitors walk past without stopping. It holds a basalt stone with an inscription cut into it roughly three thousand years ago, and the inscription records a moment: two groups of workers, digging a tunnel through solid rock from opposite ends, broke through the final barrier and met each other underground, having heard one another's voices through the rock before the breakthrough came. It is carved with evident pride. This was an engineering achievement.

The inscription is the Siloam Tunnel Inscription, and the tunnel it records is Hezekiah's Tunnel, a channel carved through the bedrock beneath the City of David in Jerusalem to protect the city's water supply against the approaching Assyrian army. The Second Book of Kings records it.

Hezekiah... made the pool and the tunnel and brought water into the city

2 Kings 20:20

The Second Book of Chronicles gives more detail, noting that Hezekiah "blocked the upper outlet of the Gihon spring and channeled the water down to the west side of the City of David" (2 Chronicles 32:30). The tunnel exists. The inscription exists. The biblical account describes it precisely. This is the pattern that biblical archaeology has established across more than a century of systematic excavation in the ancient Near East: when the Bible makes claims that can be tested archaeologically, the test tends to confirm the claim. What follows walks through the major cases, because the pattern is more compelling than any single example.

The Patriarchs and the Nuzi Tablets

For most of the nineteenth and early twentieth century, the patriarchal narratives, the stories of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and their families, were considered legendary. Critics argued that the social world described in Genesis bore no resemblance to any verifiable ancient culture; the customs seemed invented, whether the practice of giving a servant woman to a husband to produce an heir, the formal transfer of birthright, or the particular legal frameworks for inheritance. These looked like later writers' imaginative constructions of an ancient past.

Then archaeologists excavated the ancient Mesopotamian city of Nuzi and found thousands of clay tablets dating to the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BC. The tablets recorded legal and social customs that matched, in specific and sometimes striking detail, the customs described in the Genesis patriarchal narratives. The practice of a childless wife offering her servant woman to her husband, which appears in Genesis 16 with Sarai and Hagar, was not the invention of a later author; it was a legally formalized institution documented in Nuzi contracts. The procedure for formally transferring a birthright, which appears in the story of Jacob and Esau, had parallels in Nuzi legal practice. The customs of the patriarchal narratives fit the second millennium BC, not the first.

The discovery did not prove that Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob were historical individuals. But it proved that the social world described in their stories belonged to the period those stories claim, a period that ended more than a thousand years before the critical scholars assumed the texts were composed. Someone had preserved memory of a world that no later fabricator would have known.

The Tel Dan Stele and the Historical King David

The historical existence of King David was, for most of the twentieth century, a favorite target of the minimalist school, the wing of biblical archaeology that argues the Old Testament is largely legend with little historical basis. David appears prominently in the Old Testament but, critics pointed out, appears nowhere in extra-biblical sources; without corroborating evidence, they argued, David might be as legendary as King Arthur.

In 1993, archaeologists working at Tel Dan in northern Israel found fragments of a basalt victory stela, an ancient monument erected by an Aramean king to celebrate his victories over neighboring kingdoms. The relevant phrase, in ancient Aramaic, reads "the House of David" (bytdwd), referring to the kingdom of Judah, named for its founding dynasty. The stela dates to the ninth century BC, approximately 150 years after David's reign by the standard biblical chronology.

An enemy king's ninth-century BC victory monument named David's dynasty as the ruling house of Judah, and the text is not ambiguous; the reading bytdwd, while initially contested by a few scholars, is now essentially universal among specialists. David was not a legend. He was a real king whose dynasty was known and named by his neighbors within living memory of his descendants.

Assyrian Monuments That Match the Book of Kings

The Mesha Stele, also known as the Moabite Stone and dating to approximately 840 BC, was discovered in 1868 and provides another external confirmation of biblical history. The Moabite king Mesha commemorates his victories over Israel and refers explicitly to the Israelite king Omri and his son; the biblical account of this same conflict appears in 2 Kings 3, and the details align. The stele also contains a reference that most scholars read as a mention of the "House of David," different from the Tel Dan inscription but consistent with it.

The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III, now in the British Museum, depicts figures from several conquered nations paying tribute to the Assyrian emperor. One panel shows an Israelite king, named in the inscription as Jehu, son of Omri, bowing prostrate before Shalmaneser; this is the only contemporary image we have of any Israelite king. The biblical book of 2 Kings 9-10 describes Jehu's reign in detail. The Assyrian monument confirms it.

The Sennacherib Prism, three hexagonal clay prisms now divided between museums in Chicago, Jerusalem, and London, records the Assyrian king Sennacherib's account of his campaign against the kingdoms of Judah and Israel in 701 BC. The account describes his siege of forty-six Judean cities and his siege of Jerusalem itself; Sennacherib says he shut up the Judean king "like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem, but he does not say he conquered the city. The biblical account in 2 Kings 18-19, Isaiah 36-37, and 2 Chronicles 32 describes exactly this situation: Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem, his humiliation of King Hezekiah, and his eventual withdrawal without capturing the city. The Assyrian account and the biblical account agree on the key details, including the detail that Jerusalem was besieged but not taken.

William Ramsay and the Accuracy of Luke

The New Testament's historical accuracy has been confirmed most dramatically through the work of Sir William Ramsay, a nineteenth-century classical scholar who began his career as a skeptic about the historical reliability of Luke-Acts. Ramsay spent years excavating in Asia Minor, checking Luke's specific historical and administrative claims against the inscriptions and coins he found in the ground.

His conclusion reversed his original position. Ramsay published his findings in a series of books in the 1890s and announced that Luke was a historian of the first rank, whose accuracy in geographical, administrative, and political detail was what one would expect only from a contemporary with direct access to the events described.

Consider the specifics. Luke refers to the rulers of Thessalonica as "politarchs" (Acts 17:6), a precise administrative title that appeared in no other ancient literary source known to scholars, who initially cited the word as evidence of Luke's inaccuracy, until archaeological excavations produced inscriptions from Thessalonica using exactly this title. Luke was right. He calls the leader of Malta "the chief man of the island" (Acts 28:7), using a Greek phrase that was considered suspicious until inscriptions confirmed it as the official title for Malta's Roman administrator. And he refers to Gallio as "proconsul of Achaia" (Acts 18:12), an appointment confirmed by a Delphic inscription dating to 52 AD, which also allows us to date Paul's Corinthian ministry with unusual precision.

The Pool of Bethesda described in John 5 (where Jesus heals a paralyzed man at a pool with five colonnades) was once cited as evidence of John's theological invention over historical accuracy, since a pool with five colonnades was architecturally puzzling. Then excavations under the Church of St. Anne in Jerusalem revealed a first-century pool complex with four colonnades around the perimeter and a fifth colonnade dividing the two pools. Five colonnades exactly. The detail critics used against John was confirmed by archaeology. These examples could be multiplied at length, and the pattern is the same: when specific historical and geographical claims made by biblical writers are tested against the archaeological and epigraphic evidence, the test tends to confirm the claims.

The Genuine Puzzles: Exodus and Conquest

There are genuine historical puzzles, and honesty requires naming them. The Exodus narrative and the Egyptian evidence remain one of the more contested areas: the question of which pharaoh, what route, and what archaeological signature the Exodus should have left in the record has generated more scholarly heat than any single topic in biblical archaeology. The archaeological record of the conquest of Canaan described in Joshua involves real complexity that careful scholarship must acknowledge rather than paper over.

But the overall trajectory of a century and a half of biblical archaeology has not run from confirmation toward embarrassment. It has run in the opposite direction. Critics who predict that the next round of excavation will discredit the Bible have been disappointed more often than not, and the text has proven more reliable than its critics predicted.
