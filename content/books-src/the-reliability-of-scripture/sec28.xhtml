Chapter Twenty-Seven

The Parable of the Coins — An Illustration of Textual Criticism

A simple illustration of why twenty-five thousand imperfect copies give more certainty, not less.

More manuscripts means more comparison points; more comparison points means more ability to identify and correct copyist errors.

A Speech Reconstructed From Twenty-Seven Copies

Abstract discussions of manuscript variants can feel disconnected from the actual reading experience of a person who opens the Bible each morning, so I want to try an illustration that might make the work of textual criticism more concrete. Imagine that you are a historian, handed the task of establishing the exact words of a particular speech delivered in a town meeting two hundred years ago. No recording exists. The newspaper account was brief. But you have found that notes were taken at the meeting, and that those notes were copied multiple times and distributed to different people in different towns.

You start gathering copies, and you find that you have twenty-seven of them, produced over the following decade by different copyists in different locations. When you compare them, they agree with each other almost entirely, but there are small differences. One copyist spelled a particular name differently; another apparently misread a word and wrote something that does not quite make sense; two of the copies agree with each other against all the others on a particular phrase, which suggests they were copied from a common source; one copy has a note in the margin that appears to have been copied into the main text in another version. None of the differences affect the substance of the speech. The main arguments are clear across all twenty-seven copies, and the key decisions made at the meeting are unambiguous. Still, you want to establish the exact wording as precisely as possible.

What you do, which is exactly what textual critics do, is compare all twenty-seven copies systematically. Where they agree, you have high confidence in the original wording; where they differ, you analyze the pattern of differences to determine which reading is more likely to be original and which is more likely to be a copyist's error. You note the places where you remain uncertain and indicate those in your critical edition. And at the end of the process, you have a reconstruction of the original speech that is, within small margins of uncertainty, as accurate as the available evidence permits.

The Self-Correcting Power of Many Manuscripts

Now multiply the twenty-seven copies to twenty-five thousand. That is the New Testament situation, and the sheer quantity of manuscripts gives the textual critic a self-correcting system of enormous power. Scribal errors are, almost by definition, unique to the scribe who made them; when you have twenty-five thousand copies, a unique error stands out against the overwhelming agreement of the other twenty-four thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine. And when two groups of manuscripts agree against each other, you can often trace the divergence to a common ancestor and evaluate which reading is more likely original.

This is not speculative. It is the actual work that scholars like Bruce Metzger, Kurt Aland, and their colleagues have done, and that scholars like Daniel Wallace, Tommy Wasserman, Tommy Hatina, and hundreds of others continue to do. The critical Greek New Testament text published by the United Bible Societies and by Nestle-Aland represents the cumulative work of this scholarship: the best available reconstruction of what the original New Testament documents said, based on analysis of the full manuscript tradition.

From Twenty-Seven to Twenty-Five Thousand

The coins illustration is useful here. When historians want to establish the exact image and legend on a Roman coin minted in a particular year, they do not need a perfect exemplar, a coin preserved with every detail sharp and clear. They compare multiple examples from different mints and different conditions, and through that comparison they can reconstruct the original design even if no single coin preserves it perfectly. More coins means more confidence in the reconstruction, even when each individual coin carries some wear or imperfection.

The Coins Illustration and the Worn Originals

The New Testament manuscripts are like an enormous collection of coins from a single minting. No single manuscript is perfect; every copyist made occasional errors. But the twenty-five thousand copies together preserve the original with a level of confidence that increases with every additional manuscript examined. The variations are the wear and imperfections of individual coins. The overwhelming agreement is the minting: the original from which all copies were made.

Why More Copies Means More Certainty

This illustration also clarifies why more manuscripts means more certainty rather than more uncertainty. The person who encounters the large number of New Testament textual variants and concludes that nobody knows what the original said has misunderstood how textual criticism works. More manuscripts means more comparison points; more comparison points means more ability to identify and correct copyist errors. The result is greater confidence in the original, not less.
