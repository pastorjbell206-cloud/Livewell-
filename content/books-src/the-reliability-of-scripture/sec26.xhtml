Chapter Twenty-Five

A Final Word — To the Skeptic Who Made It This Far

A direct invitation to the skeptic: read Mark and ask whether this reads like legend or like testimony.

Mark reads like testimony. The details are too specific, too unglamorous, too unflattering to be the product of later legend.

A Word to the One Still Examining

If you have read this far without believing, I want to speak to you directly. You did not have to keep reading; the fact that you did suggests that you are a person who takes the question seriously, that the question is live for you, that you have not made the easy decision to dismiss this without examination and have not made the other easy decision to receive it without examination. You are doing the harder thing. You are examining it.

I respect that, because I was that person. At eighteen, I was convinced I was right and that the Christian case was intellectually thin; what I had not done was actually engage the best version of the case, and when I did, it changed my mind.

I am not trying to close your mind or manipulate you into a conclusion. I am trying to show you that the questions are answerable, that the evidence is substantial, and that the case for trusting the Bible is stronger than the culture has told you it is. You should evaluate that case on its merits, not on my authority. Follow the evidence. Read the scholars. Examine the manuscripts. Study the archaeology. Engage the strongest versions of the objections and the strongest versions of the responses.

An Experiment: Read the Gospel of Mark

If you are genuinely uncertain, I want to make a specific invitation: try an experiment. Read the Gospel of Mark (it is the shortest of the four Gospels, perhaps two hours of reading) with a single question in mind: Does this read like legendary embellishment, or does this read like someone reporting what they saw? Notice the specific details: the location of the events, the names of the people, the frank portrayal of the disciples' failures, the way Jesus's emotions are described.

Ask yourself whether a legendary, theologically motivated composition would include the detail that when Jesus was at his most exhausted, his family came to take him away because they thought he was out of his mind (Mark 3:21). Ask whether a document designed to promote the authority of the disciples would include the story of Peter denying Jesus three times and weeping (Mark 14:66-72).

Why Mark Reads Like Testimony

Mark reads like testimony. It has the texture of someone who was there, or who received the account from someone who was there; the details are too specific, too unglamorous, too unflattering to the people at the center of the story to be the product of later legend.

If you read it and remain unconvinced, I am not going to accuse you of bad faith. The evidence invites but does not compel. You are not logically required to cross the gap between evidence and trust. You are invited. But I want to name what is on the other side of the gap, because it deserves to be named directly.

What the Invitation Costs and What It Offers

On the other side is a world that has its Maker present within it, a world in which you are not an accident but a creature made in the image of God, with dignity that nothing can take and purpose that nothing can erase, a world in which the worst things that have been done, by others to you and by you to others, have been addressed, at cost, by the one with the authority to address them. It is a world in which the future belongs to the God who raised Jesus from the dead, and in which every act of love and justice and faithfulness you perform is not swallowed by entropy but is somehow included in the final account.

That world is available. The door to it is the acknowledgment that Jesus is who he claimed to be: Lord of the universe, risen from the dead, the word through whom all things were made and the bread by which they are sustained.

The text that testifies to all of this is reliable. The case has been made. The evidence is in. The only question left is the one that has always been the question: What will you do with it?
