Appendix B

Key Archaeological Sites and What They Proved

A compact reference to the finds that corroborate the biblical record, from the Tel Dan Stele to the Pilate Inscription.

What follows is a reference summary of the most significant archaeological finds relating to biblical reliability, gathered for the reader who wants a compact place to return to. It is not an argument so much as a ledger; the case has already been made, and this is where the stones are listed by name, by date, and by the thing each one settled.

A detail once cited against the text turned out, again and again, to be precise historical confirmation.

Old Testament Monuments: Tel Dan, Mesha, Sennacherib, Cyrus

The Tel Dan Stele, a ninth-century BC basalt monument erected by an Aramean king in northern Israel, carries the phrase "House of David," the first extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty, and one that confirms the historical existence of David's royal line within roughly a hundred and fifty years of his reign. The Mesha Stele, also called the Moabite Stone and dated to 840 BC, was raised by King Mesha of Moab to describe his wars against Israel; it confirms the biblical account of the conflict in 2 Kings 3, names the Israelite king Omri, and possibly contains its own reference to the House of David.

Two later monuments push the same pattern into the heart of the prophetic histories. The Sennacherib Prism, a clay prism from around 700 BC, records the Assyrian king's campaigns, including his siege of Jerusalem in the days of Hezekiah; it matches the account in 2 Kings 18-19 and Isaiah 36-37, down to the striking detail that Jerusalem was besieged but never captured. The Cyrus Cylinder of 539 BC records the decree of Cyrus the Great permitting deported peoples to return to their homelands, the very policy that, in Ezra 1:1-4 and Isaiah 44:28-45:1, returned the Jews from Babylonian exile.

Hezekiah's Tunnel and the Siloam Inscription

Cut into the rock at the exit of Hezekiah's Tunnel in Jerusalem, the Siloam Tunnel Inscription dates to the eighth century BC and records the completion of the tunnel described in 2 Kings 20:20 and 2 Chronicles 32:30. The tunnel itself stands among the most impressive surviving engineering works from the biblical period, not a fragment of pottery but a passage you can still walk, hewn through stone, ending in an inscription that says it was finished.

The Dead Sea Scrolls and the Nuzi Tablets

The Dead Sea Scrolls (nearly nine hundred documents recovered from caves near Qumran between 1947 and 1956, ranging from 250 BC to 68 AD) include fragments of every Old Testament book except Esther. The Great Isaiah Scroll, dated to about 125 BC, is essentially identical to the medieval Masoretic text, which demonstrates the remarkable stability of the Old Testament manuscript tradition across more than a thousand years of copying.

Older still are the Nuzi Tablets, thousands of clay tablets from the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BC recovered from the Mesopotamian city of Nuzi. They record legal and social customs that match the world of the Genesis patriarchs, and in doing so they anchor the authenticity of the patriarchal narratives to the second millennium BC, the period the text itself claims.

New Testament Confirmations: Pilate, Caiaphas, Gallio

The Pilate Inscription, a first-century stone discovered at Caesarea Maritima in 1961, bears the name "Pontius Pilatus" and identifies him as "Prefect of Judaea," direct archaeological confirmation of Pilate's existence and title, matching the New Testament's references. The Ossuary of Caiaphas, an ornate first-century bone box unearthed in Jerusalem in 1990, carries the inscription "Joseph son of Caiaphas" and is widely accepted as the ossuary of the high priest who presided at Jesus's trial in Matthew 26.

Then there is the Gallio Inscription, found at Delphi and dated to 52 AD, which records an imperial rescript to Gallio as "proconsul of Achaia." It confirms Luke's reference in Acts 18:12 and lets scholars date Paul's Corinthian ministry with unusual precision to roughly 50-52 AD, a fixed point in a chronology that is otherwise hard to pin down.

The Pool of Bethesda and the Erastus Inscription

Excavation beneath the Church of St. Anne in Jerusalem uncovered a first-century pool complex with five colonnades, matching exactly the description in John 5:2; a detail once cited against John's accuracy turned out to be precise historical confirmation. The Erastus Inscription, a first-century stone pavement in Corinth, records that "Erastus, in return for his aedileship, laid the pavement at his own expense," and Paul names Erastus as a Corinthian associate in Romans 16:23, so the inscription offers a potential confirmation of that individual. Not proof of everything. Confirmation of the thing at hand, one stone at a time.
