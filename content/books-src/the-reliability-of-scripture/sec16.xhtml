Chapter Fifteen

Prophecy, Fulfillment, and the Coherence of Two Testaments

A pattern of expectation built across centuries converges on one life in ways that demand an explanation.

The most startling feature of the Bible is one that first-time readers almost never notice, because they have been told not to trust it. The feature is this: the New Testament makes sense only as the fulfillment of the Old, not in the vague sense that one religious text draws on another, but in the specific, numerically substantial sense that the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth corresponds to a pattern of expectation embedded in the Hebrew Scriptures across hundreds of years, produced by dozens of authors in different literary genres, representing traditions that were not in communication with each other and that in some cases postdate by centuries the events they are said to predict.

Something remains that is harder to explain away than critics usually acknowledge.

The messianic prophecy argument for biblical reliability is not new, and it has been attacked vigorously; the attacks deserve honest engagement. Some of what Christian apologists have presented as prophecy is not specific enough to count as genuine prediction; it is pattern and type rather than precise foretelling. Some of the correspondences between the Old and New Testaments are the result of the New Testament writers consciously shaping their accounts in the light of the Old, which raises the question of whether the correspondences are historical or literary. And the question of whether the specific predictions in Isaiah, Micah, Zechariah, and elsewhere are genuine predictions or post-facto compositions is a live one in the scholarly literature. But when these caveats are taken seriously and the argument is made carefully, something remains that is harder to explain away than critics usually acknowledge.

The Narrowing Trajectory of Messianic Expectation

Consider the trajectory of messianic expectation in the Old Testament. It begins vaguely. Genesis 3:15 speaks of a seed of the woman who will crush the serpent's head, a promise of decisive divine action through a human agent. From there it narrows: from all humanity in Genesis 3, to the line of Shem in Genesis 9, to the descendants of Abraham in Genesis 12, to the tribe of Judah in Genesis 49, to the royal line of David in 2 Samuel 7. Each specification narrows the field while expanding the scope of what the coming figure will do.

The Davidic Covenant as the Pivot

The Davidic covenant is the pivot. Second Samuel 7:12-16 contains God's promise to David:

I will raise up your offspring to succeed you, your own flesh and blood, and I will establish his kingdom. He is the one who will build a house for my Name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever.

2 Samuel 7:12-13

The ambiguity of this promise (does it refer to Solomon, or to a future figure greater than Solomon?) is played out through the rest of the Old Testament, and the prophets interpret it messianically. Psalm 2, Psalm 22, and Psalm 110 develop the picture of a royal figure who is simultaneously king and priest, who suffers and is vindicated, who rules not just over Israel but over the nations.

Isaiah 7:14, interpreted by Matthew as a prediction of the virgin birth, is contested; the Hebrew word almah can mean "young woman" without necessarily implying virginity, and the immediate context suggests a sign for Ahaz's own time. But Isaiah 9:6-7 is harder to explain away:

For to us a child is born, to us a son is given; and the government shall be upon his shoulder, and his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end, on the throne of David and over his kingdom.

Isaiah 9:6-7

This is not a description of any Davidic king who actually sat on the throne of Jerusalem. No historical king of Judah was called "Mighty God" or "Everlasting Father" with any credibility. The text is reaching for something beyond what the political history of Israel could provide.

Isaiah 53 and the Suffering Servant

Isaiah 53 is the passage that most troubles critics of a later dating for the text, because its correspondence to the New Testament's account of Jesus's death is not superficial. The passage describes a figure who suffers on behalf of others, who is rejected by his own people, whose death is a death for the sins of others, who is silent before his accusers, who is numbered with criminals, and who is buried with the wealthy:

he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed.

Isaiah 53:5

He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief.

Isaiah 53:3

the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all.

Isaiah 53:6

He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth.

Isaiah 53:7

he was numbered with the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors.

Isaiah 53:12

And they made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death.

Isaiah 53:9

The Dead Sea Scrolls contain this chapter in the Great Isaiah Scroll, dated to approximately 125 BC. This chapter was not composed after the life of Jesus; it was in existence, recognized as prophetic Scripture, more than a century before Jesus was born. And its correspondence to the New Testament's account of the crucifixion involves not one or two incidental parallels but a sustained description of a suffering servant whose death is vicarious, who is innocent, who is rejected, who is silent, who is executed between criminals, and who is buried in a rich man's tomb.

The critics' response is to argue that either the passage refers to the historical Israel, not a messianic individual, or that the correspondences are the result of the Gospel writers shaping their accounts to match the text. Both responses face difficulties. The "Israel" interpretation struggles with the singular forms throughout the passage and with the statement that the servant bears the sin of "the many," hard to read as Israel bearing the sin of Israel. And the New Testament writers were not the first to read the passage as referring to an individual messianic figure; second-temple Jewish sources, before the time of Jesus, interpreted the Servant Songs in multiple ways, including individually. The "Gospel writers shaped their accounts" argument, for its part, requires that events genuinely open to manipulation (the place of burial, the behavior under questioning, the method of execution) were fabricated to match the text. This is possible, but it is a significant claim that requires evidence; it explains the correspondences by multiplying historical inventions.

Bethlehem, the Donkey, and the Thirty Pieces of Silver

Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem as the birthplace of the coming ruler of Israel:

But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah, who are too little to be among the clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel, whose coming forth is from of old, from ancient days.

Micah 5:2

Matthew and Luke both record the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem, and Matthew explicitly cites this text. The counter-argument that Matthew invented the Bethlehem birthplace to match the prophecy does not explain Luke's independent account of the census that brought Joseph and Mary there: two independent witnesses to the Bethlehem birthplace, from different traditions, with different stated reasons for the birth occurring where it did.

Zechariah 9:9 describes a king coming to Jerusalem "humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey." All four Gospels record Jesus's triumphal entry into Jerusalem on a donkey, and Matthew and John both explicitly cite Zechariah. The triumphal entry was a public event witnessed by crowds; it would be difficult to fabricate in a community where the event was remembered by living people.

Zechariah 11:12-13 describes thirty pieces of silver paid for a shepherd who is rejected, and the money being thrown "to the potter." Matthew 26-27 records Judas's betrayal for thirty pieces of silver and the priests' use of the returned money to buy "the potter's field." The specificity of the parallel (the amount, the return, the field) is not easily dismissed as coincidence or as literary construction.

Weighing the Two Available Explanations

The prophecy argument for biblical reliability is not knockdown. It requires careful engagement with the difficult questions about genre, interpretation, and literary shaping. But when the argument is made carefully, the cumulative pattern is significant: the Old Testament describes, with increasing specificity over centuries, a figure whose characteristics match the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth in ways that require explanation. The two most available explanations are that the correspondence is the result of God's superintendence of the text, or that the Gospel writers shaped their accounts to create the appearance of fulfillment. The evidence (particularly the early dating of the Gospels, the multiple attestation of key events, and the specificity of details that would have been impossible to control) weighs, on balance, toward the former.
