Chapter Twenty-Eight

The Church Under Pressure — How Persecution Tested and Confirmed the Text

A text people will hide in their clothing, memorize, and die rather than surrender tells you what they believed it was.

What had been copied and carried across the empire could not be destroyed.

Diocletian and the Surrender of the Scriptures

There is a particular test of a text's importance that discussions of biblical reliability rarely consider: the test of persecution. When the Roman emperor Diocletian issued his edicts against Christians in 303 AD, among the first acts demanded of believers was the surrender of their sacred books. The Latin word traditio — "handing over" — was applied to the act of surrendering the Scriptures to Roman authorities, and Christians who complied became known as traditores, the origin of our word "traitor." Those who refused, who hid the books or died rather than give them up, demonstrated by their willingness to suffer that the texts were worth that cost.

This willingness tells us something about what these communities believed about the text. They believed it was what it claimed to be — not because they could not imagine it being false, but because the evidence of its truth had been so thoroughly embedded in their lives, their worship, their pastoral practice, and their corporate memory that surrendering the text was equivalent to surrendering the reality the text described.

Why No One Died for Their Copy of Plato

The contrast is illuminating. We do not have accounts of Christians dying to protect their copies of Plato, their Stoic manuals, or their copies of Tacitus, though all of these were valued literature. What produced the willingness to die was the conviction that the text being targeted was not a human composition but a divine communication — that to hand it over was a betrayal not merely of culture but of God.

The Unintended Proof of Wide Distribution

The persecution of Diocletian, which lasted approximately a decade, had an unintended consequence: it demonstrated how thoroughly the text had been distributed. Roman officials could destroy books in Rome, in Carthage, in Alexandria; they could not destroy what had been copied and carried across the empire. The very failure of the persecution to eliminate the text is evidence of how deeply it had penetrated the ancient world — how many copies existed, how widely they had been distributed, how effectively the network of Christian communities had propagated the textual tradition.

The Waldensians and Bibles Carried in Clothing

The Waldensians, a medieval reform movement in northern Italy and southern France, maintained their faith for centuries against enormous pressure from both church and secular authorities. They translated the New Testament into the vernacular Provençal language at a time when this was officially forbidden; they carried hand-copied Bibles hidden in the lining of their clothing; and they were periodically massacred for their insistence on Scripture's authority. The survival of the Waldensian movement, and the survival of their tradition's emphasis on biblical authority, is itself a kind of testimony to the text's durability under pressure.

The Chinese Church and Scripture Held in Memory

The Chinese church under the Cultural Revolution, when Bibles were confiscated and burned and Christians were imprisoned for possessing Scripture, produced the phenomenon of believers who had memorized entire books of the Bible because they knew the paper copies were vulnerable. Portions of the New Testament were preserved in human memory, recited in secret to communities that could not access a physical text. When restrictions eased and paper Bibles became available again, the texts recovered from memory were compared with the available manuscripts — and the accuracy of memorized transmission was striking.

This is a different kind of manuscript evidence. It is the evidence of communities for whom the text was valuable enough to be carried in their bodies when it could not be carried in their hands — and who preserved it accurately because accuracy mattered.

The text has been tested by fire, by confiscation, by persecution, by the determined effort of multiple empires across multiple centuries to eliminate it. It survived. Not magically, not miraculously in a way that bypasses human effort; it survived because communities of people believed it was worth the cost of preservation. And their belief proved correct.
