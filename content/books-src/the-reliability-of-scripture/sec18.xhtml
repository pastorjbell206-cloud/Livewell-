Chapter Seventeen

The Transmission of Meaning — Translation, Language, and Trust

Translation involves real judgment, but the core message survives the crossing into every language.

The gospel that Tyndale died to put in the hands of English plowboys is the same gospel that readers encounter in a twenty-first century English translation.

What Is Lost and Kept When Greek Becomes English

One question deserves direct treatment, and it is the one that does not usually get asked in books about biblical reliability: what happens when the Bible is translated? You cannot read Greek or Hebrew, and neither can most of the billion-plus people who read the Bible regularly. The text that most readers access is not the original; it is an English version, produced by a team of scholars, representing a particular set of textual, linguistic, and theological decisions. How much is lost in that process, and how much is altered? Is the translation you are reading a faithful vehicle for what the original said, or is it a culturally conditioned paraphrase at two removes from the source? These are legitimate questions. They deserve honest answers.

The translation of ancient languages into modern ones inevitably involves interpretation, because there is no word-for-word correspondence between Greek and English that preserves both the meaning and the structure of the original. Greek is inflected — it uses word endings rather than word order to signal grammatical relationships — which means a Greek sentence can arrange its words in an order that produces an emphasis a literal English rendering cannot capture. Some Greek words have no English equivalent that conveys their full range; the word agape, used for divine love in the New Testament, carries connotations the English word "love" does not, naming a committed, self-giving love that is not conditioned by the worthiness of its object. Honest translation acknowledges these limitations and works within them.

Why We Have Multiple Faithful Translations

The best modern translations — the English Standard Version, the New International Version, the New American Standard Bible, the Christian Standard Bible — are produced by teams of scholars who know the original languages, who work from the best available Greek and Hebrew texts, and who are accountable to one another and to the scholarly community for the decisions they make. They do not agree on every translation choice; that is precisely why we have multiple translations, and why comparing them is a useful practice.

But the variations between careful modern translations do not indicate that the meaning of the text has been corrupted in transmission. They indicate that translation involves genuine judgment calls — choices between word options, decisions about how to render a Greek idiom in English, decisions about whether to follow a formal equivalence approach that preserves the structure of the original as much as possible or a functional equivalence approach that conveys the meaning as naturally as possible in the target language. These are not disagreements about what the text says. They are disagreements about the best English way to say it.

From the Vulgate to Tyndale to the King James

The history of Bible translation is itself a testimony to the text's remarkable durability. Jerome's Latin Vulgate, produced in the fourth and fifth centuries, rendered the Greek and Hebrew into the lingua franca of the Western Roman world; and when the Western church lost access to Greek and Hebrew scholarship in the medieval period, that Latin translation sustained biblical Christianity for a thousand years. Wycliffe's English translation in the fourteenth century, Tyndale's in the sixteenth, the King James Bible in 1611 — each generation produced translations from the available manuscript evidence, using the linguistic tools of its time. What is remarkable is not the differences between these translations but the consistency of the core message across them. The gospel that Tyndale died to put in the hands of English plowboys is the same gospel that readers encounter in a twenty-first century English translation.

The Translatability of the Biblical Message

What this history demonstrates is that the core of the biblical message is not held hostage to its original languages. The theological DNA of the text — the character of God, the reality of human sin, the nature of redemption, the identity and work of Jesus Christ — survives translation into every language because it is embedded in the structure of the text, not in the specific vocabulary of any single linguistic rendering.

This is confirmed by the remarkable fact that the Bible has been translated into more languages than any other document in human history. Wycliffe Bible Translators and similar organizations have worked to produce translations in languages with no previous writing system — languages that required not just translation but the creation of an alphabet, the development of a written form, the establishment of a reading culture. And what the translators report, consistently, is that the text translates; its meaning survives the crossing of radical cultural and linguistic distance in ways that no other literary document does.

Why Translation Is Not the Same as Corruption

The text's translatability is not a proof of its divine origin. But it is consistent with the Christian claim that the Bible is not a culturally particular document that can only be understood within its original culture. It is addressed to every human being in every culture, because it addresses the condition every human being shares — the condition of being a created being in need of a Creator, a broken person in need of restoration.
