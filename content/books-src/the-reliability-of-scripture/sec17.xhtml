Chapter Sixteen

The Resurrection — The Historical Core of Everything

If the tomb was not empty, none of the rest matters — so the historical case deserves the closest look.

Every argument in every chapter is a prologue to a story that would be fiction if the tomb were not empty.

Paul's All-or-Nothing Claim

I want to say the quiet part out loud. If the resurrection did not happen, none of the rest of this book matters. Not the manuscript tradition, not the archaeological evidence, not the eyewitness testimony, not the fulfilled prophecy — all of it, every argument in every chapter, is a prologue to a story that would be fiction if the tomb were not empty. The reliability of Scripture, in its deepest sense, is the reliability of the central claim: that a man who was executed by Roman crucifixion on a specific Friday outside Jerusalem walked out of a sealed tomb on the following Sunday morning, appeared to multiple witnesses over multiple occasions, and was physically present with his disciples for forty days before ascending into heaven.

Paul understood this with a clarity that modern Christians sometimes fail to match. He wrote it plainly to the Corinthians.

if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain... if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins.

1 Corinthians 15:14, 17

Not: your faith needs reinterpreting. Not: the spiritual meaning remains even if the physical event did not occur. Futile. In vain. Worthless. Paul is not a mystic who can reframe the resurrection as an internal spiritual experience; he is a Jewish man who has staked his entire life on the physical, historical reality of a bodily resurrection. He knows the difference between a resurrected body and a vision, and the difference between genuine transformation and wishful thinking. He was willing to die — and did die — on the testimony that he had personally encountered the risen Jesus.

The historical case for the resurrection has been made more carefully and more thoroughly than perhaps any other question in the intersection of history and theology, and I am going to summarize the argument without pretending it is simpler than it is. The key evidence can be organized around four facts that are broadly accepted across the spectrum of New Testament scholarship, including by many scholars who are not Christians.

Fact One: Jesus Died by Crucifixion

The first fact is as historically certain as any ancient death: Jesus died by crucifixion. Roman crucifixion was effective — the Romans had refined it into a reliable execution method — and no credible historian, Christian or secular, doubts that Jesus of Nazareth was executed under Pontius Pilate, probably in 30 or 33 AD. This is attested not only by the Gospels but by the Roman historian Tacitus, the Jewish historian Josephus, the Roman satirist Lucian of Samosata, and others. The swoon theory — that Jesus merely fainted and recovered in the tomb — has not found a single serious advocate among historians in the modern era.

Fact Two: The Empty Tomb in Jerusalem

The second fact is more contested than the crucifixion, but the evidence is substantial: the tomb was found empty on the third day. The empty tomb is attested in all four Gospels from independent traditions, and it is consistent with the 1 Corinthians 15 creed, which implies an empty tomb in the sequence "died, buried, raised." Most significantly, the early Christian preaching about the resurrection occurred in Jerusalem, the very city where the burial had taken place, weeks after the death. If the tomb had not been empty, the Jewish authorities — who had every motivation to disprove the resurrection claim — could have produced the body. They did not. Their response to the resurrection preaching, as reported in Matthew 28:11-15, was to spread the story that the disciples had stolen the body, an explanation that implicitly acknowledges the tomb was empty.

Fact Three: The Multiple Appearances

The third fact is that there were multiple appearances of someone they identified as the risen Jesus. This is attested in the 1 Corinthians 15 creed: appearances to Peter, to the twelve, to more than five hundred at one time, to James, to all the apostles, and to Paul himself. Paul is writing approximately 25 years after the crucifixion, describing himself as the last to receive an appearance, and inviting his readers to consult the other witnesses — "most of whom are still alive." This is not the language of a person creating legend. It is the language of a person making a falsifiable claim while the witnesses are still available.

Fact Four: The Transformation of the Disciples

The fourth fact is perhaps the most underrated of the four: the disciples' belief in the resurrection transformed them. They did not merely claim to have seen the risen Jesus; they were transformed by the claim in ways that had concrete historical consequences. Peter, who had denied Jesus three times in the courtyard of the high priest, stands up weeks later in the middle of Jerusalem and preaches the resurrection to crowds that include people who could have laughed him out of the street. James, the brother of Jesus, who during Jesus's ministry was a skeptic — John 7:5 records that "even his brothers did not believe in him" — became the leader of the Jerusalem church and was eventually executed for his testimony to the resurrection. Paul, who was persecuting the church and supervising the execution of Stephen, was transformed into the most vigorous missionary the early church produced.

Why the Alternative Explanations Fall Short

What explains this transformation? The alternatives that have been offered — the disciples hallucinated, the disciples fabricated the story, the disciples were psychologically deluded by grief — all face significant difficulties.

Hallucinations do not explain an empty tomb. They typically occur to individuals in grief or under emotional distress, not to groups of five hundred; they do not produce the transformation from terrified, scattered disciples into bold, death-defying proclaimers; and they do not produce the kind of specific, detailed, physical encounter that Paul describes — Jesus eating, being touched, being present with disciples over forty days.

Deliberate fabrication faces the obvious problem: why would you fabricate a claim and then die for it — not die in a moment of passion but live out years of suffering, imprisonment, and eventual execution rather than recant? The disciples had nothing to gain from fabrication and everything to lose. The social position of claiming resurrection in first-century Judaism was not one of prestige and power. It was a recipe for persecution.

The psychological substitution theory — that the disciples transformed their grief into a resurrection belief that functioned for them as real — requires that a grief-born conviction produced exactly the same behavioral, theological, and social effects as an actual resurrection would have, but without the physical event. This is psychologically possible for an individual; it is not a plausible explanation for a group that included people who were not grief-stricken, like Paul and James, and that produced transformations of the specific, concrete, body-affirming kind that the New Testament describes.

The historian who engages this evidence without the prior philosophical commitment that miracles cannot occur — who applies to the resurrection the same methodological openness they apply to other extraordinary claims — faces a conclusion that is more difficult to escape than the culture has suggested. The resurrection happened. Not "the disciples had an experience they interpreted as resurrection." The tomb was empty. They saw someone. That someone was identified as the same person who had been crucified. And the result was a movement that produced the most examined, most attacked, most defended document in the history of human civilization — a document that, two thousand years later, is still being read, still being trusted, still being lived by people on every continent in every language.
