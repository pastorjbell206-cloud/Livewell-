Chapter Three

The Canon Question — How We Got These Books and Not Others

The canon was recognized by the early church over centuries, not voted into existence by Constantine at Nicaea.

The question students are almost never taught to answer is this one: Why these sixty-six books? Not who decided them — most people know, vaguely, that there was a council somewhere. But why these books and not others? What were the criteria, who applied them, and was the process transparent and honest, or was it the power play that popular culture assumes?

The church did not give the books of the Bible their authority. The church recognized an authority that was already there.

The Popular Story: Constantine, Nicaea, and a Vote That Never Happened

The popular narrative about canon formation goes something like this. After centuries of competing Christian texts, the Emperor Constantine called the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, where bishops voted on which books would be included in the Bible; the books that supported the winning theological party were included, and the books that challenged that party's power — the Gnostic gospels, the Gospel of Thomas, other alternative Christianities — were suppressed and destroyed. The canon, on this telling, was a political act, not a spiritual one.

The narrative has the texture of truth. It names real institutions — the Council of Nicaea, Constantine's involvement in church affairs — and it appeals to the instinct that power corrupts; it has been told and retold in popular books and films until it feels like established fact. It is largely false.

The Council of Nicaea in 325 AD was called by Constantine to address the Arian controversy, the dispute about the nature of Christ's divinity — specifically whether the Son was of the same substance as the Father or a created being subordinate to him. The council produced the Nicene Creed. What it did not do is produce a list of canonical books. There is no surviving record of a canonical debate at Nicaea, because there was no canonical debate at Nicaea. The canon question was not the agenda.

What the Church Fathers Quoted Before Nicaea

The reason the canon question was not Nicaea's agenda is that the church's canon was substantially settled before Nicaea — a fact that can be demonstrated from the writings of church fathers who, long before Constantine's reign, quote from and appeal to the same books we now call the New Testament. Clement of Rome, writing around 95 AD, roughly the same time the last New Testament letters were being written, quotes the Synoptic Gospels and Paul's letters as authoritative. Ignatius of Antioch, writing to seven different churches around 108 AD, saturates his letters with language from Paul, John, and the Synoptics. Polycarp of Smyrna, writing around 110 to 120 AD, quotes multiple Pauline letters and 1 John. Justin Martyr, writing around 150 AD, describes the regular practice of reading "the memoirs of the apostles" in Christian worship — these are the Gospels.

By the time of Irenaeus, writing around 180 AD, the four Gospels are treated as a settled and complete unit. Irenaeus argues, in a passage that has struck later readers as odd, that there must be four Gospels in the same way there are four winds and four corners of the earth — an argument from theological symbolism rather than from historical evidence. Why would he feel the need to justify a four-Gospel canon by appeal to symbolism? Because the four-Gospel canon was already so firmly established that he was not arguing for it. He was explaining it. It needed explanation, not defense.

The Muratorian Fragment and an Early Settled Core

The Muratorian Fragment, a document from approximately 170 to 200 AD, lists the books recognized in the New Testament, and the list matches our canon almost exactly. Twenty-seven books are in our New Testament; twenty-two of them are clearly listed in the fragment. A handful — Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude, Revelation — were debated in some regions for another century or two, but the core canon was recognized and in use. By the end of the second century, more than a century before Nicaea, the four Gospels, the Pauline letters, Acts, 1 Peter, 1 John, and Revelation were accepted across the church. Origen, writing in the early third century, distinguishes between books universally accepted, books disputed in some regions, and books clearly not canonical; his list of universally accepted books matches our New Testament closely.

The councils that produced formal canonical lists — Hippo in 393 AD and Carthage in 397 and 419 AD — were not inventing a canon. They were codifying one that had been functioning for two centuries. The formal lists were produced not because the church had not known what books it used, but because the Donatist controversy and other disputes created a practical need to define the boundaries clearly.

Recognition Versus Creation

The key distinction, which the popular narrative entirely misses, is between recognition and creation. The church did not give the books of the Bible their authority; the church recognized an authority that was already there. The process was not a vote where a simple majority determined the truth. It was a long process of communal discernment in which the church asked one question of each text: Which of these bears the marks of genuine apostolic origin and teaching?

The Four Criteria: Apostolicity, Catholicity, Orthodoxy, Use

The criteria scholars have identified for canonical recognition are worth spelling out. The first was apostolicity: Was the book written by an apostle or by someone in close association with one? The four Gospels were recognized as bearing apostolic testimony — Matthew and John directly, Mark through his association with Peter, Luke through his association with Paul and his documented historical investigation. The second was catholicity: Was the book accepted across the church broadly, not merely in one region or tradition? A book used only in Alexandria but unknown in Antioch carried less weight than one recognized throughout the church.

The third was orthodoxy: Did the book's theological content cohere with the apostolic teaching as it had been passed down in the churches? This was not a test of conformity to a later theological development; it was a test of consistency with what the apostles themselves had taught. The fourth, less formalized but historically significant, was liturgical use: Had the book been regularly read in Christian worship from early times? Books that had functioned in the community's worship bore a history of use that later compositions could not have.

Why the Gnostic Gospels Were Not Suppressed but Never Recognized

The Gnostic gospels fail these criteria. The Gospel of Thomas is a collection of sayings with no narrative, no crucifixion, no resurrection, and no evident relationship to first-century Palestinian Judaism; its closest literary parallels are Valentinian Gnostic texts from the mid-second century, and even sympathetic scholars generally date it to 140 to 170 AD, long after the apostolic generation. The Gospel of Philip is an even later Gnostic text, saturated with second- and third-century Gnostic mythology. The Gospel of Peter contains a literally cosmic resurrection scene — a cross that walks out of the tomb and speaks — that reads nothing like any first-century source.

These documents were not suppressed. They were not included, which is different. And we know they were not suppressed because we have them; they survived in manuscript traditions, in libraries, in sealed jars buried in caves like the Nag Hammadi library discovered in 1945. If Constantine or the bishops of Nicaea had mounted a systematic campaign of suppression, these documents would not exist. The fact that they survive is itself evidence that no suppression occurred.

What occurred was recognition. The church, over a long process, recognized the books that bore the marks of genuine apostolic origin; the books we have are the ones that, by consistent, multi-criteria assessment applied across centuries and across the diversity of the ancient church, were found to bear those marks. This does not settle the question of inspiration. But it is a coherent, historically defensible account of how we got the Bible we have.
