Chapter Two

Scribes, Scrolls, and Centuries — The Manuscript Trail

How many manuscripts, how early, and how much agreement — the three tests that put the New Testament in a class by itself.

The Three Tests of Manuscript Reliability

When you want to know whether an ancient text has been reliably preserved, you ask three specific questions: how many manuscripts do we have, how early are they, and how much do they agree with each other? Those are the three metrics of textual reliability (quantity of manuscripts, earliness of manuscripts, and agreement between manuscripts), and by all three measures the New Testament stands alone in the ancient world. Understanding why requires knowing what we are comparing it to.

By all three measures, the New Testament stands alone in the ancient world.

Comparing the New Testament to Homer, Plato, and Caesar

Consider Julius Caesar's Gallic Wars, one of the most famous documents from the ancient world. We have approximately ten manuscripts, the earliest dating to around 900 AD, roughly 950 years after the original composition, and yet no serious historian doubts that we are reading essentially what Caesar wrote. The manuscript tradition is accepted as reliable on the basis of ten copies and a gap of nearly a millennium.

Plato's dialogues, the foundational texts of Western philosophy, survive in approximately 210 manuscripts, the earliest complete one dating to around 895 AD, some 1,200 years after Plato composed them. No serious philosopher doubts we are reading Plato; no classical scholar suggests that the Socratic dialogues are medieval inventions. The pattern holds across the other pillars of the ancient world: Tacitus, the Roman historian whose accounts of the early empire are among our most important sources, survives in twenty manuscripts, the earliest from the ninth century AD; Thucydides, the great Greek historian, in eight, the earliest from 900 AD; Herodotus in eight as well. Even Homer's Iliad, considered the best-attested ancient text outside the New Testament and the document classical scholars treat as the gold standard for manuscript evidence, comes down to us in approximately 1,800 manuscripts.

Now set the New Testament beside them. We have more than 5,800 Greek manuscripts, more than 10,000 Latin manuscripts, and more than 9,000 manuscripts in other early languages (Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopic, Georgian), for a total of more than 25,000 copies. Twenty-five thousand copies, against Homer's 1,800. And the New Testament's manuscript tradition is not only larger; it is earlier.

The Earliest Fragments: From the John Rylands Papyrus to Sinaiticus

The John Rylands Papyrus, known as P52, is a small fragment containing portions of John 18, and it dates to approximately 125 AD, perhaps as little as thirty years after the Gospel of John was originally composed. Set that against the earliest manuscript of Caesar's Gallic Wars, from 900 AD, nearly a thousand years after it was written. From there the evidence accumulates: the Bodmer Papyri, containing substantial portions of John and Luke-Acts, date to approximately 175 to 200 AD; the Chester Beatty Papyri, containing large portions of Paul's letters and the Gospels, to approximately 225 to 250 AD; and the Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, remarkable fourth-century manuscripts that contain virtually the entire New Testament, to approximately 325 to 350 AD.

No other ancient document is attested by manuscripts that close to the original composition. The gap between the writing of the Iliad and our earliest complete manuscript is roughly 500 years; the gap for Plato's dialogues is 1,200 years; the gap between the writing of the New Testament and our earliest substantial manuscripts is less than 150 years, and for specific fragments, less than fifty. The implications are significant, because the shorter the gap between original composition and earliest manuscript, the less time there is for legendary development and textual corruption, and the more manuscripts available, the more ability scholars have to compare them, identify variants, and reconstruct the original. By both measures, the New Testament is in a category by itself.

What 400,000 Variants Actually Mean

This is where critics score rhetorical points, and where defenders of Scripture sometimes respond with unnecessary alarm. The variations are real. There are, by scholarly estimate, somewhere between 200,000 and 400,000 textual variations across the New Testament manuscripts, and those numbers, stripped of context, sound alarming; the skeptic uses them to suggest we have no idea what the original text said, while some defenders panic and start minimizing, which is also wrong.

The first thing to understand is that the variation count is directly proportional to the number of manuscripts. If you have 25,000 copies of a document and they contain variations in 200,000 places, that means each manuscript differs from others in roughly eight places; if you had only ten manuscripts of the same document and they differed in forty places, that is a four-per-manuscript difference, and it would never make the news. The very number that critics use as evidence of instability is partly a function of how extraordinarily well-attested the New Testament is.

Why Most Variants Are Spelling and Word Order

The second thing to understand is the nature of the variations, because when textual scholars categorize them, the picture changes dramatically. Roughly 75 percent of all textual variations are spelling differences, and in many cases, spelling differences that do not even affect pronunciation, because different copyists in different regions spelled words differently, just as English speakers in different countries spell "colour" and "color" differently. These are not meaningful textual differences; they do not affect the sense of the text in any way.

Another 15 to 20 percent of variations involve minor grammatical or stylistic differences (word order variations, the presence or absence of the Greek definite article, the addition or omission of connecting particles) that do not affect meaning, since Greek word order is flexible enough that rearranging words in a sentence often changes nothing about what the sentence communicates. The remaining variations, perhaps 5 to 10 percent of the total, involve actual differences in wording, and of these the vast majority are instances where scribes clearly made a copying error, corrected a perceived error, or harmonized a text with a parallel passage. The number of variants that affect any significant reading is a very small fraction of the total, and the number that affect any significant doctrinal claim is, by the careful assessment of even skeptical scholars, extremely small.

Bart Ehrman, perhaps the most prominent skeptical New Testament scholar writing today, has made the manuscript variations the centerpiece of his popular argument against the New Testament's reliability in Misquoting Jesus. His work is worth reading; he is a serious scholar. But in his technical scholarship, and in a public exchange with his mentor Bruce Metzger, Ehrman acknowledged that no central Christian doctrine is in jeopardy because of the textual variants. The resurrection is attested by manuscripts that agree. The divinity of Christ is attested by manuscripts that agree. The crucifixion, the teachings of Jesus, the letters of Paul: all attested by thousands of manuscripts that overwhelmingly agree with each other.

What this means is that textual criticism has given us an extraordinarily high degree of confidence in what the original New Testament said. The scholars who have devoted their careers to this study, including scholars who are not Christians and have no theological ax to grind, regularly affirm that the text is extraordinarily well-established. Daniel Wallace, perhaps the leading contemporary New Testament textual critic, has stated that we can reconstruct the original Greek text to a level of confidence of 92 to 95 percent, and that the remaining uncertainty concerns mostly minor questions of wording rather than theological substance.

The Old Testament and the Masoretes Who Counted Every Letter

The Old Testament case is equally remarkable, though the evidence operates differently. For most of Christian history, the oldest available Hebrew manuscript of the Old Testament was the Leningrad Codex, dated to 1008 AD, nearly three thousand years after the events described in the earliest parts of the Old Testament, and roughly five hundred years after the composition of the latest books. Critics used this enormous gap as evidence that the text had been significantly altered in transmission. A thousand years of copying, across political upheaval, exile, persecution, and cultural transformation: how could the text not have changed substantially?

Then in 1947, a Bedouin shepherd threw a stone into a cave near the Dead Sea and heard it shatter something ceramic. Inside were pottery jars containing ancient scrolls, and the subsequent excavation of the Qumran caves would produce nearly nine hundred documents, including fragments of every book of the Old Testament except Esther, and including the complete book of Isaiah. The scrolls date to between 250 BC and 68 AD, pushing the manuscript tradition of the Hebrew Old Testament back more than a thousand years earlier than what had previously been available. And they said, in their substance, virtually the same thing.

The Dead Sea Scrolls revolutionized the field, not by overturning the standard text, but by confirming it. The thousand-year gap in the manuscript tradition, which critics had used to generate doubt, was found to be a gap of faithful preservation, not a gap of invention or alteration. This result was not accidental. It was the product of an extraordinary system of scribal accuracy developed by Jewish scribes, particularly by the Masoretes, the keepers of the standard Hebrew text from roughly 500 to 1000 AD. The Masoretes did not simply copy the text. They counted it. Every letter of every book was counted, the middle letter of every book was identified, and any scroll that failed the count was destroyed. This meticulous approach to preservation was not obsessive procedure for its own sake. It was theology made practice. They believed they were handling the word of the living God, and they treated it accordingly.

The manuscript tradition of the Bible, for both Old and New Testaments, is a remarkable story of care and preservation. It is not a story of inspired copying that never made errors. It is a story of a community that took its sacred text seriously enough to develop systematic methods for preserving it, and those methods worked.
