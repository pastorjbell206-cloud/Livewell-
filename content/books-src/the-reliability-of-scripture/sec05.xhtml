Chapter Four

The Dead Sea Scrolls and What They Proved

A thousand-year gap in the Hebrew text closed overnight — and the ancient scrolls matched the Bible we already had.

The gap was a gap in our available manuscript evidence, not a gap in faithful transmission.

Albright's Verdict: The Greatest Manuscript Discovery of Modern Times

I want to tell you about a moment in 1948 that changed the entire conversation. William Foxwell Albright was, at the time, America's leading biblical archaeologist — a figure of towering authority in the field — and when he received photographs from John Trever, a graduate student who had managed to photograph several of the Qumran scrolls before they were dispersed to various buyers, he was looking at something he understood instantly. Trever had sent photographs of the Isaiah Scroll, all seventeen feet of it, containing the complete book of Isaiah in Hebrew.

Albright's response was swift and unambiguous. He wrote back: "My heartiest congratulations on the greatest manuscript discovery of modern times." He was not engaging in hyperbole. He was right.

A Shepherd, a Stone, and a Shattered Jar

The discovery itself was unglamorous. Muhammad edh-Dhib, a young Bedouin shepherd from the Ta'amireh tribe, was looking for a stray goat near the cliffs of Qumran on the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea in late 1946 or early 1947 — scholars dispute the precise date — when he threw a stone into a cave opening in the cliffs and heard, instead of the expected sound of a stone hitting rock, the sound of something shattering. Ceramic. When he and his companions investigated, they found pottery jars, and some of them contained leather scrolls wrapped in linen.

The initial response was not dramatic. When the scrolls were taken to a dealer in Bethlehem, their significance was not recognized immediately; part of one scroll was reportedly used as a belt, and the scrolls moved between buyers for months before scholars realized what they were dealing with. By 1948, they were being examined by people who knew exactly what they were looking at, and by the early 1950s the caves around Qumran had been systematically excavated and the full scope of the discovery was understood.

The find included fragments of nearly nine hundred separate documents, among them portions of every book of the Hebrew Old Testament except Esther — multiple copies of some books, with thirty-six copies of Psalms, twenty-nine of Deuteronomy, and twenty-one of Isaiah. The Great Isaiah Scroll, 1QIsa(a), is the crown of the collection: the entire book of Isaiah, all sixty-six chapters, on a single scroll seventeen feet long, in remarkably well-preserved condition. The scroll dates to approximately 125 BC, based on paleographic analysis confirmed by carbon-14 dating.

That date is the crucial fact. One hundred twenty-five years before Christ — more than eleven hundred years before the Leningrad Codex, which had been the oldest available Hebrew Old Testament manuscript before the Qumran discovery.

The Great Isaiah Scroll Compared to the Masoretic Text

The question scholars immediately wanted to answer was simple to state and consequential to settle: How much had the text changed? If the Masoretic tradition — the standard Hebrew text, preserved by the Masoretes from 500 to 1000 AD and underlying our modern Old Testament — diverged significantly from these ancient scrolls, it would be evidence that the transmission was unreliable. If it matched, the thousand-year gap had been successfully bridged.

The answer was striking. The Great Isaiah Scroll, when compared to the Masoretic text of Isaiah in the Leningrad Codex, proved to be essentially the same document; the differences that existed were minor — occasional spelling variations, a handful of word differences, some stylistic changes — and nothing altered the meaning of the text in any doctrinally significant way. Gleason Archer, a Hebrew scholar at Trinity Evangelical Divinity School who spent years studying the scrolls, reported that the text was "word-for-word identical with our standard Hebrew Bible in more than 95 per cent of the text."

The implications cascade. First: the transmission is reliable. The thousand-year gap between the Qumran scrolls and the Masoretic text — a gap spanning the destruction of Jerusalem, the dispersion of the Jewish people, the rise and fall of empires, the emergence of Christianity and Islam — was crossed by scribes who preserved the text with extraordinary fidelity. Second: the Isaiah scroll's existence at Qumran around 125 BC indicates that the book of Isaiah was already in use as a sacred text well before the first century, which matters for understanding the New Testament's use of Isaiah as prophecy; Jesus and Paul were not improvising or selecting conveniently from a text still in flux, but citing a text already recognized as authoritative Scripture for generations. Third: the scrolls pushed back the manuscript tradition of the entire Hebrew Old Testament, not just Isaiah, and every book represented showed the same basic pattern — substantial agreement with the later Masoretic text, with minor variations of the kind found in any copying tradition. The Masoretes, whose systematic approach to preservation seemed almost obsessive, were preserving an already-stable text, not creating a new one.

The argument critics had deployed for generations — that the long gap between original composition and available manuscripts was evidence of possible corruption — was answered directly. The evidence showed that the gap was a gap in our available manuscript evidence, not a gap in faithful transmission. The scrolls gave us a window into the text as it existed more than a thousand years earlier, and what we saw through that window was recognizably what we already had.

Isaiah 53 a Century Before Christ

Chapter 53 of Isaiah — the Suffering Servant passage that Christians understand as a prophecy of Christ's atoning death — is essentially identical in the Dead Sea Scrolls Isaiah and the Masoretic text.

He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed.

Isaiah 53:5

Those words, in essentially the same form, are present in a Hebrew document written more than a hundred years before Jesus was born.

What the Scrolls Confirmed and What They Did Not Reveal

This was not the result critics hoped for, and it was not the result that would have made headlines. What made headlines — and continues to make headlines in popular media — is the idea that the Dead Sea Scrolls reveal suppressed alternatives to the canonical Bible. They do not. The Qumran community had a number of sectarian texts — the Community Rule, the War Scroll, the Thanksgiving Hymns — that are genuinely interesting documents of second-temple Jewish religion, but the biblical texts they possessed are not alternatives to the canon. They are recognizably the same books.

There is one important complication worth naming. The Dead Sea Scrolls contain some variations that align with the Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Old Testament — rather than with the Masoretic text, which suggests that multiple textual traditions of the Hebrew Bible were in circulation in the Second Temple period, and that the Masoretic tradition, though dominant, was not the only version. This is a real finding, and it requires careful scholarly work rather than simple resolution.

What it does not suggest is that the Bible was fundamentally unstable or that its transmission was unreliable in ways that affect its theological substance. The variations between the Qumran texts and the Masoretic tradition are of the same character as the variations within the Masoretic tradition itself: minor in substance, resolvable by textual criticism, and not affecting the core of the theological content. The Dead Sea Scrolls are not a problem for the church's confidence in Scripture. They are, as Albright recognized from the moment he saw the photographs, a remarkable confirmation of it.
