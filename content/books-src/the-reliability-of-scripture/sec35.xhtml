Chapter 34

Why Scribes Changed the Text — and What They Changed

The changes scribes made are visible, datable, and confined to the edges — which is itself a sign of fidelity.

The variants affect the edges. The center holds.

The Copyists Were Human, Not Machines

The copiers were not machines. They were human beings working by lamplight, tired and fallible, sometimes sick, sometimes under pressure, and over centuries of that labor they introduced errors into the text they revered. This is known. This is expected. This is not a scandal.

What is worth understanding is the nature of the changes, because understanding the nature changes the assessment. The question is never whether scribes erred; the question is what kind of errors they made, how far those errors reach, and whether the tradition that carried them also carries the means to find them.

What Misquoting Jesus Gets Right and Wrong

Bart Ehrman, in Misquoting Jesus, argues that the scribes changed the text in ways that altered meaning, and that the original text is beyond recovery. This is the popular version of the claim, and it generates real anxiety in people who have never thought about textual criticism, which is most people. The anxiety is understandable. It is also based on a misreading of the evidence.

Consider first the scale of what is actually at issue. The overwhelming majority of the textual variants in the New Testament manuscript tradition are trivial (different word order, spelling differences, synonyms substituted for one another), and they affect no doctrine, no command, no narrative; they are simply the noise produced by human copying over centuries. Scholars estimate that roughly 70 to 80 percent of all variants fall into this trivial category.

The remaining variants, the ones that require attention, are the subject of an entire scholarly discipline whose tools are sophisticated, transparent, and publicly available. When two manuscripts differ in a significant way, scholars examine the age of the manuscripts, the distribution of the reading across text-types, the internal logic of which reading is more likely to have produced the other, and the pattern of scribal behavior in the manuscripts in question. The discipline is not guesswork. It is careful historical reasoning with well-developed methods.

The Longer Ending of Mark and the Pericope Adulterae

Two famous passages show the discipline at work. The longer ending of Mark, the verses at 16:9-20, is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts, and most modern translations now include it with a footnote noting that fact. Its absence from Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, combined with a stylistic analysis showing that the passage's vocabulary differs from Mark's usual style, suggests it was added later. This finding is not a crisis; it is the textual tradition working as it should: preserving the early evidence, allowing scholars to identify later additions, and giving the reader access to the full picture.

The same is true for the Pericope Adulterae, the story of the woman caught in adultery at John 7:53-8:11. The story is absent from the earliest manuscripts, and it appears in different places in different manuscript traditions (some insert it after Luke 21:38 rather than in John), so the evidence strongly suggests it was not in John's original Gospel. Whether it preserves genuine tradition about Jesus is a separate question. But the transparency of the textual tradition allows us to identify the question at all.

Why Visible Changes Prove Reliability

Here is the move the popular account inverts. When scholars identify likely scribal changes (additions, harmonizations, theological adjustments), those findings actually demonstrate the reliability of the textual tradition, not its corruption. We know these changes were made because we can see them in the manuscript record; we can trace where they entered the tradition, and we can date them. That transparency is possible only because the manuscript tradition is so rich that the variants stand out, identifiable against the mass of consistent testimony surrounding them.

What the scribes changed, and why, and when, and in which manuscripts, is knowable precisely because the tradition was preserved with such breadth that the changes are visible. The anxiety that Misquoting Jesus creates is not proportionate to the actual evidence. The evidence shows a text transmitted with remarkable fidelity, studied with extraordinary care, and whose significant variants have been identified, discussed, and documented in the apparatus of every responsible scholarly edition.

The Center Holds: No Doctrine Rests on a Disputed Passage

Here is what Ehrman does not emphasize, though he knows it: no cardinal doctrine of Christian faith rests on any disputed passage. The deity of Christ, the resurrection, the atonement, the commands of Jesus, the mission of the church: none of these depend on the longer ending of Mark, or the Pericope Adulterae, or the Comma Johanneum, the disputed Trinitarian formula in 1 John 5:7-8. The doctrines are established by multiple independent lines of text, spread across the manuscript tradition in forms that are universally attested. The variants affect the edges. The center holds.

The text is not lost. It is not unrecoverable. The original is not gone. It lives in the convergence of the manuscript tradition, reconstructed by scholars working with tools Erasmus could not have imagined and a discipline that has been refined for two centuries.
