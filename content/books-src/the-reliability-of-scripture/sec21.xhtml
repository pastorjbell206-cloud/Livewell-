Chapter Twenty

What Remains After the Argument — A Word to the Persuaded and the Still-Uncertain

A direct word to two readers — the one now convinced and the one still standing at the edge.

That gap is where you live, and only you can cross it.

I want to say something to two different people. The first is the person who has followed the argument of this book and has found it persuasive — who has arrived at the end with a conviction that was not quite there at the beginning, or that was there but shaky and is now more stable, and who feels the ground firmer underfoot. The second is the person who has followed the argument honestly and remains uncertain; who finds the evidence interesting but not conclusive, who is not ready to say yes and is not ready to say no, and who is sitting with the question with genuine openness.

To the Person the Argument Persuaded

Welcome to a fellowship that is older than the word "Christianity." You are joining the company of Abraham, who trusted a word he did not fully understand; of David, who found in the Psalms a language for the whole range of his experience — the triumph, the failure, the abandonment, the return; of the disciples, who followed a man they did not fully understand and were rebuilt by an encounter with him they could not have anticipated; of Augustine, who resisted for years and then found that his restless heart had been made for the rest he was avoiding; of John Newton, the slave trader who encountered grace and wrote it down so that generations could sing it.

The ground is firm. Walk on it. Let the text do what it has always done to people who receive it with open hands: tell you who you are, name what is broken, offer what is needed, and ask more of you than you thought you could give.

A Warning: Reliable Is Not the Same as Confirming

One warning, though, before you settle in too comfortably: receiving the Bible as reliable is not the same as receiving it as confirming everything you currently believe. The text will challenge your politics; it will complicate your cultural instincts; it will name sins you have made your peace with, and it will call for a generosity that is uncomfortable and a forgiveness that feels impossible and a love for people you would prefer to dislike at a comfortable distance. This is not a problem with the text. This is the text doing its work. The God who is the Bible's author is larger than your current framework of him, and the text will keep showing you the difference.

To the Person Who Remains Uncertain

To the second person — the one who remains uncertain — I respect your uncertainty more than I respect cheap conviction. The person who says "I have looked at this and I am not sure" has done more honest intellectual work than the person who was handed faith and never examined it.

What I want to say to you is this: stay in the room with the question. Do not let uncertainty become a permanent posture that never moves toward resolution. Uncertainty is useful when it drives you to examine the evidence more carefully; it becomes evasive when it becomes a way of avoiding the demand that evidence makes.

The Gap Only You Can Cross

The evidence we have examined does not require you to believe. It invites you to believe; it gives you warrant to believe; it shows you that the ground is firmer than the culture told you. What it cannot do is cross the gap between evidence and trust for you. That gap is where you live, and only you can cross it.

I made the crossing a long time ago, and I have not regretted it for a single day — not because life has been easy, not because every question has been answered, not because the faith has cost me nothing, but because on the other side of the crossing I found that the text told the truth about the world and about myself; that the God it describes is the God who is; that the grace it offers is the grace that holds.

The text is reliable. The argument is made. The invitation is extended. What will you do with it?
