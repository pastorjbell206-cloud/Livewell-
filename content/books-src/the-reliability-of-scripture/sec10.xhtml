Chapter Nine

What the Church Has Always Believed — A History of the Doctrine of Scripture

Inerrancy is not a twentieth-century invention but the consistent conviction of the church from Augustine forward.

It is not the ceiling of what Christians believe about Scripture. It is the floor.

Origen and Augustine on a Text Free From Error

I grew up hearing "inerrancy" used as a battle cry. It was a tribal marker, a boundary word, the password that identified you as one of the faithful rather than one of the compromisers; theologians argued about it, churches split over it, and whole denominations produced lengthy statements on it. The word had become so charged with the history of twentieth-century evangelical debate that it almost stopped being useful as a theological description. So let me try to get behind the word to what the church has actually believed, across its entire history, about the nature and authority of Scripture.

The early church's testimony on this question is remarkably consistent. Origen, writing in the third century, articulates a doctrine in which the Holy Spirit is the ultimate author of Scripture and the human writers are instruments — and the implication of divine authorship is the impossibility of error, since it would be unworthy of the Spirit to speak falsely. Origen does not argue for this. He assumes it. It is the common understanding of his community.

Augustine of Hippo, in the fourth and fifth centuries, states the classic formulation with characteristic precision. He writes to Jerome: "I have learned to yield this respect and honor only to the canonical books of Scripture: of these alone do I most firmly believe that the authors were completely free from error." His argument is not merely that Scripture is authoritative; it is that the character of the authors, in light of the character of the divine Author, requires accuracy. An inspired text produced by a God who cannot lie cannot contain falsehood in what it affirms.

Augustine's Hermeneutical Principle for Difficult Passages

Augustine goes further in a letter responding to critics who point to apparent difficulties: "If I am perplexed by any passage in the text which seems to be at variance with truth, I do not hesitate to suppose that either the manuscript is faulty, or the translator has not caught the meaning of what was said, or I myself have failed to understand it." This is not naive confidence. It is a hermeneutical principle that follows from the premise of divine authorship — that when the text seems wrong, the first question is whether we are reading it rightly, not whether the text has made an error.

From Aquinas to the Westminster Confession

Aquinas in the thirteenth century follows the Augustinian line, and the same conviction carries forward across every later division of the church. The First Vatican Council in 1870, speaking for Roman Catholicism, affirms that the books of Scripture, "written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit... have God as their author." Martin Luther, who believed the church needed reformation in many things, did not believe the doctrine of Scripture's authority needed reformation; for Luther, Scripture was the norma normans — the norming norm — the standard by which all other claims were evaluated and by which the church stood or fell. Calvin, similarly, treats Scripture's authority as foundational and non-negotiable in the Institutes.

The Westminster Confession of Faith of 1646 articulates the Protestant consensus with that same precision: "The authority of the Holy Scripture, for which it ought to be believed, and obeyed, dependeth not upon the testimony of any man, or church; but wholly upon God (who is truth itself) the author thereof: and therefore it is to be received, because it is the word of God." This is not a twentieth-century innovation. It is not a reaction to Darwinism or German biblical criticism. It is the consistent position of the church across its entire history — East and West, pre-Reformation and post-Reformation.

What the Chicago Statement Does and Does Not Claim

The contemporary term "inerrancy" was developed and refined in the context of twentieth-century debates, most decisively articulated in the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy in 1978. The Statement was produced by a large group of evangelical scholars responding to theological drift in American Christianity, and it represented, in their own assessment, a clarification of what orthodox Christianity had always affirmed. It is worth understanding carefully, both for what it claims and for what it explicitly does not claim.

It does not claim that the Bible uses modern scientific or historical precision. The Statement explicitly acknowledges "the use of round numbers, free citations, topical arrangements of material, variant selections of material" as fully compatible with inerrancy, and it treats phenomenological language — speaking of the "sun rising," describing events from the observer's perspective — as not implying a false cosmology. Nor does it claim that every quotation is verbatim transcription; ancient citation conventions allowed for paraphrase and summary, and the New Testament's use of the Old Testament reflects those conventions.

What it does claim is significant: when Scripture makes affirmations, those affirmations are true. Not approximately true. Not true for their cultural moment. True. The authors of Scripture were guided by the Holy Spirit in such a way that what they wrote and affirmed corresponds to reality.

Inerrancy as the Floor, Not the Ceiling

Critics of inerrancy, both from outside evangelicalism and from within it, argue that the concept forces readings on the text that the text does not require. They argue for "infallibility" — the position that Scripture is fully trustworthy for faith and practice — without claiming historical or scientific inerrancy. This is not a trivial distinction. It has serious advocates and serious arguments.

The counter-argument for the more expansive formulation remains what it has always been: if the text can be errant in historical detail, we need a principle for determining where it errs, and that principle, inevitably, becomes our own judgment — our contemporary knowledge, our current moral sensibilities, our cultural framework. Once our own judgment becomes the arbiter of where Scripture errs, we have effectively reversed the relationship between the reader and the text. The reader now sits in judgment on the text, rather than the text sitting in judgment on the reader. The history of liberal Protestantism in the twentieth century illustrates where that road leads.

The doctrine of biblical inerrancy is not the ceiling of what Christians believe about Scripture. It is the floor. It establishes the minimum: this text is trustworthy. What it says corresponds to what is. You can build on it.
