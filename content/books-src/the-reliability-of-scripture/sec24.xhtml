Chapter Twenty-Three

Objections Answered — The Most Persistent Challenges to Biblical Reliability

Five recurring objections to the Bible, given clear answers rather than anxious deflection.

No book about biblical reliability can be complete without directly addressing the most common objections. Not in a dismissive way; some of these objections have real force, and I want to give them the honest engagement they deserve. But clearly — because the objections deserve clear answers rather than the anxious deflection that too often passes for apologetics.

The human element is real. So is the divine element.

Objection: Written by Men, Not God

The first objection runs that the Bible was written by men, not God, and so cannot be trusted as a divine word. It rests on a false alternative. The Christian claim is not that God bypassed human writers to produce a text with no human involvement; the claim is that God used human writers — with their personalities, their experiences, their literary skills, their cultural frameworks — and superintended the process so that what they produced was what God intended. This is called concursive inspiration. The human element is real. So is the divine element.

The objection would have force if the claim were divine dictation, the notion that the human writers were simply transcribing words God spoke audibly. Some passages are, explicitly, direct speech from God, quoted by the prophet. But most of Scripture does not present itself this way: Paul's letters present themselves as Paul's letters, Luke presents his Gospel as the product of careful historical investigation, and the Psalms present themselves as the prayers and songs of specific individuals.

What the divine authorship claim means is not that the human element is erased but that it was guided. Just as a human author selects, shapes, and arranges material in service of a purpose, so God selected, shaped, and arranged — through the choices and writing of the human authors — in service of a purpose the human authors did not fully understand. The human authors are real. The divine authorship is also real. Both are true simultaneously.

Objection: Translated Beyond Recognition

The second objection holds that the Bible has been translated so many times that we cannot know what the original said. It confuses translation with manuscript transmission. Translation is the process of rendering a text from one language into another; manuscript transmission is the process of copying a text within the same language. The reliability questions addressed in this book are primarily about manuscript transmission — whether the Greek and Hebrew texts underlying our translations accurately represent what was originally written.

We have already seen that the answer is yes, in extraordinary measure. The Greek and Hebrew texts underlying our modern translations rest on a manuscript tradition of 25,000-plus copies that, despite natural scribal variations, preserves the original text with high confidence. The translation question is the secondary one: given an accurate Greek and Hebrew text, how well does the English render it?

The answer is, very well. Modern Bible translations are produced by teams of scholars with PhD-level competence in the original languages, working from the best available critical texts, accountable to each other and to the scholarly community. The major translations — ESV, NIV, NASB, CSB — differ in approach, formal versus functional equivalence, but not in accuracy about the substance of what the original says.

Objection: The Gospels Contradict Each Other

The third objection claims that the Gospels contradict each other, which proves they were fabricated. We addressed this at length in Chapter Seven, but it recurs often enough to deserve reiteration. Gospel differences do not prove fabrication; they demonstrate the opposite. If four independent accounts of the same events agreed in every particular, with no difference in wording, sequence, or detail, the most natural explanation would be that the accounts had been harmonized and coordinated. It is the independent variations — the different emphases, the different sequences, the different selections of material — that bear the marks of genuine independent witness.

What the Gospel differences do not show is contradiction in substance. The central facts are agreed across all four accounts: Jesus was crucified, the tomb was found empty, Jesus appeared to his disciples after death, and the movement of people claiming to be his followers began in the same city where he was executed. The differences occur in peripheral details and in the arrangement of material. This is the profile of genuine independent testimony, not of manufactured agreement.

Objection: The Bible Supports Slavery

The fourth objection insists that the Bible supports slavery, and that a morally flawed book cannot be divinely inspired. This raises a real issue — the Bible's relationship to slavery is complex, and parts of the Old Testament and even the New Testament have been read as permitting or regulating rather than condemning the institution. But the objection requires careful analysis rather than simple concession or simple dismissal.

The Old Testament's treatment of slavery is embedded in a social context in which slavery was a universal institution of the ancient world. Its legislation regulates an existing practice and, in several cases, limits it in ways that were remarkable by ancient Near Eastern standards. Hebrew slaves were to be released in the seventh year. Runaway slaves were not to be returned to their masters. The treatment of slaves was regulated with unusual attention to their humanity.

Paul's letter to Philemon presents the case of a runaway slave, Onesimus, whom Paul is sending back to his master Philemon with a remarkable request: receive him "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a dear brother." Paul does not explicitly command Philemon to free Onesimus, but the logic of the letter makes freedom the obvious implication. The slave who is a brother in Christ occupies a status the institution of slavery cannot contain.

There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus.

Galatians 3:28

That principle, taken seriously, is incompatible with chattel slavery. It took the church centuries to draw the implication fully, and the delay is a genuine moral failure that must be owned. But the principle was there. When the abolitionists finally made the full argument — when Wilberforce and his colleagues built the case for abolition — they built it from the Bible: the image of God in every human person, the principle of Galatians 3:28, the Golden Rule. These are not cherry-picked proof texts. They are the theological DNA of the text, and the abolitionists were reading it better than the slaveholders were. The text, read carefully and honestly, does not support chattel slavery. It was read to support it by people who wanted it to, and that is a failure of interpretation, not a failure of the text.

Objection: A God Who Commands Genocide

The fifth and hardest objection asks how, if God is good, the Bible can portray him as commanding genocide. This is the hardest objection in the list, and I will not pretend otherwise. The commands to exterminate the Canaanites in Joshua 6-11 are genuinely difficult. The most honest thing I can say is this: I hold these texts with humility. I believe God's justice is better than mine, and I believe there are dimensions of these events I cannot fully comprehend from my position in history. What I do not believe is that these texts undermine the overall reliability of the biblical witness.

The canon as a whole presents a God whose fundamental character is love — who pursues rebellious creation with extraordinary patience, who is "slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love," who sends his own Son to die for the people who reject him. The Canaanite passages are a dark and difficult thread woven through that larger story; they require theological engagement, not dismissal. C.S. Lewis, Paul Copan, Tremper Longman, Christopher Wright, and others have worked seriously on this question, and I recommend their work for those who want to pursue it. What I will say here is that the difficulty of these passages is not a reason to abandon the text. It is a reason to engage it more carefully.
