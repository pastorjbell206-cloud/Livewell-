Chapter Seven

The Hard Sayings — Living with Difficulty

The Bible's most troubling passages call for honest engagement, not pretended ease.

Naming the Difficulty Without Apology

Let me name the thing directly, without apology or hedging: there are passages in the Bible that are genuinely difficult. Some are morally difficult: the commands to exterminate Canaanite peoples in Joshua, the imprecatory psalms that call for violent revenge on enemies, the Levitical laws whose logic is not immediately apparent to modern readers. Some are historically difficult, where the biblical account seems to conflict with archaeological evidence, where numbers seem impossibly large, where the sequence of events in parallel accounts differs. And some are intellectually difficult, where the text seems to endorse a cosmology that modern science has revised, where the miraculous intersects with the ordinary in ways that challenge modern epistemological instincts.

Any honest treatment of biblical reliability must engage these difficulties rather than pretend they do not exist; a faith that requires the reader to pretend everything is easy is not faith, but avoidance, and avoidance cannot hold when life gets hard, when a skeptic asks a real question, when the difficulties demand honest engagement. So let me engage them.

A text that is easy is a text that asks nothing of you.

Ancient Historiography Versus Modern Journalism

The most commonly cited category is apparent contradictions between parallel accounts. The Gospels describe the same events differently; the Synoptics and John sometimes differ in the sequence and setting of events; two accounts of the same story in Samuel and Chronicles sometimes differ in details. These discrepancies trouble modern readers because we instinctively apply modern standards of precision to ancient texts.

The first corrective is historical and literary. Ancient historiography did not operate under modern conventions: ancient historians shaped, selected, and arranged their material to serve rhetorical and theological purposes; they summarized speeches rather than transcribing them verbatim; they reported numbers sometimes symbolically, sometimes representationally, often with less precision than modern journalism requires; and they arranged events thematically when thematic arrangement served the argument better than strict chronological sequence. These were not failures of accuracy. They were features of the genre.

Consider Thucydides, the most rigorously historical of the ancient Greek historians, who explicitly says in his introduction that he paraphrased speeches, reporting the gist of what was said rather than the exact words. When we read Thucydides, we do not conclude that his history is unreliable because his speeches are paraphrases; we conclude that we are reading ancient history, which works differently than modern journalism.

So when we read the Gospels and find that Matthew's account of the Sermon on the Mount is substantially longer than Luke's account of what appears to be the same teaching, we have two explanatory options. One: the writers are recording two different occasions when Jesus taught related material; Jesus almost certainly repeated his teachings in different forms for different audiences, as any traveling teacher does. Two: the writers are presenting the material differently in service of different theological and rhetorical purposes, with Matthew grouping teaching material thematically and Luke maintaining a more chronological framework.

Both of these explanations are available without requiring either writer to be inaccurate on the terms of his own genre. What is not available is the explanation that the differences are evidence of fabrication or corruption, because that explanation imposes modern journalistic conventions on ancient historiography and then blames the ancient writers for not meeting standards they never claimed to meet.

The Temple Cleansing and the Two Explanations

The Temple cleansing, placed by the Synoptics near the end of Jesus's ministry and by John near the beginning, is a genuine difficulty that has been discussed by scholars for centuries. The two most common proposals both involve taking the texts seriously.

The first holds that John and the Synoptics are describing two separate Temple cleansings. Jesus visited Jerusalem multiple times during his ministry; John's Gospel makes this explicit while the Synoptics focus predominantly on the final visit. A second cleansing is not implausible (the practices Jesus objected to in the Temple courts would have resumed after any single confrontation), and many careful scholars hold this view.

The second holds that John has moved the account for theological reasons, placing it early in his Gospel as a programmatic statement about Jesus's mission rather than in its chronological location. This reflects a convention of ancient historiography: arrangement for rhetorical and theological purposes rather than strict chronological sequence. Scholars who hold this view do not thereby conclude that John is unreliable; they conclude that John is using the conventions of ancient biography.

What both explanations share is a refusal to import modern expectations into ancient texts. The contradiction dissolves when you read the texts as the kind of texts they are.

Numerical Discrepancies and Ancient Counting

The numerical discrepancies (David's census in 2 Samuel 24 reporting 800,000 men of Israel versus 1 Chronicles 21 reporting 1,100,000) are best understood in the context of ancient numerical conventions. Numbers in ancient literature frequently function symbolically, rhetorically, or representationally rather than with modern precision, and different counting methods (including or excluding certain classes of soldiers, including or excluding certain territories) can produce different totals from the same underlying data.

This is not a uniquely biblical problem; ancient historians generally are less precise with numbers than modern readers expect. What would be alarming is if the discrepancy were in the core theological claim rather than in a numerical detail.

The Canaanite Conquest: Three Honest Responses

The morally difficult passages require a different kind of engagement. The command to exterminate the Canaanites in Joshua 6-11 is a genuine moral difficulty; nobody who is taking the Bible seriously can pretend otherwise. These texts describe God commanding the killing of entire populations, including women and children. This is not a difficulty that can be resolved by reading the text more carefully. It requires theological engagement.

Several responses are available to the careful reader. The first is contextual and historical: the Canaanite religious practices that the texts cite as the reason for destruction (child sacrifice, fertility cult prostitution, practices that would corrupt the covenant community Israel was called to be) were not invented as later justification but are documented in ancient Near Eastern sources. The judgment described is, in the text's own presentation, a response to wickedness that had reached a tipping point; Genesis 15:16 suggests the punishment of the Amorites was delayed until their iniquity was complete.

The second is canonical and progressive: the New Testament does not present ethnic extermination as a live option for the church. The trajectory of the biblical narrative moves from Israel as an ethnic nation called to specific territorial holiness, through the cross and resurrection, to a universal community defined by faith rather than ethnicity. What was appropriate to a specific moment in the history of redemption is not necessarily normative across the entire canon.

The third, and most honest, is pastoral. I find these texts difficult. I hold them with humility. I believe that God is just and that his justice is better than mine, and I believe that there are dimensions of these events that I cannot fully comprehend from my position in history. This is not a satisfying answer in every respect, but it is honest. The alternative, cutting the hard passages out of the canon because they make me uncomfortable, is worse. I do not get to edit God's word to conform to my moral intuitions; I am required to engage it, honestly, and to trust that the difficulty is not evidence of error but evidence that I am reading something larger than myself.

The Bible is not easy. It was never meant to be easy. A text that is easy is a text that asks nothing of you, and what we have is a text that asks everything, including the willingness to sit inside difficulty without resolving it prematurely.
