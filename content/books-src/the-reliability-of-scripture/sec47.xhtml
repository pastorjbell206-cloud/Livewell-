Appendix E

Frequently Asked Questions — Brief Answers to Common Objections

Quick, sourced answers to the questions that come up most in conversations about the Bible.

The following questions surface again and again in conversations about biblical reliability. These are not comprehensive treatments; most of them have full chapter-length responses elsewhere in this book. But for the reader who wants a quick reference, or a starting point before digging deeper, these brief answers are offered.

Faith, in the biblical sense, is not belief without evidence. It is trust grounded in evidence, extended to embrace what the evidence cannot finally prove.

Nicaea, the Canon, and Who Wrote the Bible

"Didn't the Council of Nicaea decide what books would be in the Bible?" No. The Council of Nicaea, meeting in 325 AD, addressed the theological question of the nature of Christ — specifically the Arian controversy — and produced the Nicene Creed; it did not vote on the biblical canon. The list of New Testament books recognized at Nicaea was essentially the list that had already been in broad use for over a century. The formal canon discussions happened at later councils — Laodicea, Hippo, Carthage — and those councils ratified what was already established in practice. They did not invent what was theologically convenient.

"Wasn't the Bible written by men?" Yes. And it was written by men who claimed to be writing under divine inspiration. The question is not whether human hands held the pens; the question is whether the claim of divine inspiration is warranted. The reliability of the transmission and the content of the text are evidence that bears on that question — but the question itself requires engagement with the claims about Jesus, the resurrection, and the prophetic tradition, which is precisely what this book has tried to frame.

Slavery, Genocide, and the Gospels' Differences

"How can we trust a book that condones slavery and genocide?" This is a serious question that deserves a serious answer, and this is not the place for the full one. The short version: the biblical texts on these topics require careful contextual reading, an understanding of the literary genres involved, attention to the trajectory of biblical ethics — from permission to prohibition in the case of slavery — and a willingness to distinguish between what a text describes, what it commands, what it permits, and what it condemns. Paul Copan and Matthew Flannagan's Did God Really Command Genocide? is the most thorough treatment available. The question does not discredit the text's reliability; it raises questions about interpretation and ethics that the tradition has engaged seriously for centuries.

"Don't the Gospels contradict each other?" The Gospels have differences. Whether those differences constitute contradictions depends on how carefully they are read, and four independent witnesses describing the same events from different perspectives, for different audiences, with different emphases, will never tell the story identically. The differences are exactly what you expect from honest independent testimony. The agreements — on the major events, the crucifixion, the empty tomb, the resurrection appearances — are extensive. The chapter on alleged contradictions in this book addresses the question in more detail.

Science, Denominations, and the Question of Faith

"Hasn't science disproved the Bible?" The Bible makes claims about God, human beings, the nature of sin and salvation, and the historical events through which God acted; science addresses the mechanisms of the natural world. The two domains overlap at certain points — origins, miracles, the age of the universe — and those overlaps require careful treatment. But the claim that science has disproved the Bible is almost always a claim made about specific interpretations of specific passages, not about the Bible as a whole. The overwhelming majority of what the Bible claims is not addressed by science at all. The question of Jesus's resurrection is a historical question, not a scientific one.

"Why are there so many different denominations if the Bible is clear?" The existence of denominational differences reflects differences in how Christians have interpreted the Bible, not whether the Bible is reliable. The core claims of historic Christianity — the deity and humanity of Christ, the atonement, the resurrection, the authority of Scripture — have been essentially consistent across the major Christian traditions for two thousand years. The areas of genuine disagreement are secondary: church governance, baptism, spiritual gifts, eschatology. Their existence no more discredits the primary text than the existence of different legal traditions discredits the authority of law itself.

"Isn't belief just a matter of faith, not evidence?" This is the question this entire book has been written to challenge. The case for biblical reliability is not a matter of blind faith; it is a matter of evidence — manuscript evidence, archaeological evidence, historical evidence, the internal coherence of the text, and the structure of eyewitness testimony. Faith, in the biblical sense, is not belief without evidence. It is trust grounded in evidence, extended to embrace what the evidence cannot finally prove. The distinction matters enormously. The evidence is there. The question is whether you have examined it.

A Note on Sources and Methodology

Every significant claim in this book rests on a foundation of scholarship built by specialists across multiple disciplines over the past two centuries. The manuscript comparison statistics, the archaeological findings, the dating of documents, the textual variants, the prophetic fulfillment analysis — none of these are original research. They are the synthesized conclusions of scholars who have spent lifetimes in the primary sources.

Archaeological findings are drawn from peer-reviewed excavation reports and standard reference works in biblical archaeology. Where a finding is contested — where scholars disagree about what an artifact proves — this book has tried to represent the spectrum of scholarly opinion rather than presenting the most favorable interpretation as settled fact. The Erastus inscription is a possible confirmation of Romans 16:23, not a certain one; the Mesha Stele may contain a reference to the House of David. These qualifications are not concessions to doubt. They are the markers of honest inquiry.

The manuscript statistics — the number of New Testament manuscripts, comparative manuscript counts for other ancient documents, the time gaps between writing and earliest copies — are drawn from the standard treatments: Metzger's The Text of the New Testament, Daniel Wallace's work at the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts, and the critical editions produced by the United Bible Societies and Nestle-Aland. These numbers are not disputed by mainstream scholarship; they are sometimes underreported in popular treatments of the subject, but they are not in question. The dating of New Testament documents relies on the consensus of critical scholarship, not conservative apologetics — the dates given for Paul's letters, the Synoptic Gospels, and John reflect mainstream critical dating, which already places these documents within the lifetime of eyewitnesses. The case for biblical reliability does not depend on special pleading. It rests on evidence the mainstream scholarly tradition has established.

How the Reader Can Verify Every Claim

The reader who wants to verify the claims made here will find the pathway into that scholarship in Appendix D's reading plan. What matters in this note is a word about the kind of evidence the book draws on and why it is reliable: not original discovery but careful synthesis, weighing contested findings honestly and reporting the standard figures without inflation.

This book is not a work of scholarship. It is a work of synthesis and argument, drawing on scholarship to make a case for a conclusion that the evidence, honestly examined, supports. The scholars cited throughout are not infallible. The conclusions drawn are not beyond challenge. What they are is honest — the best reading of the best evidence available by someone who has followed the argument where it leads.
