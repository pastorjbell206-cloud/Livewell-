A NOTE ON FURTHER READING

The best scholarship on each question, from accessible introductions to standard academic treatments.

For those who want to pursue any of the arguments in this book further, the following works represent the best of serious scholarship in each area. These are starting points, not conclusions; the honest reader follows the argument wherever it leads, and the best work in each field continues to develop.

On Manuscript Evidence and Textual Criticism

F.F. Bruce's The New Testament Documents: Are They Reliable? remains one of the best accessible introductions to the question of manuscript evidence, the doorway through which most readers first walk. For the standard scholarly treatment there is Bruce Metzger, The Text of the New Testament: Its Transmission, Corruption, and Restoration; and Metzger and Ehrman's fourth edition of The Text of the New Testament presents the same evidence in a form accessible to careful general readers. Those who want to examine the papyri directly will find Philip Comfort and David Barrett, The Text of the Earliest New Testament Greek Manuscripts, invaluable.

On Archaeology and the Canon

On archaeological confirmation, K.A. Kitchen's On the Reliability of the Old Testament is the definitive defense of Old Testament historical reliability, written by one of the world's leading Egyptologists and ancient Near Eastern scholars. Iain Provan, V. Philips Long, and Tremper Longman III, in A Biblical History of Israel, provide a rigorous methodology for approaching the relationship between the biblical text and the archaeological evidence; and William Ramsay's St. Paul the Traveller and the Roman Citizen remains a fascinating account of an archaeologist's conversion from skepticism.

On the canon, F.F. Bruce's The Canon of Scripture is accessible and thorough. Michael Kruger's Canon Revisited: Establishing the Origins and Authority of the New Testament Books is the best recent scholarly treatment, and Lee McDonald's The Biblical Canon: Its Origin, Transmission, and Authority presents a comprehensive historical survey.

On Inerrancy and the Doctrine of Scripture

Benjamin Warfield's The Inspiration and Authority of the Bible remains the classic conservative formulation of the doctrine of Scripture. John Webster's Holy Scripture: A Dogmatic Sketch is a careful contemporary theological treatment; Timothy Ward's Words of Life: Scripture as the Living and Active Word of God is at once accessible and rigorous; and Wayne Grudem's Systematic Theology provides a comprehensive evangelical treatment.

On Answering Popular Critical Accounts

For those wrestling with the popular skeptical literature, Timothy Paul Jones, Misquoting Truth: A Guide to the Fallacies of Bart Ehrman's Misquoting Jesus, is a helpful direct response to the most influential popular skeptical account. Craig Evans, in Fabricating Jesus: How Modern Scholars Distort the Gospels, addresses the broader claim that Jesus has been invented by later tradition, and Ben Witherington III's The Living Word of God: Rethinking the Theology of the Bible offers a pastoral-academic synthesis.

On the Dead Sea Scrolls and Hermeneutics

On the Dead Sea Scrolls, James VanderKam and Peter Flint's The Meaning of the Dead Sea Scrolls: Their Significance for Understanding the Bible, Judaism, Jesus, and Christianity is the best single-volume treatment, and Lawrence Schiffman's Reclaiming the Dead Sea Scrolls: Their True Meaning for Judaism and Christianity is equally valuable from a Jewish perspective. On hermeneutics and interpretation, Gordon Fee and Douglas Stuart's How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth is the standard introductory text, while Kevin Vanhoozer's Is There a Meaning in This Text? confronts the philosophical challenges of postmodern interpretation with rigorous care.

That is what the Bible asks of its readers: honesty. A willingness to let the text be what it is, examined by the best available evidence, and then met with the response its testimony demands.
