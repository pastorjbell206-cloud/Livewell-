Appendix D

A Reading Plan for the Serious Inquirer

A staged path through the scholarship, beginning with the Gospels themselves.

If this book has raised more questions than you arrived with, that is not a failure of the book; it is the beginning of the work. The following reading plan will help you pursue those questions systematically, moving from accessible introductions to thorough academic treatments, so that you can engage the scholarship rather than absorb a verdict secondhand.

The case is stronger than the culture has told you. The more carefully you follow the evidence, the more clearly you will see it.

Begin With the Gospels Themselves

Whatever else you read about the Bible, nothing substitutes for reading the primary source. Begin there. Read Mark in one sitting — it takes about two hours — then read John, then Matthew, then Luke-Acts as the two-volume work it was written to be.

Read them the way a historian reads any document, asking the questions a historian asks: What kind of document is this? What can I infer about its origin? What does the internal evidence suggest about the author's sources and purposes? The text will not resent the scrutiny; it was written to withstand it.

For the Manuscript and Archaeological Questions

On the manuscript question, start with F.F. Bruce, The New Testament Documents: Are They Reliable?, available free online in older editions; then, if you want more depth, take up Bruce Metzger and Bart Ehrman, The Text of the New Testament, 4th edition. Metzger represents the tradition and Ehrman represents the critical challenge, and reading them together — rather than either alone — gives you the actual state of the scholarly question rather than a partisan slice of it.

On the archaeological question, read K.A. Kitchen, On the Reliability of the Old Testament — at least the introduction and the chapters on the periods you are most interested in. It is long and technical in places, but the introduction is accessible. For the New Testament side, William Ramsay's The Bearing of Recent Discovery on the Trustworthiness of the New Testament (1915) is old but still valuable, and freely available online.

For the Canon Question

Read Michael Kruger, The Question of Canon — scholarly but accessible. Then, if you want more context on the Gnostic gospels and what was excluded, read Bart Ehrman's Lost Christianities alongside Craig Evans's Fabricating Jesus; one presents the critical case and the other responds to it directly, and the two read together will keep you from mistaking a popular narrative for a settled conclusion.

For the Resurrection and the Doctrine of Scripture

On the resurrection, read N.T. Wright, The Resurrection of the Son of God — the most thorough historical treatment of the question in modern scholarship, written by a scholar who is both rigorously historical and committed to the resurrection's reality. Gary Habermas and Mike Licona, The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus, is more accessible and handles the objections directly.

On the doctrine of Scripture, read Wayne Grudem's chapter on Scripture in Systematic Theology for a comprehensive evangelical treatment; then read John Webster, Holy Scripture, for a more academic but genuinely illuminating dogmatic perspective. If you want the classic conservative statement, B.B. Warfield's Inspiration and Authority of the Bible is available free online and remains essential reading.

For the Hardest Old Testament Questions

Paul Copan and Matthew Flannagan, Did God Really Command Genocide?, addresses the most difficult moral questions in the Old Testament directly and carefully — not by softening the difficulty, but by facing it.

This list is not exhaustive. Every one of these books carries a bibliography that will take you deeper still. But the list gives you a path through the scholarship — from accessible introductions to thorough academic treatments — that will equip you to engage the questions with confidence. The case is stronger than the culture has told you. The more carefully you follow the evidence, the more clearly you will see it.
