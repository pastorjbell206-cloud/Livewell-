Appendix C

Key Scholars and Their Contributions

The specialists whose work underwrites the reliability case, from F.F. Bruce to Richard Bauckham.

This appendix offers brief notes on the scholars whose work is foundational for understanding the reliability case, written for readers who want to pursue the arguments further. None of what follows in this book rests on assertion alone; behind every claim stands a specialist who spent a career inside the evidence. These are some of them.

Textual Critics: Bruce, Metzger, Wallace

F.F. Bruce (1910-1990) was a Scottish-born biblical scholar and professor at Manchester whose work remains a doorway into the whole field; his book The New Testament Documents: Are They Reliable? (1943, in multiple editions since) is still one of the most accessible and well-argued introductory treatments, and his Canon of Scripture (1988) is comprehensive and authoritative.

Bruce Metzger (1914-2007), the Princeton New Testament scholar widely considered the dean of American textual criticism, set the standard for the discipline itself; his Text of the New Testament, revised across several editions, is the standard scholarly treatment, and his Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament works through the significant variants in patient detail.

Daniel Wallace (born 1952), New Testament professor at Dallas Theological Seminary and director of the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts, carries that work into the present. His Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics is the standard intermediate grammar; his own work on textual criticism is widely respected; and his responses to Bart Ehrman's popular books are particularly valuable for the reader meeting those arguments for the first time.

Archaeologists: Albright, Kitchen, Ramsay

William Foxwell Albright (1891-1971), the Johns Hopkins archaeologist and orientalist, was arguably the twentieth century's most influential biblical archaeologist; his work on the Dead Sea Scrolls and on ancient Near Eastern archaeology helped establish the historical reliability of the Old Testament on the firm ground of the spade.

K.A. Kitchen (born 1932), the Liverpool Egyptologist and ancient Near Eastern scholar, wrote what remains the field's most demanding defense: his On the Reliability of the Old Testament (2003) is the most comprehensive and technically rigorous case yet made by a first-rank specialist in the very ancient civilizations the text describes.

William Ramsay (1851-1939), the Scottish classical archaeologist and New Testament scholar, is the case study in changed minds; his excavations in Asia Minor and his study of Luke-Acts turned an initial skepticism into a confident affirmation of Luke's historical reliability, and his books remain valuable to this day.

The Eyewitness Case: Richard Bauckham

Richard Bauckham (born 1946), the British New Testament scholar and professor at St. Andrews, gave the eyewitness question its fullest treatment. His Jesus and the Eyewitnesses (2006) is the most thorough scholarly study of the eyewitness dimension of the Gospels, and its implications reach far wider than its subject, touching the reliability question at its root.

The Dating Question: John A.T. Robinson

John A.T. Robinson (1919-1983), the Anglican bishop and New Testament scholar, was no conservative; that is precisely what makes his contribution weigh. His Redating the New Testament (1976) made a rigorous case for early dates across all the New Testament books, argued purely on internal evidence, with implications far more favorable to historical reliability than his own liberal theological commitments might have led anyone to expect.

The Canon Scholars: Kruger and Jones

Michael Kruger (born 1973), president of Reformed Theological Seminary, has written the most thorough recent scholarly treatments of how the church received its books; his Canon Revisited (2012) and The Question of Canon (2013) argue against the conspiracy narratives and for the organic development of canonical recognition.

Timothy Paul Jones (born 1970), professor at Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, answers the same set of questions where most readers first encounter them. His Misquoting Truth (2007) is an accessible and thorough response to Ehrman's Misquoting Jesus, engaging the evidence carefully rather than dismissively. Not a defensive crouch. A patient reading of the record.
