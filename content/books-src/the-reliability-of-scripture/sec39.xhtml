Chapter 38

The Question Behind All the Questions

Every academic question is a proxy for one: is this book trustworthy enough to build your life on?

The Real Question Beneath the Academic Ones

Every question this book has addressed — about manuscripts, archaeology, prophecy, eyewitness testimony, canon, textual criticism — is a proxy for a single deeper question, the one that sits beneath all the others and waits for them to clear: is this book trustworthy enough to bet your life on? That is the real question.

Reliability establishes that we have the book. It does not establish that the book is right.

The academic questions matter, and they deserve the careful treatment this book has tried to give them. But the person who picks up a Bible and reads it is not doing so to write a paper on textual transmission; they are trying to figure out whether what this book says about God and human beings and salvation and eternity is actually true.

What Reliability Establishes and What It Does Not

The reliable transmission of the text is necessary to that question, but it is not the whole of it. Reliability establishes that we have the book. It does not establish that the book is right. What reliability does — and what this book has tried to establish — is clear away the false impediments.

Clearing Away the False Impediments

The person who cannot take the Bible seriously because they have heard that the manuscripts are unreliable can, after careful investigation, set that concern aside; the manuscript tradition is robust, and the text we hold is substantially what was written. The false impediment is removed.

The person who cannot take the Bible seriously because they have heard that archaeology has discredited it can, after careful investigation, set that concern aside as well — the archaeology has not discredited it, and in significant cases it has confirmed it. The false impediment is removed.

And the person who cannot take the Bible seriously because they have heard that the canon was determined by political manipulation in the fourth century can, after careful investigation, set that concern aside too; the canon was recognized, not invented, and the books that were included had been in circulation for centuries. The false impediment is removed.

With the false impediments removed, a different question comes into focus. Not: Can I trust the transmission? But: what do I do with what this book actually says?

A Demanding Document

This is the question the book itself wants you to ask. The Jesus of the Gospels is not interested in being studied at a safe distance; he is interested in being followed, trusted, obeyed. The Paul of the letters is not writing a philosophy but making demands on people's lives, and the psalmists are not performing sentiment — they are crying out to a God they believe can hear them.

The Bible is a demanding document, and it makes demands of its readers that are not comfortable: demands about who is Lord, about what human beings are, about what we have done wrong and what has been done to fix it. The reliability question is, in one sense, a way of deferring those demands — if the book is unreliable, I do not have to engage with what it says; if the transmission is corrupt, I can set it aside.

Why the Demands Deserve Engagement

This book has tried to show that the deferral is not warranted. The book is reliable. The text is trustworthy. The documents come to us from witnesses with access to the events they describe — which means the demands deserve engagement.

I came to faith as an atheist who asked honest questions and received honest answers. I was not satisfied by easy reassurance; I was not converted by emotion. I followed the argument, and the argument led me somewhere I had not expected to go — and that process, of genuine inquiry followed by genuine encounter, has never left me. It is the structure of everything I have done since.

The questions this book has addressed are the questions I once asked, and the answers I have given are the answers I found. They are not complete; no treatment of these questions can be complete in a single volume. But they are sufficient to establish what they set out to establish.

The Bible you hold in your hands is not a corrupted, unreliable, politically manufactured artifact of late antiquity. It is a carefully transmitted, archaeologically confirmed, historically grounded collection of documents written by people who believed they were speaking for God — and who paid for that belief with their lives. Whether they were right is the question only you can answer. But you owe it the hearing it has earned.
