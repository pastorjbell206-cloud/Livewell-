Chapter Eleven

Reading the Bible Right — Genre, Hermeneutics, and Honest Interpretation

Most apparent biblical problems dissolve when a text is read according to the genre it was actually written in.

The Bible is not one genre. It is a library.

The Reading Posture You Already Use Without Noticing

The single most common reason people stumble on apparent biblical difficulties is not that the difficulties are insurmountable. It is that they are reading a document produced in one cultural and literary context with expectations drawn from a completely different one, and they never notice the substitution.

We do this instinctively with contemporary texts, shifting gears so smoothly that the shifting itself becomes invisible. When you open a novel, you take up a particular posture: you do not demand literal accuracy in every detail; you follow an imaginative world meant to be compelling and illuminating rather than factually precise. When you open a newspaper, you change posture again, expecting accuracy in names, dates, and places. A scientific paper asks for quantitative precision, hedged claims, documented sources; a poem invites metaphor, compression, image. You do all of this without thinking about it.

The trouble begins when modern Western readers bring a single posture to the entire Bible and apply it uniformly — either the novel posture, which assumes the text is imaginative rather than factual, or the newspaper posture, which assumes modern precision throughout. Both applications produce distortions, because both ask the text to behave like something it never claimed to be.

The Bible Is a Library, Not a Single Genre

The Bible is not one genre. It is a library, spanning roughly fifteen hundred years of composition and containing law, narrative, poetry, prophecy, wisdom literature, apocalyptic, epistle, and gospel. Each genre operates according to its own conventions, serves its own purposes, and creates its own reading expectations — and the reader who flattens those differences into one will misread nearly everything.

Poetry, Apocalyptic, and Historical Narrative

Poetry does not make factual claims the way narrative does. When Psalm 98 calls on the rivers to clap their hands, nobody — not the psalmist, not the original audience, not the responsible modern reader — believes rivers have hands. This is not a problem with biblical reliability; this is poetry doing what poetry does, deploying metaphor and image to evoke a reality that literal description could not capture. The rivers clapping their hands is an image of creation joining the praise of its Creator. To demand literal accuracy from such an image is a genre error.

Apocalyptic literature runs on conventions still more foreign to modern readers. The books of Daniel and Revelation use symbolic imagery — beasts, horns, numbers, cosmic battles — that their original audiences would have recognized at once as a shared visual vocabulary for political and cosmic reality. The four beasts of Daniel 7 are not zoological descriptions; they are political entities, empires, rendered through symbols that convey their character and their standing under God's sovereignty. Reading that imagery as a literal forecast of specific future events, as much popular end-times speculation does, is a different kind of genre error. The imagery is meant to communicate reality — but through symbol, not through literal report.

Historical narrative is historical. This is not a concession to naive literalism. It is the straightforward recognition that the writers of biblical narrative were making historical claims. The evangelists — Matthew, Mark, Luke, John — were not writing fiction or mythology. Luke opens his Gospel by saying so plainly:

I myself have carefully investigated everything from the beginning, so it seemed good also to me to write an orderly account for you, most excellent Theophilus, so that you may know the certainty of the things you have been taught.

Luke 1:3-4

That is a historian's introduction; he is claiming to have done careful research and to be producing a reliable account. But historical narrative in the ancient world operated under conventions different from modern historical writing. Ancient historians selected, shaped, and arranged their material to make arguments; they paraphrased speeches rather than transcribing them, emphasized different aspects of the same events for different purposes, and arranged material thematically when the argument was better served that way. None of this counts as unreliability by ancient standards, because these were the acknowledged conventions of the genre.

What Kind of Text Is Genesis 1?

The principle that resolves most apparent difficulties is the hermeneutical principle of genre sensitivity: interpret a text according to the conventions of the genre in which it was written, for the audience for which it was produced, in the cultural context in which it appeared. Apply that to Genesis, and the first question is not whether it is true but what kind of text it is.

Its closest parallels in the ancient Near East are texts like Enuma Elish, the Babylonian creation account — and yet Genesis 1 is not simply another ancient Near Eastern cosmogony. It is a systematic polemic against the theology of those cosmogonies. Where Enuma Elish depicts creation as the by-product of cosmic warfare among competing deities, Genesis 1 depicts a single sovereign God who creates by speech. Where Enuma Elish makes the sun and moon cosmic deities, Genesis 1 demotes them to lights — not even named, presumably to avoid the very names that were also the names of pagan gods. Genesis 1 is making theological arguments about who God is and what the creation is, borrowing the conventions of ancient Near Eastern cosmological literature and subverting them at every point.

To read Genesis 1 as a scientific account of the mechanisms and timeline of physical cosmology is to impose on it a genre expectation the text itself never invites. The text is not answering the question by what mechanisms and in what sequence did the physical universe form; it is answering the question who made the world, what kind of being is the Maker, and what kind of creatures are we. These are profoundly different questions, and the text addresses the latter set, not the former.

This does not mean Genesis 1 makes no factual claims. It makes factual claims of great significance: that God made the world, that creation is good, that human beings are made in the image of God and therefore carry a peculiar dignity and responsibility. These are historical and theological claims that matter enormously, and they are not compatible with a purely random, purposeless, accidental universe. But they are not claims about the age of the earth or the mechanism of species development.

Genre Sensitivity and the Creation Debate

The creation-evolution debate is, in significant part, a debate about genre. Not entirely — there are genuine exegetical and theological questions on all sides. But much of the heat that debate generates comes from the application of modern scientific genre expectations to an ancient Near Eastern theological text. Genre sensitivity does not solve every question. It dissolves many of the apparent contradictions before they ever harden into real ones.

The hermeneutical posture this book commends is neither naive literalism nor skeptical relativism. It is the posture of a careful reader who takes the text seriously enough to ask what it is actually claiming, in what genre it makes those claims, for what audience they were first intended, and what a responsible and informed reading of those claims in their context requires. That posture does not make every difficulty easy. But it makes sure we are arguing about the right things.
