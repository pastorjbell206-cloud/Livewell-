Chapter Twelve

The Internal Testimony — What Scripture Claims About Itself

What "God-breathed" means, and why the Bible's self-witness carries weight only after its credibility is established.

You cannot assess whether a document's self-description is accurate if you have not first determined what the self-description is.

Avoiding the Circular Argument

Any serious treatment of biblical reliability must attend to what the Bible says about its own nature. Not because the Bible's self-testimony is self-validating; the argument that the Bible is true because the Bible says the Bible is true is indeed circular, and we should not make it. We attend to it because understanding what the text claims about itself is prerequisite to evaluating the claim — and you cannot assess whether a document's self-description is accurate if you have not first determined what the self-description is.

"God-Breathed" and the Word Theopneustos

The New Testament's most direct claim about Scripture appears in 2 Timothy 3:16-17.

All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness, so that the servant of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work.

2 Timothy 3:16-17

The Greek word translated "God-breathed" is theopneustos, a compound of theos (God) and pneo (to breathe). It appears nowhere else in the Greek New Testament and nowhere in the Septuagint; it was either coined by Paul for this context or drawn from a usage that has not survived in other sources. The question of its precise force — does it mean that God breathed into the Scripture, or that God breathed out the Scripture? — has been carefully analyzed by scholars, and Benjamin Warfield's classic study concluded that the active sense is grammatically correct. God breathed out. Scripture is the product of God's breath.

The metaphor of divine breath runs throughout the Old Testament as an image of divine creative power. God breathed into Adam and he became a living being (Genesis 2:7); the Spirit of God moves over the waters at creation; the breath of God gives life. The claim that Scripture is "God-breathed" places the origin of the text in the same category as that divine creative activity — not a human invention that God later endorsed, but a text whose origin is in God's own utterance.

Divine Authorship and Human Voices Together

This does not mean the human authors were passive instruments. The biblical writings bear unmistakable marks of individual human authorship. Paul's rhetoric is distinctive and recognizable — his passionate, long-winded sentences, his tendency to interrupt his own argument with doxologies, his facility with complex logical arguments alongside his disarming personal vulnerability. John's vocabulary is different, simpler and more repetitive, built on a smaller set of profound concepts deployed over and over. Isaiah's poetry is dense and magnificent, unlike Jeremiah's, which is more emotional and confessional. The diversity of human voice in Scripture is real. It is a feature, not an embarrassment.

The theological category that holds both divine authorship and human diversity together is concursive inspiration — the idea that God superintended the human process of writing in such a way that what was produced was precisely what God intended, without overriding the writers' personalities, experiences, or literary gifts. The writers wrote freely, from their own perspectives, in their own styles, from within their own historical contexts; and what they produced, under the superintendence of the Holy Spirit, was exactly what God intended to be written.

Peter's second letter offers a different angle on the same reality.

For prophecy never had its origin in the human will, but prophets, though human, spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.

2 Peter 1:21

The Greek word translated "carried along" is pheromenoi — the same word used in Acts 27 for a ship being driven by the wind. The Spirit did not override the prophets; the Spirit moved them, directed them, sustained them in a direction they would not have arrived at on their own.

What Jesus Assumed About the Old Testament

The words of Jesus himself provide the most striking self-testimony in the New Testament, for he treats the Old Testament as the very words of God in a way that requires attention.

For truly I tell you, until heaven and earth disappear, not the smallest letter, not the least stroke of a pen, will by any means disappear from the Law until everything is accomplished.

Matthew 5:18

The smallest letter in Hebrew is the yod, approximately the size of a comma; the stroke of a pen refers to the small ornamental marks that distinguish one Hebrew letter from another. Jesus is claiming that the Old Testament is authoritative down to its tiniest textual details.

In John 10:35, in the middle of a controversy with Jewish authorities who accuse him of blasphemy, Jesus makes a brief argument from a single word in Psalm 82 and then adds, parenthetically, "and the Scripture cannot be broken." This is not the main point of the argument; it is a foundational assumption stated so briefly it almost disappears. The Scripture cannot be broken. Cannot err. Cannot fail. This is the bedrock of Jesus's relationship to the Old Testament.

What do we do with Jesus's claims about Scripture? There is an internal logic to the question worth pressing. If Jesus rose from the dead — if the resurrection testimony is early, multiple, and circumstantially specific in the way we have established — then his claims deserve a level of epistemic confidence different from the claims of anyone who has not demonstrated that kind of authority. A person who defeats death is not a person you can simply place in the category of interesting thinker whose views you will weigh against other interesting thinkers. Either he is who he claimed to be, or he is something else entirely. And if he is who he claimed to be, his claims about the nature of Scripture — stated not incidentally but as foundational premises in multiple argumentative contexts — are not views to take lightly.

When Internal Testimony Carries Its Full Weight

The self-testimony of Scripture does not prove the Bible's reliability in the way external evidence proves it. We cannot build the case on the foundation of the Bible's own testimony about itself without first establishing, by other means, that the text is the kind of text whose testimony is credible. That is what the previous chapters have been doing.

But now that we have established the credibility of the text by external means — manuscript evidence, early dating, archaeological confirmation — the internal testimony carries its full weight. The text says it is God-breathed. And the text's track record of reliability gives us reason to take that claim seriously rather than dismiss it reflexively.
