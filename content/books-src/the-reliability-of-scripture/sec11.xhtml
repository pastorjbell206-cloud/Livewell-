Chapter Ten

When the Culture Says All Texts Are Suspect

The dismissal of the Bible is usually an atmosphere absorbed, not an argument examined.

This is not an argument. It is an atmosphere. And atmospheres are powerful precisely because they do not have to argue.

Suspicion as Atmosphere, Not Argument

The challenge to biblical reliability in the twenty-first century is not primarily archaeological. It is atmospheric. The atmosphere of late-modern culture in the Western world carries a particular suspicion of authoritative texts, especially religious ones; and that suspicion does not usually articulate itself as a philosophical argument, because it does not have to. It is more like a humidity: the climate in which all other ideas form.

By the time a student reaches college, they have already absorbed, through television and social media and a thousand small cultural signals, the assumption that the Bible is a human document produced by people with biases, that religion generally is a social construction serving political purposes, and that anyone who claims objective truth from a religious text is either naive or manipulative. This is not an argument. It is an atmosphere. And atmospheres are powerful precisely because they do not have to argue; they simply are the air people breathe.

The philosophical tradition that produced this atmosphere is worth naming, because naming it allows us to evaluate it rather than simply inhabit it.

Derrida, Foucault, and the Claim That All Truth Is Power

Postmodern theory (primarily the work of French philosophers like Jacques Derrida, Michel Foucault, and Jean-François Lyotard, developed in the 1960s through the 1980s and filtering through universities into popular culture over the following decades) made a set of specific claims about the nature of texts, knowledge, and power. The most significant for our purposes is this: all texts are produced from within ideological frameworks, and every claim to truth is simultaneously a claim to power. The text that presents itself as objectively true is always the text of whoever holds the power. There is no view from nowhere. There is no neutral standpoint. All interpretation is politically situated.

If this is true, then the question "is the Bible reliable?" is itself an ideologically constructed question, and any answer is an ideologically constructed answer. The Bible's reliability cannot be assessed neutrally, on this account, because there is no neutral.

The Self-Refuting Problem in Strong Relativism

This argument has an internal problem that postmodern theory never adequately solved: the claim "all truth claims are power claims" is itself a truth claim. Is it, then, a power claim? If so, by whom? The postmodern critic occupies a social position as surely as the bishop defending orthodoxy; their critique of power presupposes a standpoint from which they can see the power dynamics, but where does that standpoint come from? The self-refuting character of strong relativism has been noted in every serious course in epistemology since ancient Greece.

What postmodern theory did succeed in doing was revealing that interpretation is never innocent: that readers bring assumptions to texts, that communities of interpretation shape how texts are read, that what seems obvious in one cultural moment can seem anything but obvious in another. These are real insights. They should make us humble about our interpretations and attentive to our own cultural blind spots.

Charles Taylor and the Immanent Frame

Charles Taylor's A Secular Age is the most sophisticated account of the contemporary situation. Taylor argues that the secular age, the age in which religious belief has become optional rather than default, is not the result of evidence establishing that religious claims are false; it is the result of a long series of philosophical moves that created what Taylor calls "the immanent frame": the assumption that the natural order is a self-sufficient, closed system, and that reference to anything transcendent is at best unnecessary and at worst superstitious. People in a secular age do not generally believe they have made these philosophical moves. They believe they are simply being rational. But the immanent frame is itself a construction, a particular way of seeing that became so habitual it stopped seeming like a choice.

What Taylor's analysis establishes is not that secular people are wrong and religious people are right. It establishes that both secular and religious people are operating within frameworks that cannot be fully scrutinized from the inside. The secular person who dismisses the Bible as a human document is not operating without a framework; they are operating from within the immanent frame, which has its own commitments and its own exclusions. The claim that supernatural events cannot have occurred is not a neutral historical judgment. It is a philosophical commitment embedded in a worldview.

Why Ruling Out Miracles Is a Philosophy, Not a Finding

This matters for how we evaluate the Bible's reliability. Critics who dismiss the Bible because it contains miracle narratives are not following the evidence to a neutral conclusion; they are applying a prior commitment (that miracles cannot occur) to the evidence. That prior commitment is a philosophical choice, not a historical finding.

A genuine historicism, one that follows the evidence wherever it leads rather than excluding categories of evidence on philosophical grounds, must be open to the possibility that unusual events occurred, and then ask what the evidence says about whether they did. When we do this with the resurrection of Jesus, the central miraculous claim of the New Testament, we find, as we have noted, a creed that dates to within years of the event, attested by named witnesses many of whom were still alive when Paul wrote, circulated in a community that was in direct contact with those witnesses.

The cultural atmosphere that dismisses this is not evidence. It is mood. And mood, however powerful, is not a substitute for examining the actual case.
