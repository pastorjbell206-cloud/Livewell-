Chapter Fourteen

Eyewitness Testimony and the Four Gospels

The named witnesses inside the Gospels point to testimony, not anonymous legend.

Simon of Cyrene was not a character invented to carry Jesus's cross. He was a real person.

The Knowledge That Comes From Being in the Room

There is a particular kind of knowledge you get only from being in the room. Not the knowledge of having read about a thing, not secondhand knowledge reconstructed from sources, but the knowledge that belongs to a person who was there, who saw what happened, who heard what was said, and who carries the memory of the event in their own body. This knowledge has a texture that secondhand knowledge does not. You can tell the difference, usually, when you are reading it.

Richard Bauckham's book Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, published in 2006, made a case that changed the field of New Testament studies for those willing to engage it seriously. Bauckham argued that the four Gospels are not folk literature — the product of anonymous traditions passed through communities over generations, losing historical particularity and gaining theological embellishment as they traveled. They are, instead, rooted in eyewitness testimony, and the Gospels themselves preserve the evidence of it. His argument rests on a cluster of observations, most of which had been noted before in isolation but had never been assembled into a coherent case.

Named Individuals as Living Guarantors

The first observation concerns named individuals. In the oral traditions of antiquity, stories about unnamed people tended to become even more anonymous over time, because the identity was irrelevant to the teaching or the theological point; stories about named individuals, by contrast, tended to preserve the name, because the named person was available to be consulted, could confirm or deny the account, and functioned as a living guarantor of the tradition.

The same pattern appears throughout the Gospels. Blind Bartimaeus in Mark 10:46 is identified by name and patronymic — the son of Timaeus. The two Marys at the tomb in Mark 16:1 are identified specifically. Jairus, the synagogue ruler whose daughter Jesus raises, is named in Mark 5:22 and Luke 8:41 but unnamed in Matthew 9:18 — a variation Bauckham argues reflects Matthew's practice of dropping the name once the individual was no longer available to confirm the account. Joseph of Arimathea, who provides the tomb for Jesus's burial, is named in all four Gospels and identified with specific detail.

Simon of Cyrene, His Sons, and the Rufus of Romans

The Gospels are full of named individuals who appear in narratively unnecessary ways. Simon of Cyrene, who carries Jesus's cross in Mark 15:21, is identified not just by name but by the names of his sons — Alexander and Rufus. Why would a later legendary tradition preserve the names of Simon's sons? Because Simon's sons were apparently known in the early church; Rufus appears as a named individual in Romans 16:13, where Paul describes him as someone whose mother treated Paul like her own. The Rufus of Mark and the Rufus of Romans appear to be the same person, and the Gospel tradition preserved the names because the family was available to be consulted.

Simon of Cyrene was not a character invented to carry Jesus's cross. He was a real person from Cyrene, in North Africa, conscripted for the task, whose sons were known to the communities for which Mark wrote.

Peter Behind Mark's Gospel: The Testimony of Papias

The second observation concerns the inner circle. Throughout the Gospel narratives a pattern emerges in which Jesus is accompanied by a small group of disciples, and within that group an even smaller inner circle of three — Peter, James, and John — present at moments others are not. Peter is consistently the most active character in the narratives, constantly speaking, acting, failing, recovering; in Mark in particular, his behavior is narrated with a frankness that borders on embarrassing — his impetuous speech, his misunderstanding of key moments, his denial. The early church had a strong tradition that Mark's Gospel preserves the preaching of Peter, that Mark was Peter's interpreter in Rome, and that the Gospel represents Peter's own testimony about the ministry of Jesus.

This tradition is not late legendary development. It is attested by Papias of Hierapolis, writing around 125 AD, who records the words of an earlier authority he calls the Elder: "The Elder used to say this as well: 'Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately everything he remembered, though not in order, of the things either said or done by Christ. For he neither heard the Lord nor accompanied him, but later, as I said, accompanied Peter, who used to give teachings as the need arose, but not making an arrangement of the dominical oracles, so that Mark did not sin in writing some things as he remembered. For he had one intention: to omit none of the things he heard and to make no false statements in them.'"

Papias was born around 60 to 70 AD, during the apostolic generation, and the Elder he quotes is John the Elder, who knew the apostles directly. Papias is reporting a tradition from someone who had direct contact with the eyewitnesses themselves, and the tradition says Mark's Gospel preserves Peter's own preaching — accurate but not in order, meaning not arranged according to a rhetorical scheme but following the flow of Peter's teaching as the occasion demanded. The picture this gives us is not of Gospels assembled by anonymous communities from floating traditions. It is of Gospels rooted in specific individuals — named eyewitnesses — whose presence in the narrative and whose availability for consultation guarded the tradition against the kind of legendary development critics have assumed.

John's Gospel presents another dimension of the question. It refers repeatedly to the disciple whom Jesus loved, an unnamed figure present at the Last Supper, at the cross, at the empty tomb, clearly distinguished from Peter throughout the narrative; and the Gospel ends with a claim that this disciple is the source of its testimony.

This is the disciple who is testifying to these things and has written them down, and we know that his testimony is true.

John 21:24

The early church universally identified this figure with John, one of the Twelve and one of the inner circle. The Gospel's claim to eyewitness testimony is not implicit. It is stated.

Why Differences Between the Gospels Confirm Independent Witness

Opponents of the eyewitness argument note that memory is fallible, that the Gospels cannot be a verbatim transcription of what eyewitnesses said, and that the very differences between the accounts prove they are not straightforward historical reporting. These objections are real, but they do not carry the force their critics intend.

The differences between the Gospel accounts do not disprove eyewitness testimony; they confirm it. Any trial lawyer knows that when multiple witnesses tell the same story in exactly the same words, with no differences, the most likely explanation is that they have coordinated their testimony. It is the independent accounts — different in incidental detail but consistent in substance — that carry the marks of genuine independent witness. The differences between the Gospels are exactly what you would expect from eyewitnesses telling the same story from different perspectives, to different audiences, for different purposes: they agree on the central facts — the crucifixion, the empty tomb, the resurrection appearances, the teachings of Jesus — and they differ in peripheral details, in sequence, in emphasis. This is the profile of genuine multiple attestation, not of manufactured agreement.

What Bauckham's work established, and what the broader case for eyewitness testimony supports, is that the Gospels are not the product of decades of legendary development. They are, at their core, the testimony of people who were there — filtered through the theological and literary purposes of the evangelists, shaped by the communities in which they were produced, but grounded in the actual history of what Jesus did and said. The ground does not give way when you press it.
