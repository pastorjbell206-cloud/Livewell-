Chapter 35

The Bible That Endured Everything

The most systematically targeted book in history outlasted every empire that tried to destroy it.

You do not dedicate imperial resources to destroying a document you consider irrelevant.

The Most Targeted Document in History

Across the span of recorded history, no document has been more systematically targeted for destruction than the Bible. The targeting of the Bible by its enemies is itself a form of testimony; you do not dedicate imperial resources to destroying a document you consider irrelevant, and the campaigns against the Bible have always been predicated on the belief that it is dangerous: that what it says, believed by people who act on it, changes things in ways incompatible with the power structures that wish to remain unchanged.

That assessment, made by the Bible's enemies, is in a strange way a confirmation of what the Bible's friends have always claimed. The enemies understood what the Bible was. They understood what it would do if left alone. They acted accordingly. And they failed.

Diocletian's Monument and Its Rubble

Diocletian, Roman emperor from 284 to 305 AD, issued edicts ordering the destruction of Christian scriptures, and the penalty for possessing them was death. Imperial soldiers conducted raids on churches and private homes, confiscating and burning what they found; Christians who surrendered their copies to avoid death were called traditores, the Latin root of our word "traitor." This is where the vocabulary of betrayal comes from. The surrender of the scriptures was understood, even by those who did it, as a profound act of faithlessness.

Diocletian thought he had succeeded. He reportedly had a monument erected reading: "The Christian name has been extinguished." Within a decade, Constantine had legalized Christianity, and within two decades, Diocletian's monument was rubble.

The pattern repeated. The Albigensian Crusade in thirteenth-century France targeted the Cathar heresy and, in the process, destroyed enormous numbers of manuscripts; the Spanish Inquisition burned texts; Stalin's Soviet Union suppressed the Bible; Mao's Cultural Revolution destroyed millions of religious texts; and in North Korea today, possession of a Bible can bring a death sentence.

The Architecture of Indestructibility: Many Languages, Many Lands

They failed not just in the obvious sense that Christianity survived (though that is remarkable enough) but in the deeper sense that the campaign of destruction left the textual tradition more robust, not weaker. Because Christianity spread across multiple linguistic communities (Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Gothic, Ethiopian), and the scriptures were translated into each of those languages early, no single political campaign could touch all of it. Diocletian could burn Greek manuscripts in Nicomedia and Latin manuscripts in Rome; he could not simultaneously reach the Coptic churches of Egypt, the Syrian churches of Antioch, the Armenian churches of the Caucasus, the Celtic churches of the British Isles.

The geographic distribution of Christianity, which its enemies used as an argument against it (a religion of slaves and barbarians, scattered across the empire's margins) was actually the architecture of its indestructibility. The text was already everywhere. Destroy the library at Caesarea, and the manuscripts in Antioch survive; destroy the churches of Rome, and the churches of Alexandria survive; destroy the monasteries of Egypt, and the churches of Constantinople survive. The multiplicity that developed from Christianity's early spread became, paradoxically, the guarantee of the text's survival.

Ancient Translations as Independent Witnesses

When scholars today reconstruct the New Testament text, they work not just from Greek manuscripts but from the ancient translations: the Latin Vulgate, the Syriac Peshitta, the Coptic versions, the Armenian, the Gothic. These versions, produced independently by different communities in different centuries using different linguistic conventions, constitute independent witnesses to the text. When they agree with the Greek tradition, the agreement is powerful; when they differ, the difference is a data point for scholars to analyze.

The emperors who tried to destroy the book did not understand what they were dealing with. They were not destroying a single library. They were attacking a tradition that had already embedded itself in human communities across three continents, in dozens of languages, copied by hands that did not know each other, preserved by communities that would never meet.

What the Enemies of the Book Understood

Two thousand years after Diocletian erected his monument, the book endures. The monument is gone. The book is the most translated, most published, most distributed document in human history. That is not nothing. It is, in fact, extraordinary, and it deserves to be named as what it is: evidence of a kind that the book's content predicted, that the word of the Lord endures forever, that no weapon formed against it would succeed, that the gates of Hades would not prevail.

The enemies of the Bible have always known what it was. They have always understood the stakes. That understanding, expressed through two thousand years of sustained campaign to destroy it, is its own testimony.
