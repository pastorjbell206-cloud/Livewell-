Conclusion

The Cross and the Color Line

Why the gospel demands more of the church than any secular theory of justice

The cross is the ultimate statement about racial justice.

What the Cross Says and What It Breaks

Critical Race Theory is not the gospel. It cannot save you, it cannot heal you, it cannot reconcile enemies, and it cannot transform the human heart. What it can do is name injustice, identify systems, and call attention to patterns that matter; for that much we can be grateful. But the gospel does something more; it offers what CRT cannot, the power to actually change everything: systems and hearts, the way we treat each other, the way we understand ourselves. And the gospel demands more, too. It does not merely ask us to see injustice; it asks us to dismantle it. It does not merely ask us to acknowledge racism; it asks us to lay down our lives against it.

At the cross, a Roman instrument of execution becomes an instrument of redemption; a condemned criminal is executed in the most shameful way the empire could devise; and all the hierarchies of Rome (power, status, culture) are exposed as illusion. Consider who is crucified. The God-man, Jesus: the one who has all authority, the one who is equal with God, the one deserving of worship and power. He is the one nailed up to die.

Now consider who is saved. Not the powerful: the Gospels record that the powerful mocked Jesus on the cross, that the religious leaders, the people in authority, rejected him. The ones saved are the thieves and the women, the gentiles and the outcast, the people the system had already condemned, the people the law had already thrown away, the people who held no status, no power, no claim on the kingdom. That is the cross: the ultimate reversal, where the most powerful becomes the most powerless, the most righteous becomes the most condemned, the judge becomes the judged. And through that shame, redemption comes; through that death, life comes.

So the cross breaks every hierarchy: the hierarchy of race, the hierarchy of power, the hierarchy of status. Paul makes the claim explicit.

As many of you as were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ. There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female; for all of you are one in Christ Jesus.

Galatians 3:27-28

The barriers are broken: not in theory, not in some distant spiritual realm, but in practice, in the church, in the body of Christ. If the church really is that body, then it should be the first place racial hierarchies fall, the place where the most marginalized person carries as much voice, as much value, as much power as the most privileged. This is not a political statement. It is a theological one. It is what the gospel means.

What the American Church Has Done Instead

Instead of embodying what the cross breaks, the American church has reinforced it. It has maintained racial hierarchy, kept the most marginalized in the back, and handed power and voice and resources to the privileged. Worse, it has done this theologically: building doctrine that justifies injustice, that tells the oppressed to be patient, that assures the privileged their privilege is no responsibility of theirs. This is not the gospel. This is a denial of it.

The Gospel's Radical Demand Beyond Justice

Here is what makes the gospel more demanding than any secular theory: it does not just require that systems change: it requires that we change. CRT can call for systemic change and demand that structures be dismantled, but it cannot demand that we love our enemies, forgive those who have harmed us, or lay down our lives for those we have hurt. The gospel can. The gospel does.

Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who spitefully use you and persecute you.

Matthew 5:44

This is not political wisdom and it is not strategy; it is subversion. It is the demand that you would actually prefer the good of the person you have hurt over your own comfort, that you would work for their liberation, not because it benefits you, but because they are God's beloved.

Greater love has no one than this: to lay down one's life for one's friends.

John 15:13

And Jesus says this to the very people he is about to die for, the people about to betray him, the people who will benefit from his death: the faithful, yes, but not only the faithful. The betrayer too. The denier too. The mockers too. The gospel demands everything: that you become a new creation, that your self-interest dies, that you are willing to sacrifice your own advantage for the sake of the other. That is what reconciliation requires, not just justice, but justice plus a willingness to die for the enemy.

And this is exactly what the American church has become too comfortable to offer. It has grown too invested in keeping power, too willing to use the gospel to bless the status quo. To offer true reconciliation, it would have to give up power, redistribute resources, listen to people it has harmed, change structures that have benefited it, repent in ways that cost something, and actually become the body of Christ. Instead it offers diversification, programs, statements of support: everything except what would require real change. But change is what reconciliation requires. Real change. Costly change.

The Vision That Is Possible

And yet the gospel's vision is possible: not easy, not quick, but possible. There are churches already doing this work: congregations where people of different races gather as family, leadership teams where power is genuinely shared, communities where resources are actually distributed, places where the cross is breaking down the walls. These are not the mega-churches or the famous ones. But they exist, and they point to what could be: a church that actually believes the gospel, that practices reconciliation instead of merely preaching it, where the cross has its real power, breaking every hierarchy and making new what was divided.

So the prophetic task for the American church right now is not to defend itself against CRT; it is to offer something better. To offer a vision of justice rooted in God's character rather than human theory, a vision of reconciliation rooted in the blood of Christ rather than human ability to forgive. To say to the culture: you are right that something is wrong, you are right that systems are broken, you are right that injustice is embedded in our institutions, but we offer you more than a critique of the systems. We offer transformation, the power to actually change, reconciliation that is real: not a surface-level agreement to disagree, but actual healing. This is what the gospel offers. This is what the church could offer, if the church believed it.

What that requires is a fundamental shift in how white Christianity understands itself. It must admit: we have gotten this wrong. We defended slavery, we maintained segregation, we built theology to justify injustice, we stayed comfortable while the marginalized suffered, we refused to repent in ways that cost. That admission is hard; it requires humility; it requires being willing to be wrong. But until white Christianity makes it, white Christianity cannot move forward, and it cannot offer the gospel's vision of reconciliation.

The hope this book offers, then, is not easy hope. It is not the hope that says everything will be fine now that we are paying attention to race, nor that systemic change will be quick, nor that reconciliation will be comfortable. It is costly hope: the hope that says the work of justice is hard and will take generations, that reconciliation requires sacrifice and will cost us something, that we will make mistakes and will have to keep repenting. But it is also real hope, because the gospel is real, because the cross is real, because Christ has already paid the ultimate price. The gospel does not promise that justice will be easy; it promises that justice is possible, that enemies can become family, that what is divided can be reconciled. Not cheap hope. Costly hope. The only hope worth believing in.

The Call: Will the Church Actually Believe the Gospel?

So the call to the American church comes down to a single question: will you actually believe the gospel? Will you actually dismantle systems of injustice, or maintain them? Will you actually listen to voices you have ignored, or keep speaking for others? Will you actually share power, or perform diversity while keeping control? Will you actually repent, changing your mind and your behavior, or pretend you did not know what you did? Will you actually lay down your life for the other (for the person of color you have harmed, for the people you have excluded, for the communities you have marginalized) or will you ask them to accept your comfort as reconciliation? Will you actually become the body of Christ, or remain a white institution with a cross on top?

The gospel's demand is clear. Its vision is clear. Its power is real. The question is what the American church will do with it, and that question will determine whether Christianity in America is prophetic or complicit, whether the church is a sign of the gospel or a contradiction of it, whether we point the world toward the cross or away from it. The answer is not yet written. The work is still to be done. The time is now. Will you do it?
