Chapter 12

Building a Church That Does Justice and Loves Mercy

Moving a congregation from reaction and diversity to justice and shared power

For years, the American church has been reactive on race. The culture shifts, and the church scrambles to respond; someone launches an initiative, and the church either opposes it or adopts it without thinking; critical race theory arrives in the public square, and the church panics. This is not leadership. It is reaction, and underneath the reaction is fear.

What if the church led on racial justice instead of reacting to it?

What if the church, grounded in Scripture and in the gospel, offered a vision of justice and reconciliation more searching than anything the culture was offering? That is possible. But it requires a fundamental shift in how churches actually operate, and that shift begins by refusing to confuse a feeling with a structure.

What Justice Actually Looks Like in a Local Church

Justice is not abstract, and it is not a social policy you adopt and then file away; justice is how a church actually operates day to day. It is whom you hire, how you distribute resources, whom you listen to, and whom you make decisions alongside. A church that does justice does five concrete things, and none of them are optional.

First, it has diverse leadership — not diversity for its own sake, but leadership that genuinely reflects the community the church sits inside. If your church is in a diverse neighborhood and your leadership is all white, something is wrong, and naming it is the beginning of honesty. This means actually recruiting, hiring, and ordaining people of color; it means paying them equally; it means trusting them with real power rather than handing out token positions. The work is hard because it requires admitting you have been wrong about who is qualified to lead, it requires white leaders stepping aside to make space, and it requires resources to support the transition. But for justice it is non-negotiable.

Second, it listens to voices it has long ignored. For years, white churches have preached about what Black people need from the church, what they should do, and what they should accept. Justice requires actually asking — not in token listening sessions where the conclusions are already decided, but in genuine listening where you remain open to being changed. Ask Black members what would make them feel valued, what barriers they face, and what they think of the church's response to racial injustice. Then listen. Believe what they say. Change in response.

Third, it uses its resources to support justice, because money talks and a budget reveals what a church truly values. A church that funds new facilities but not the cause of racial justice is saying something out loud whether it means to or not. Justice requires redistributing resources — funding Black churches, supporting Black pastors through education and mentorship, investing in Black communities, and paying Black staff equally and generously. This costs something, and that is the point: real justice costs.

Fourth, it changes systems, because changing individual attitudes is not enough. If a church has been segregated for a hundred years, it cannot simply announce that everyone is welcome now; it has to actively dismantle the systems that produce segregation, asking why the church is segregated, what maintains that segregation, and what integration would actually require. Sometimes that means changing where you meet, sometimes the style of worship, sometimes the very theological grid you have been teaching. All of these changes cost. All of them create discomfort. All of them are necessary.

Fifth, it teaches the full history. Sunday school and Bible studies cannot offer only the comfortable past; they must teach the past that is true. What did your denomination do during slavery — defend it or oppose it? During segregation — maintain it or resist it? During the civil rights movement — stay silent, oppose, or support? Teach the full truth, and if the truth is uncomfortable, that discomfort is the point. The truth should unsettle you. The truth should call you to repentance.

The Difference Between Diversity and Justice

Here is a distinction many churches miss: diversity is not the same as justice. Diversity means you have people of different races present in your church, which is good — but diversity can be purely cosmetic. You can have Black members in the congregation while maintaining an all-white leadership; you can have Black people in the pews while treating them as less than.

Justice means that power, resources, and voice are genuinely shared. It means people of color are not merely represented but actually listened to and trusted; it means resources are distributed in ways that address historical inequities. You can have diversity without justice, and many churches do — Black faces in the congregation and in the marketing materials while the structures of injustice stay intact. But you cannot have justice without diversity. If only white people hold positions of power, if only white perspectives shape decisions, if only white people control the resources, then whatever you have, it is not justice.

The Gospel Vision for Multiethnic Community

What would a local church look like if it actually lived the gospel's vision of community? It would be a place where people of different backgrounds could gather without anyone being pressed to assimilate into someone else's cultural form, where difference was celebrated as a gift from God. It would be a place where the wealthy served alongside the poor, where power was shared rather than hoarded, where decisions were made together rather than imposed.

It would be a place where the history of injustice was acknowledged, where repentance was real and restitution was attempted; a place where the prophetic tradition was alive, where injustice was named and comfort was not the goal. It would be a place where reconciliation was possible — not easy, but possible — where people once divided by injustice could become family again. This is the vision of the book of Acts, where Jews and Gentiles gathered together, where people from different nations understood one another as family, where resources were shared and the barriers that had divided people were broken down. This is not an outdated vision. It is one the church desperately needs to recover.

The Prophetic Role of the Church

The church's role in the culture should be prophetic, which means it speaks truth the culture does not want to hear; it names injustice when the culture is comfortable, and it calls for justice when the powerful do not want to change. For too long the American church has been captured by its culture instead — baptizing American values, wrapping the flag and the cross together, making it possible to be Christian while maintaining injustice.

A prophetic church cannot do this. It must say plainly that the injustice is wrong, the system is broken, this must change. But the prophetic church does not stop at naming the problem; it offers an alternative vision, saying here is what justice looks like, here is what reconciliation looks like, here is what community could be. That is the church's unique contribution — not merely political activism or social commentary, but an alternative community that models what becomes possible when people actually believe the gospel.

Starting Where You Are — and Counting the Cost

You do not have to be a mega-church or a major denomination to do this work; you can start where you are. If you are a pastor, you can begin by telling the truth about your denomination's racial history, by inviting voices you have not listened to before, and by asking hard questions about your own church's systems. If you are a member, you can ask your leadership why the leadership team is all white, why most of the giving goes to building maintenance rather than racial justice, and why people of color in the congregation are not being heard.

If you are a church planter, you can plant a church with integration as a core value from the very beginning, building structures of shared power from the start and making clear that this is a congregation where people of all races are not just welcome but fully valued. If you are a seminary student, you can demand that your seminary teach the full history of Christianity and race, that the faculty include voices from the Black church tradition, and that racial justice sit at the center of theological education rather than at its margins. The work does not require permission from denominational leadership or the approval of powerful institutions. It requires commitment.

Let me be honest: doing justice in a local church will cost you something. It will cost you comfort, because you will have difficult conversations and hear painful truths about your own complicity. It will cost you members, because some people will leave a church they judge too political, too focused on race, too willing to challenge the status quo. It will cost you resources, as money once spent on maintenance or salaries goes toward justice initiatives, diverse staff, and Black communities. And it will cost you power, because if you have been accustomed to holding it, sharing it means having less — and that is uncomfortable. Real justice costs. If your commitment to justice costs you nothing, it is not real.

But here is what justice also offers: integrity — the possibility of actually living what you say you believe, and the possibility that your church might become a glimpse of the kingdom of God, a place where people of all races and backgrounds gather as family.

The Hope

The hope is not that the American church will do racial justice perfectly. We will make mistakes, move imperfectly, and struggle. The hope is that the American church could be prophetic — that it could lead on racial justice instead of lagging behind, that it could offer the culture a vision of reconciliation deeper than the culture can offer on its own.

The gospel has power to reconcile, power to transform, power to make enemies into family. A church that actually believes the gospel and actually practices it could be a sign of that power; it could show the world what becomes possible when people take the cross seriously — when they truly believe that Christ has broken down the wall between us. That is the hope. That is what is possible if the American church will do the hard work of justice and the deeper work of reconciliation.
