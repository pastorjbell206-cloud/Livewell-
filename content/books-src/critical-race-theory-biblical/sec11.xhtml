Chapter 10

Racial Reconciliation Is Not Enough

Why the Bible demands justice before unity, and what cheap reconciliation costs

A particular problem has shadowed the American church's approach to racial reconciliation, and it is not a problem of insufficient goodwill. It is a problem of order. We have tried to reconcile without first doing justice, and the attempt has produced something that wears reconciliation's face without its substance. The church has said: let us simply love each other, simply be brothers and sisters in Christ, simply lean on our common faith and move past the division of race.

Reconciliation built on injustice is not reconciliation. It is complicity.

This is cheap reconciliation, and it is not biblical. The Scriptures are clear that justice must precede peace; a reconciliation built on unaddressed injustice is not reconciliation at all. It is complicity wearing the language of grace.

What Cheap Reconciliation Looks Like

Cheap reconciliation is what happens when the person who has been harmed is asked to forgive before the person who did the harm confesses and makes restitution. Consider a marriage: if one spouse has been unfaithful, cheap reconciliation sounds like, "I did wrong, but I don't want to talk about it; let's just move forward, let's just focus on loving each other now." The unfaithful spouse refuses the work of confession and restitution, while the faithful spouse is asked to forgive without ever receiving an apology. This does not work in a marriage. It does not work in a nation, and it does not work in a church.

Cheap reconciliation in race sounds much the same: let us not focus on what happened in the past, let us just be united now, let us be colorblind, let us not bring up old divisions. Underneath the gentle phrasing, this asks Black Christians to set aside the injustice they have experienced: to forgive without the white church confessing and changing, to pretend that centuries of slavery, segregation, and marginalization do not weigh on the present. This is not reconciliation. This is silencing. It becomes a way for those in power to preserve the status quo while claiming to want unity.

The Prophetic Demand for Justice First

The prophets refuse the shortcut. Their witness is consistent: justice must come before peace, and the order cannot be reversed without falsifying both. Isaiah names the sequence directly.

Justice will dwell in the desert and righteousness will live in the fertile field. The fruit of that righteousness will be peace; its effect will be quietness and confidence forever.

Isaiah 32:16-17

Notice the order he gives. Justice first, then righteousness, then peace, and only then the quietness and confidence that follow. Reverse it, and what you produce is not peace but a false peace, oppression with a friendly face. Amos presses the same vision into a single image.

Let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream!

Amos 5:24

Amos describes a movement in which justice is the primary thing, rolling on like a river, not a peaceful coexistence negotiated around the wound, but justice itself as the current. Micah reduces the requirement to its essentials.

What does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God.

Micah 6:8

Even here, justice stands first and mercy follows; you cannot arrive at mercy by stepping over justice. The prophetic tradition will not be moved on this point. Any peace not founded on justice is a false peace.

Reconciliation Without Repentance Is Cheap Grace

Dietrich Bonhoeffer drew the line between cheap grace and costly grace. Cheap grace is grace without discipleship, grace without repentance, grace without cost, the grace that says you are forgiven and may remain exactly as you were. Reconciliation without repentance is cheap grace applied to race, and naming it that way clarifies what is actually missing.

True reconciliation, by contrast, has a shape. The person who did the harm confesses it. The person who did the harm takes steps to change the structures that produced it. The person who did the harm makes restitution where restitution is possible. The person who was harmed receives a genuine apology and compensation. And both parties commit to the new relationship that follows. Only when these steps are taken does reconciliation become real.

The American church has wanted to skip the first four and arrive at the fifth: reconciliation without the labor of justice. When the white church says to the Black church, "Let's just be reconciled," without confessing what was done, without altering its structures, without making restitution, the Black church rightly hears something else underneath: a request to fall silent about injustice and pretend all is well so that the powerful can feel better about themselves. That is cheap grace. It is not the gospel.

What Justice Actually Requires: Reckoning, Change, Repair

Justice in the wake of racial harm asks for several concrete things, and the first is honest historical reckoning. The white church must tell the full story of what it did, not the sanitized version but the whole of it: the segregation academies, the defense of slavery, the silence during the civil rights years, the ongoing segregation of institutions and leadership. Many white churches will not do this; many have rewritten their own history to minimize their complicity, telling stories of how progressive and ahead of their time they were. The record does not support those stories. Honest reckoning means telling the truth even when the truth is uncomfortable.

The second requirement is institutional change. Changing hearts is not enough if the structures stay intact, because a church can feel sincere regret over its racial history while keeping all-white leadership, all-white staff, and all-white pews. Justice asks for actual structural change: hiring Black leaders, investing in Black communities, dismantling the systems that keep congregations segregated.

The third is resource redistribution. The wealth of the white church was built, in part, by benefiting from systems of injustice, and justice therefore asks that resources be redistributed: funding Black churches, supporting Black businesses, working to close the wealth gap. This will cost something, because real justice costs. If your reconciliation costs you nothing, it is not reconciliation. It is performance.

The fourth is changed power dynamics. As long as white people remain in power and Black people remain at the margins, there is no reconciliation; real reconciliation requires that power be shared. Black voices must not merely be heard but trusted to make decisions, and Black leaders must be actual leaders rather than consultants brought in to add color to a leadership that remains white.

The Danger of Premature Reconciliation

Premature reconciliation is dangerous precisely because it forecloses the deep healing it claims to offer. Reconcile before doing justice, and you reconcile around the injustice rather than through it; the wound stays embedded in the relationship. Later the injustice resurfaces; the one who was harmed feels the harm again, and the one who was never held accountable feels entitled to keep his power. Reconciliation that comes too early is reconciliation that will not hold.

The South Africa Model and the Gospel's Vision

South Africa offers a crucial example of what real reconciliation demands. After apartheid, the country could have chosen simple retribution, killing the perpetrators, or premature reconciliation, simply moving on without addressing the past. It chose neither. Through the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, perpetrators of apartheid violence admitted what they had done, victims heard those admissions, and communities began the work of healing; some perpetrators were held accountable, and some were granted amnesty on the condition of full confession. The process was not perfect, but it attempted to hold justice and reconciliation together: truth-telling and accountability on one hand, the possibility of moving forward together on the other. The American church has attempted nothing of the kind. It has wanted reconciliation without truth, without accountability, without cost. Until that changes, reconciliation will not be real.

And yet the gospel does offer genuine reconciliation, reconciliation built on the foundation of justice, not in place of it. Jesus teaches that if you bring your offering to the altar and remember that your brother has something against you, you are to leave the offering and go be reconciled first.

Therefore, if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother or sister has something against you, leave your gift there in front of the altar. First go and be reconciled to them; then come and offer your gift.

Matthew 5:23-24

The order matters: reconciliation comes before the offering. But notice who is told to move. You are the one who has done wrong, and you must go, you must initiate, you must make it right. Paul writes to Philemon about Onesimus in the same key. He does not ask Philemon merely to forgive and move on; he asks him to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a beloved brother." Paul is asking for a fundamental change in the relationship: no more slave-master dynamic, now brother and brother. That is reconciliation built on justice: the injustice of slavery is ended, the relationship is remade, and only then does reconciliation become possible.

The Work the American Church Must Do

If the American church wants genuine racial reconciliation, the work in front of it is plain. It must tell the full truth about its racial history. It must change its structures so that Black people hold real power and voice. It must redistribute resources to close racial wealth gaps. It must support Black-led churches and institutions without trying to control them. It must make reparative justice a priority rather than an afterthought. And it must accept that reconciliation will take generations, not years.

This is the costly work, and it is the only work that actually produces reconciliation. Without it there will be no reconciliation, only racial management, only the appearance of progress while the injustice quietly persists. The gospel demands justice; it demands that the one who has wronged others do the work of restitution and change, and that reconciliation be built on that foundation. Until the American church does this work, the American church is not reconciled. It is still divided. And the division is still rooted in injustice.
