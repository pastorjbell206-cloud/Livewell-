Chapter 6

Where CRT Gets It Right

The observations about American systems that Scripture also makes

Critical race theory is not the gospel; it cannot transform a human heart, and it does not offer reconciliation. But on one narrower question it deserves a careful hearing: some of what its scholars observe about how American systems function is accurate, and several of those observations are ones the Bible makes too. A Christian can disagree with CRT's ultimate solutions while acknowledging where its diagnosis is correct. That is not capitulation. That is honesty.

How Systems Embed Power: Law, Housing, and Criminal Justice

CRT scholars observe that institutions (law, education, economics, criminal justice) are not neutral; they were built by people in power, built to serve people in power, and they continue to serve people in power. This is not a subversive claim but a reading of the historical record. American law was written by men, and it protected male property rights and male political power. That is not judgment. That is history.

The pattern repeats across the institutions that shaped American life. American criminal law was written by people who benefited from slavery, and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery except as punishment for crime, a clause that left the law open as an instrument of racial control, which it has been ever since. American housing policy was written by people who set out to segregate the country by race; the FHA's redlining was not accidental but deliberate policy, designed to keep Black families out of white neighborhoods.

Look at the history of these institutions, CRT scholars say, and you will see that they were designed by people in power to serve people in power; even where the language has since changed, the function remains. This is correct. You can read the record, trace how the institutions were built, and follow the continuities from the past into the present.

The Bible teaches something similar. The prophets looked at the institutions of their time and asked who benefits and who is harmed. When Amos looked at the law courts of Israel, he saw that the poor could not get justice: the law was tilted toward the wealthy. When Isaiah looked at the kings of Israel, he saw them stealing land from the poor, the economic structures arranged to enrich the rich and impoverish everyone else. CRT scholars are doing what the prophets did: asking whether the institutions that claim neutrality actually serve justice or injustice.

Lived Experience as a Source of Knowledge

CRT scholars hold that people who have experienced injustice carry legitimate knowledge about how injustice works. A Black person stopped by police again and again knows something that data collection cannot fully capture; a person denied a mortgage knows something about housing discrimination that statistics do not convey. This, too, is biblical. The psalms are full of people crying out because they have suffered injustice, and God calls his people to listen.

Defend the weak and the fatherless; uphold the cause of the poor and the oppressed.

Psalm 82:3

How do you come to know the cause of the poor and the oppressed? By listening to them, by treating their testimony as a primary source of knowledge. Proverbs makes the same point about the limits of a single account:

The first to speak in court seems right, until someone comes forward and questions him.

Proverbs 18:15

You do not fully understand an issue until you have heard from the people it affects. A man who has never been denied a mortgage does not understand housing discrimination the way someone does who has been turned away repeatedly; a white person who has never been stopped for "matching a description" does not understand police profiling the way a Black person does. None of this means individuals cannot grasp systemic injustice; of course they can. It means lived experience is a legitimate form of knowledge. Not the only form, but one that belongs in the conversation.

Here the American church has failed. White churches have made decisions about race without adequately listening to Black Christians; white pastors have preached about racial justice without consulting Black pastors; white theologians have written about race without engaging Black theological traditions. CRT is simply saying: listen to the people who have experienced the injustice you are trying to address. That is also what Scripture says.

Interest Convergence: When Majority Groups Choose Change

Derrick Bell made an observation he called interest convergence. He looked at Brown v. Board of Education, the Supreme Court decision that declared school segregation unconstitutional, on its face a clear victory for racial justice, the Court announcing that segregation is wrong. But Bell noticed the timing: Brown came down precisely when America needed to project an image of racial progress to the world. In the Cold War the country was competing with the Soviet Union for global influence, Soviet propaganda kept pointing to American segregation, and America needed to show the world it was moving forward on race.

Brown arrived at the moment when ending segregation served America's interests in that global contest. Bell's insight follows from this: progress on race happens not only because justice demands it, but because it serves the interests of the majority group. Cynical? Perhaps. It is also historically accurate. Emancipation came after a brutal war. The Thirteenth Amendment passed once the North held military victory. The Civil Rights Act passed when the country had been shaken by the assassination of Kennedy and the moral force of the civil rights movement had made silence impossible.

Progress happens when justice serves the interest of the majority group; before that moment the majority resists, and after the moment passes it often tries to reverse what was won. American religious life shows the pattern plainly. The church integrated somewhat during the civil rights era, when the nation was integrating and integration seemed inevitable; but as soon as it became inconvenient, as soon as white flight became possible and charter schools became available, the church re-segregated.

The church did not change its theology about race; its behavior changed because the circumstances changed. The interest convergence ended, and the church returned to segregation. This is not a verdict that people are evil. It is an observation about how systems work: they change when the people in power find it advantageous, and they persist when they keep benefiting the people in power. CRT scholars are making a sociological observation, and the Bible makes a similar one, noting that the wealthy resist justice because justice threatens their wealth.

They hate the one who reproves in court and despise him who tells the truth.

Amos 5:10

The wealthy resist justice. That is what CRT scholars observe, and what the Bible also teaches.

Naming Patterns Without Naming Villains

One of CRT's real strengths is that it lets you name a pattern without requiring that every person caught in the pattern be a conscious villain. A white banker in 2026 is not defending slavery; the banker is probably a decent person. But the banker operates within a system that still carries the weight of centuries of racial exclusion from wealth-building: the banker is part of a pattern. Naming that pattern is not accusing the banker of being racist. It is observing that the system the banker works within perpetuates racial inequity.

This distinction matters because it lets white people have honest conversations about race without having to defend their own virtue. You do not have to argue that you are not racist; you can simply acknowledge that the systems you benefit from have racial histories and continue to produce racial outcomes, and then ask what you should do about that.

The Bible teaches the same distinction, because God judges nations and systems, not only individuals. When God says through Amos, "For three sins of Israel... I will not turn back my wrath," he is not declaring every individual Israelite evil; he is saying the nation as a whole has sinned, that the system operates unjustly. An individual can acknowledge corporate sin without personalizing it as an attack on his own virtue.

The Limits of CRT for a Christian Vision

Having said where CRT is right, let me be equally clear about where its structure has limits that Christianity must transcend. CRT sees human beings primarily as situated within relationships of power: you are what you are because of where you sit in the system, oppressed or oppressor, and that position becomes your essence. Christianity sees human beings primarily as image-bearers. That is your essence. Your status as oppressed or oppressor is real, but it is not who you are. You are God's beloved, and that is where your identity comes from.

The difference shapes what each vision believes is possible. If a person's essence is defined by position in a power system, then changing the system might change the position but not the essence. But if a person's essence is being God's beloved, then transformation is possible: a person can move from being shaped by injustice to being shaped by God's love. This is the gospel's promise. This is what CRT cannot offer.

So the account can be stated plainly. CRT scholars are observing real things about American systems: that systems are not neutral but were designed by people in power and continue to serve them; that the lived experience of marginalized people is a source of knowledge; that systems change when the interests of the majority group align with the change; and that you can name patterns without requiring that every person in the pattern be consciously evil. All of these observations are consistent with Scripture. The difference is that Scripture not only names the systems: it offers power to transform them and power to transform the people within them.

The church does not need to choose between naming injustice and offering transformation. It needs to do both, because the prophets did both and Jesus did both; he named the hypocrisy of the religious system and offered forgiveness to the woman caught in sin. CRT can help us see the systems more clearly. The gospel can help us transform them.
