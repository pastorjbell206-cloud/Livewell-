Chapter 9

Black Church Theology

The prophetic tradition white Christianity ignored — Exodus, Cone, Thurman, and the spirituals

The Black church understood systemic injustice centuries before Critical Race Theory gave it academic language.

What White Christianity Missed and Still Refuses to Learn

There is a theological tradition in America deeper, more searching, and more prophetic than white evangelical theology has any claim to be: the theology of the Black church, a theology forged in suffering, a theology that understood systemic injustice centuries before Critical Race Theory gave it academic language. White Christianity has largely ignored it. White seminaries did not teach it, white theologians did not engage it, white churches did not listen; and in that refusal, white Christianity impoverished itself and turned away the gift Black Christianity was offering.

The Exodus Theology of the Black Church

The Black church understood Exodus as the central biblical narrative: not merely the story of Israel escaping Egypt, but the story of God liberating enslaved people. When Black Christians read Exodus, they read their own story. "Let my people go" was the word, and it was God's word to the slaveholder: let them go, you do not own them, they are God's people, they must be free.

White Christianity had effectively abandoned that theology. It had read the Bible in terms of individual salvation (Jesus died for your sins, repent and believe), which is true theology, but incomplete; the white church had minimized the liberationist element of Scripture. The Black church never abandoned it. The Black church understood that faith without liberation is not faith, that God cares about systems of oppression, that the God of Exodus is the God who breaks chains. This is not radicalism. It is biblical. But white Christianity had so thoroughly domesticated the gospel that liberation felt subversive when Black Christians named it.

James Cone and the Theology of Liberation

James Cone was the first major Black systematic theologian in America, writing in the 1960s and 1970s amid the civil rights movement and the Black Power movement, and he asked a question white Christianity was not asking: what does the gospel say to Black people who are being oppressed? His answer was subversive: God is on the side of the oppressed, God's liberation is the central theme of Scripture, and the Cross is not only about individual forgiveness but about liberation, Jesus's victory over death a victory for the oppressed over systems of death.

Cone said that if the gospel is not good news to the oppressed, it is not the gospel; if Jesus did not come to liberate the enslaved, then Jesus is not the Jesus of Scripture; if the church does not side with the oppressed against the oppressor, then the church is not the church of Jesus. This was too demanding for white Christianity, which wanted a gospel that worked for the middle class, affirmed American values, and did not challenge the systems that benefited white people. Cone offered something different: a gospel that made sense of suffering, that explained why God would allow oppression not to teach the oppressed a lesson, but to call the oppressed to liberation and to call the oppressor to repentance. This is not the gospel white Christianity was teaching.

Howard Thurman and the Spiritual Discipline of Suffering

Howard Thurman, a generation before Cone, was a pastor and theologian who worked with both white and Black audiences, and he wrote about Jesus and the disinherited: what the gospel meant for people who had been excluded and marginalized. Thurman's insight was that the oppressed have spiritual resources the oppressor does not, because the oppressed must face the deepest questions: How do I survive this? How do I maintain my humanity in a system designed to strip it from me? How do I find joy in a context of suffering? In facing them, the oppressed develop spiritual depths the comfortable never reach.

This is not to romanticize suffering. Suffering is not good; oppression is not good. But suffering can deepen a person's capacity for faith, for hope, for love: the oppressed, having nothing else to depend on, depend on God, and having no other comfort, find comfort in prayer and in community. White Christianity, uncomfortable with this truth, largely ignored Thurman, yet his theology is deep, and his charge is plain: listen to people who suffer, for they have something to teach you about faith. This is what the Black church has always done: gathering people who had suffered, and in that gathering creating a space where suffering could be named, where community could be experienced, where God could be met.

The Spirituals: Theology Forged in Suffering

One of the deepest sources of Black church theology is the spirituals: the songs enslaved people created. These are not entertainment. They are theology set to music. "I Want to Go Home" speaks of heaven, but the home is freedom; heaven is not merely spiritual escape but the freedom the enslaved could not find on earth. "Go Down Moses" is a call to liberation, recounting the Exodus story and summoning the contemporary enslaver to let the people go.

"Wade in the Water" tells enslaved people how to escape: follow the North Star, wade in the water so the dogs cannot track you. It is survival theology, practical wisdom, faith that trusts God while taking action. "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot" speaks of a chariot that will carry the singers home, of death, but also of hope: that death is not the end, that there is something beyond this suffering, that God has not abandoned the people. These songs are theology, deeper than most theological treatises because they come from the place where faith has to be real; they come from people who could have given up on God, who could have concluded that God did not care about their suffering, but who instead sang, believed, and testified that God sees them, God loves them, God will act. White Christianity has largely treated these spirituals as entertainment. We have sung them in concert, taught them in school as cultural artifacts, but we have not learned from them; we have not let them teach us about the God who sides with the oppressed.

The Prophetic Preaching Tradition White Churches Lost

The preaching in the Black church differs from the preaching in the white church. White evangelical preaching tends to be expository or topical; it explains the text or explains a doctrine, and it is cerebral. Black church preaching is prophetic: it reads the Scripture in the context of the congregation's reality, names what is happening in the world (injustice, suffering, oppression) and speaks God's word to that reality. The preacher does not stand above the congregation explaining truth; the preacher stands with the congregation, naming their suffering, and calling them to hope and action.

This tradition is biblical. It is what the prophets did. It is what Jesus did when he stood up in the synagogue and read Isaiah.

The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor... to proclaim freedom for the prisoners... to set the oppressed free.

Luke 4:18

Then Jesus said, "Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). He was reading the Scripture into the context of his people's reality; he was saying, God is speaking to your situation right now. The Black church has maintained this tradition, reading Scripture as a living word that speaks to the congregation's actual life, preaching as if it matters, as if God cares about what the people are facing, as if the gospel is not a spiritual abstraction but a concrete word for a concrete people. White churches have largely abandoned this. White evangelical churches preach as if the gospel is timeless and contextless, as if the congregation has no particular reality the gospel needs to address, as if the gospel concerns the spiritual realm and has little to do with justice, systems, economics, politics. This is not biblical preaching. It is domesticated preaching.

What White Christianity Lost in the Refusal

When white Christianity refused to listen to Black church theology, it lost a living connection to the liberating God of Exodus; an understanding of how faith actually works in contexts of suffering; a prophetic tradition that reads Scripture as a living word for the congregation's reality; an appreciation for how the oppressed develop spiritual depths the comfortable do not reach; and a vision of God that is not domesticated, not patriotic, not comfortable, but revolutionary. What it gained in exchange was comfort, respectability, access to power, and the ability to domesticate the gospel to serve the status quo. That was a bad trade. That was a betrayal of the gospel.

The Ongoing Refusal

Even now, even as white Christianity is beginning to acknowledge that it got race wrong, it remains largely ignorant of Black church theology. White seminary students do not study Cone as part of their core curriculum; white evangelical churches do not invite Black pastors to teach about the theology of liberation; white denominational leaders do not consult Black theologians as authorities on what the gospel means. The refusal continues: the white church goes on treating Black church theology as a supplement, as something to learn "about" Black people rather than as a central source of Christian wisdom.

This is racism. Not overt racism. Institutional racism: the refusal to recognize that Black church theologians are authorities on what Christianity should be, the refusal to treat the Black church as an equal dialogue partner. As long as this refusal continues, the white church will remain impoverished; as long as it treats Black theology as supplemental, it will not be fully Christian.

The Prophetic Word

The Black church has a prophetic word for white Christianity, and that word is this: you have domesticated the gospel, turned it into a religion that serves your comfort, made it possible to be Christian while maintaining oppression, created theology that justifies injustice. This is not a new word (the prophets have been saying it to the people of God for thousands of years), but the Black church is saying it specifically and prophetically to white Christianity. The question is whether white Christianity will listen, or whether we will continue to refuse the gift the Black church is offering. The answer will determine whether Christianity in America can be prophetic, or whether it will remain domesticated.
