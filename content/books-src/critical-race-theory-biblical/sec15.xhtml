Deeper background — SBC Resolution 9, the scholars, the Scriptures, and the buried history

The resolution had meant to settle something; instead it clarified what had always been true.

SBC Resolution 9 and the Departure of Black Pastors

In June 2019 the Southern Baptist Convention did something most people never actually read. The denomination adopted Resolution 9, a statement on Critical Race Theory and Intersectionality, and the statement was careful: it affirmed that critical race theory is not to be employed as a tool to interpret Scripture, while also acknowledging that some insights from critical race theory can prove helpful to understanding the history of racial injustice. That second clause—not the first about rejection, but the acknowledgment—was the sentence that started everything. A thousand words of measured theological and institutional language amounted to a single claim: we recognize systemic racism is real, and some of CRT's analytical tools can help us see it, but we do not accept CRT's anthropological structure.

It was an attempt at an impossible thing—to name systemic injustice while keeping institutional distance from the system doing the naming. The resolution tried to have it both ways. Within three years it had fractured the denomination.

What happened in those three years was not that anyone read CRT. What happened is that the term became a sign for something else: for the possibility that white supremacy was embedded in American institutions, for the chance that white Christianity might have to change, for the force that people who felt their power threatened reached for to name the thing threatening them. By 2021 the backlash was visceral. Pastors were writing resolutions against CRT; megachurch pulpits were preaching against it; conservative activists were mobilizing. At its 2021 annual meeting the SBC moved to distance itself from the 2019 resolution, claiming it had been misinterpreted or pushed too far.

Underneath the institutional posturing, something else was happening. Black pastors were leaving. They had never demanded CRT; they had been asking for something far more basic—that the denomination honestly reckon with slavery, with segregation, with the fact that it was founded to defend slavery. They had asked for the integration of leadership. They had asked for resources to flow toward Black churches rather than away from them. When the SBC adopted Resolution 9, some thought: finally, institutional acknowledgment. When the SBC reversed course, some left, concluding that the denomination would acknowledge the problem only as long as the acknowledgment cost nothing.

This was the real crisis—not whether CRT existed, but that the denomination's attempt to manage the conversation had failed to address what Black pastors had actually requested. The SBC wanted the intellectual debate about CRT while avoiding the structural change that justice would require. By 2023 the exodus had grown alarming to denominational leaders. Preachers who had served for decades were walking away; young Black pastors were declining to affiliate at all. The denomination that had built its strength on Black churches—and on the resources those churches generated—was losing the very people who had sustained it.

The resolution had meant to settle something. Instead it clarified what had always been true: the denomination wanted racial justice as long as that justice cost it no power, no resources, no theology—as long as justice could stay an intellectual exercise instead of becoming a structural transformation. The tragedy is not that CRT exists. The tragedy is that a denomination had to be pressed by secular theory even to ask whether its own systems perpetuated injustice, and that when Black pastors asked for change, it took an academic structure gaining cultural traction to make white leadership listen. When that structure finally threatened white comfort, the leadership showed what it had believed all along—that its comfort mattered more than the voices of the pastors who had been asking for change from the start.

Intersectionality, Counter-Narratives, and Interest Convergence

When Kimberlé Crenshaw published "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" in 1989, she was trying to solve a specific legal problem: Black women were falling through the cracks of civil rights law. They could sue for discrimination, but the law required them to prove either race discrimination or sex discrimination; it had no category for a claim that was both at once and reducible to neither. A Black woman fired from a job did not experience racism the way a Black man did, nor sexism the way a white woman did—she experienced a distinct, intersecting harm produced by race and gender operating together. Crenshaw's move was to name that intersection. She was not inventing something new; she was naming something that had always existed. Naming it changed everything.

Intersectionality does not claim that every dimension of identity always matters equally. It claims that identity is never one-dimensional, that systems of power operate along multiple axes at once, and that a person's lived experience is shaped by where they sit in relation to all of those axes simultaneously. The practical implication follows: if you want to understand how injustice operates, you cannot study race apart from gender, class, sexuality, disability, and age, because these reinforce one another. A policy can appear race-neutral until you ask how it affects Black women versus Black men versus white women versus white men—and then the pattern changes.

This is where intersectionality becomes threatening to anyone invested in simpler narratives. It refuses to let people off the hook by claiming that only one form of injustice matters or that they are untouched by any axis of oppression. A white woman can oppose racism while benefiting from gender solidarity with white men who also benefit from racial hierarchy; a Black man can be oppressed by racism while holding patriarchal power over Black women. Systems do not sort neatly—people benefit from some hierarchies while being marginalized by others. Crenshaw's structure is not subversive because it is untruthful. It is subversive because it refuses to allow anyone the claim of innocence, and because of the precision with which it names harm.

The second concept CRT scholars developed was the counter-narrative. Richard Delgado and Jean Stefancic observed that legal systems and historical accounts have always been told from the perspective of those in power: American legal history was written by the judges, lawyers, and white men with access to courts and documents, and their narratives became "the" narrative. Counter-narratives are the stories told by people excluded from that official account. They are testimony. A counter-narrative to slavery is not merely an academic reinterpretation; it is the lived testimony of enslaved people—the accounts they created, the songs they sang, the resistance they organized. The claim is that these are not supplements to history or anecdotes that complicate the official story but sources of knowledge about what actually happened. To understand slavery, you must listen to the enslaved; to understand segregation, you must listen to the segregated.

Here CRT becomes explicitly epistemological—a claim about what counts as knowledge and who gets to be a knower. American law had built a system in which the knowledge of the powerful counted and the testimony of the powerless did not, and CRT scholars insisted this was not objective but a choice, and that different choices would yield different understanding.

Derrick Bell's concept of interest convergence is perhaps the most cynical observation CRT made, and also the most historically accurate. Bell looked at moments of racial progress in America and asked what made the people in power willing to accept them. Brown v. Board of Education is the classic case. In 1954 the Supreme Court ruled that "separate but equal" was unconstitutional, that school segregation was inherently unequal, that it had to end. It was a moral and legal victory. But Bell asked: why 1954? Why not 1944, or 1934? His answer was interest convergence. America in 1954 was waging the Cold War, and the Soviet Union held a propaganda advantage—America preached democracy while enforcing segregation, and its image in the world suffered for it. For America's international interests to be served, segregation had to end.

This does not mean those who fought for desegregation lacked genuine conviction. It means progress arrived at the moment justice served the interests of the powerful—and that once Cold War pressure eased, once the world stopped watching, once integration became inconvenient, the momentum reversed. Bell's point is not optimistic: do not expect those in power to choose justice unless justice serves their interests; plan accordingly. The framework is therefore predictive. You can anticipate that the white majority will integrate schools as long as private schools allow re-segregation, that businesses will adopt diversity initiatives as long as those initiatives cost no profit or power, that the church will acknowledge racial injustice as long as the acknowledgment requires no structural change.

How CRT Differs From Marxism: Race vs. Class

Then comes the familiar allegation: is CRT Marxist? The syllogism runs that CRT came out of the critical legal studies movement, that critical legal studies was influenced by Marxist thought, and therefore CRT is Marxist. This is neither entirely false nor entirely true. Marxist theory is concerned with class; it reads history as driven by class conflict between those who own production and those who labor, and it holds that capitalism inevitably produces oppression and that only revolutionary transformation of the economic system can create justice.

CRT scholars were reading some of the same critical theory, but they were asking a different question. Marxists asked how class determines what is possible; CRT scholars asked how race determines what is possible. And they observed something Marxism could not absorb: you cannot reduce race to class. You cannot say racism is merely a mask for class exploitation, that eliminating class would eliminate racism. The historical evidence refused the claim. In the Soviet Union, under a supposedly classless system, racial minorities were still oppressed. In the United States, white working-class people with no economic stake in racism still supported it, and Black elites benefited economically from systems that oppressed Black people. Race operates on its own logic. It is not reducible to class.

This distinction carried weight historically, because Marxist movements in the United States had often asked Black people to set aside racial grievances for the sake of class solidarity—the logic being that all workers are one, that racial division distracts from class consciousness, that race will resolve itself once class is addressed. Black people repeatedly rejected this. Black communists understood class, but they knew poor white workers would still choose whiteness over working-class solidarity, and that the communist movement would still subordinate Black interests to white ones. CRT scholars made a parallel but distinct claim: race is a primary organizing principle in America, not secondary to class, and injustice cannot be addressed by ignoring race or reducing it to something else.

Here CRT's intellectual contribution comes clear, and so do its limits. CRT is precise and historically grounded about how race works in American systems. It has far less to say about what comes after you name those systems. Not a balanced ledger. A diagnosis. CRT is better at diagnosis than prescription—and that gap is exactly where a Christian account has work to do.

That gap matters, because one danger of CRT's rise is co-optation: a movement of critique can be absorbed into the system it set out to critique. It happened before with liberation theology, which emerged to challenge capitalism and imperialism and then was incorporated into seminary curricula until it became academic, became safe. CRT can be absorbed the same way. Universities offer courses on it; corporations hire consultants to teach about systemic racism; the system claims to be addressing the problem when it is merely containing it. This is why Christianity matters here. A CRT-influenced change without gospel foundation will not be sustained—it will be reversed the moment the cultural moment shifts and it becomes politically inconvenient.

What sustains justice work is conviction—the belief that it matters beyond politics, beyond culture, beyond trends. The Black church sustained the civil rights movement because it was rooted in faith, not in political fashion, in the conviction that God cares about justice. A church rooted in the cross, in the belief that Christ died for the marginalized, can carry the work even when the culture turns against it. This is why Christian anthropology matters. It is not enough to name systems; you have to believe that people can change, that the gospel has power to transform hearts and systems, that reconciliation is possible. CRT cannot offer this. The gospel can—and that is precisely the thing the church could offer that no secular theory can, the thing that would give the church credibility to speak prophetically rather than merely compete with CRT.

The Table of Nations, Babel, Ruth, and Rahab

The Table of Nations is one of the most deliberately boring passages in Scripture, and its boredom is the point. Genesis 10 lists the descendants of Noah's three sons—from Japheth the peoples of the coastlands, from Ham the peoples of Africa and Mesopotamia, from Shem the peoples of the Middle East—and for each it gives the genealogies and the territories. This is not random. This is God establishing that the world is ordered into peoples. God makes nations; God marks out their lands; the diversity is intentional and so are the boundaries.

The very next chapter explores what happens when that diversity is disrupted. Babel is usually read as punishment: God confuses the languages, scatters the people, prevents the tower from reaching heaven—God punishing human pride by destroying human unity. But there is another reading. The people of Babel wanted one language, one civilization; they wanted to eliminate the very diversity Genesis 10 had established as God's creation; they wanted to consolidate power over land, over one another, against God. God's response was not to punish diversity but to preserve it—to restore the different languages, the different peoples, the inability to fully consolidate power into one totalitarian structure. Not the punishment of diversity. The preservation of it against human attempts to homogenize it for power. In this reading God is defending the Table of Nations, and the whole biblical narrative about ethnicity reframes around a single claim: God loves diversity, God ordained it, and when humans try to destroy it to consolidate power, God resists.

This is the context for understanding Ruth. Ruth is a Moabite, a foreigner from an enemy nation Israel had clashed with repeatedly; she has no claim on the people of Israel, no right to be included in their community. And yet the entire book is about her inclusion. Her name ends up in the genealogy of David. She is not merely included—she is honored, her loyalty to Naomi, her hard work, her moral character all celebrated by the text. The message is subversive: the person you thought was outside, the person from the enemy nation, is actually in; the foreigner is the one who models faithfulness.

Rahab's inclusion is even more demanding. Rahab is a Canaanite, and Canaanites are not merely foreigners but the people God has ordered Israel to drive out—the enemy, under the curse Genesis establishes. And yet Rahab is not driven out. She becomes part of Israel, part of David's genealogy, a hero in the story. How? By crossing a boundary, by siding with the people God is bringing in rather than the people God is displacing, by choosing faithfulness to God over loyalty to her own nation. The pattern holds: people from outside are brought in, people from enemy nations are included, the boundaries that seemed permanent are crossed.

The Samaritan woman at the well is the New Testament version of this. Samaritans were the people Jews looked down on; the woman is a Samaritan, and a woman, and a woman with a complicated sexual history—layers of outsider in the Jewish world. And Jesus speaks to her. He asks her for water; he treats her with dignity; he tells her of the living water; he reveals himself as the Messiah to her before he reveals it to his own disciples. The message is consistent across Scripture: the outsider, the foreigner, the enemy, the one from the other nation—these are exactly the people God is interested in, the ones Jesus seeks out, the ones who become central to the story. This is not abstract theology. It is a statement about whose voice matters, whose testimony counts, whose dignity is non-negotiable.

When the American church excluded people from other nations, excluded people who looked different, built hierarchies on ethnicity, it was not following Scripture. It was opposing it. Scripture says that from one man God made all the nations, that God preserved diversity when humans tried to destroy it, that the foreigner and the outsider and the enemy are the ones God is interested in—the ones the law forgot are the ones God cares about. Revelation describes the new creation as including every nation, tribe, people, and language. The church's vocation, then, is to embody diversity, not to erase it: a genuinely Christian community is one where people from different nations actually gather. But gathering is not enough. Gathering while maintaining hierarchy, while maintaining white leadership, while maintaining patterns of dominance is not what God intends. What God intends is genuine multiethnic community where power is actually shared, where different traditions are genuinely valued, where the perspectives of people from every background shape the whole—not assimilation into a single dominant culture, but distinct peoples standing together, united in worship before God. The diversity of Scripture is not decoration. It is the core of what Scripture teaches about who God is and who God cares about.

Buried History: Three-Fifths, Dred Scott, Reconstruction, the Red Summer

The Constitution of the United States was written by men who owned enslaved people, and they had a problem to solve: how to count enslaved people for purposes of representation without granting them the right to vote. The solution was the three-fifths compromise. Enslaved people would be counted as three-fifths of a person for apportionment—determining how many representatives a state sent to Congress—which meant Southern states gained more political power than their white populations alone would justify. Enslaved people would increase Southern power even though they had no voice and no vote. It was genius mathematics for oppression: maximize the political power of slave-holding states without the inconvenience of granting the enslaved any political participation.

Where were the churches—the churches that now claim to be the moral conscience of America? When this compromise was written and debated, the churches were silent. The ones that should have said "this is an abomination" said nothing; the ones that should have mobilized prophetic witness against slavery said nothing; the ones that owned enslaved people themselves, and many did, said nothing. The silence had a cost. It meant one of America's founding documents was written with religious sanction, that Christian communities could point to the Constitution and call slavery legitimate, lawful, accepted by society.

One hundred years later Chief Justice Roger Taney wrote the Dred Scott decision. Dred Scott was an enslaved man who sued for his freedom on the grounds that he had been taken to a free state, and Taney's response was brutal: Black people, enslaved or free, had no rights that the white man was bound to respect. He went further, declaring that slavery itself was not wrong and that the Constitution protected it. The Christian response? Churches in the South hailed the decision; Southern theology doubled down; Southern churches grew more aggressive in defending slavery on theological grounds. The decision gave religious language legal authority, and legal authority gave religious language moral authority—a machine of mutual reinforcement in which the law endorsed slavery, the church endorsed the law, and the law protected the church's right to endorse slavery.

After the Civil War, Reconstruction offered a moment. For roughly twelve years it looked as if America might actually integrate: Black people were voting, Black men were holding office, Black churches were being built, Black communities were organizing. It was not perfect and not without violence, but the arc of history was bending toward justice. Then white resistance mobilized, and the church was part of it. Southern churches had lost property and influence during the war and Reconstruction; they became organizations of resistance, organizing against Black political participation, and Southern theology justified violence against Black communities. By 1877 Reconstruction was over. The federal government withdrew. White Southern power reasserted itself. Jim Crow was coming, segregation was coming, and the churches that had resisted Reconstruction would be the churches that defended segregation.

The Red Summer of 1919 is barely known by most Americans, white or Black, and it should be remembered the way we remember lynch mobs—because it was organized racial violence, the burning of Black neighborhoods, the murder of Black people, and it happened in the summer Americans were supposed to be celebrating victory in the First World War. In Chicago a young Black man drowned after white swimmers threw rocks at him, a white mob rioted, and the riot burned: thirty-eight people died and over a thousand Black homes were destroyed. The mobs were white. The police protected the mobs. The newspapers covered it as a race riot, as though the violence had been mutual. The churches said nothing. The white churches of Chicago said nothing about the violence; they did not open their buildings to refugees from the burned neighborhoods; they did not speak prophetically against the mobs. The silence was compliance. The neutrality was participation.

This is the history white Christianity does not teach its children, the history Sunday school omits, the history seminary students never learn unless they go looking for it. Because it is not taught, white Christianity can claim ignorance—we did not know, we were not part of that, that was a different generation. But you cannot claim the benefits of history while refusing to acknowledge its costs. You cannot benefit from the property, the power, the social position built through slavery and segregation and then claim no responsibility for what built it. The churches of America built their wealth on slavery, defended segregation, stayed silent during violence against Black communities, then moved to all-white suburbs and planted new churches in neighborhoods that had been redlined and kept poor. That is the unfinished history. That is what the American church has not confessed.

And it raises the deeper question the whole book is built on—why CRT arrived now, and what it means that a secular structure is naming what Scripture has named all along. For centuries Black Americans spoke about systemic racism; for centuries Black churches taught liberation theology; for centuries Black voices said the systems are rigged, the law is not neutral, racism is embedded in how institutions work. White America did not listen. White Christianity answered that there was no race problem, only individual sinners; that anyone could succeed by working hard; that racism was ending, so be patient. Then a secular academic structure said the same thing—systems are rigged, the law is not neutral, racism is embedded in institutions—and suddenly white America began to listen, suddenly there were conferences and a national conversation, not because CRT said anything new but because it carried secular cultural authority.

This should be shameful for white Christianity: that we needed CRT to tell us what Scripture had been saying all along, that we were more willing to listen to secular academics than to Black prophets. The cost of continued silence is becoming clear—young Black people leaving the church, the church's credibility destroyed, people rightly wondering whether the church can be trusted on racial justice. But the deeper cost is spiritual. To maintain the systems, to choose comfort, to read Scripture in ways that protect the status quo, is not a neutral choice; it is choosing the culture over the gospel. Scripture itself treats injustice not as the mere sum of individual sins but as systemic—something nations participate in, institutions perpetuate, generations inherit.

When Amos spoke to Israel he was not chiefly addressing individual moral failures but a nation that had built its wealth on the oppression of the vulnerable.

You trample on the heads of the poor as on the dust of the ground and deny justice to the oppressed.

Amos 2:7

That is corporate indictment. When Isaiah stood before Jerusalem he was not asking whether any particular person had committed robbery; he was asking whether the city was a city of justice, whether it protected the vulnerable, whether its courts defended the widow and the orphan—and the answer was no, and so judgment came. The prophets understood what American Christianity has largely lost: a nation can be full of morally upright people and still maintain a profoundly unjust system, because individual virtue and systemic justice are not the same thing. If justice is only about individual morality, then the gospel is only about individual salvation; if justice includes the transformation of systems, then the gospel is about the redemption of all things. The book of Revelation describes the new creation not as an escape to heaven but as the healing of creation—the nations restored, the city rebuilt, justice established, tears wiped away, the mourning ended.

If the church addresses only personal sin, it preaches half the gospel; if it addresses systemic injustice without the power of the gospel, it preaches a gospel without power. The real gospel addresses both: your personal sins matter and are forgiven in Christ, and your participation in unjust systems matters and can be transformed through the gospel. The American church has largely abandoned half of that, preaching personal salvation while staying silent on systemic injustice, building institutions that perpetuate injustice while claiming the gospel. A church that preached the whole gospel would look radically different—it would unsettle comfortable Christianity, demand structural change, require sacrifice, announce judgment and offer reconciliation, and look like the early church, a countercultural movement that challenged every power structure it met.

Unjust systems do not persist because people are obviously evil. They persist because they are normal—the water we swim in, the way things are done. A white child in 1950s America learned that segregation was necessary, that Black people preferred separation, that integration would bring chaos; the child absorbed it from parents, school, and church without ever choosing it. By adulthood the belief feels like truth, rooted in nature rather than in the system, and the adult has acquired a stake in it—a home in a segregated neighborhood, a job in a segregated workplace, membership in a segregated church. When someone challenges the system, the adult meets cognitive dissonance and does what people do under threat to their worldview: searches for reasons to dismiss the challenge, looks for evidence the system is just, reinterprets facts to fit. This is not conscious evil. It is human psychology—and its effect is that unjust systems become durable, defended by the very people who benefit from them.

How do such systems actually change? When they become unsustainable—when the cost of maintaining them exceeds the benefit, when enough people decide the cost is not worth paying. And when those in power face people who refuse to accept the system, who organize, who refuse to cooperate with their own oppression, who build alternatives proving another way is possible. In the American South, segregation persisted as long as Black people seemed to accept it and cracked when they refused—when they organized and were willing to pay the cost. White people did not desegregate because they suddenly became moral; they desegregated because Black people made segregation too costly. The bus boycott cost the city money. The sit-ins disrupted commerce. The march on Washington demanded change.

Dismantling an unjust system takes pressure from the oppressed—organized, persistent, costly resistance, people willing to sacrifice. It also takes some who benefit from the system deciding it is not worth maintaining: the moral courage to say, I benefited from this, and I am going to stop defending it. Justice requires both—the oppressed organizing, and the oppressor choosing to stop participating in their own oppression and the oppression of others. That does not happen automatically. It happens when people of conscience are confronted with what the system does and decide they cannot participate anymore. The gospel is supposed to create people of conscience—people who see the oppressed as fully human, people willing to sacrifice their own comfort for the sake of justice. If the American church offered this, it would not merely talk about change. It would be an agent of it. The time for silence is past. The time for half-measures is past. The time for cheap reconciliation is past.
