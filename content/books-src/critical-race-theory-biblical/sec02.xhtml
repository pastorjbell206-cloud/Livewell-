Chapter 1

What Critical Race Theory Actually Says

A fair summary of what CRT scholars actually claim — before you agree or disagree

Before you can honestly engage with Critical Race Theory, you have to know what it actually says, not the caricature, not what conservative or progressive activists claim it says, but what the academics who created it wrote. Most people have never read a single work by a CRT scholar. They have read Twitter attacks on it; they have heard sermons warning against it; they have seen listicles claiming it is Marxism. They have adopted or rejected an entire intellectual structure without understanding what is in it.

If you are going to disagree with something, you owe it to yourself and to everyone listening to disagree with what it actually says.

That is intellectual laziness. That is not thinking. That is panic. If you are going to disagree with something, you owe it to yourself and to everyone listening to disagree with what it actually says, and that begins with reading the people who built it.

The Origins of CRT: Derrick Bell and the Law School

Critical Race Theory was born in American law schools in the 1980s, and its intellectual father was Derrick Bell, a Black law professor at Harvard. Bell was not trying to create a revolutionary ideology; he was trying to answer a legal question that the formal victories of the era had left unresolved: why, despite the Civil Rights Act of 1964, did racial inequality persist in America?

The conservative answer is straightforward. The law is neutral now, racism is individual, and if you work hard you will succeed; any ongoing racial inequality is personal failure, not systemic bias. Bell looked at the legal record and rejected that account, because he saw patterns: that even after formal laws changed, the racial outcomes did not change proportionally, and that institutions continued to embed racial bias into their structures, not always through explicit rules but through facially neutral rules that nonetheless had racial effect.

This became Bell's central insight: racism is not merely an attitude individual people hold; racism is embedded in how systems work. That is not a sweeping claim in 2026. But it was distinctive when Bell made it in 1980.

Intersectionality Explained: Kimberlé Crenshaw's Contribution

Derrick Bell's work was primarily about race and law. In the early 1990s, Kimberlé Crenshaw expanded the system with a concept she called intersectionality, and her starting point was a simple observation: a Black woman does not experience racism the way a Black man does, nor does she experience sexism the way a white woman does. Her experience is not racism plus sexism; it is a distinct experience created by the intersection of race and gender together.

Crenshaw's broader argument was that people carry multiple identities and that systems of power operate through multiple axes (race, gender, class, sexuality, disability) so that a person's lived experience depends on where they sit in relation to all of those axes at once. Again, this is not inherently revolutionary; it is an observation about complexity. But it became revolutionary when Crenshaw drew the conclusion plainly: if you want to understand injustice, you cannot study race without studying gender, and you cannot study gender without studying class, because the systems are interlocking.

The Core Claims of Critical Race Theory

Richard Delgado and Jean Stefancic took CRT from the law school into a broader cultural analysis, examining how race had been understood in American law, American history, and American culture. Their work identified several claims that together form what people call Critical Race Theory.

The first claim is that race is a social construct, not a biological reality, and this is widely misunderstood. When CRT scholars say race is a social construct, they do not mean race is not real; they mean that the categories white, Black, Asian, and the rest are human inventions, created to serve a function, which was to organize power. The biological reality is human genetic variation. The social reality is that we organized that variation into racial categories and built hierarchies on top of them.

This is historically true. The concept of whiteness, as we understand it, did not exist in the seventeenth century; it was constructed. Irish people were not white. Jews were not white. Mexicans were not white. Over time the boundaries shifted and people moved in and out of whiteness, which is the very definition of a social construct, something human-made rather than natural.

The Bible teaches the same thing. Genesis 10 lists nations, the prophets speak of tribes, and Revelation 7 celebrates diverse peoples; nowhere does Scripture teach that racial hierarchy is natural or God-ordained. If it has been constructed to justify injustice, it can be deconstructed.

The second claim is that racism is systemic, not merely individual, and this is the claim that most threatens the conservative narrative. CRT scholars argue that racism is not primarily about individual prejudice but about power structures embedded in how institutions function. Consider a bank that denies a loan to a Black applicant: the loan officer might have no conscious racial animus, and the bank's lending criteria might be written without any mention of race, but if those criteria privilege credit history, and if historical discrimination created a pattern of Black communities having less accumulated credit history, then the facially neutral criteria perpetuate racial outcomes.

Is that discrimination? Legally, it depends on intent. The bank did not intend racial discrimination, but the effect is racial discrimination, and here CRT becomes more searching than the law allows. The law asks whether you intended discrimination; CRT asks whether your systems perpetuate racial inequality. Those are different questions, and CRT scholars insist the effect matters more than the intent: if your neutral systems produce racial outcomes, your systems are serving racism, whether or not your heart is racist.

The third claim is epistemological: the lived experience of marginalized people is a legitimate source of knowledge. If you want to understand how racism actually operates in America, you should listen to people who have experienced it, because their lived experience is not merely anecdotal; it is data. CRT scholars argue that American law and American policy have largely been designed by people in the majority group without adequately listening to people in the marginalized group, with the result that policy often misses the actual impact of systems on the people they affect.

The Bible teaches this same thing. Psalm 82 says God hears the cry of the afflicted; Proverbs 31 says open your mouth for the mute; the prophets constantly charge that you have not listened to the poor, the widow, the stranger. Their witness matters.

The fourth claim is that the law can be an instrument of racial oppression, and this is obvious when you read American legal history. The law once explicitly protected slavery, created segregation, allowed redlining, the explicit denial of mortgages based on race, and built Jim Crow. But CRT's claim goes deeper, arguing that even after formal laws changed, the legal system can continue to serve racial oppression through facially neutral mechanisms: the war on drugs, sentencing disparities, qualified immunity for police are not explicitly racial laws, yet they have racial effects.

This is not a fringe argument; it is supported by mountains of data. Sentencing disparities between Black and white defendants for identical crimes are real, the racial impact of drug laws is real, and the data is overwhelming.

What Critical Race Theory Does NOT Say

Before moving on, it is worth naming what CRT scholars do not actually claim, because this is where the caricature departs from reality. CRT does not say all white people are oppressors. It does not say all Black people are victims. It does not say racism is genetic or that white people are inherently racist. It does not claim that individual prejudice does not matter. And it does not say that wealth disparities are solely the result of racism, though it does treat racism as one significant factor among many.

The actual claim is narrower: racial categories were created to organize power, systems continue to distribute that power along racial lines, and understanding this requires looking at the structures, not just the attitudes, that perpetuate inequality. That is an empirical claim, and it can be debated, but the debate should be about what CRT actually claims, not about a cartoon version of what someone thinks it claims.

Where Christians Should Listen and Where Christians Must Question

The Bible teaches several things that align with CRT's diagnosis, even if CRT's solutions are inadequate. It teaches that God cares about systems that oppress the vulnerable; Exodus is not just the story of individual redemption but of liberation from an economic system that enslaved people, and the prophets do not merely call individuals to stop exploiting the poor; they call for jubilee, for structural debt forgiveness, for the return of land, for systems that protect the vulnerable.

Scripture also teaches that sin has corporate dimensions. Nehemiah confessed the sins of his ancestors; Daniel confessed the sins of his people; the concept that a people can share guilt for institutional injustice is thoroughly biblical. And it teaches that lived experience matters in bearing witness. Job's friends came to comfort him, but when they heard his testimony they had to reconsider their theology; the disciples had to hear the witness of women before they believed in the resurrection; Peter had to hear Cornelius's witness before he understood that the gospel was for gentiles. When people tell you what they have experienced, you do not get to dismiss it because it does not fit your theory.

These are not CRT arguments. These are biblical arguments. CRT is naming things the Bible already named, which raises an uncomfortable question: why did the American church need a secular structure to name what Scripture already said?

But CRT's structure also conflicts with Christian anthropology in important ways. CRT tends to see identity as primarily group-based and defined by power relationships, so that a person's essence becomes their position in the system, oppressor or oppressed, and society is understood at its core as organized around these power categories. Christianity sees identity as primarily rooted in the image of God: every person, regardless of race or status, bears the divine image, an image that cannot be taken away by power or privilege and cannot be conferred by victimization. My identity is not my racial category or my oppressor status. My identity is God's beloved, redeemed through Christ.

This is not a minor difference; it is anthropological, and it changes how we understand justice. CRT often works backward from systems to see what people are. Christianity works backward from the image of God to see what every person is, and therefore what justice demands. CRT can name systems accurately. Christianity can offer transformation that CRT cannot.

The Point

The point of this chapter is simple: understand what you are debating. Read Derrick Bell. Read Kimberlé Crenshaw. Read Richard Delgado's introduction to CRT. Then you can make an informed judgment about where CRT is right and where it is wrong.

The American church's default position of dismissing CRT without reading it is embarrassing, because it suggests we are so confident in our own worldview that we do not need to understand the worldview we are attacking. That is intellectual cowardice. That is not how people of truth operate. And if you find yourself uncritically accepting CRT as the full solution to racial injustice, ask yourself the same hard questions: have I read the scholars, do I understand their anthropological assumptions, do I understand where they might be right and where they might contradict Scripture?

You owe yourself that work. You owe the person you are speaking with that work. The conversation is worth having, but it is worth having with clarity, not panic. Read the texts. Then think. Then speak.
