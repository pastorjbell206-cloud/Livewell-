Chapter 3

The American Church's Racial History

How American churches defended slavery, chose segregation, and stayed silent on civil rights

You cannot understand the present crisis in the American church on race without first understanding what the church actually did across four hundred years, not what it said it believed, but what it did, and the theology it built to justify what it did. This will be painful to read if you are white. Good; it should be. It should also be clarifying if you are Black, a moment when someone finally says out loud what you already knew.

The history does not go away because we refuse to face it. It lives in the fabric of American Christianity.

The Biblical Defense of Slavery: Stringfellow, Thornwell, Dabney

In 1831, Thornton Stringfellow, a Virginia Baptist minister, published "A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery." Stringfellow was no fringe figure; he was a respected pastor and theologian, and his work became the standard biblical defense of slavery among Southern religious leaders. His argument ran like this: slavery appears throughout Scripture, God permitted it in the Old Testament, Jesus did not abolish it in the New; therefore slavery is not sinful, and to oppose it is to oppose Scripture.

He quoted the Levitical laws about slavery, pointed out that the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob owned slaves, and noted that Paul told slaves to obey their masters; if God had wanted to abolish slavery, Stringfellow reasoned, he would have done so explicitly, and since he did not, slavery must be consistent with God's design. But notice what he did not do. He did not engage the difference between indentured servitude in ancient Israel and the racial chattel slavery that defined America. He did not engage the texts about freeing slaves in the sabbath and jubilee years, nor the ethical vision of Exodus, God liberating enslaved people, nor the prophets' judgment against those who exploited the vulnerable. Stringfellow was selective. He was also effective, and his theology shaped Southern preaching for decades.

He was not alone. In 1856, James H. Thornwell, a Presbyterian theologian and president of South Carolina College, published a report on the religious instruction of enslaved people in which he argued that slavery was a "providential arrangement" of God, claiming the races had different capacities and that slavery was the proper condition for Black people. Thornwell's theology was explicit: Black people were not fully equal image-bearers, and their proper condition was subjugation. In 1864, Robert Lewis Dabney, a Confederate chaplain and theological giant, published "A Defence of Virginia and through her, of the South," arguing that slavery was consistent with Christianity and that abolition was not a Christian cause but a progressive delusion.

These were not marginal voices but the major theological voices of the Southern church, and they shaped what evangelical churches believed about slavery. Nor was the failure confined to the South. Northern churches were complicit too; they grew rich on the back of slavery (financing slave ships, insuring slaving ventures, engaging in the triangle trade) and while they did not defend the institution theologically with the same vigor, they did nothing to stop it, facilitated it, and profited from it. The complicity was universal.

The Curse of Ham From Slavery to Segregation

We have already looked at the curse of Ham, but it is worth seeing how systematically the American church weaponized it. Theologians and preachers took the brief text about Noah cursing Canaan and built an entire racial theology on it, arguing that all of Ham's descendants were cursed, mapping this onto the African peoples, and claiming the curse explained and justified slavery. This reading dominated the American pulpit for two hundred years. Children were raised hearing that God had cursed African peoples to slavery; ministers preached it, missionaries carried it, and it hardened into doctrine.

The curse of Ham was so effective as a justification for slavery that when slavery ended, the curse lived on. If African peoples were cursed by God, then segregation was not a violation of God's design but obedience to it; if they were marked by God, then separate accommodations and separate schools and separate entrances were biblical. The curse of Ham became the theological foundation for two hundred years of American racism. It was completely made up. It had no basis in Scripture. But it was so effective that generations of Christians believed it. That history matters; it is in the bones of American Christianity, and if you do not know it, you do not understand why race is so intractable in American churches.

Slavery's Defenders and the People Who Said Nothing

There were Christians who opposed slavery. William Lloyd Garrison was a Christian abolitionist; Harriet Beecher Stowe was a Christian abolitionist; there were white abolitionists in the churches. But they were a minority, and they were opposed; Southern churches disfellowshipped them, Northern churches soft-pedaled their message, and mainline denominations split over slavery into North and South branches that did not reunite until the 1960s, some not even then.

The historical reality is hard but clear: the American church did not lead on abolition. It lagged. It fought against it. It provided theological cover for slavery long after the general culture had moved against it. This is not a minor thing; it is the defining failure of American Christianity. We had the gospel of liberation, the story of Exodus, the prophetic witness, the cross that broke every hierarchy, and we used Scripture to defend slavery instead. The shame of that should never fully leave us.

Segregation Was a Voluntary Choice, Not Forced by Law

Here is a fact that often gets minimized: segregation in American churches was not primarily forced by law. It was chosen. After slavery ended, the Southern church could have chosen integration, because the law that required segregation in schools, public accommodations, and housing did not require it in churches. They did not integrate. They chose segregation, consciously, and on theological grounds. The theology of segregation held that God made the races different and intended them to remain separate, that integration was against God's design, and that the church should model the separation God intended.

Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal, and Holiness churches alike chose segregation. Some established "colored" churches as a supposedly benevolent way to let Black people worship in their own way, but those churches received a fraction of the resources and remained under white denominational control; the benevolence masked the hierarchy. Black churches themselves were created partly by necessity and partly by choice (a choice to have control over their own spiritual lives, free from white domination) and the Black church tradition became one of the most spiritually vital in American Christianity. But it was forged against the backdrop of white churches choosing separation.

This matters for understanding the current moment. The segregation was not external imposition but internal choice, and the American church chose it; that choice shaped everything: church architecture, theology, worship styles, biblical interpretation, pastoral priorities. To this day, American churches remain largely segregated. That is not accident. That is inheritance.

Billy Graham's Complicated Legacy on Race

Billy Graham is often held up as an example of evangelical progress on race, and he did some things right. In the 1950s, when it was genuinely countercultural, Graham desegregated some of his crusades and invited Black pastors to participate in his events; he did not make a large public stand on civil rights, but he moved in the right direction. He also preached to segregated audiences, held crusades in the South where Black people had to sit in separate sections, and declined to use his massive platform to prophetically confront segregation, preaching the gospel in a way that left the systems unchallenged.

Graham was better than most. He was also not good enough, a transitional figure who pointed the direction the church should have gone without fully going there. What makes his legacy complicated is the use later made of it: evangelicals have pointed to Graham's minimal steps toward integration to claim that evangelicalism was actually progressive on race, using a good man's partial steps as cover for our own inaction while most evangelicals remained segregated. Not honesty. Self-justification.

The Civil Rights Movement and Evangelical Silence

The civil rights movement of the 1960s was, in many ways, a Christian movement. Martin Luther King was a pastor; John Lewis was a minister; James Lawson was an ordained elder; the movement was rooted in theology, in the prophetic tradition, in the Exodus story. So where were the evangelicals? Largely silent. Evangelical churches did not march and evangelical leaders did not speak; some churches actively resisted integration, some pastors called civil rights a communist plot, and some theologians argued that race relations were a matter for individual conscience rather than something for churches or government to address.

The historical record is clear that evangelicalism was, by and large, on the wrong side of the civil rights movement. There were exceptions; John Perkins committed his life to racial reconciliation, some evangelical churches integrated, and some leaders supported the movement quietly even when they would not do so publicly. But the institutional evangelical church, the denominations and the major churches and the theological voices, was silent or opposed during the moral moment of the twentieth century.

That silence had consequences. It signaled to Black Christians that evangelicalism was not their home, that evangelical Christianity was comfortable with white supremacy, and it opened a breach between Black and white evangelicalism that never fully healed. Black evangelicals exist, but evangelicalism as a whole is experienced by Black Christians as a white religion. That is not accident. That is history.

The Unfinished Business

We are now in a moment where the American church is being confronted with this history. Some churches are trying to reckon with it; some are ignoring it; some are saying the reckoning itself is the problem. But the history does not go away because we refuse to face it. It lives in the fabric of American Christianity, in predominantly white churches in neighborhoods that were redlined, in church leadership structures where Black voices are absent, in theological grids shaped by centuries of racial hierarchy.

The question is not whether the American church has a racial history; the question is what the American church will do with it. Will we face it, confess it, repent of it in ways that actually cost us something, or will we pretend it was a different era, a different church, not our responsibility? The prophetic tradition is unsparing here: you cannot move forward while carrying a lie, you cannot be reconciled while denying what you did, and you cannot claim the gospel while maintaining systems built on injustice.

The American church built systems on injustice. It defended slavery. It chose segregation. It remained silent during the fight for civil rights. That history is not behind us. It is in us. The question is what we will do with it.
