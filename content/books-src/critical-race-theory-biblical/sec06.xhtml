Chapter 5

Is Systemic Racism Real?

What Scripture teaches about corporate sin, and what the evidence shows about systems

The Biblical Case for Corporate Sin: Nehemiah, Daniel, Achan

The most common conservative argument against understanding racism as systemic runs in a clean line: the Bible teaches that sin is individual; only individuals sin, only individuals can repent; therefore we cannot speak of systemic racism, only of individual racists. The logic is tidy, and it is wrong. It is a modern individualism read backward into Scripture, because the Bible carries a strong and sustained theology of corporate sin.

A people can be responsible for the systems their ancestors created.

Consider Nehemiah. The rebuilt Jerusalem is struggling; Ezra the scribe reads the law aloud, and as the people hear it they begin to see how far they have strayed from it. They gather to pray, and Nehemiah leads them in confession. Listen to what he confesses.

We have acted very wickedly toward you. We have not obeyed the commands, decrees and laws you gave your servant Moses.

Nehemiah 1:7

Later, in chapter 9, the confession grows more elaborate: "They were disobedient and rebelled against you; they put your law behind their backs. They killed your prophets, who had admonished them in order to turn them back to you." Here is the crucial thing. Nehemiah is confessing sins committed by his ancestors. He was not alive when they broke the law; he did not personally kill the prophets. Yet he confesses these sins as though he had committed them himself.

Why? Because he understands that sin is not merely individual; it has a corporate dimension. A people can be responsible for the systems and patterns their ancestors created, and a person can inherit guilt for continuing them. Nor is this an isolated case. When Achan sinned in Joshua 7, Israel executed not only Achan but his entire household; the sin of one man implicated his whole family, and the judgment fell on the corporate entity, not the individual alone.

Daniel makes the point even clearer. Daniel is a righteous man who has not personally taken part in the sins he is about to confess. Yet in Daniel 9 he prays.

I prayed to the Lord my God and confessed: 'O Lord, the great and dreadful God, who keeps his covenant of love with all who love him and obey his commands, we have sinned and done wrong. We have been wicked and have rebelled; we have turned away from your commands and laws. We have not listened to your servants the prophets.'

Daniel 9:4-6

Daniel folds himself into a collective guilt for sins he did not commit. He stands with his people in their sin and confesses on behalf of the nation, because he grasps that nations and peoples have a corporate existence; the sins of the nation are the sins of its people, not individually but collectively. This is no marginal teaching. It runs through the whole of Scripture. Joshua confesses Israel's sins. Jeremiah speaks for Israel. Nehemiah identifies with Israel's sin. The pattern is consistent.

Corporate Responsibility in the Prophets

The prophets are clearer still about corporate responsibility, and they aim their indictments not at scattered offenders but at the structure itself. Amos speaks to the nation as a whole.

They sell the innocent for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals.

Amos 2:6

Amos is not picking out a handful of individual evil-doers who happened to sell innocent people; he is naming a system. The nation of Israel, as a corporate entity, was complicit in the practice. Isaiah presses the same charge against the laws themselves.

Woe to those who make unjust laws, to those who issue oppressive decrees, to deprive the poor of their rights... What will you do on the day of reckoning?

Isaiah 10:1-3

Isaiah is not merely condemning individual wicked lawmakers; he is condemning the structure, the laws themselves, which are unjust even though they were enacted through legitimate legal processes. The nation that wrote them bears the guilt. Micah turns the indictment toward the leaders.

Hear, you leaders of Jacob, you rulers of Israel... You eat the flesh of my people and tear away their skin.

Micah 3:1-3

Micah names structural violence. The leaders, the corporate power structure, are consuming the people, not one by one but as a system.

The Difference Between Individual and Systemic Sin

Here is what needs to be clear: naming systemic sin does not erase individual responsibility. An individual racist is still individually guilty. An individual who benefits from unjust systems while doing nothing to change them is still individually guilty. But systemic sin is real in addition to individual sin; a system can be unjust even when the people operating within it are well-meaning, and it can perpetuate harm even when no one inside it consciously intends harm.

Take a concrete case. A bank has lending criteria that look neutral on their face, asking only for credit history, income, and collateral, with nothing explicitly about race. But if those criteria systematically deny loans to Black applicants at rates significantly higher than white applicants, then the system is racist regardless of whether the loan officer harbors any racial animus. Is the loan officer individually guilty of racism? Perhaps not; he may be personally colorblind. He is, even so, participating in a system that perpetuates racism, and that system is real.

The prophets saw both levels at once. They condemned the oppressor, the individual who actively exploited the poor, and they condemned the system that enabled the oppression, recognizing that a society can run an entire machinery of injustice even when most of the people inside it do not think of themselves as unjust.

The Evidence: Housing, Criminal Justice, Employment, Education

By now this is not a theoretical question but an empirical one, and the evidence is overwhelming. Begin with housing. The Federal Housing Administration explicitly refused to insure mortgages in neighborhoods with Black residents; this was federal policy, and the FHA literally drew maps marking as "hazardous" any neighborhood that included Black residents. Black families could not access government-backed mortgages while white families could, and over decades that single fact opened a massive wealth gap; white families built equity in their homes, Black families could not, and the gap persists today.

Criminal justice tells the same story. Black people receive longer sentences than white people for identical crimes, and the data are consistent: a Black man convicted of a felony receives a sentence on average twenty percent longer than a white man convicted of the same felony. Are individual judges consciously racist? Some may be. But the pattern is systemic; it is simply how the system operates.

Employment shows it in controlled experiments. When researchers send out identical resumes, one bearing a "white-sounding" name and one a "Black-sounding" name, the resume with the white-sounding name consistently draws more callbacks. Same qualifications, same resume, different name, different outcome. Education shows it in the budgets. Schools in predominantly Black neighborhoods receive significantly less funding than schools in predominantly white neighborhoods, even within the same district; the facilities are worse and the teachers less experienced, and this is not an accident but a feature of how the system is structured.

Policing shows it in the gravest terms. Black people are killed by police at a rate three times higher than white people even after adjusting for crime rates, and they are more likely to be stopped, searched, and arrested for the same behaviors that white people engage in with impunity. These are not individual racist acts. These are patterns. This is how systems work, and the Bible would call it structural sin: a system operating in a way that produces racial injustice, a system that is itself unjust.

Where Conservatives Get Confused — and the Call to Corporate Repentance

Conservative Christianity often objects that systemic racism implies individual white people are guilty simply for being white. That is not what the term means. Systemic racism means that systems are structured in ways that benefit white people and harm Black people, so that individual white people benefit from those systems whether or not they ever consciously chose to.

An analogy helps. If you inherit a house built on stolen land, you are benefiting from an injustice you did not personally commit; you did not steal the land, but you are living in the house. That does not make you guilty of theft. It does mean you carry a responsibility to understand that your house stands on injustice, and to weigh what you owe to the people whose land was taken. Individual white people are not guilty of slavery. We did not enslave anyone. But we benefit from systems built through slavery, maintained through segregation, and still operating in ways that privilege white people and disadvantage Black people. That is not guilt. That is reality.

If corporate sin is real, and Scripture teaches that it is, then corporate repentance must be real too. A nation can repent. A people can repent. A system can be repented of and changed. Nehemiah modeled exactly this. He did not say, "I am not personally guilty, so I will not confess." He confessed on behalf of his people, identified with their sin, and led them in turning from it. This is what the American church has failed to do. It has not corporately confessed its history of defending slavery, maintaining segregation, and standing silent during the civil rights movement. Individual white Christians may have admitted that individuals were wrong, but the church as a whole has not engaged in genuine corporate repentance. That is why the work remains undone, why racial division persists in the church. You cannot move forward carrying a lie.

So the summary holds. Systemic racism is real. Scripture affirms that systems can be unjust, that corporate bodies can be responsible for injustice, and that repentance of systemic injustice is possible. The American church needs to acknowledge that American Christian systems benefited from slavery and segregation; to confess that the church defended these systems theologically; to recognize that many contemporary systems still carry the weight of that history; and to commit to changing those systems in ways that cost something. Not as individuals claiming personal innocence. As a people confessing corporate sin and committing to corporate transformation. That is what Nehemiah did. That is what Daniel did. That is what the prophets called for. The question is whether the American church will do it.
