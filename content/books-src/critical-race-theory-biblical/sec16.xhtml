Chapters 4-6

The church-led civil rights movement, corporate sin, and the policies that built the wealth gap

The march on the Edmund Pettus Bridge was not a political confrontation. It was a liturgical act.

The Montgomery Boycott and Selma as Church-Led Acts of Faith

People remember the Montgomery bus boycott as a moment of spontaneous courage: a tired woman named Rosa Parks who would not give up her seat, a single refusal that sparked a movement. That is the mythology, and it flattens what actually happened.

The real history is that the boycott was organized. Rosa Parks was a member of the NAACP; she had been trained in nonviolence; the protest was planned. The organizers had been waiting for exactly this kind of incident, one that was clear, easy for people to understand, and unentangled with any crime except the crime of being Black in a segregated system. And who did the organizing? The churches. The Black churches of Montgomery gathered, the Black pastors coordinated, and a young pastor at Dexter Avenue Baptist Church named Martin Luther King became the public face. The churches supplied the money, the meeting spaces, and the moral authority. The boycott was a church movement.

White Christianity has largely forgotten this. When we talk about the civil rights movement, we describe a secular social cause that King happened to join; we do not say that the movement was rooted in Black churches, organized by Black pastors, and sustained by church people gathering night after night to pray and sing and strategize.

Selma tells the same story. In 1965, with Black citizens being blocked from registering to vote, the churches organized a fifty-four-mile march from Selma to Montgomery to demand voting rights. They did not march as a political party. They marched as a religious movement: singing hymns, praying, carrying the spiritual tradition of the Black church into the streets.

When state troopers on horseback beat marchers with whips on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, that was not a political confrontation; it was a liturgical act. People were sacrificing their bodies, submitting to violence without retaliating, bearing witness. This is what the Black church tradition understood about resistance: that resistance is an act of faith, corporate prayer made visible, the body of Christ offering itself as testimony. White Christianity largely missed it. White Christianity saw a political act, counted the cost politically, asked whether it was wise, and never grasped that the march was an act of worship.

King's Critique of Capitalism and Militarism

Most white evangelical churches have domesticated King into a safe figure, the dreamer who wanted integration, who was nonviolent, who asked nicely for equality. The real King was asking for something far more demanding. In Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community? he explicitly attacked capitalism, arguing that "the profit motive alone is not adequate to meet the needs of every member of the society." This is the King white Christianity does not teach, the King who threatens the status quo.

He was not requesting minor reforms to help poor people; he was calling for a fundamental restructuring of the economy, arguing that capitalism itself (not merely racist capitalism, but capitalism as such) produced poverty and exploitation. In 1967 he said, "In a sense, you could say we're not even using socialism. We used capitalism in the extreme—the most wasteful nation in the world," and then, "Something is wrong with capitalism... the profit motive alone is not adequate to meet the needs of every member of the society." This is subversive. This is the King evangelicals have had to erase.

He was also deeply critical of American militarism. His 1967 sermon "Beyond Vietnam," delivered a year before his assassination, called America's military adventures in Vietnam morally wrong, insisted that money spent on bombs should be spent on poverty, and demanded "a subversive reordering of national priorities." That was the trajectory he was on when he was killed, moving toward an ever-sharper critique of American society itself, not asking for integration into the American system but asking America to change at its core.

If the real King had lived another decade, what would he have demanded? Would he have marched against American military presence around the world, mobilized for reparations, built international solidarity with liberation movements? We cannot know, because he was assassinated; we can only infer from the direction he was moving.

The tragedy is that King's radicalism was necessary in 1967 and remains necessary in 2026. The American system still produces poverty. The American military is still involved in endless wars. American capitalism still produces inequality that is obscene. If King were alive today, he would not be celebrating our progress; he would be asking why we still have poverty in the richest nation on earth, why we still spend a trillion dollars a year on the military while children go hungry. He would be calling American evangelicalism to account, asking how it can claim to follow Jesus while defending systems that exploit, how it can claim to believe the gospel while defending a capitalism that produces poverty. This is why white evangelicals have had to domesticate him. The real King is too threatening. The real King demands too much.

Corporate Sin From Achan to the Prophets

In Joshua 7, Israel is conquering Canaan. Jericho falls, but the assault on the next city, Ai, goes wrong: the soldiers are defeated, and though fewer than forty men die, the loss is shocking because it is unexpected. Joshua, confused and angry, asks God what happened, and the answer is specific. Someone has violated the covenant. A man named Achan has taken a cloak, silver, and gold from Jericho, things devoted entirely to God.

What happens next is what makes American readers uncomfortable. Achan is found out, and it is not Achan alone who is executed: his sons, his daughters, his animals, everything he has. The whole household is killed for his sin. To modern American sensibilities this is deeply offensive, because we hold a concept called individual responsibility: Achan's sin is Achan's sin, and his family should not bear it. Yet Joshua understood something we have lost: Achan is not an individual but a member of a family, and his sin is corporate; the entire household is implicated.

This is not unique to Joshua. The book of Exodus speaks of consequences reaching the third and fourth generations.

I, the Lord your God, am a jealous God, punishing the children for the sin of the parents to the third and fourth generation.

Exodus 34:7

This is not about individual guilt; it is about how sin works structurally. When a father steals and builds wealth on the theft, the children who did not steal still inherit, and so are implicated. When a nation enslaves people and builds its economy on slavery, the next generation does not enslave anyone, but it inherits the wealth that slavery built, and so it is implicated.

The prophets understood this. When Amos declares, "For three sins of Israel, even for four, I will not relent," he is not naming individual sins but the sins of a nation, the sins of Israel as a people. Isaiah tells the nation, "Your hands are full of blood", indicting not only the individuals who committed violence but the nation that allowed and benefited from it. Micah says to the leaders of Jacob, "You eat the flesh of my people and tear away their skin," naming a whole system that consumes people.

The prophetic tradition grasps corporate sin in a way American individualism cannot. American individualism says each person is responsible for their own actions, and if you personally did not commit the sin, you are not guilty. Scripture says something different: that you benefit from sinful systems, that you belong to a people perpetuating injustice, that you inherit the consequences of what your ancestors did, and that this is true not only spiritually but materially. White America inherited wealth built on slavery; it lives in neighborhoods shaped by redlining; it benefits from an educational system in which schools in white neighborhoods receive more resources than schools in Black ones. This is not individual virtue or vice. It is structural.

American Christianity, having absorbed American individualism, cannot process the prophetic understanding of corporate sin. When a Christian says, "I did not personally own slaves, so I am not guilty," he is reasoning from American individualism, not biblical logic. The biblical logic runs differently: "I benefit from systems built through slavery. I am part of a nation that perpetuates segregation. I inherit the consequences. What do I do about that?" This is the category the American church has never developed. It has no theology of corporate repentance, no structure by which an individual can identify with corporate sin and seek corporate transformation. Until we build one, we can address only individual sin, and individual sin can flourish within the most unjust systems.

Redlining, the GI Bill, and the Racial Wealth Gap

Redlining is not ancient history. It was the explicit policy of the Federal Housing Administration to refuse to insure mortgages in neighborhoods with Black residents. The FHA drew maps; the maps drew red lines around neighborhoods with Black people; those red lines meant "hazardous," and hazardous meant the government would not insure mortgages there. This was not an accident or a market force. It was government policy, a systematic exclusion of Black people from the primary wealth-building mechanism available to Americans in the twentieth century.

White families in other neighborhoods could get FHA-insured mortgages, buy homes, build equity, and pass those homes to their children. Thirty years of mortgage payments created tremendous wealth; a home that cost $5,000 in 1945 was worth $150,000 by 1975, and that wealth transferred to the next generation. Black families could not participate. Blocked from the same neighborhoods, the same mortgages, the same wealth-building, they remained renters, unable to build home equity, and they remained poor. The wealth gap between white and Black families is not the product of white people being more hardworking or more responsible; it exists because government policy explicitly excluded Black people from the mechanisms of wealth.

The church was implicated. White churches sat in the all-white neighborhoods that redlining preserved, benefiting from stable property values, building their buildings in places they knew would stay white and prosperous. Black churches sat in the redlined neighborhoods the government had marked hazardous, the ones where no one would invest, where property deteriorated and people stayed poor.

The GI Bill is another example. After World War II, the federal government offered education and mortgage benefits to veterans, presented as an opportunity for all Americans, but distributed unequally in practice. Universities in the South refused to admit Black students, so Black veterans could not use their education benefits there; Northern universities admitted them, but housing segregation meant they could not live where white students lived, and discrimination limited where they could settle afterward. Mortgage benefits were available to Black veterans on paper, but mortgage discrimination meant banks and sellers would not sell to them, so the benefit stayed theoretical. The GI Bill created a generation of home-owning, educated white Americans; the same benefits, offered to Black veterans, were implemented in ways that excluded them. White wealth increased dramatically. Black wealth did not increase proportionally.

Again, this is not individual choice but policy: systems that look neutral, a benefit available to all veterans, that function to exclude on the basis of race. These are the facts that CRT names accurately, the policies that created the wealth gap, the structures that remain in place because no one has dismantled them. And they press a question onto Christian ethics: if you benefit from policies that excluded others, what is your responsibility? If you live in a neighborhood that exists because others were excluded, what do you owe? American Christianity has avoided these questions, teaching an individual responsibility under which a person who did not personally discriminate has done nothing wrong, while developing no account of structural or systemic responsibility. Biblical ethics would ask different questions: Am I benefiting from injustice? Am I complicit in systems that harm others? What do I owe the people those systems harmed?, and these are not comfortable questions, but they are the questions the prophets ask.

Reading Scripture From Below: King's Hermeneutic

King's theological method differed from white evangelical theology. King read Scripture against culture, asking what Scripture was saying to the situation he and his people faced right now. White evangelical theology tends instead to read Scripture as timeless truth applied universally, asking what a text teaches about doctrine or individual spirituality. King asked what Scripture was saying to people who are oppressed: what Exodus teaches enslaved people, what the prophetic tradition teaches people being crushed by injustice. This is a hermeneutics of resistance, reading Scripture from below, from the vantage of the oppressed, asking what it has to say to them.

When King read "Let my people go," he heard God speaking to enslaved people in America; when he read the prophets condemning injustice, he heard God addressing Black Americans under segregation. White evangelical theology read the same texts and heard something else: that God wanted order, that God wanted obedience to authorities, that God wanted people content with their spiritual condition even when their material condition was oppressive. This was not accidental. It is the difference between reading Scripture from the position of power and reading it from the position of powerlessness. If you have power, you naturally read Scripture as teaching that power is legitimate, that order should be maintained, that your position should be preserved; if you are powerless, you naturally read it as teaching liberation, calling for justice, promising that God will overthrow oppressive systems.

This is why white evangelical theology missed what King saw: it could not read Scripture as liberation because its theologians were not living in oppression but defending the system rather than resisting it. The Black church kept a different hermeneutic, reading Scripture as liberation literature because it was living the reality of oppression. This hermeneutical difference is not a side issue. It is fundamental to what it means to read Scripture faithfully.

The Black church in the civil rights movement was not prophetic because its preachers decided to be political. It was prophetic because it read Scripture as the word of God spoken to its condition. When Black preachers read Exodus, they were not reading ancient history but their own liberation; when they read the prophets, they were not reading abstract theology but God's judgment on the systems oppressing their people. This was not an unusual reading. It is how the oppressed have always read Scripture: the persecuted Christian in first-century Rome read Revelation not as a blueprint for the end times but as a word of hope: the Roman Empire will fall, God will judge oppression, there is a future beyond Rome's power. Scripture did not change; the reader's position did. The persecuted read it as a word of liberation; the powerful read it as a word of order.

This is why the gap between prophetic witness and institutional power matters. A prophet is rarely comfortable inside the institution; a prophet challenges it and calls it to account. But a person determined to keep his position within an institution rarely becomes a prophet; he learns to speak in ways that do not threaten power. That is what happened to white evangelicalism: as it gained platforms, influence, and money, it lost its prophetic edge. The Black church kept that edge because it was never in institutional power; it could not afford to domesticate the gospel; it had to read Scripture as a word about liberation because it needed liberation.

What would it take for the American church to recover prophetic witness? A willingness to lose institutional power: leaders willing to speak prophetically even if it costs them their platform, their income, their influence. Most institutional leaders will not do this; they have too much invested in maintaining the institution. But some pastors and leaders are willing, and though they often face opposition from institutional leaders and lose position and influence, they keep their credibility. They sound like the prophets of Scripture, like people speaking truth to power. This is what the American church needs, not more leaders managing the conversation, but prophetic voices willing to challenge the institution even at the cost of their position.

Naming systemic injustice means naming the persons harmed by it; you cannot speak of systemic racism without speaking of the people who experience it. This is where the personhood of the oppressed becomes decisive. If you see the oppressed as fully human, bearing the image of God, you have to ask what the system is doing to them. But if you have absorbed the system's logic, you may not see them as fully human at all; you may see them as less intelligent, less capable, less worthy, their suffering deserved. This is the power of racism: it does not merely create systems, it creates a way of seeing people that justifies those systems, telling you that Black people are poor because they are lazy and undisciplined. Once you accept that way of seeing, the system looks natural, inevitable, deserved.

Addressing systemic racism requires rejecting that way of seeing: recognizing the oppressed as fully human, fully capable, fully worthy of dignity, and then asking: if this person is fully human and fully capable, why are they poor? The answer cannot be that they are lazy. The answer must be that the system deliberately excludes them, that the system is rigged. This is where individual solutions fail. "Work harder, educate yourself, pull yourself up by your bootstraps" cannot work if the system is designed to exclude you no matter what you do. A Black person can be as educated as a white person and still face discrimination in hiring, can work as hard and still remain poor, can do everything right and still be denied the opportunities given to a white person who does less. Only systemic solutions can address what the system has created, and that is what corporate repentance requires: that those who have benefited acknowledge what the system has done and work to dismantle it, and that those who have been harmed be recognized as fully human and have the system changed for their good.

One limit of CRT is that it is concerned chiefly with material systems and material outcomes. It asks how resources are distributed, who holds power, what the material consequences of injustice are. These are important questions, but they are not the only ones. The gospel asks what living in injustice does to the human soul, what dignity looks like, how broken people can be healed. CRT can show that racism produces poverty and demonstrate its material consequences, but it cannot tell you what it does to a person's soul to be treated as less than human. The Black church knew this. It responded not only to material deprivation but to spiritual violence, helping people keep their dignity, their hope, their humanity in a system designed to strip all three away.

A purely materialist response would say: provide resources, redistribute wealth, and the problem is solved. The gospel knows that is not enough. It knows that the oppressed need healing, that those who have oppressed others need forgiveness and transformation, that reconciliation requires more than economic justice. This is why the cross matters. The cross is God's response to systemic injustice, not only to individual sin, but God standing with the oppressed, refusing to accept that the execution of the innocent is justified. And the cross is also about redemption: the possibility that even those who maintain systems of oppression can be redeemed, that enemies can become family. A purely secular structure cannot offer this. It can identify the problem. Only the gospel can offer transformation.

In the law of Moses, jubilee was mandated. Every fiftieth year, land returned to its original owners, debts were forgiven, enslaved people were freed, and the economic system reset. This was not optional. It was law, what kept Israel's society from hardening into permanent inequality. Without jubilee, the rich grow richer and the poor grow poorer until inequality becomes hereditary: the child born to wealth has wealth, the child born to poverty has nothing and may have to sell himself into servitude, and within a few generations you have a permanent class system. Jubilee interrupted that. Jubilee said you do not get to inherit permanent power over others; the system resets; equity is restored. It is a subversive economic theory, one that assumes God cares about economic justice and that permanent inequality is unjust.

Modern American Christianity has largely ignored jubilee. We do not preach it, do not think about it, have erased it from the theological imagination, because jubilee is incompatible with capitalism. It requires regular redistribution of wealth, requires that some people give wealth up, requires limiting the ability of the rich to accumulate. American Christianity has aligned with capitalism, teaching that the accumulation of wealth is virtuous, that the rich deserve their wealth because they worked hard, that redistribution is theft. This is anti-jubilee at its core. It is anti-biblical at its core.

What would a modern jubilee look like? It would look like reparations, like education funding for communities that have been disinvested, like healthcare access for communities that have been neglected, like wealth transferred from the wealthy to those who have been robbed. It would cost something: the wealthy would lose some wealth, the comfortable would become less comfortable. It would also mean that inequality decreases, that a child born into poverty has a real chance to build a life, that the system resets. Is it possible in modern America? Not if you think politically; the wealthy will not vote to redistribute their own wealth, and politically jubilee is impossible. But theologically jubilee is non-negotiable. Theologically, regular economic reset is what God requires, and permanent inequality is idolatry. The American church has chosen the political calculation over the theological conviction; it has decided that capitalism matters more than biblical justice.

What would it look like for the American church to recover jubilee? It would look like churches giving away resources, funding Black institutions, redistributing wealth to repair what was stolen. It would look like churches resisting capitalism: saying, we will not accumulate wealth, we will distribute what we have, we will practice jubilee. This is what would make the American church prophetic: not just talking about justice, but practicing it, actually redistributing. Most churches are not willing. Most churches like their wealth and have no interest in jubilee. But this is what Scripture requires. This is what God mandates. This is the test of whether the American church actually believes the Bible, or just likes the parts that do not cost anything.
