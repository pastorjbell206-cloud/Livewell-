Chapter 2

What the Bible Actually Says About Race, Ethnicity, and the Image of God

The image of God, the goodness of ethnicity, and the prophets' demand for justice

The Image of God Is Universal and Cannot Be Cursed

If you are going to think biblically about race, you have to begin where Scripture begins: every human being bears the image of God. That is not a sentiment to be admired; it is the premise everything else stands on.

So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them.

Genesis 1:27

Read the verse carefully, because the precision is the point. Not "males bear the image." Not "people of certain races bear the image." All mankind. The imago Dei is universal; it is not earned, it cannot be taken away by circumstance or power, and it is constitutive of what it means to be human at all.

If a person bears the image of God, then every attempt to reduce them to a category is an assault on the image of God himself.

Everything in this chapter flows from that single foundation. If a person does not bear the image of God, if they are somehow less fully human, then hierarchy and exploitation become, in their own cold way, logical. But if they do bear it, then every attempt to reduce them to a category, to extract their labor, to deny them dignity, is an assault on the image of God himself.

This is precisely the foundation the American church tried to erode. It defended slavery by carving out exceptions to the imago Dei: cursed Hamites, they said; natural slaves, they said; less developed, they said: each argument a way of claiming that some humans bore the image more fully than others. Scripture will not allow that argument. The image of God is not partial. Not hierarchical. Not dependent on race, intelligence, ability, or status.

Ethnicity Is Real and Good in Scripture

Here is where the Christian teaching on race grows complicated in the best way: ethnicity is real, and it is good. In Acts 17, standing before the philosophers of the Areopagus, Paul tells the Athenians that "from one man he made all the nations, that they should inhabit the whole earth; and he marked out their appointed times in history and the boundaries of their lands."

God made nations. God marked out their boundaries. God appointed their times. Ethnicity, then, is not a mistake to be erased; it is intentional, woven deliberately into creation. This is why the most-quoted verse on the subject is so often misread.

There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus.

Galatians 3:28

People reach for that verse to argue that ethnicity does not matter, but that is not what Paul is saying. He is not denying that Jew and Gentile are different; he is insisting that in Christ those differences no longer create hierarchy. The distinction remains; the ranking is abolished.

And the final vision of Scripture confirms it, because the end is not a homogenized humanity. Revelation 7 shows "a great multitude that no one could count, from every nation, tribe, people and language, standing before the throne and before the Lamb." Not "no nations": every nation. Not "no cultures": cultures preserved. Not "all the same": a diverse multitude celebrating difference before the throne of God. Ethnicity is real, ethnicity is beautiful, ethnicity belongs to God's design, and ethnicity will still matter in the new creation. What will not survive is ethnicity as the grounds for hierarchy, exploitation, or marginalization.

The Curse of Ham: What Scripture Says and What the Church Invented

The most consequential lie the American church ever told about Scripture was the doctrine of the "curse of Ham." To see how consequential, you have to start with the text itself, which is sparse and strange. Noah gets drunk and lies naked in his tent; his son Ham sees his nakedness and tells his brothers; the brothers cover him without looking; Noah wakes and pronounces a curse, not on Ham, but on Canaan, the son of Ham.

Cursed be Canaan! The lowest of slaves will he be to his brothers.

Genesis 9:25

That is the entire text. A father, dishonored, curses a grandson. Now hear what American preachers did with it. They took the curse of Canaan and mapped it onto Black Africans; they argued that Canaan was the ancestor of African peoples, that God had marked Black people for slavery, and they made this single curse the biblical justification for the entire enterprise of American bondage. Every major theological defense of American slavery leaned on the curse of Ham.

This is not a minor misreading. It is a fundamental distortion of Scripture pressed into the service of an evil practice, and what makes it so pernicious is that the curse is real (the text is genuinely in the Bible) while the application is a lie. Three things expose the lie.

First, the curse of Canaan has nothing to do with Africa or with the descendants of Ham in general. Canaan is one son, and the curse falls on him specifically; historically the Canaanites were the people who lived in the land that became Israel, which is why the text explains the conquest of Canaan in Joshua, not the peopling of a continent. Second, even if it somehow applied to African peoples, it would still be overridden, because the curse says "cursed be Canaan" while the foundation says "in God's image he made them", and when the foundation is the image of God, no curse can justify slavery. Third, the whole arc of Scripture bends the other way: Jesus comes to reverse curses, to restore what was broken, to make unmistakable that the image of God cannot be cursed, cannot be enslaved, cannot be reduced to property.

The doctrine of the curse of Ham is the American church's most explicit sin against Scripture. We invented it. We weaponized it. We used the word of God to justify the enslavement of millions, and that history has consequences still playing out.

What the Prophets Say About Systemic Injustice

If you want to know what the Bible says about what we now call systemic injustice, you have to read the prophets, because they did not confine sin to the individual heart. Amos stands up and thunders against an entire arrangement of power.

Hear this, you who tread upon the poor and who trample upon the needy... The Lord has sworn by the Pride of Jacob: 'I will never forget anything they have done.'

Amos 8:4-7

Notice that Amos is not merely calling individual rich men bad people; he is naming a system (treading, trampling) that manufactures poverty. The system has human agents, but the judgment falls on the system itself. Isaiah goes further and names the machinery directly.

Woe to those who enact unjust decrees and write oppressive laws, depriving the poor of justice and robbing the needy of their rights... What will you do on the day of reckoning, when disaster comes from afar?

Isaiah 10:1-3

That is a prophet talking about law: unjust law, statutes that look neutral on their face yet produce oppression, laws drafted by the powerful to benefit the powerful while the poor are left defenseless. Micah names the violence of it in the most visceral terms.

Hear, you heads of Jacob and rulers of Israel... You hate good and love evil; you tear the skin from my people and the flesh from their bones... Will you not be ashamed of your shameful deeds?

Micah 3:1-7

This is not individual sin. This is systemic violence: a structure of power that enriches some while destroying others. The prophets read the structures of their societies and rendered a verdict: these structures serve injustice, and God will judge not only the individuals who perpetrate it but the systems that embed and perpetuate it.

This is also the point where the Bible's vision becomes more searching than critical race theory, and it is worth saying plainly so neither side mishears it. CRT can name systems; the Bible names systems and offers power to transform them. But the starting point is the same. Systems matter. Structures matter. It is not enough to change hearts if the structures that perpetuate harm are left standing.

Corporate Sin, Inherited Guilt, and the Jubilee

One of the strangest parts of Scripture for Western Christians is the concept of corporate sin: the idea that a people can bear guilt for the sins of previous generations or for the sins embedded in their systems. Nehemiah models it without flinching.

Lord... We have acted very wickedly toward you. We have not obeyed the commands, decrees and laws you gave your servant Moses.

Nehemiah 1:5-7

Nehemiah was not personally responsible for the sins he confesses; his ancestors were. Yet he stands before God and confesses as though he carried the guilt himself, identifying fully with his people. Daniel does precisely the same thing.

I prayed to the Lord my God... while I was speaking and praying, confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel...

Daniel 9:4, 20

Daniel, too, is confessing the sins of his people rather than private failings, standing in the gap between what his people have done and what God's judgment will be. This is thoroughly biblical, and it is also antithetical to American individualism. We have been taught that sin is individual, that guilt is individual, that you answer only for your own actions; Scripture teaches something more complex, that you can bear corporate responsibility for systems you did not create but from which you benefit.

This does not mean white Americans alive today are personally guilty of slavery. It means that if you benefit from systems built through slavery, you carry a corporate responsibility to understand that history and to help dismantle the structures it still shapes. The Bible's ethical vision is simply larger than the individual conscience; it takes in the conscience of a people, the integrity of systems, the justice of structures.

Scripture even designs a mechanism for it, in one of the most subversively ignored passages in the law: the jubilee of Leviticus 25. Every fiftieth year the jubilee came, and all debts were forgiven, all enslaved people were freed, all land returned to its original owners, and the entire social structure reset. This was not charity, and it was not individual generosity; it was a structural intervention built into the law to prevent the permanent accumulation of wealth and power, because the Bible recognizes a hard truth: left to itself, the wealthy accumulate more wealth, the poor accumulate more debt, and you eventually produce a permanent underclass.

The jubilee was God's answer to that drift: no. Every fifty years we reset. We remember that the land belongs to God and not to us, that people are not property, that the limitless accumulation of power and wealth runs against God's design. So ask the obvious question. Has the American church ever attempted to apply jubilee theology to racial injustice? Has any major denomination called for the structural reset that would correspond to it: reparations, land return, debt forgiveness? Has the church recognized that the wealth gap between Black and white families is not individual failure but the residue of systematic exclusion from the very mechanisms that build wealth? Has it tried to imagine what jubilee would look like after four hundred years of slavery and another hundred of segregation? The answer is no. The church ignored jubilee, treating justice as individual righteousness rather than structural transformation.

What the Bible Teaches, and the Real Scandal

Gather the threads, and the testimony of Scripture is consistent. Every person bears the divine image and cannot be reduced to a category or enslaved. Ethnicity is real and beautiful and will matter in the new creation. God cares about the systems and structures that oppress the vulnerable; the prophets read their societies structurally, not merely individually; corporate sin is real, and a people can bear responsibility for inherited injustice; and God himself designs structural mechanisms to prevent permanent oppression.

All of this is in Scripture. All of it sits comfortably with the reality that racism can be systemic, and all of it insists that the church's response cannot be merely individual conversion: it has to include structural transformation. Which is what makes the recent history so painful. When CRT arrived naming systemic racism, much of the American church was shocked, as though the idea were foreign. It was not foreign. The church had simply abandoned its own prophetic tradition; it had grown so individualistic it could no longer read its own Scripture.

That is the real scandal. Not that CRT exists. That we needed CRT to wake us up to what our own Bible had been saying all along.
