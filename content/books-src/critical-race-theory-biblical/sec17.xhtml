Chapters 7-9

The limits of CRT, the roots of evangelical politics, and the two religions of slavery

Knowledge, on this view, is no longer about truth. It is about identity.

Standpoint Epistemology and the Risk of Essentializing Race

Standpoint epistemology is the claim that people who have experienced oppression understand how oppression works more accurately than people who have not. Part of this is plainly true; lived experience is valuable, and a person refused a mortgage because of race knows something about housing discrimination that no column of statistics can convey.

Taken to its extreme, though, the claim develops a problem. It begins to assert that the oppressed carry automatic authority and the oppressor automatic disqualification: that a white person cannot understand racism because they have not suffered it, that an oppressor cannot speak of oppression because they have benefited from it. Pressed that far, the structure goes epistemologically bankrupt fast. If only the oppressed may speak about oppression, then only women may speak about sexism, and where does that leave people who are gender non-binary? Only the poor may speak about poverty, but poverty changes, so may someone speak who is no longer poor? Only people with disabilities may speak about ableism, but the disabilities are many, and do all of those perspectives count equally?

The deeper trouble is this: if only the oppressed can speak, we have conceded that knowledge is not about truth but about identity, about who you are rather than what you know. That is not a structure for finding truth. It is a structure for managing power. CRT in its academic form is more careful here; Derrick Bell and Kimberlé Crenshaw both acknowledged that people outside a group can understand the systems that oppress that group. Their claim was epistemological, that we should value listening to those who have experienced oppression, not a claim that no one else can understand. In popular application, however, standpoint epistemology gets weaponized: if you are white, your voice does not matter on race; if you are a man, your voice does not matter on gender. The result is that genuine dialogue stops, and the very people who have benefited from unjust systems are barred from the work of changing them.

The second weakness is the risk of essentializing racial identity. CRT scholars are careful to call race a social construct, yet in popular application race gets treated as an essential category, as if all white people were the same, all Black people the same, all Asian people the same. That is not true. There is tremendous diversity within racial categories. A Black person raised in an upper-middle-class home and a Black person raised in poverty have radically different experiences; both are Black, both have met anti-Blackness, and their material circumstances are not the same. When CRT-influenced frameworks treat race as essential, when they cast all white people as oppressors or all Black people as victims, they forfeit the very nuance CRT promised, and they become what CRT accused other frameworks of being. Reductive.

How Cancellation Replaces Repentance

The third weakness is the absence of grace and redemption. CRT is good at naming what went wrong and at analyzing how systems perpetuate injustice; it has far less to say about what happens after the analysis. In some applications, especially in activist spaces shaped by CRT, the response to racism has become cancellation. A person says the wrong thing, their past statements are scrutinized, their career ends, on the assumption that the person is irredeemable, that they cannot change, that the only response is removal.

This is where CRT-influenced activism parts ways with the gospel. The gospel assumes that people can change, that confession and repentance are possible, that someone who has perpetuated injustice can actually repent and become an agent of justice. Some CRT-influenced activism has no room for this; it has room only for condemnation. So the people who might otherwise join the work of change are pushed away instead; they grow defensive, they grow convinced they can never be redeemed.

The prophetic tradition in Scripture works differently. The prophets judge. They condemn systems and the people who maintain them. But they also offer repentance: you can change, you can turn, you can take part in a different future. Jonah is the extreme case (he does not want Nineveh to repent, he wants Nineveh destroyed) and yet God offers Nineveh repentance, the city repents, the city is saved, and Jonah is left angry that God is merciful. That is the tension Scripture holds: God judges, and God offers redemption; God condemns systems, and God calls people to abandon those systems and join the new thing he is doing. Some CRT-influenced activism loses this and becomes purely judgmental (purity tests and calling out, making sure you are on the right side of the analysis) until it stops being about actually changing systems at all. CRT can tell you what is wrong. The gospel can tell you how to fix it, and how to change as a person in the process.

The prophets, in fact, offer a model that goes beyond both pure condemnation and cheap reconciliation. They named injustice ferociously and condemned the leaders of Israel without hesitation, yet they also held out a path forward that included redemption. Jeremiah is the difficult case. He preached that Jerusalem should surrender to Babylon, not a popular message, not one that made him beloved. The people wanted him to prophesy victory; he prophesied defeat. Why surrender? Because the system Jerusalem had built was unjust and unsustainable, a city of oppression, corruption, and violence against the vulnerable, and the judgment was coming. But Jeremiah also offered something: surrender, acknowledge what you have done, and there is a future; resist, cling to power, and the future is destruction. Repentance leads to restoration.

Ezekiel gives another version. He sees Jerusalem destroyed (the Temple burned, the people scattered, the city emptied) and it is devastating; then he sees a vision of restoration, the dry bones coming alive, the Temple rebuilt, the people returning, the city renewed. Judgment is real and its consequences are real, but it is not the end of the story. This differs from CRT-informed activism that sees judgment and has no structure for restoration, and it differs just as sharply from white evangelicalism that sees judgment and forgives at once without requiring any change. The prophetic structure says judgment is necessary (the system has to be exposed as unjust, the people who maintain it have to face what they have done), but judgment is not the goal. Restoration is the goal; judgment is the means that makes restoration possible. Practically, that means when we expose systemic racism we are not chiefly interested in condemning white people but in transforming the system so it no longer perpetuates injustice, and in white people joining that work, though the work requires first acknowledging what the system has done and the benefit one has received from it. Not cancellation. Not absolution. Accountability that leads to change.

Segregation Academies and the Southern Strategy

Jerry Falwell Sr. founded Liberty University in 1971. The date matters. This is after desegregation, after the Civil Rights Act of 1964, in the very moment integration was being forced onto American institutions. Liberty was founded as a segregation academy, founded so that white Christian families could educate their children without their attending integrated schools. Falwell was explicit. He said he was concerned about what secular universities were teaching, about the moral decline of America, and he framed it as building a Christian university where children would be educated in a Christian environment. But that environment was a segregated one. He did not accept Black students at Liberty until 1971, when federal pressure forced him to, and even then he discouraged Black enrollment.

Bob Jones University is the clearer example. BJU was founded in 1927 as a Bible college, and by the 1950s it was explicit: the university forbade interracial dating. Not implicit, not suggested: an explicit, stated rule, no dating between races. Why? BJU claimed it was biblical, that God made the races distinct and intended them to remain separate, that interracial dating violated God's design. The rule stayed in place until 2000. Two thousand. Not 1960. Not 1970. And when it was finally removed, it was removed quietly, with minimal acknowledgment. Consider what that means: a Christian university explicitly forbade young people of different races from dating on biblical grounds, enforced segregation on personal relationships, and kept the rule for forty years after the Civil Rights Act.

And evangelicals acted shocked when outsiders criticized evangelicalism for racism. They pointed to figures like Billy Graham, who had taken small steps toward integration, and claimed they were actually progressive on race. But Liberty and Bob Jones reveal the truth. The evangelical response to desegregation was to build alternative institutions that maintained segregation, not to lead on integration but to resist it and find ways around it.

The Southern Strategy is part of this history. It was the political strategy Republicans adopted in the 1960s and 1970s: if you want Southern votes, you do not have to say you support segregation explicitly; you use coded language. You talk about law and order. You talk about states' rights. You talk about traditional values. You never mention race, but the message to Southern white voters is clear: we will defend your way of life against the changes coming. Evangelicals embraced it. Leaders like Falwell shifted from being apolitical or Democratic to being Republican precisely because the Republican Party was using language that signaled opposition to integration without saying it. This is the origin of the evangelical-Republican alliance, and it came not from abortion but from resistance to integration. The Moral Majority, which Falwell founded in 1979, was presented as a response to moral decline and secular humanism; but the timing is telling. It was founded after desegregation had been forced on the country, and it gave evangelicals a way to organize politically against the changes they opposed. Abortion was part of the coalition. The core energy, the organizing principle, was resistance to the loss of white evangelical cultural dominance. Once you grasp this history, you understand why white evangelicalism has been so resistant to racial justice, why it panics when race comes up, why it attacks CRT so viciously: CRT names exactly what white evangelicalism has been defending for fifty years.

This resistance was not accidental. It was the result of conscious choices made in Christian educational institutions; what evangelicals were taught shaped how they think about race. Evangelical seminaries in the twentieth century trained pastors on a curriculum that had little to nothing about systemic racism, focusing on biblical exegesis, theology, and church practice while leaving out the history of the church's complicity in slavery, segregation, and racial terrorism. Bible colleges taught Bible, but in a way that severed Scripture from its sociopolitical context, so that a pastor could graduate knowing the Greek and Hebrew, understanding theology deeply, and still have no structure for hearing how Scripture speaks to systemic injustice. Again, not accident but choice. Seminaries could have required courses on church history and racism, on biblical justice; they chose not to. What they taught instead reinforced the status quo: submission to authorities, individual piety, a gospel about personal salvation rather than social transformation. So when civil rights activists, many of them Christian, began organizing against segregation, white evangelical pastors had no theological grid to engage. Some aligned with civil rights; many stayed silent, and some actively opposed, not because they were uniquely evil, but because their education had not equipped them to see systemic racism as a gospel issue.

What would it look like to teach racial justice history in evangelical institutions? It would mean that every seminarian learns the history of the church's complicity in slavery and segregation, including the complicity of their own denominations: Southern Baptist Seminary students learning about the SBC's defense of slavery, Bob Jones Seminary students learning about the interracial dating ban. It would mean studying the prophetic tradition and understanding that prophetic ministry includes calling out systemic injustice, reading James Cone and Frederick Douglass alongside traditional theology. It would mean every Bible college course on a biblical book asking what that book says about justice, about the vulnerable, about the systems we live in. It would mean graduates who can see that a church building in a segregated neighborhood, built with the proceeds of slavery and maintained through the exclusion of Black people, is not just a building but evidence of systemic racism, and who understand that silence on racial injustice is not neutrality. Silence is participation.

Slave Religion vs. Slaveholder Religion

James Cone wrote a book called The Cross and the Lynching Tree, and the title is its argument. The cross, the central symbol of Christianity, is the execution of an innocent person by an unjust state. The lynching tree, the central image of American racial terrorism, is also the execution of an innocent person by a racist mob. Cone is saying these are the same thing: both are executions, both the killing of the powerless by the powerful, both acts of violence meant to maintain hierarchy, both supposedly justified as necessary, both claiming some moral legitimacy.

Christianity has traditionally read the cross as redemption: the death of Jesus purchasing salvation. Cone asks what it would mean to read the cross as lynching, to say that Jesus was lynched, executed by a mob that had the law's approval, an innocent person murdered to keep a power structure in place. If Jesus was lynched, then the cross is not primarily about individual salvation; it is about God standing with the executed, identifying with the victims of state and mob violence, refusing to accept that the killing of the innocent is justified. This reframes the entire gospel. The gospel is not good news to the powerful. It is good news to the lynched. It says your execution was wrong, that the system that executed you will be judged, that God is on your side and not on the side of the mob or the state.

Frederick Douglass wrote something similar. An enslaved man who escaped and became an abolitionist, he wrote an essay called The Slaveholder's Sermon, contrasting the religion of the slave with the religion of the slaveholder. The slave's religion was about God's liberation: about Exodus, about freedom; the enslaved gathered in secret and sang about getting to the promised land, about freedom by and by, about a God who sees the enslaved and will act. The slaveholder's religion was about obedience: the slaveholder preached to the enslaved to obey their masters, honor their masters, accept their condition, and took Scripture and used it to justify slavery, building theology to defend the indefensible. These are not two interpretations of the same Christianity. They are two different religions. They share the same book and read it in opposite directions: the slave as a book about liberation, the slaveholder as a book about submission.

Douglass's insight cut deep. The religion taught by the slaveholder would destroy the soul of anyone who believed it; believe the slaveholder's sermon and you accept slavery as legitimate, accept your own oppression, accept the oppression of others. The religion practiced by the enslaved, by contrast, creates freedom: the possibility of resistance, the conviction that God is on your side, that your freedom is coming, that the system oppressing you will fall. This is the difference between slave religion and slaveholder religion, between a gospel that liberates and a gospel that domesticates. White Christianity has largely inherited the slaveholder's religion, reading Scripture as a book about obedience, submission, and acceptance of the status quo, building theology that justifies the system. Black Christianity has kept the slave's religion, reading Scripture as a book about liberation, building theology that empowers resistance and hope. This is why Cone insists that white Christians must learn from Black theology and the Black church: white Christianity cannot see the liberating Jesus because it has been trained by the slaveholder's sermon to read in a way that domesticates the text. The real Jesus is the Jesus of the enslaved, the Jesus who identifies with the lynched, the Jesus who says your oppression is not your fault, your dignity cannot be taken from you, God is working for your liberation, the Jesus white Christianity has largely lost and Black Christianity has never lost.

William Wilberforce complicates the evangelical version of this story. A British politician who came to faith and became convinced slavery was wrong, he spent decades working to abolish it throughout the British Empire and eventually succeeded. Evangelicals point to him as proof that the gospel produces justice: a man converted, convicted by conscience, who changed a system. But the story is more complicated. Wilberforce also supported colonialism; he believed Britain should colonize Africa and India and elsewhere, and he believed that colonization was a civilizing mission, a Christian mission. So he freed people from the direct institution of slavery while supporting systems that kept colonized people in dependent, exploited relationships with the Empire. That is the problem with redemption narratives built on individual conscience. A person can be converted, their conscience transformed, can work for change in one area, and still be committed to injustice in another. Wilberforce was moral and cared about justice for the enslaved, yet he lived inside colonialism and did not see it as injustice; he saw it as Christian civilization.

This is how systems maintain themselves. Individuals work for change in one area while remaining blind to injustice in another, and the system adapts; it gives up slavery but keeps colonialism, gives up segregation but keeps redlining. Personal conversion, then, is necessary but insufficient. A white pastor whose conscience is awakened about racism and who decides to pastor differently is doing something good; but if the church still benefits from segregated neighborhoods, from property purchased through redlining, from wealth accumulated by excluding Black people, his individual conversion does not solve the systemic problem. Real change requires structural transformation: churches that built wealth through segregation actually redistributing it to repair what they damaged, seminaries that trained pastors to defend segregation actually teaching a different history, denominations that maintained segregation taking concrete steps to become genuinely multiethnic. Personal conversion is the beginning. It cannot be the end. The system has to change, and the people within it have to be willing to pay a cost to change it.

There is one more paradox in this inheritance, and it concerns the Book itself. White evangelicalism prides itself on biblical literacy; its pastors study Greek and Hebrew, its churches emphasize careful reading, its people claim to take Scripture seriously. And yet white evangelicals are biblically literate while being biblically blind. They can parse the Greek, explain the literary structure of the psalms, defend the reliability of the Gospel accounts, and miss what the Bible is at its core about: God's commitment to justice for the vulnerable. This is not accidental. White evangelical biblical scholarship was shaped heavily by the German historical-critical school, which treated Scripture as a historical document to be analyzed objectively, the scholar standing back to examine what the text says rather than listening to what the text says to the scholar's own condition. But Scripture was not written for detached scholars. It was written for the people of God living in oppression, written to be heard and not only analyzed, written to transform and not merely to inform.

The Black church has always understood this, reading Scripture as a living word, as God speaking to their condition, asking what God is saying to us in our situation. White evangelicalism has read Scripture as a collection of doctrines and principles, asking what a text teaches about theology or individual spirituality. Those are not bad questions, but they are not the primary one. The primary question is what God is saying about justice, about the vulnerable, about the systems we participate in. White evangelical literacy has produced pastors who can explain the theology of atonement without asking whether they are complicit in the systems that require atonement, scholars who can defend the authority of Scripture while resisting what Scripture actually says about justice. To read Scripture as the oppressed read it would mean beginning with the recognition that I do not stand above the text as an objective analyzer; I am inside it, it is speaking to my situation, asking whether I am part of an oppressed people or part of a system that oppresses. It would mean recognizing that the story of God standing with the vulnerable is not one theme among many but the fundamental narrative arc, consistent from Exodus to the prophets to Jesus. It would mean reading every text with the question of whether it challenges the systems of power I am invested in, expands my sense of whom God is concerned about, and calls me to change, being willing to be transformed by Scripture rather than using it to confirm what I already believe. If white evangelicalism recovered this, the American church would have prophetic power. But it would require white evangelical scholars to question the system they were trained in, to admit that objective detachment is not the most faithful way to read, to submit to Scripture as a word that judges and not only a text that teaches. For most, that is too costly. So the literacy continues, and so does the blindness.

Holding Grace and Justice Together at the Cross

American evangelicalism has emphasized grace. It preaches that all are sinners, all have fallen short, all are justified by faith in Christ alone, and this is true. But American evangelicalism has often used grace to avoid justice. If everyone is a sinner, then no one is uniquely culpable; if everyone needs grace, then no one specifically needs to repent of perpetuating injustice; if God forgives all, then we can move on without addressing what was done. This is grace distorted into sentimentalism, grace that costs nothing and changes nothing. Its danger is that it lets injustice continue unchecked, lets people apologize without repenting, lets systems be defended on the grounds that everyone is sinful anyway.

But there is an opposite error: justice without grace. If the whole focus falls on naming systems and condemning the people who maintain them, with no possibility of redemption or transformation, then justice becomes despair. Justice without grace says: you have benefited from injustice, and therefore you are irredeemable; the system is too broken, and there is no point in trying to change it. That is a justice that has lost hope.

What does the cross offer? Both at once. The cross is God's judgment on injustice (the execution of the innocent is inexcusable, and God refuses to accept it) and it is God's grief over what humans have done to each other. But it is also God's offer of redemption, God saying: you have done terrible things, and they matter, the consequences are real, but there is a way forward, there is forgiveness, there is transformation. Grace-without-justice says it does not matter what you did, you are forgiven, move on. Justice-without-grace says what you did matters so much that you can never be forgiven, you are condemned forever. The cross says what you did matters immensely, the consequences are real, the people you harmed deserve restitution, and you can change, you can repent, you can join the work of transformation; your guilt does not have to define you forever.

This is the message the American church needs to recover. Not grace that avoids justice. Not justice that avoids grace. Both, simultaneously. In a church pursuing racial justice it would mean that people who have benefited from systems of injustice are not absolved without repentance: no saying I had racist thoughts but I am sorry now, so we can move on, because repentance means addressing what the system did and working to change it. And it would mean that those who repent are not permanently condemned, that transformation is real, that a white person who spent decades defending racism can actually change and actually take part in the work of justice. This is the tension the cross holds. This is what the American church needs.
