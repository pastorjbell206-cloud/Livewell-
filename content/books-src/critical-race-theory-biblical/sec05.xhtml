Chapter 4

Martin Luther King's Theology

The radical theology behind the dream — and what the white church has tried to forget

Martin Luther King Jr. is quoted in nearly every American church during Black History Month. His "I Have a Dream" speech is celebrated, his name invoked, his image printed into Sunday school materials; the mainstream evangelical church now honors him without hesitation. But honoring a man and reading him are not the same thing. Almost no white evangelical church has actually sat with what King wrote and said, and if it had, it would have to confront an uncomfortable fact: King was far more demanding than contemporary evangelicalism is willing to be.

He was not asking the church to do diversity training. He was asking it to actually believe the gospel it claimed to believe.

King was not asking the church for diversity training, and he was not asking for representation in its leadership. He was asking for something far more searching: that the American church actually believe the gospel it claimed to believe, and live as if that belief carried a cost.

The Letter From Birmingham Jail and the White Moderate

In April 1963, King was arrested in Birmingham, Alabama, for leading protests against segregation, and from that cell he wrote what would become his most important theological document, the "Letter from Birmingham Jail." The letter was addressed to a group of white clergy who had published a statement calling King's actions "unwise and untimely." These clergy were not defending segregation; they were asking King to slow down, to wait for the right time, to work within the system, to be patient. His response was theologically devastating.

I must confess that I am not afraid of the word 'tension' in this connection. I have earnestly opposed violent tension, but there is a type of constructive, nonviolent tension which is necessary for growth.

Letter from Birmingham Jail

King was naming what the request for patience really was. You are asking me to stop creating tension about injustice, he was saying; you want me to keep the peace, but the peace you want is the peace of injustice left undisturbed, and that is not peace at all. That is complicity. Then he wrote the line that should still arrest every white Christian who has ever preached about social order.

I must make two honest confessions to you, my Christian and Jewish brothers. First, I must confess that over the past few years I have been gravely disappointed with the white moderate. I have almost reached the regrettable conclusion that the Negro's great stumbling block in his stride toward freedom is not the White Citizen's Counciler or the Ku Klux Klansman, but the white moderate, who is more devoted to 'order' than to justice.

Letter from Birmingham Jail

This is the thing white Christianity has never quite forgiven King for saying. Not that white racists were bad; of course they were. But that the white moderate, the person who claimed to believe in equality yet would not act, who valued order more than justice, was the greater obstacle to freedom. The white moderate is the pastor who says he believes in civil rights but worries about offending people; the church leader who supports equality but not now, not like this; the Christian who agrees that segregation is wrong while continuing to benefit from it. King named the moderate as the problem precisely because the moderate held just enough legitimacy to slow justice down: the one always counseling patience, working within the system, asking everyone not to be too aggressive.

Then King went further, refusing the comforting idea that the passage of time would, on its own, do the work of justice.

Actually, time itself is neutral... There is 'time for all things.' It is often said that time will tell. Actually, time is always on the side of the oppressor, not on the side of the justice-seeker.

Letter from Birmingham Jail

Waiting, patience, slow and deliberate process: these all quietly favor the people already in power. The longer justice is deferred, the more time the powerful have to organize against it, and the people being harmed are the ones who cannot afford to wait.

King on Unjust Law and the Higher Law of God

From there King brought down the theological hammer, reaching for the hardest possible comparison to make his point about law and conscience.

We should never forget that everything Adolf Hitler did in Germany was 'legal.' It was illegal to aid and comfort a Jew in Hitler's Germany. But I would have aided and comforted my Jewish brothers.

Letter from Birmingham Jail

King was setting segregation law beside Nazi law to make a single claim: the law is not the same thing as justice. An unjust law is not binding on the conscience, because there is a higher law than the law of man, and where human law contradicts that higher law, obedience belongs to the higher. The American church largely heard this and rejected it. It wanted to believe that law equals order equals God's design. King answered: no. When the law serves injustice, it is you who must resist the law.

Beyond the Dream: King's Radical Critique of the System

Most people know King's theology from a single source, the "I Have a Dream" speech. The speech is beautiful and hopeful; it imagines a day when people will be "judged not by the color of their skin but by the content of their character." But if that is all you have read of King, you do not yet know his theology. The Dream is one moment inside a much larger and more searching body of work.

Read his sermons "Why Jesus Called a Man a Fool" and "A Knock at Midnight"; read his book "Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community?" In these texts King is prophetic and unsettling, not merely calling for integration but for a fundamental restructuring of American economic and political life. In "Where Do We Go from Here," he turns directly to the problem of poverty.

Something is wrong with capitalism... the profit motive alone is not adequate to meet the needs of every member of the society... true compassion is more than flinging a coin to a beggar; it comes to see that an edifice which produces beggars needs restructuring.

Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community?

King was not arguing for minor reforms to soften poverty's edges. He was arguing that the system producing the poverty needed to be restructured at its core: that the foundation itself was wrong. He was equally critical of militarism; in his 1967 sermon "Beyond Vietnam" he said America needed a subversive reordering of its priorities, its foreign policy, its commitment to war. And he was clear about what he was prepared to die for. In his final sermon, delivered the night before his assassination, he said, "Well, I don't know what will happen now. We've got some difficult days ahead. But it doesn't matter with me now." He was not afraid, and he was not hedging. He was willing to die for justice.

King's Eschatology and the Arc of Justice

Part of what made King powerful was his eschatological conviction: his belief that God had already decided the outcome of history, that the arc of history bent toward justice, and that God's kingdom would come whether America cooperated or not. This is easy to miss. King was not optimistic about American progress, and he was not trusting in human ingenuity or American goodness. He was trusting in God. His confidence rested on the conviction that God had already settled the matter, and that his own work was simply to align himself with what God was already doing.

This is, in large measure, the faith the contemporary American church has lost. We have become pragmatists who calculate what is possible and work within existing systems, no longer believing that God might be asking us to do something that looks impossible from a human vantage point. King believed impossible things because he believed in God; he believed segregation would fall because God had decided it would, and that justice would come because God was bringing it. His task was to obey, not to calculate the odds of success. The distance between his faith and ours is the distance between conviction and compromise.

How Evangelicalism Domesticated King

Evangelical Christianity has adopted King's name while quietly setting aside his theology. We quote the dream and ignore his critique of capitalism; we celebrate his nonviolence while maintaining our own structures of advantage and exploitation; we have turned a revolutionary into a safe figure, a man of the past who said agreeable things about equality. If the church had actually listened to him, it would have had to ask itself hard questions: whether its churches benefit from unjust economic systems, whether it loves order more than justice, whether it asks oppressed people to wait, whether it is more devoted to comfort than to prophetic witness, and what systems in our own moment we defend when we should be resisting. Those questions have gone mostly unasked. We have quoted King's dream while maintaining our nightmares.

His real challenge to white Christianity comes as a series of professed beliefs held up against a way of life. You say you believe the gospel. You say all people are made in God's image, that Jesus broke down the wall between Jew and Gentile, that the cross has power to reconcile enemies. But the churches were segregated, the leadership was segregated, the money flowed toward people who looked alike, and the theology had been shaped by the very systems it benefited from. The question King posed remains unanswered: if you actually believed the gospel, how could you maintain segregation, and how could you stay silent while your brothers and sisters were beaten, jailed, and killed for asking to be treated as human? You could not. That is the indictment, and that is why the church has worked so hard to domesticate him: to make him a dreamer who asked nicely rather than a prophet who demanded repentance for complicity with injustice.

The American church owes King something, beginning with the acknowledgment that he was right and we were wrong: that we were silent, that we were comfortable, that we chose order over justice, that we remained in segregated churches and theological institutions and leadership structures while he was jailed and beaten for calling us to believe what we claimed to believe. We owe him the work of reading him honestly: not the sanitized King of Black History Month, but the real one, the man killed because he threatened too much, who called American capitalism, American militarism, and American Christianity to account. And we owe him one more question. If he were alive today, what would he be calling the contemporary church to account for: which systems, which marginalized people, which theology built to justify the unjust? That is the real work of honoring him. Not quoting him. Hearing him, actually hearing what he said, and actually changing in response.
