Chapter 8

White Evangelicalism's Blind Spot

Why the loudest critics of CRT have not reckoned with their own racial history

You cannot credibly critique a theory that names racial systems while maintaining the very systems it is trying to name.

The Southern Baptist Convention's Founding to Defend Slavery

There is an irony in the current debate that should not be missed. The loudest voices attacking Critical Race Theory from evangelical pulpits and platforms come, very often, from institutions and traditions that have never honestly reckoned with their own racial history; and once that is said plainly, the difficulty becomes hard to set aside. You cannot credibly critique CRT while maintaining the very systems CRT is attempting to name. You cannot credibly warn people about the dangers of critical theory while continuing the theology that once defended slavery. You cannot credibly claim to value truth while refusing to tell the truth about your own past.

Consider the founding facts. The Southern Baptist Convention was founded in 1845, and it was founded to defend slavery. Before that year Baptists in America operated within a single denomination, the American Baptists, but tensions over slavery were rising; Northern Baptists had begun moving toward abolition while Southern Baptists remained committed to the institution, and the Convention was created specifically to allow Southern Baptists to maintain and defend it. This is not interpretation. This is history. The Convention was literally formed as a religious defense of slavery.

For the next 120 years the SBC defended slavery, then defended segregation, then resisted integration with vigor; it did not integrate its seminaries until the 1980s, and it elected its first non-white denomination-wide leader only recently, many decades after the civil rights era. In recent years the SBC has officially apologized for its stance on slavery and segregation, and that apology is genuinely good, but an apology is not the same as fundamental change. Its leadership structure remains overwhelmingly white, and the wealth gap between white and Black Baptist communities remains vast, a direct inheritance of centuries of slavery and segregation. Yet SBC leaders are among the loudest voices attacking CRT. The credibility problem is hard to escape: you cannot credibly attack a structure that names systemic racism while maintaining an institution founded to defend racial slavery, nor warn about systemic injustice embedded in institutions while declining to acknowledge the injustice your own institution perpetuated and, in measurable ways, continues to perpetuate.

The Evangelical Realignment and the Segregation Academies

After Brown v. Board of Education declared segregation unconstitutional in 1954, white evangelicals faced a choice. They could accept integration, they could lead on racial justice, they could say that the gospel compels a movement toward reconciliation. They did not. Instead, white evangelicals built a massive network of segregation academies, private schools designed specifically to allow white children to avoid integrated public schools.

These were not fringe institutions. They were mainline evangelical ones: Bob Jones University, Oral Roberts University, hundreds of Christian schools founded for the explicit purpose of maintaining separation. Not integration academies. Not schools designed to offer Christian education while modeling reconciliation. Segregation academies, whose stated purpose was to let white families keep their children racially separate. Many of them maintained explicit rules against interracial dating into the 1980s and 1990s; Bob Jones University did not remove its ban on interracial dating until the year 2000. Two thousand. Not 1960. Not 1970. 2000.

This is the history of white evangelicalism, and it implicates the tradition I am writing from. We created a vast infrastructure to maintain racial separation in the name of Christian education, and we did it not in the 1920s but in the 1950s and 1960s, in the very moment the nation was beginning to integrate.

The Religious Right's Real Origin

The contemporary religious right is often credited with beginning around abortion: the standard account holds that evangelicals, alarmed by Roe v. Wade in 1973, mobilized into politics. That account is wrong. The religious right began around segregation. It was mobilized to defend the segregation academies and to preserve racial separation.

In 1981 Jerry Falwell declared, "The time has come to call our nation back to God, back to morality, back to sensibility, and back to conservatism." His moment of calling the nation back was happening precisely as the segregation academies came under legal threat for their discriminatory policies. Falwell was defending segregation. That was the origin. Abortion came later and became the public face, but segregation was the root. This matters because it means white evangelicalism's entry into politics was built on the defense of racial separation; the religious right was birthed as a resistance movement to integration. Once you know that history, the present comes into focus, when white evangelicals attack CRT today, what you are watching is a tradition defending its own racial systems while attacking the system that names them.

The Credibility Problem in Attacking CRT

Here is what makes the attack on CRT from white evangelical pulpits ring hollow. CRT is attempting to name how systems perpetuate racial injustice; it argues that institutions embed bias in ways that are not always visible, and that people can benefit from unjust systems without being consciously malicious. That description, set beside the record, is uncomfortably close to a self-portrait. White evangelicalism benefited from unjust racial systems. It created and maintained the segregation academies. It remains overwhelmingly white in its leadership. It built much of its wealth and influence on racial separation and racial hierarchy. CRT is naming the very thing white evangelicalism has done.

So when white evangelical leaders attack CRT, the message underneath the argument often amounts to this: do not name the systems we benefit from, do not point out how our institutions perpetuate racial hierarchy, do not force us to reckon with our history. Whatever else it is, that is not, at bottom, intellectual critique. It is institutional self-protection.

There is an intellectual dishonesty here as well. The loudest evangelical attacks on CRT tend to come from people who have made no serious attempt to understand it; they have not read Derrick Bell, they have not read Kimberlé Crenshaw, and they are attacking a caricature rather than the argument. If you are going to critique CRT, you should at least know what CRT says; you should be able to quote it accurately and engage what it actually claims, and most evangelical attacks fail that basic test. More than that, if you critique CRT while maintaining your own unjust systems, you should expect to be called hypocritical. And you should be.

There is also the matter of selective memory. Many white evangelicals know about Billy Graham's modest steps toward integration, and many can point to individual evangelical leaders who opposed racism; but many do not know about the segregation academies, do not know that the religious right was founded to defend segregation, do not know that SBC seminaries were segregated into the 1980s. That selective memory lets white evangelicals feel progressive on race while maintaining systems that perpetuate injustice; they can quote Martin Luther King while remaining in predominantly white churches, oppose racism in principle while declining to integrate their institutions in practice. CRT is attempting to make this impossible. It says you cannot claim to be colorblind while benefiting from systems that sort by color, you cannot claim to be anti-racist while maintaining segregated institutions, you cannot ignore systemic injustice. What threatens evangelicals is not, finally, the truth of CRT's analysis. It is that CRT will not let us hide anymore.

What Genuine Repentance Would Require

The devastating part is that Scripture offers a vision of racial justice more searching than CRT could. The gospel offers reconciliation where CRT can only offer justice; it offers transformation where CRT can only offer structural change. Had white evangelicalism actually offered that vision (had evangelicals said, the gospel demands that we integrate our churches, our schools, our communities, and we are leading the way), the tradition would have the standing to critique CRT. Instead it did the opposite. It built systems of segregation and constructed theology to justify them, and when CRT arrived and named those systems, it panicked, because it had no answer. The answer it should have had was within reach: yes, systems matter; yes, we have embedded racial bias in our institutions; yes, we need to repent and change, but the gospel offers more than CRT, the power to actually transform hearts and reconcile enemies. Evangelicalism could not say that, because it had not done the work. It had not repented. It had not changed its institutions. It had tried to hide its history.

If white evangelicalism wanted the credibility to critique CRT, the requirements would be concrete and costly. It would need to reckon honestly with the history of the segregation academies, acknowledging what they were and what they were for. It would need to account honestly for why evangelical institutions remain overwhelmingly segregated, with reasons more searching than "it happened that way." It would need to commit to integration of leadership in ways that cost something, not token diversity but substantive change. It would need to teach the actual history of evangelicalism and race to the next generation, not the sanitized version but the whole story. It would need to support Black-led institutions and Black evangelical voices without trying to control them. And it would need to commit to structural change that redistributes resources and power. That is the work of genuine repentance, the work that would give white evangelicalism the moral authority to speak prophetically about the real problems with CRT. Until that work is done, when evangelicals attack CRT they sound like people trying to silence a voice that is naming their own sin, because that is largely what is happening.

The final irony cuts deepest. If white evangelicalism actually believed what it claims to believe about the gospel (that Christ breaks down the wall between Jew and Gentile, that Christ reconciles enemies, that Christ's blood makes us one), it would not need CRT to be proved wrong. It would have already integrated. It would have already modeled reconciliation. It would have already offered the world a better vision than any secular theory could. That it did not, that it built the segregation academies, that it created the religious right to defend segregation, that it remains segregated in its pews and its leadership, that it is now panicking about CRT: all of it suggests that white evangelicalism does not, in practice, believe what it claims to believe. That is the real crisis. Not CRT. The crisis is that white evangelicalism has, in large measure, abandoned the gospel's vision of reconciliation while continuing to defend systems that perpetuate racial division. You cannot credibly attack CRT while maintaining that history; you cannot credibly warn about systemic injustice while benefiting from your own; you cannot credibly claim the gospel while refusing to live it. That is not politics. That is spiritual accountability.
