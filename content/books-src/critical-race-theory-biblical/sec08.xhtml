Chapter 7

Where CRT Gets It Wrong

Where critical race theory collides with the gospel's view of the human person

Christianity's ultimate concern is not power. It is wholeness, healing, reconciliation.

The Anthropological Problem: Identity in CRT vs. the Image of God

Critical race theory gets some things right about American systems, as the previous chapter granted at length. But its ultimate structure contradicts Christian anthropology in ways that matter profoundly. A Christian cannot adopt CRT wholesale; a Christian can learn from CRT without being captured by it. The line between those two postures runs through the question of what a human being actually is.

In CRT's account, human identity is understood primarily through position in a system of power. Who you are is determined by what group you belong to and what power relationships that group holds; identity is group-based and power-based. Christianity teaches something radically different. A human being is first a creature made in God's image, loved by God, redeemed by God. That is your identity, more foundational than your race, your gender, your class, or your status as oppressor or oppressed. Your identity is being God's beloved.

This is not a semantic quibble. It is foundational, and it changes everything about how you understand yourself and how you engage the people around you. If identity is primarily power-based, then the goal of justice becomes the inversion of the power relationship: the oppressed should become the oppressors, the marginalized should become the centered, and justice reduces to the question of who gets to be in power. If identity is rooted instead in being God's beloved, then the goal of justice is not to invert hierarchies but to eliminate them — to build a community where no one stands in power over anyone else, where everyone is equally beloved, where everyone serves everyone else. These are not the same thing.

The Problem of Guilt Without Redemption

One limitation of CRT as a structure for justice is that it often embeds unforgiveness into the system itself. If a person's identity is determined by their oppressor status, then that person cannot be healed of that identity; they remain locked in relationship with the system that damaged them. Christianity offers something different. It offers forgiveness — not forgetting, not pretending the harm did not happen, but the genuine possibility of moving beyond the harm that was done. In the CRT structure, a person who has been harmed carries that harm as part of their identity. In the Christian structure, a person can be healed of that harm and move forward, freed from the identity of victim.

None of this is to say that acknowledging harm is unhealthy; acknowledging harm is necessary. But being defined by harm is not freedom. Being defined by harm is remaining in bondage to it. Jesus offers another way. "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery."

For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery.

Galatians 5:1

That verse is often quoted in individual contexts — freedom from sin, freedom from guilt. But it applies to systems too. You do not have to remain in the identity the system tried to hand you; you can be freed from it. The same problem runs the other direction. CRT often leaves white people with guilt but no path to redemption. You have benefited from unjust systems — true. You carry the benefit of centuries of injustice — true. But now what? CRT can say you owe restitution, that you need to dismantle the systems, that you need to acknowledge your complicity, and yet it struggles to answer whether you can actually be forgiven, whether you can actually move beyond the guilt.

In a structure where identity is fixed by oppressor status, a white person is locked in that status, unable to escape being an oppressor because that is what the system has made them. Christianity offers forgiveness instead — genuine forgiveness, not getting off the hook, not pretending the harm didn't happen, but real forgiveness that opens the possibility of being freed from the guilt. This matters because without the possibility of forgiveness, reconciliation is impossible. If a person is locked in guilt with no way out, they cannot genuinely meet the other person; they can only meet them as someone trapped in their own shame.

CRT and the Missing Piece of Reconciliation

CRT is concerned with justice, and justice is important. But CRT often settles for justice without reconciliation. It can name the perpetrator and demand restitution; it can change systems and redistribute power. Where it struggles is the question of what happens after justice: can the perpetrator and the victim actually become one people again? Christianity insists on reconciliation — not at the expense of justice, for justice must come first, but as the goal, so that people who have been enemies can become family again.

Consider Philemon, a short letter in the Bible. Paul writes to Philemon about Onesimus, a slave who ran away from Philemon's household, and Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus back — not as a slave, but as a beloved brother. This is wild. A slave owner in the Roman system held the legal right to execute a runaway slave; a slave in that system had no legal recourse, no dignity, no chance. And Paul, operating within that system, asks for something the system does not allow: reconciliation. He asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as a brother. "Perhaps the reason he was separated from you for a little while was that you might have him back forever—no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a beloved brother."

Paul is not asking Philemon to do nothing about the injustice of slavery. He is asking for more than justice. He is asking for love — for the possibility that these two people might actually become family. This is what Christianity offers that CRT does not: the possibility that people who have been divided can actually be reconciled. There is a deeper missing piece as well. CRT's structure assumes that systems are the problem and that changing systems is the solution; redistribute power, change laws, embed justice in structures, and you have solved the problem. But systems are not the only problem. People are. People carry sin, people carry brokenness, and people — even inside just systems — can still choose injustice.

The gospel offers the transformation of people, not just systems. It says you can be made new. "Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, the new creation has come: The old has gone, the new is here!"

Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, the new creation has come: The old has gone, the new is here!

2 Corinthians 5:17

This is not positive thinking, and it is not ignoring systems. It is the claim that human hearts can actually change — that people can repent, that people can turn from injustice. CRT does not have tools for this. It can identify who is responsible for systemic injustice, but it cannot offer the power to repent and change.

When Justice Without Reconciliation Fails

History offers examples of justice pursued without reconciliation. After World War I, the Treaty of Versailles imposed terms so harsh that the German people felt humiliated and hungry for revenge; they wanted to overturn the terms, convinced that what was called justice was not justice at all but oppression. The result was resentment that festered and helped clear the road for the rise of Hitler. After the Rwanda genocide, the country could have chosen simple retribution — the perpetrators could have been killed. Instead Rwanda chose Truth and Reconciliation: perpetrators admitted what they had done, victims heard the admissions, and communities worked toward healing. It was not simple and it was not easy, but it opened the possibility of people actually living together again.

The pattern is clear enough to state as a verdict. Justice without reconciliation breeds resentment; justice with reconciliation opens the possibility of actually healing the breach. That is the difference between a settlement that festers and a settlement that holds.

Where Contemporary CRT Application Becomes Ideology

To be clear, I am not saying CRT is evil. CRT is a tool. It can help you see systems more clearly and name what you might not have named before; for a Christian, it can be a useful ally in naming systemic sin. But it cannot be your structure for understanding justice, because its ultimate concern is power — who has it, who doesn't, how to redistribute it. Christianity's ultimate concern is not power. It is wholeness. It is healing. It is reconciliation. A Christian should be able to say: I see what CRT names about systems, and that is real; I also see what Christianity offers beyond that, and that is more real; I am committed both to naming injustice and to offering transformation; I am committed both to justice and to reconciliation; I believe that systems should change and that people should be made new. That is not compromising the gospel. That is fulfilling the gospel.

In its most extreme contemporary applications, CRT becomes something other than what its original scholars intended. In some applications it comes to mean viewing all white people as inherently oppressive regardless of their actions, viewing all Black people as inherently victims regardless of their agency, refusing to recognize that individuals have choices within systems, making identity categories absolute and untraversable, and offering no hope for genuine reconciliation or transformation. These applications have moved away from CRT as an academic analysis toward something closer to ideology. And ideology — whether it comes from the left or the right — is dangerous in the church. The gospel is not ideology. The gospel is the story of God's redemption, and it transcends any human system of thought.

So a Christian can engage CRT critically: learning from it what is true while rejecting what contradicts the gospel, naming systemic injustice while offering hope for systemic transformation and personal redemption. That posture requires holding several things in tension at once. Systems are real, and unjust systems must be named and changed. People are real, and people can be transformed and redeemed. Justice must happen, and reconciliation is the goal. Power structures should be flattened, and all people are equally beloved. These are not contradictions. They are the fuller vision of what the gospel offers compared to what any secular structure can offer.
