Second Round Expansions for Chapters 1, 8, 12

What CRT is not, the 2020-2024 evangelical panic, and the vision of beloved community

If you are going to disagree with critical race theory, disagree with what it actually says.

What CRT Is Not: The Straw Men That Block Engagement

The most common misrepresentation of critical race theory is that it says all white people are racist. It does not say that, and no major CRT scholar has ever made the claim. What CRT scholars do say is narrower and harder to dismiss: that systems operate along racial lines, that people in dominant groups benefit from those systems, and that this benefit accrues whether or not the individuals in those groups consciously intend it.

Consider an analogy. If you grow up in a house built on stolen land, the house is real, you benefit from living in it, and yet you did not personally steal the land; you are not guilty of theft. But you are benefiting from theft, and you have a choice about what to do with that knowledge. This is not the same as saying you are a racist. It is saying you are benefiting from racism. The two are different claims, and the distortion collapses them — substituting "you are racist" for what CRT actually argues, which is that the systems around you are structured in ways that benefit you because of your race.

The second misrepresentation is that CRT rejects individual agency — that it sees people as purely determined by systems, with no choice and no responsibility. This too is a distortion. Scholars like Derrick Bell and Kimberlé Crenshaw are clear that individuals have agency: people make choices, people resist systems, people work to change them. What CRT adds is that those choices do not happen in a vacuum; they are shaped by the systems a person is in. You can choose to be generous or selfish, but your generosity or selfishness plays out inside arrangements that privilege some and disadvantage others. Some people can choose to be poor and it is read as noble sacrifice; others are poor and it is read as personal failure. The individual choice is the same. The system interprets it differently. The claim is not that you have no agency. The claim is that your agency is exercised within structured constraints.

The third misrepresentation is that CRT is Marxism — addressed already, but worth naming here for how it shuts down honest engagement. If you decide CRT is Marxism, you decide it is un-American, that it comes from a foreign ideology, that it threatens democracy; and that frame stops you from engaging what CRT says about American systems. But CRT came out of American law schools, developed by American scholars trying to understand American legal history. It is as American as Brown v. Board of Education. The Marxism label is useful if your goal is to dismiss CRT without engaging its arguments. It is an obstacle if your goal is to understand them.

The real tragedy is that these misrepresentations have become so dominant that most people who attack CRT are not attacking what it says; they are attacking caricatures of it. That is intellectually dishonest. If you are going to disagree with CRT, disagree with what it actually says. Read Derrick Bell. Read Kimberlé Crenshaw. Read Richard Delgado. Then make your argument against what they actually wrote. Anything less is not thinking. It is just reaction.

The 2020-2024 Evangelical Response to CRT

In 2020, after George Floyd's murder, American churches were flooded with conversations about race. Many churches, evangelical ones included, issued statements acknowledging racism and committing to change. But as soon as "critical race theory" became a cultural flashpoint — as soon as politicians and media figures began using CRT as shorthand for anything touching race — the evangelical response shifted. Where there had been acknowledgment of racism, there was now fear of CRT; where there had been promises of change, there was now defensive posturing; where there had been openness to listening, there was now resistance to anything that could be labeled.

That shift revealed something. The commitment to racial justice was conditional — conditional on not threatening evangelical theology, not demanding structural change, not challenging evangelical leadership structures. Several leaders who had spoken about racial justice found themselves under fire from other evangelicals; some were fired from their positions, some left their denominations. The speed with which the conversation moved from "racism is wrong" to "CRT is dangerous" suggested the commitment had not been deep. The real story was not that CRT was dangerous. The real story was that evangelical institutions were terrified of any structure that would require them to acknowledge and change their own systems.

Consider what the labeling swept up: teachers who taught the honest history of America's racial past, targeted for teaching CRT; books about Black history pulled from school libraries as CRT; any acknowledgment of systemic racism, any call for diverse hiring, any mention of white privilege — all of it labeled CRT. A specific academic structure became shorthand for anything that makes white people uncomfortable about race. And many evangelical leaders, sensing the cultural moment, pivoted. Instead of continuing the conversation about racial justice, they began attacking CRT. Instead of asking how to change their institutions, they began asking how to defend their institutions against CRT.

What should have happened is not hard to state: evangelical leaders could have said they were committed to racial justice on the basis of Scripture, unconcerned with the political labeling of the conversation, committed to structural change in their own institutions. What largely happened instead was alignment with the panic and a defense of the status quo. The departures tell the story. Black evangelical pastors who had hoped 2020 would lead to change watched leadership reverse course; young evangelicals who thought the movement was bending toward justice were left disappointed. The scandal here is not that CRT exists. The scandal is that when pursuing racial justice became politically inconvenient, much of evangelical leadership abandoned it.

The Failure to Prophesy and the Cost of Safety

There are white evangelical leaders with platforms — books, podcasts, speaking engagements, influence — who could speak prophetically about race, about the evangelical alignment with white nationalism, about the failures of white evangelicalism. Most of them do not. The reason is plain: prophetic speech costs something. It makes people uncomfortable. It can mean loss of audience, loss of platform, loss of income. And so leadership has chosen safety over prophecy, comfort over truth-telling, alignment with power over standing with the vulnerable.

Some have tried to thread the needle, saying things like: racism is bad, but we should not talk about systemic racism; injustice is wrong, but we should not challenge the systems that perpetuate it; the gospel calls us to justice, but we should not be political. That is what compromise sounds like. It is what it looks like when someone wants the credibility of a prophetic voice without the cost of prophetic speech. Real prophecy does not thread the needle. It names the thing, it makes clear what is at stake, and it is willing to lose the platform.

Some white evangelical leaders have done exactly this. They have spoken clearly about the failures of white evangelicalism, and they have lost audience; some have lost speaking engagements, some have been attacked by other evangelical leaders. But they have kept their integrity, and the right to call their speech prophetic. To see more leaders do the same would mean using platforms to say that white evangelicalism has failed, that it has aligned with forces of oppression, that it has lost credibility by valuing safety and status over justice. It would mean leaders publicly repenting for their own participation in systems of injustice, acknowledging that their success and their platforms are built on systems that oppress, and calling out other leaders who have not repented — so that the tribe would no longer protect its own at the expense of justice. This is costly. It is what prophecy costs. Without it, the institution continues as it is: comfortable, defensive, and complicit.

Reading the Whole Bible as God Standing With the Vulnerable

To read the Bible seriously on race, we cannot read it the way white evangelical Christianity often has — as a text that confirms what it already believes. To read it seriously means letting it challenge what we believe, asking what the text is actually saying rather than what we want it to say. And read that way, the Bible is a story of God standing with the vulnerable. This is not a theme confined to a few verses. It is the consistent pattern of Scripture.

God hears the cry of enslaved people in Egypt, not the cry of Pharaoh. God stands with Moses — a fugitive from justice who has killed an Egyptian — not with the state apparatus that maintains slavery. When Israel enters the promised land, God commands jubilee: every fiftieth year, debts forgiven, land returned to its original owners, enslaved people set free. This is not optional. It is central to the law. The prophets do not spend their time comforting the comfortable; they spend it confronting the powerful — you have taken the land of the poor, you have made slaves of the vulnerable, the Lord will judge you. In the Gospels, Jesus's first sermon announces good news to the poor, release to the prisoners, sight to the blind, freedom for the oppressed; he spends his time with the vulnerable, and with the powerful only to confront them.

Read with that lens, the whole narrative looks different from what white evangelical Christianity has taught. You see a God who is not interested in maintaining order but in establishing justice. This requires reading the Bible in its own logic rather than importing modern categories — understanding the social structure of ancient Israel, what jubilee would have meant, what it meant for women and slaves and foreigners to be included in the covenant. It requires being willing to let the text say things that challenge your power and your comfort. To read the Bible seriously on race is not to hunt for verses that support your position; it is to ask what the whole narrative is saying, what God is doing throughout Scripture, and how that calls you to act. Read seriously, the Bible will not let you support systems that oppress, will not let you be comfortable with injustice, will not let you use Scripture to defend the status quo. That is why this kind of reading has so often been avoided. Because the Bible, read seriously, demands everything.

The Question Beneath the Question Is Power

The arguments about CRT are proxy arguments. They are not really about whether CRT is academically coherent or whether intersectionality is a useful analytical tool. They are about power. The real question runs underneath: if we acknowledge systemic racism, if we acknowledge that systems were designed to benefit white people and harm Black people, if we acknowledge that white Christians have been complicit in those systems, then what does that mean for our authority, our institutions, the way we have understood ourselves?

That question cannot be stated directly, so it gets stated through arguments about CRT — through claims that it is Marxist, un-American, dangerous. But the panic reveals what is actually feared: the loss of the ability to see oneself as inherently good, to see the institutions one built as inherently good, to see America as inherently good. If systemic racism is real, if systems were designed to exclude Black people and benefit white people, then white people cannot claim innocence, white institutions cannot claim neutrality, and America cannot claim to be a simple beacon of freedom. This is the argument evangelicals cannot articulate, because naming the fear would require admitting what they have been unwilling to acknowledge.

What is feared is not CRT. It is the possibility of having been on the wrong side of history, the possibility that the institutions perpetuated injustice, the possibility of having benefited from systems one claimed to oppose. That is the real conversation that needs to happen — not a debate about whether CRT is academically sound, but an honest reckoning: have we been complicit, have we benefited from injustice, are we willing to change? These are harder and costlier questions, so the debate about CRT becomes a way to avoid them. Anyone paying attention can see through it. A church that wants to do justice has to be willing to have the real argument, to ask whether it has benefited from injustice — the answer will almost certainly be yes — and then to do something about it. That, not the academic structure, is what the resistance to CRT is really about: the fear of what acknowledging the system would require.

What Other Churches Have Learned: South Africa, Brazil, and the UK

South Africa's churches faced a choice during apartheid: participate, or resist. Some resisted, some collaborated. After apartheid, the ones that had resisted — churches like the Dutch Reformed Mission Church and the Anglican Church — had credibility, because they had already paid a cost for their convictions, and they became leaders in the reconciliation work that followed. The lesson is sharp: a church that keeps its power while injustice continues loses its moral authority, and a church willing to sacrifice for justice gains it.

Brazil's churches have engaged race differently. Brazilian evangelicalism, with its large populations, has in many cases avoided the conversation by emphasizing spiritual equality — the message that in Christ, race does not matter. But that avoidance lets racial hierarchies stand, and in a country with significant racial inequality, churches that do not address racial injustice become complicit in it. The lesson: spiritual equality without structural justice is not the gospel. The gospel requires both.

The UK's churches have had to reckon with colonialism and immigration, and with more experience of non-white membership than many American churches, they have in some cases done better work on racism. But they also contain resistance — British nationalism, the desire to protect British identity — which can erect barriers to genuine racial justice. The lesson is that nationalism and racial justice sit in tension; a church identified with national identity will struggle to pursue justice when justice threatens that identity. American churches should learn from both the successes and the failures abroad: that the church which opposes injustice in real time has credibility, that spiritual equality without structural change is not the gospel, that nationalism and racial justice are in tension, and that reconciliation requires the perpetrator to pay a cost rather than the victim to forgive without the perpetrator changing. The most important lesson is that what the church does now shapes what it becomes. If the American church keeps choosing comfort over justice, it becomes a symbol of comfort rather than of the cross; if it is willing to sacrifice for justice, it can become a sign of what is possible when people take the gospel seriously. The choice is still in front of us.

Beyond Reconciliation: Toward Beloved Community

Reconciliation is the goal white evangelicals most often hold out. It sounds like healing; it sounds like moving forward together. But reconciliation, as it is usually understood, is too small. It can mean that the oppressor and the oppressed agree to stop fighting and move on — which does not heal what was broken or restore what was taken. Cornel West offers a larger vision. He speaks of beloved community, which is more than reconciliation: it is the possibility of genuine love across difference, of people who have been enemies becoming family. This is more than white people and Black people deciding to be civil; it is the possibility of actually loving one another, caring for one another's flourishing, being willing to sacrifice for one another. The Black church kept this vision alive even when white Christianity abandoned it, believing that genuine community was possible and that enemies could become family in Christ.

But beloved community has a requirement. It asks the people who have benefited from injustice to give something up, the comfortable to become uncomfortable, the powerful to share power. Most churches are not willing. Most want reconciliation without cost — to reconcile with Black people without changing how power operates. Beloved community is not possible while white leadership keeps control, while resources stay concentrated in white hands. So what would it look like in an American church? It would look like white Christians sitting with Black Christians and hearing the stories of what was done; genuine grief over what the church has done; a willingness to make things right that goes far beyond a sermon or a statement. It would look like white Christians becoming the minority in their own churches, power actually shifting, the perspective of Black Christians shaping the life of the church rather than the one Sunday a year that highlights diversity.

It would look like a church willing to become something radically different from what it was — because beloved community is not reconciliation to the old thing; it is the creation of something new. And that points to the crisis evangelicalism cannot evade. Young people are leaving, credibility is eroding, the institution is declining. Some blame secularization, some the rise of the nones, some broad cultural trends. But the core crisis is credibility. Evangelicalism has lost moral credibility because it defended systems of injustice, and it has lost the ability to speak prophetically because it aligned itself with power. Young people see it: that evangelicals defended segregation academies while claiming to follow the Jesus who broke every boundary of separation, that evangelicals defend the existing order while claiming to follow the Jesus who cared for the poor, that evangelicals stay silent on racism while claiming to preach the whole gospel. The disconnect is real, and the young are not wrong about it.

Can evangelicalism survive without transformation? Possibly — as a subculture, as a political movement, as an institution. But it will not survive as a prophetic movement, as a credible voice on morality, as a witness to the gospel. Rebuilding that credibility would take three things. First, an honest reckoning with history: acknowledging complicity in slavery, segregation, and systemic injustice, that evangelical leaders defended these systems and evangelicals benefited from them — which requires more than a statement; it requires that every pastor, educator, and leader learn the real history. Second, structural change: putting people of color in positions of real authority rather than token ones, and redistributing resources from white evangelical institutions to Black ones. Third, prophetic leadership willing to challenge its own movement and to lose white members who reject the message. This is costly, which is why most leaders will not do it. But without it, evangelicalism will keep declining and grow more identified with nationalism and the seeking of political power, losing the possibility of being a prophetic voice. The choice is before the movement now. It can transform or it can decline. But transformation requires genuine repentance, and genuine repentance requires change.
