Expansions 4

Chapters 10-12 and Conclusion

Truth and reconciliation, the practical theology of reparations, and the cost of discipleship

Cheap reconciliation costs nothing. Genuine reconciliation costs everything.

The Truth and Reconciliation Model and a Church's Apology

When apartheid ended, South Africa faced a choice between retribution and reconciliation. The country could have hunted down every person involved in apartheid violence and executed them, and the victims would have had that justice; instead, South Africa chose a different path. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission was a subversive idea: perpetrators of apartheid violence would confess what they had done, telling the truth in public, while victims listened, asked questions, and in some cases watched amnesty granted to those who confessed fully.

This was not cheap grace. It required the perpetrator to face the person they had harmed, to speak the truth about what they had done, to stand in vulnerability and exposure. For the victim, it meant hearing the confession, getting a chance to speak, and receiving public acknowledgment that what happened was real and was wrong. Some perpetrators refused to confess and faced prosecution; some victims could not accept the confession even when it was offered, and sought prosecution instead. But the structure was clear: truth first, then reconciliation: justice through acknowledgment and change, not through retribution.

What would an American version look like? Imagine evangelical leaders going to Black churches and saying plainly: here is what we did. We defended slavery, created segregation academies, remained silent during violence, built wealth from systems of injustice, and taught theology that justified oppression. We did this. We benefited. We have not changed. Now what — what would reconciliation require?

Briarwood Presbyterian Church in Birmingham, Alabama offers a small example. Founded in 1971 in explicit response to desegregation, Briarwood came into being when parents pulled their children out of integrated public schools to maintain segregation. In 2021, the church's pastor stood up and apologized; the church officially acknowledged and repented of its founding as a segregation academy, conceding that it had been created to support white flight and to maintain segregation. This is what genuine acknowledgment looks like. Not: we had some members who were racist. But: the institution itself was created for racist purposes.

The Practical Theology of Reparations: Repair, Not Charity

Acknowledgment is only the beginning of reconciliation; the question that follows is what to do about it, and this is where reparations enter. Reparations is not about money; it is about structural change, about the perpetrator attempting to repair what was broken. If Briarwood was founded to benefit white families by allowing them to escape integration, then reparations from Briarwood might mean financially supporting integrated public schools, committing a percentage of its budget to schools in historically Black neighborhoods, and working to actually become integrated, not just in membership but in decision-making power. If churches benefited from slavery and segregation, then reparations from those churches might mean funding Black churches, closing the wealth gap between white and Black congregations, and redistributing power and resources.

Reparations is a confusing concept for white evangelicals because evangelicals have been taught that justice is about individual guilt and forgiveness. From that vantage, reparations make no sense: if I personally did not enslave anyone, why should I pay, and if a Black person in my church was never enslaved, why would they receive anything? But this misses what reparations actually are. They are not about individual guilt; they are about systemic harm and systemic repair.

The practical theology runs in four movements. First, acknowledge what was taken: slavery extracted trillions of dollars of labor from African Americans, segregation extracted opportunity and resources and dignity, redlining extracted the very ability to build wealth; these were massive systemic extractions, not small harms. Second, acknowledge the ongoing benefit: white Americans have benefited from this extraction whether they intended to or not, because white neighborhoods exist on account of redlining that excluded Black people, white schools received resources because segregated funding favored them, and white wealth accumulated because slavery was free labor and segregation prevented Black families from accumulating any. Third, acknowledge that the harm continues: the effects of slavery, segregation, and redlining are not historical but current, experienced by anyone born into a segregated neighborhood, into a family with less wealth, into a community with fewer resources. Fourth, recognize that repair is necessary; repair is not charity, not feeling sorry, but systematic restitution for what was taken.

What might this look like at a church level? A church located in a neighborhood preserved through redlining, benefiting from property values kept stable because Black people were excluded, could acknowledge the history explicitly: "Our church building is located on a street that was preserved as all-white through FHA redlining. This exclusion created stable property values for white homeowners and prevented wealth accumulation by Black families. We acknowledge this history and our church's role in it." It could commit perhaps fifteen to twenty percent of its annual budget to repair work: funding Black churches in disinvested neighborhoods, funding schools there, funding economic development led by people of color. It could work with the community on what repair means rather than assuming it knows, asking what is actually needed. And it could change its own structure so that power is genuinely shared with the communities being repaired, not a white-led charity initiative but a partnership in which the people harmed by the system hold decision-making authority.

Most churches will not do this, because most churches prefer charity. Charity allows the giver to feel good, requires no structural change, and demands no shift in power. Reparations requires all of it: that you acknowledge what you did, pay what you owe, and change the systems that perpetuated the harm. This is what justice requires. This is what the gospel requires. This is the work that would make the American church prophetic and credible. Not: let's move forward and not talk about the past. But: let's acknowledge what happened, understand what it cost, and change the systems that benefited from it. This is costly, which is exactly why cheap reconciliation is so appealing.

Building Multiethnic Churches Without Tokenizing

A white pastor can preach a sermon series called "Justice and Reconciliation" without changing anything about the church. The preaching can be eloquent, the condemnation of systemic racism sincere, the call to action stirring, and then the budget stays the same, the leadership stays the same, and the congregation stays segregated. Real change starts when the pastor makes clear that this is not an intellectual exercise, that justice is central to what the church is about, not a special series but the ongoing work of the church.

One structure is a cross-racial pastoral partnership: a white pastor and a Black pastor sharing leadership, not a Black assistant under a white senior pastor, but actual shared preaching and shared decision-making power. This is uncomfortable, because it requires the white pastor to relinquish control, to accept that their perspective is not the only one that matters, to be challenged. It also requires the denomination or board to value the partnership enough to hire and pay equally; if the Black pastor is always the assistant, always paid less, always holding less authority, then it is not a partnership at all.

Budget tells the truth about priorities. A church that says it values justice but allocates two percent of its budget to justice work and fifty percent to building maintenance has revealed its real priorities in its ledger. Allocating resources to justice might mean directing fifteen to twenty percent of the annual budget to racial justice work, funding Black churches in the community, supporting Black pastors through education and mentorship, investing in historically disinvested neighborhoods, and paying diverse staff equally and generously. This is not charity. This is repair. This is the attempt to restore what was taken.

Hiring practices reveal who a church actually values. A church that claims to value diversity while its hiring process remains all-white gatekeeping (where only people who look, sound, and think like the current leadership get hired) is not committed to diversity. Real commitment means recruiting from Black seminaries and Bible colleges, valuing different theological perspectives and different ways of articulating faith, hiring people who will challenge the status quo, paying fairly enough to attract talented people, and committing to retention rather than hiring someone and then creating an unwelcoming environment so they leave.

Building a multiethnic church without tokenizing is the hardest work. Tokenizing is when a church brings in a few people of color, features them prominently in marketing, and keeps them excluded from real power; the church gets to call itself diverse while maintaining white control. Genuine multiethnic community means people of color in leadership, including senior leadership; worship that reflects diverse traditions rather than white evangelical style with a few gospel songs added; music and preaching from diverse perspectives; children's and youth ministry that reflects diverse representation; small groups and fellowship that intentionally cross racial lines; and the clarity that this is not a program but who we are.

There is one more layer, and it is the sharpest. A church that is actually committed to justice will need to be willing to lose members. Some white members will leave if they perceive the church as too political, too focused on race, too willing to challenge the status quo; that is the cost. But keep those members and you sacrifice the possibility of genuine multiethnic community; you remain segregated, you remain comfortable. A church serious about justice has to choose. You choose either comfort or integrity.

Institutionalizing Justice Beyond the Sermon Series

Most churches that want to pursue racial justice begin with a sermon series or a study or a statement, something visible, something that can be pointed to as evidence of commitment. But a sermon series is not a system; a sermon series is words, and systems are what remain after the series ends. So how do you know whether your church is actually committed to justice or merely performing it? You look at the budget, because the budget never lies and tells you what a church actually values. A church that claims commitment to racial justice but spends two percent on justice work and seventy percent on building maintenance has been answered by its own ledger; a church that claims commitment to diversity but pays white staff better than Black staff has been answered by its payroll; a church that claims commitment to community but does nothing to repair the neighborhoods where Black members live has been answered by its inaction.

Institutionalizing justice means building systems that survive the sermon series, systems that remain when the pastor who preached about justice leaves, systems that continue when the cultural moment shifts. In practice that means several things. First, a racial equity committee with actual power, not one that meets monthly to discuss equity while real authority stays with white leadership, but a committee with decision-making power over hiring, budget allocation, and strategic direction. Second, measurable goals with accountability, not vague commitments to "become more diverse" but specifics: by 2028, twenty-five percent of leadership positions held by people of color; by 2030, a budget reflecting a twenty percent allocation to justice work; by 2027, an established reparations fund with a set amount allocated to it. Third, external accountability: inviting outside evaluators to assess whether the church is actually pursuing justice or just talking about it, rather than relying on internal evaluation alone. Fourth, staffing that ensures continuity: a director of racial justice whose whole job is to oversee the systems so that justice becomes embedded in how the church operates rather than a special project. Fifth, financial investment in communities that have been harmed, not individual charity but systemic investment in schools, housing, and small businesses in historically disinvested neighborhoods.

This is what institutionalization looks like, and it is not comfortable; it requires resources, and it requires power to actually shift. The hard truth is that most churches are not willing to do it. Most churches want the prophetic witness without the structural cost, want to say they care about justice without actually changing. Until churches are willing to institutionalize justice, the work will not happen.

The Cost of Discipleship and the Next Generation

A white pastor who leads a church toward racial justice will face real resistance, from members who leave, from other pastors who question his commitment to Scripture, from denominational pressure. The pastor has to be willing to pay that cost, not out of a martyr complex but out of a realistic understanding that if you challenge comfortable Christianity, comfortable Christianity will push back. He will also face pressure from Black members and Black community leaders to go deeper, to do more, to cost more, and they will be right; that pressure is justified.

A pastor serious about this will need community: a pastors' group or a coaching relationship with others doing similar work, accountability, and the willingness to be challenged especially by Black pastors so that he does not slide back into comfort. He will need to be willing to be taught: to read Black theologians, to listen to the concerns of Black members without defensiveness, to be wrong about things he thought he understood. This is demanding work that white evangelical culture does not support; that culture rewards pastors who build big churches, hold a platform, and become thought leaders, not pastors who challenge white evangelicalism itself. But this is the work. This is what it means to take the gospel seriously.

What sustains a pastor in this work is the knowledge that it is true; that what the prophets say is true, that what James Cone says is true, that what the Black church has known all along is true. The gospel is bigger than white evangelicalism; it is not captive to white evangelical culture; it breaks free from every culture that tries to claim it and stands in judgment over every human system. A pastor who really believes this will be willing to lead his church in this direction, whatever the cost.

And the next generation is watching. What are we telling young Christians about the gospel when we preach about justice on Sunday and stay silent about injustice in our community on Monday? What are we telling them about integrity when we say racism is wrong but defend every system that perpetuates it? What are we telling them about the truthfulness of Scripture when we refuse to let Scripture speak to the most visible injustice in our country? Young Christians are leaving the church, and some leave precisely because they see the gap between what we say and what we do: a church that talks about justice for Instagram posts but stays segregated on Sunday morning, that condemns racism as sin but supports the political party most committed to opposing racial justice work, that preaches love and practices exclusion.

The institutional church is hemorrhaging young Christians, and the reason is not that Christianity is false; the reason is that institutional Christianity has become comfortable with injustice in a way they find impossible to stomach. They do not want to belong to a religion comfortable with injustice, a religion that claims to love Jesus but refuses to do what Jesus did: stand with the vulnerable, challenge the powerful, announce good news to the poor. If the church is to have a future with them, it has to change: to challenge the systems that oppress rather than defend them, to stand with the vulnerable rather than the powerful, to be willing to cost something. This is not about being hip or relevant. This is about being faithful.

Bonhoeffer wrote about the cost of discipleship from inside Nazi Germany, watching the German church collaborate with Nazi ideology, writing to pastors who wanted to follow Jesus in a context of totalitarian evil. His point was that discipleship costs something: that it is not about personal salvation but about following Jesus into conflict with the systems of power. The American church has largely lost this understanding. American evangelicalism preaches that following Jesus leads to blessing: follow Jesus and you will be happy, your life will improve, you will have peace. This is a prosperity gospel, a gospel domesticated and adapted to fit American consumer capitalism.

But the gospel Jesus preached was not a gospel of blessing; it was a gospel of challenge.

If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me.

Mark 8:34

The cross is not a metaphor. The cross is death. The cross is what happens to people who challenge power.

Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account.

Matthew 5:11

Jesus did not say: follow me and you will be successful. He said: follow me and you will face opposition, follow me and you will be persecuted, follow me and you might die. In the context of systemic injustice this means that discipleship includes challenging unjust systems, standing with the vulnerable, and accepting the cost of opposition. A pastor who challenges his denomination's complicity in racism will face opposition; a pastor who leads his church toward justice will lose members; a pastor who redistributes resources will face criticism from wealthy donors. This is the cost of discipleship: what following Jesus looks like where power structures are invested in the status quo.

The American church has avoided this cost, preaching a gospel of comfort and asking how one might follow Jesus in a way that costs nothing. But that is not following Jesus; that is using Jesus as a spiritual comfort object, making him a servant of your own interests. Real discipleship means accepting that you might lose something, that you might be opposed, that you might be handed the cross. This is what the American church needs to recover, not a gospel of blessing but a gospel of challenge, not a gospel of comfort but a gospel of costly change. If the American church offered this, it would be powerful; it would be a sign of what is possible when people actually commit their lives to something beyond themselves.

The choice is before the American church now. A pastor has to choose: am I going to lead my church toward comfort or toward faithfulness; am I going to defend the systems we have benefited from, or work toward their undoing? These are not complicated questions, and the answers are clear. What is unclear is whether pastors will have the courage to live out the answers.
