Chapter 11

Raising Children to See Race Biblically

A path between colorblindness and critical theory for Christian parents

Your child is going to notice that people have different skin colors, that some people are treated differently, that certain faces are missing from the rooms where decisions get made. That noticing will not wait for your permission; it will arrive through peers, through school, through media, through the ordinary traffic of a world your child is learning to read. The only question left to you is the one you actually get to answer: what will you, as a Christian parent, teach your child about race?

Most Christian parents have worked from one of two frameworks, and each one feels, from the inside, like the responsible choice. The colorblind framework says we do not notice race, we treat everyone the same, race does not matter. The critical framework says race is the primary lens through which we understand everything, that power dynamics are rooted in race, and that you must actively work to dismantle racism. Both contain something true. Neither, taken whole, is biblical, and neither is adequate to the child in front of you.

The only question left to you is the one you actually get to answer: what will you teach your child about race?

The Problem With Colorblindness

Colorblindness sounds beautiful. It sounds like the dream Dr. King named on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial—that people would be judged not by the color of their skin but by the content of their character. The aspiration is real, and it is worth honoring. But the framework that grew up around it is not what the Bible teaches. Scripture does not ask us to stop seeing color; it tells us that God saw the color, saw the ethnicity, and called it good.

From one man he made all the nations, that they should inhabit the whole earth; and he marked out their appointed times in history and the boundaries of their lands.

Acts 17:26

God made the nations. God made the diversity. God looked at what he had made and said it was very good. To teach a child not to see what God created as good is not neutral instruction; it trains the eye away from something Scripture treats as a gift. And it asks the child to pretend race does not matter at the very moment race is producing real consequences in the world around them.

Consider the child who asks why all the presidents look like grandpa. If your answer is that we do not notice those things, you have not protected innocence—you have taught a small person to stop seeing, to stop tracking patterns, to stop asking why the world is ordered the way it is. That is not the preservation of innocence; it is the manufacture of blindness, and it quietly disables the very capacity a child needs in order to recognize injustice later. Colorblindness carries a second cost, too, one borne unequally: tell a child of color that you do not notice race, and you have told that child you will not acknowledge an important part of who they are, that their experience of the world—which includes their race—is something you would rather not see. Colorblindness is not a virtue. It is a failure to see.

The Problem With Uncritical Adoption of CRT

The opposite error is just as available. Adopt the system of Critical Race Theory wholesale and you teach your child that identity is primarily about power, that the self and everyone else are to be read first through the lens of oppression or being oppressed. This creates its own distortions. It can teach a child of color that their primary identity is victim; it can teach a white child that their primary identity is oppressor; and it can keep both from seeing the full complexity of a human person.

The deeper problem lies underneath those effects. To absorb the framework uncritically is to teach a child that the human person is, at bottom, a product of systems—that what finally matters is the group you belong to rather than the God whose image you bear. That is not biblical either. Not the white person. Not the Black person. Every person bears the image of God, and any account of race that loses hold of that is an account the church cannot hand to its children.

What Biblical Parenting About Race Looks Like

Biblical parenting about race starts with one foundational claim: every person bears the image of God. Not the white person. Not the Black person. Every person—male and female, rich and poor, powerful and marginalized—is God's beloved, created in God's image. From that foundation, several things can be taught, and they hold together only because they rest on it.

First, ethnicity is real and beautiful. You can teach your child about different cultures—the food, the music, the traditions, the particular beauty of different peoples—and you can teach that ethnicity is a gift, not a problem. If your child is of color, you can teach them to be proud of their heritage, to know the contributions their people have made and the strength of their traditions. If your child is white, you can teach them about their heritage too: not as superior, not as the default human, but as theirs—one particular way of being human among many.

Second, race creates real consequences in the world, and your child needs to hear you name it in age-appropriate ways. When your child comes home and says the principal is always a white man, the answer is not that we do not notice those things. The answer is closer to this: you are right; most principals in America are white men; that is because of history, and here is why. You teach your child to see patterns, to ask questions, to notice when certain groups are overrepresented in positions of power.

Teaching Responsibility Without Shame

Third, being benefited by injustice does not make you bad. If your child is white and you are teaching them about racial injustice, you must be careful not to teach them that they are bad because they are white. That is not biblical, and it produces shame where it should produce responsibility. There is a better sentence to give a child: you have benefited from systems that were unfair to other people; that is not your fault; your ancestors did not create those systems, but they benefited from them, and you benefit from them now—you did not choose that, but now that you know, you have a choice about whether you will work to change them. You teach responsibility, not shame.

Fourth, we are all called to work for justice. The God of the Bible is a God of justice who cares about systems that exploit the vulnerable and who calls his people to pursue what is right. You can teach your child that justice is not political but biblical—that it means pursuing systems that are fair to everyone, and using whatever power or privilege you have to help people who have less. You can teach your child to pay attention to what is fair and unfair, to speak up when something is unfair, and to understand that sometimes doing the right thing costs something.

Fifth, reconciliation is possible through the gospel, and here you move past anything a secular framework can offer. You teach your child that the gospel makes possible what politics cannot—genuine reconciliation, the kind in which people divided by injustice actually become family again. Not by ignoring the injustice, but by moving through confession, restitution, and forgiveness. You teach your child that there is hope: not the cheap hope that says everything is fine now, but the costly hope that says the work is hard and possible, and that Christ has already paid the ultimate price for reconciliation.

Practical Ways to Teach Children About Race

How do you actually do this? Mostly in the ordinary furniture of a household. Read diverse stories, so that the books in your home include Black protagonists, Asian protagonists, Latino protagonists—people of color as the main characters, not merely the supporting ones. Celebrate diverse holidays; if you are white, celebrate your own heritage, but also learn and celebrate the heritage of your neighbors and friends, not as exotic curiosities but as legitimate parts of your community's life.

Build diverse friendships, and take seriously what it means if your child has only white friends—not because white friends are bad, but because a child who never crosses that line is not learning to see the full humanity of people different from themselves. So build those friendships on purpose: go to churches that are integrated, schools that are diverse, neighborhoods that are not segregated. And have honest conversations. When your child asks questions about race, answer them honestly; do not deflect, do not say we do not talk about those things, do not pretend the questions do not exist.

Name injustice when you see it. When a child of color is treated unfairly, name it; when a system is working against a particular group, name it; model for your child that noticing and naming injustice is normal and healthy. Teach them their own history, and teach it real rather than sanitized. If your child is of color, teach them the contributions their people have made, the struggles their people have faced, the figures from their tradition who worked for justice—and teach them about slavery and segregation and the people who resisted and fought for freedom. If your child is white, teach them their real history too: where the family came from, the family's relationship to issues of race, the complicity where it is there and the resistance where it is there.

Above all, do justice, do not merely teach it, because your actions teach more than your words. Teach your child to work for justice while you work to maintain segregation, and your child will notice the contradiction. Teach your child that race matters while your church stays all white, and your child will notice. Teach your child that all people are equal while voting for policies that harm people of color, and your child will notice. Live what you teach. That is where the real teaching happens.

The Risk and the Hope

None of this is safe. Teaching a child to see race clearly and to work for justice carries a real risk: your child might get angry, might grow disillusioned with injustice, might turn and demand that you yourself do more to change things. That is the risk. But it is a good risk, and the alternative is worse—a child raised blind to injustice, unable to see other people fully, comfortable with the way things are.

The hope runs the other direction. It is the hope of raising a child who sees clearly, who loves fully, who works for justice, who believes reconciliation is possible, and who trusts that God is in control even when the work is hard. That is what biblical parenting about race looks like.
