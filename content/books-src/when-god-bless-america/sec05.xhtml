Chapter Four

"Jesus Is Lord"—The Most Dangerous Sentence in the Empire

"To confess 'Jesus is Lord' was not a private spiritual sentiment. It was a public act of political defiance."

The Sentence That Could Kill You

In the modern American church, the phrase "Jesus is Lord" is the most familiar and least understood confession in all of Christianity.

We say it in worship songs, print it on coffee mugs, embroider it on throw pillows, and post it on social media with sunset backgrounds and curated fonts. It appears on bumper stickers and T-shirts and church marquees. It is so ubiquitous, so comfortable, so thoroughly domesticated that it has lost virtually all of its original power.

And that loss of power is not accidental. It is, in fact, the most successful act of theological neutralization in the history of the church.

Because in its original context, "Jesus is Lord" was not a worship lyric, a devotional sentiment, or a statement of personal spiritual preference. It was a political claim that could, and did, get you killed.

To understand why, you need to understand the world in which the earliest Christians lived. You need to understand what the word Lord meant before it became a hymnal cliché. And you need to understand what it cost to say it.

In the first-century Roman Empire, there was one sentence that every citizen, every subject, every conquered people was expected to affirm. It was not a request. It was the price of participation in Roman society. And the sentence was this:

"Kaiser kyrios." Caesar is Lord.

This was not merely a political statement, though it was certainly that. It was a theological claim. The Roman emperor was not simply a political ruler. He was divus, divine. Beginning with Augustus, the imperial cult presented the emperor as a god, or at least as the son of a god, worthy of worship, offering, and absolute allegiance. Temples were built to the emperor, festivals celebrated his divine status, and incense was burned before his image. The phrase Kaiser kyrios was the loyalty oath of the empire, the verbal act of submission that marked you as a good Roman, a loyal subject, a person who knew your place in the cosmic order.

And into that world, the earliest Christians walked with a counter-confession that constituted treason:

"Iesous kyrios." Jesus is Lord.

It was the same word and the same grammatical construction, directly and deliberately contradictory. To say "Jesus is Lord" in the first century was to say, simultaneously and necessarily, "Caesar is not Lord." It was not a statement about your personal devotional life. It was a public declaration that the ultimate authority in the universe was not the man on the throne in Rome but the man on the cross outside Jerusalem, and that this crucified man had been raised from the dead and was now seated at the right hand of God, ruling over every principality and power, including the one that thought it ruled the world.

That is what the earliest Christians meant when they said "Jesus is Lord." And the Roman Empire understood perfectly.

Treason in Thessalonica: Acts 17 and the Political Gospel

The book of Acts gives us a window into exactly how the Roman world heard the early Christian message. And what they heard was not a private religion. They heard a political revolution.

In Acts 17, Paul and Silas arrive in Thessalonica. Paul does what he always does: he goes to the synagogue and reasons from the Scriptures, explaining that the Messiah had to suffer, die, and rise again, and that "this Jesus, whom I proclaim to you, is the Christ" (Acts 17:3). Some Jews and some Greeks are persuaded. And a mob forms.

The opponents drag some of the new believers before the city authorities. And the accusation they bring is one of the most revealing sentences in the entire New Testament:

"These men who have turned the world upside down have come here also, and Jason has received them, and they are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus." —Acts 17:6–7

Read that accusation carefully. The opponents of the gospel do not say, "These men are teaching a new philosophy." They do not say, "These men have an interesting private spirituality." They say, "These men are acting against the decrees of Caesar" and "saying that there is another king."

They understood the gospel perfectly, better, in fact, than many modern Christians do.

The accusation is that the early Christians are proclaiming a rival king: not a rival spiritual guru, not a rival moral teacher, not a rival source of personal peace and inner fulfillment, but a rival king, one whose authority supersedes Caesar's, whose kingdom renders the Roman Empire penultimate, whose decrees override the decrees of the most powerful government on earth.

The phrase "turned the world upside down" is itself revealing. The Greek word is anastatoo, to disturb, to unsettle, to cause political upheaval. These are not people engaging in quiet spiritual devotion. They are people whose message has the power to restructure the political order. And the Roman authorities know it.

Luke, the author of Acts, records this accusation without correcting it. He does not say, "Of course, they misunderstood, the gospel is not political." He does not explain that the kingdom of God is purely spiritual and has no implications for earthly power. He simply records the accusation and moves on, because the accusation is essentially correct. The early Christians were proclaiming another king. They were announcing a kingdom that relativized every earthly government. They were living in a way that undermined the absolute claims of the Roman state.

The difference, and this is the critical distinction for our moment, is in how they were doing it. They were not forming a political party, running candidates, lobbying for legislative influence, or building a voting bloc. They were not making alliances with sympathetic Roman officials in exchange for protection and cultural power. They were worshiping, sharing meals, caring for the poor, and telling the truth about who was actually in charge. And the empire found that more threatening than an army.

The Hymn That Dethroned Caesar: Philippians 2

Philippians 2:5–11 is one of the most magnificent passages in the New Testament. It is often called the Carmen Christi, the Hymn of Christ, and it may well be the oldest piece of Christian liturgy we possess, predating even Paul's letter. It is a theological masterpiece. And it is an act of political subversion disguised as a worship song.

To hear it the way the Philippians heard it, you need to know one thing about Philippi: it was a Roman colony. Not just a city in the empire, a colonia, a settlement of Roman military veterans who had been given land as a reward for service. Philippi was proud of its Roman identity. Its citizens had Roman rights. Its public life was saturated with Roman culture, Roman law, and Roman religion, including the imperial cult. To live in Philippi was to live surrounded by reminders that Caesar was lord.

And into that context, Paul writes this:

"Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross." —Philippians 2:5–8

This is already a radical claim. Jesus, who possessed divine equality, did not grasp at it, did not use it as a tool of domination, did not exploit it for power. Instead, He emptied Himself and became a servant. He submitted to the most humiliating form of execution the Roman Empire had devised, a death reserved for slaves, rebels, and the lowest of criminals.

But it is the next verses that would have sent a shockwave through the Roman colony:

"Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." —Philippians 2:9–11

Every knee will bow. Every tongue will confess. Jesus Christ is Lord.

In a Roman colony where every citizen was expected to bend the knee to Caesar, Paul is announcing that there is a name higher than Caesar's. In a city built on imperial loyalty, he is declaring that the ultimate confession of the universe is not Kaiser kyrios but Iesous Christos kyrios. In a culture that exalted power, military conquest, and political domination, Paul says the one who was exalted above all things is the one who emptied Himself and became a servant and died on a cross.

This is not merely theology. It is a complete inversion of the Roman value system. Rome said greatness comes through power. Paul says greatness comes through service. Rome said the path to lordship is domination. Paul says the path to lordship is self-emptying. Rome said the cross is the ultimate shame, the mark of defeat, powerlessness, and subhumanity. Paul says the cross is the doorway to the highest exaltation in the cosmos.

And the claim that every knee will bow, not just willing knees, not just believing knees, but every knee, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, is a direct challenge to the totality of Caesar's claims. Caesar demanded universal allegiance. Paul says someone else has it. Caesar claimed divine authority. Paul says the truly divine one chose humiliation. Caesar built his empire on the sword. Paul says the true kingdom was built on a cross.

The Philippian Christians would have heard this not as abstract theology but as a manifesto. They would have understood, with perfect clarity, that to sing this hymn was to commit an act of allegiance that put them at odds with the most powerful empire on earth. And they sang it anyway.

The True King of Kings: Revelation's Counter-Imperial Vision

If Acts shows how the Roman world heard the Christian confession, and Philippians shows how Christians sang it, then Revelation shows how they hoped in it, especially under the pressure of active persecution.

The book of Revelation was written during a period of intense conflict between the early church and the Roman state. The precise date is debated, but the political reality is clear: Christians were being pressured, sometimes by social exclusion, sometimes by economic penalty, sometimes by imprisonment and death, to participate in the imperial cult, to burn incense before the emperor's image, to say Kaiser kyrios. And they were refusing.

Into that context, John writes an apocalyptic vision that is, among many other things, the most sustained piece of political resistance literature in the Bible. And at its climax, John sees the true ruler of the world:

"Then I saw heaven opened, and behold, a white horse! The one sitting on it is called Faithful and True, and in righteousness he judges and makes war... On his robe and on his thigh he has a name written, King of kings and Lord of lords." —Revelation 19:11, 16

King of kings. Lord of lords. These were not original phrases. They were imperial titles. The Roman emperor styled himself as the supreme ruler over all lesser kings and lords. Revelation takes those titles and reassigns them, not to the man on the throne in Rome, but to the Lamb who was slain. The entire book is structured as a counter-narrative to imperial propaganda. Where Rome says, "We bring peace through military conquest," Revelation says the true peace comes through a slaughtered Lamb. Where Rome says, "The emperor is the savior of the world," Revelation says the savior was crucified by the empire. Where Rome says, "Our kingdom will last forever," Revelation says Babylon, Rome's code name, will fall.

But here is what makes Revelation so important for our discussion: the weapon the King of Kings carries is not a sword wielded in violence. It is a sword proceeding from His mouth: His word, His truth, His declaration of how things really are. The conquering is not done through military force or political maneuvering. It is done through the proclamation of truth and the faithfulness of the saints who refuse to bow to the beast, even when it costs them everything.

The martyrs in Revelation conquer through their testimony and through the blood of the Lamb (Revelation 12:11). They overcome not by seizing political power but by refusing to surrender their confession. They win not by electing a friendlier emperor but by maintaining their allegiance to a different King. Their political action is worship, their resistance is faithfulness, and their revolution is the refusal to say "Caesar is lord" even when saying "Jesus is Lord" means death.

That is the political vision of the New Testament. And it looks nothing like what American Christianity has become.

How the Most Dangerous Sentence Became the Safest

So how did we get from there to here? How did "Jesus is Lord", a confession that overthrew empires, sustained martyrs, and functioned as the most subversive political statement in the ancient world, become a bumper sticker? The story is complex, but the trajectory is clear, and it begins with Constantine.

In 313 AD, the Emperor Constantine issued the Edict of Milan, ending the persecution of Christians and granting them legal toleration. Within a generation, Christianity went from a persecuted minority faith to the official religion of the Roman Empire. And everything changed.

On one level, this was an extraordinary gift. Persecution ended and churches could be built. The Scriptures could be copied and distributed without fear, theological councils could meet to clarify doctrine, and the gospel could spread without the constant threat of imprisonment or death.

But on another level, something was lost, something that the church has never fully recovered. When Christianity became the religion of the empire, the confession "Jesus is Lord" lost its edge. Because when Caesar himself claims to be a Christian, saying "Jesus is Lord" no longer implies "Caesar is not." The confession is no longer a counter-claim. It is a reinforcement of the existing order. Jesus is Lord and Caesar is His representative. The kingdom of God and the kingdom of Rome are on the same side. The cross and the sword serve the same master.

This is what historians call the Constantinian settlement, and its effects have shaped Western Christianity for seventeen centuries. The church gained access to power, protection, and cultural influence. In exchange, it gradually lost the ability to speak prophetically to power, because it was in power. It lost the ability to critique the empire, because it was the empire's religion. It lost the ability to say "Caesar is not lord", because Caesar was sitting in the front pew.

The domestication deepened over centuries. In medieval Christendom, the church and the state became so thoroughly intertwined that it was virtually impossible to distinguish between them. The pope crowned emperors, and emperors enforced theological orthodoxy. The sword served the altar, and the altar blessed the sword. "Jesus is Lord" no longer functioned as a claim that relativized all earthly power. It functioned as the religious foundation for earthly power.

The Reformation challenged aspects of this arrangement but did not overthrow it. Luther depended on German princes for protection. Calvin shaped civic structures in Geneva. The Church of England was a state church by design. Reformation Christianity, for all its theological radicalism, remained largely Constantinian in its understanding of the relationship between church and political power.

And when European Christians came to America, they brought the Constantinian instinct with them. The specific arrangements varied, the First Amendment formally separated church and state in ways that were genuinely revolutionary. But the cultural assumption persisted: America is a Christian nation. Our government reflects Christian values. Our laws are built on Christian foundations. "Jesus is Lord" means "America is blessed." The confession no longer challenges the nation. It baptizes it.

That is how we arrived at our current moment, where "Jesus is Lord" can appear on the same flag as a political candidate's name without anyone sensing a contradiction, where a confession that once meant "I will die before I grant ultimate allegiance to any earthly ruler" now means "My preferred candidate shares my values," where the most subversive sentence in the history of the world has been reduced to a tribal identifier.

The domestication is complete. And it is killing us.

What We Lost When We Tamed the Confession

When "Jesus is Lord" stopped meaning "Caesar is not," we lost several things that the American church desperately needs to recover.

We lost the ability to critique our own nation prophetically. If "Jesus is Lord" and "America is a Christian nation" are functionally the same confession, then criticizing America feels like criticizing Jesus. Patriotism and discipleship merge. National interest and Kingdom interest become indistinguishable. And the prophetic tradition that runs from Moses to Amos to Jesus, the tradition that says "Thus says the Lord" to the nation's face, calling it to repentance, becomes impossible. You cannot prophetically challenge a system you have sacralized.

The Old Testament prophets stood within Israel but against its unfaithfulness. They loved the nation precisely by refusing to bless its sins. They were patriots of the deepest kind, patriots who cared more about the nation's soul than its comfort, more about its covenant faithfulness than its military success. But their patriotism was subordinated to their loyalty to God. When the two conflicted, God won. Always.

A domesticated church cannot do this. A church that has identified the Kingdom of God with the American project cannot prophetically challenge the American project without feeling like it is challenging the Kingdom of God. And so it remains silent when it should speak, compliant when it should resist, comfortable when it should be grieved.

We lost the independence that makes the church's voice credible. The early church's moral authority came precisely from its independence. When Christians cared for plague victims while Roman elites fled the cities, the world noticed, not because the church was in power, but because it acted with sacrificial love while out of power. When Christians refused to participate in the imperial cult even under threat of death, their testimony carried weight, because there was obviously nothing to be gained by it except suffering.

A church that is aligned with political power loses this credibility. When Christians speak about justice while holding the levers of cultural influence, the world hears self-interest disguised as righteousness. When the church advocates for policies that happen to benefit its own position, even genuinely good policies lose their moral force because the advocacy looks strategic rather than sacrificial. The church's voice is most powerful when it has the least to gain from being heard.

We lost the freedom to hold all political arrangements with open hands. The early church survived, and often thrived, under emperors who were hostile, indifferent, or friendly. It did not depend on the political environment for its vitality. It could function under Nero, under Marcus Aurelius, under Diocletian, under Constantine. Its life was not contingent on having the right person in power, because its life came from the resurrected Christ, not from the sitting emperor.

A domesticated church has lost this flexibility. It has become so intertwined with a particular political arrangement that the loss of that arrangement feels like the loss of the faith itself. When cultural influence wanes, the church panics, not because the gospel is threatened (the gospel cannot be threatened by an election), but because the particular political environment in which the church is comfortable is changing. And the panic reveals the depth of the dependency.

We lost the scandal of the cross as a political statement. In the first century, saying "Jesus is Lord" was scandalous because it claimed that ultimate authority belonged to a crucified man, not a military conqueror, not a political genius, not a wielder of coercive force, but someone Rome had executed as a criminal. The cross was the empire's ultimate statement of power: "We can do this to you." And the resurrection was God's ultimate counter-statement: "Yes, you can. And it does not matter."

When "Jesus is Lord" is domesticated, the scandal of the cross disappears. The crucified Lord becomes a mascot for cultural power. The one who was executed by the state is reimagined as the patron saint of the state. The one who refused political power is invoked to legitimate political power. And the fundamental meaning of the confession, that God's power is made perfect in weakness, that the last shall be first, that the way up is the way down, is quietly discarded in favor of a Jesus who looks a lot like Caesar with a cross necklace.

Recovering the Subversive Edge Without the Culture War Posture

So how do we recover the original power of "Jesus is Lord" without turning it into another weapon in the culture wars? How do we restore the confession's subversive edge without adopting the very posture of power that the confession was meant to overthrow?

This is the question, and it requires extraordinary care. Because the danger on one side is a domesticated faith that challenges nothing. And the danger on the other side is a weaponized faith that challenges everything except itself.

The recovery begins with understanding what kind of Lord Jesus is. And the Philippians hymn gives us the answer: He is the Lord who emptied Himself. He is the Lord who took the form of a servant. He is the Lord who humbled Himself to the point of death. He is the Lord who received the name above every name not by seizing power but by surrendering it, not by climbing to the top but by descending to the bottom, not by crushing His enemies but by being crushed for them.

This means that when we confess "Jesus is Lord," we are not merely saying that Jesus has authority. We are saying that authority itself has been redefined. The nature of lordship has been transformed by the cross. Power that dominates is not the Lord's kind of power. Authority that coerces is not the Lord's kind of authority. A kingdom that requires the weapons of the world to advance is not the Lord's kind of kingdom.

To confess "Jesus is Lord" is therefore to commit yourself to a particular way of engaging the world, a way marked by service rather than domination, by truth-telling rather than propaganda, by enemy-love rather than enemy-destruction, by willingness to suffer rather than willingness to inflict suffering.

That means the recovery of the confession is not accomplished by being louder, angrier, or more politically aggressive. It is not accomplished by "taking back" culture or "reclaiming" institutions or "fighting" for influence. All of those strategies assume that the way to confess Jesus as Lord is to exercise the kind of lordship Caesar exercised, to dominate, to control, to win. But the confession itself declares that the Lord conquered by being conquered. He won by losing. He reigned by serving.

The subversive power of "Jesus is Lord" is recovered when the church lives so differently from the surrounding culture that people have to ask why: when Christians love their enemies so conspicuously that it baffles the tribalized world, when the church serves the poor so lavishly that secular observers cannot explain it by self-interest, when believers speak truth so consistently, to both political parties, to both sides of every cultural divide, that no faction can claim them.

The early church did not conquer Rome by outmaneuvering it politically. It conquered Rome by out-loving it, out-dying it, and out-hoping it. It offered a community that was more compelling than anything the empire could provide, a community where slaves and masters ate at the same table, where ethnic divisions were healed rather than exploited, where the sick and abandoned were cared for rather than discarded. The confession "Jesus is Lord" was credible because the community that confessed it was visibly different from the world that rejected it.

That is the recovery we need: not a church that confesses Jesus is Lord and then acts like Caesar is the model, but a church that confesses Jesus is Lord and then acts like Jesus is the model, which means service, sacrifice, truth, and love, even when those things bring no political advantage whatsoever.

The Confession That Costs Something

In the early church, to confess "Jesus is Lord" was to accept a cost. It could mean social exclusion, losing your place in the guild, losing business relationships, being shut out of civic life. It could mean economic loss, forfeiting the commercial advantages that came with participating in the imperial cult. It could mean imprisonment. It could mean death.

The early Christians did not confess "Jesus is Lord" because it was convenient or because it aligned with their cultural identity or because it reinforced their political preferences. They confessed it because it was true. And they were willing to pay whatever price that truth demanded.

Now consider the American context. What does it cost to say "Jesus is Lord" in the contemporary United States?

In most settings, the honest answer is: nothing. It costs nothing to say "Jesus is Lord" in a church that already believes it. It costs nothing to post it on social media to an audience that already agrees with you. It costs nothing to wear it on a T-shirt in a culture where Christianity, though declining in influence, is still the dominant religious tradition.

But here is where the confession begins to cost something again: when it means refusing to grant ultimate allegiance to any political movement, when it means declining to treat a candidate as a savior, when it means speaking truth that your own party does not want to hear, when it means caring for people your tribe has labeled as enemies, when it means losing relationships because you will not subordinate your confession to your politics.

The confession costs something when it actually functions as it was designed to function, as a declaration that relativizes every other claim to ultimate authority.

If "Jesus is Lord" does not challenge your politics, it is not functioning. If it only challenges the other side's politics, it is being weaponized, not confessed. If it sits comfortably alongside your partisan loyalty without creating any tension, then it has been domesticated, tamed and declawed and turned into a mascot for something that is not the Kingdom of God.

The test of whether you are truly confessing "Jesus is Lord" is not whether you say the words. The test is where the confession creates friction. Because if Jesus is actually Lord, if His authority actually supersedes every earthly power, then His lordship will inevitably conflict with your preferred political arrangement at some point. It will demand something of you that your party does not demand. It will prohibit something that your movement celebrates. It will call you to love someone your side has decided to hate.

If your confession of Jesus' lordship never creates friction with your political identity, one of two things is true: either you have perfectly aligned your politics with the Kingdom of God (which no one in history has managed to do), or you have subordinated the lordship of Christ to the lordship of your political tribe.

The early Christians faced a choice: Caesar or Jesus. The choice was clear, the cost was high, and they chose Jesus.

The American church faces a more subtle version of the same choice: not Caesar or Jesus, but a particular vision of America, or Jesus; a particular political movement, or Jesus; a particular cultural arrangement that feels safe and familiar and right, or the crucified and risen Lord who transcends every culture, every nation, and every political project.

The choice should be just as clear, but it is not, because we have been saying "Jesus is Lord" for so long without it costing us anything that we have forgotten what the words mean.

It is time to remember. It is time to say "Jesus is Lord" and mean it, not as a tribal slogan, not as a political weapon, not as a comfortable affirmation of the status quo, but as the most radical, dangerous, costly, and hopeful sentence a human being can utter. It is time to confess that the crucified carpenter from Nazareth is the rightful King of the universe, and then to let that confession reshape everything: our politics, our allegiances, our loves, our enemies, our hopes, and our willingness to suffer for the truth.

Because if Jesus is truly Lord, then nothing else can be: not America, not a party, not a candidate, not a movement, not an ideology. And if nothing else can be Lord, then you are free: free from the tyranny of political tribalism, free from the fear that the wrong election will undo the Kingdom of God, free from the desperate need to control the levers of power in order to feel safe. You are free because the Lord you serve does not need your political project to succeed. He has already conquered, already been exalted, already been given the name above every name. And every knee will bow, not because the church amassed enough political capital to force compliance, but because the crucified King has been raised from the dead and is seated at the right hand of God.

That is the confession. It cost the early church everything. It should cost us something too.

*Key Takeaways*

• "Jesus is Lord" was not a private devotional sentiment in the first century, it was a direct, deliberate counter-confession to "Caesar is Lord," and it could get you killed. • Acts 17:6–7 reveals that the Roman world heard the gospel as a political claim: "They are saying there is another king, Jesus." The opponents of the gospel understood its implications better than many modern Christians do. • Philippians 2:5–11, written to a Roman colony saturated with imperial cult, declares that the name above every name belongs not to Caesar but to the crucified servant, inverting the entire Roman value system. • Revelation reassigns the imperial titles "King of kings" and "Lord of lords" to the slaughtered Lamb, and the saints conquer not by seizing power but by maintaining their confession even unto death. • The Constantinian settlement, when Christianity became the empire's religion, domesticated the confession by making "Jesus is Lord" compatible with, rather than subversive of, earthly political power. • We lost four critical things when the confession was tamed: prophetic critique of our own nation, independence that makes the church's voice credible, flexibility to thrive under any political arrangement, and the scandal of the cross as a political statement. • Recovery does not come through louder culture war but through living so differently that the confession becomes visible, through service, enemy-love, truth-telling, and willingness to suffer without political advantage. • The test of whether you truly confess "Jesus is Lord" is not whether you say the words but whether the confession creates friction with your political identity.

*Reflection Questions*

1\. When you say "Jesus is Lord," does the confession carry any cost in your life? Does it create tension with anything you value, or has it become entirely comfortable? 2\. Can you identify a specific area where the lordship of Christ conflicts with the priorities of your preferred political party or movement? How have you responded to that conflict? 3\. If you were living in first-century Rome, would you refuse to say "Caesar is Lord"? Now ask: Is there a modern equivalent, some loyalty oath to a political tribe, that you might be unwilling to refuse? 4\. Has the confession "Jesus is Lord" ever caused you to change a political position, or does your politics always determine how you interpret Christ's lordship? 5\. What would it look like for your church to recover the subversive edge of the confession without adopting a combative culture-war posture? What would change in your worship, your community, your public witness? 6\. The early church's moral authority came from its sacrificial love while out of power. Does your church's public witness rely more on political influence or on visible, sacrificial love?

*Practices*

• Personal: For one week, every time you say or think "Jesus is Lord," pause and ask yourself: "What does this confession demand of me right now that I am not currently giving?" Write down what comes to mind. • Personal: Read Philippians 2:5–11 daily for two weeks. Each day, journal one way the self-emptying pattern of Jesus challenges your current approach to political engagement. • Family: At dinner, discuss this question together: "If our church lost all political influence tomorrow, no access to legislators, no cultural clout, no media presence, what would our mission look like? Would it change? Should it?" • Church: Propose a worship service or series that explores the original political context of the earliest Christian confessions. Discuss what it meant to confess Christ as Lord under threat of persecution, and what it should mean for us today.
