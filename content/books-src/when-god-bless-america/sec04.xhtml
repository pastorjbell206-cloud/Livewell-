Chapter Three

The Messiah You Want vs. the Messiah You Need

"They wanted a sword. He brought a towel."

The Crowd That Tried to Make Jesus King

In the sixth chapter of John's Gospel, something remarkable happens, something that reveals, more clearly than almost any other moment in the New Testament, the distance between what people want from God and what God actually came to do.

Jesus has just performed one of His most famous miracles. With five loaves of bread and two fish, He has fed five thousand men, plus women and children. The crowd is astonished. They have seen supernatural power on display. They have tasted provision that defies the laws of economics and agriculture. And they reach a conclusion that feels inevitable:

"When the people saw the sign that he had done, they said, 'This is indeed the Prophet who is to come into the world!'" —John 6:14

They are referencing the prophecy in Deuteronomy 18, where Moses promises that God will raise up a prophet like himself, a new liberator, a new lawgiver, a new leader who will do for Israel what Moses did. And they believe they have found Him. Here is a man with the power to feed armies, heal the sick, command nature, and, presumably, drive out the Romans and restore Israel to its former glory.

The crowd is ready to make Him king. Not metaphorically. Not in some spiritualized, internal sense. Literally. They want to take Him by force and put Him on a throne.

And then John records one of the most startling verses in the Gospels:

"Perceiving then that they were about to come and take him by force to make him king, Jesus withdrew again to the mountain by himself." —John 6:15

Jesus ran from the crown.

Let that sink in. The crowd is offering Him exactly what any political messiah would want: popular support, enthusiastic followers, a mandate to rule. And Jesus does not accept it. He does not negotiate terms. He does not say, "Let me think about it." He withdraws. He leaves. He climbs a mountain alone.

Not because He was not a king. He was, and is, the King of Kings. Not because He lacked authority. He possessed all authority in heaven and on earth.

But because the crowd wanted the wrong kind of king. They wanted a messiah who would use supernatural power to solve their political problems. They wanted sovereignty without surrender. Victory without repentance. A throne without a cross. National restoration without personal transformation.

And Jesus knew that if He accepted the crown on their terms, He would become exactly what the devil had offered Him in the wilderness, a ruler of earthly kingdoms who had bypassed the way of suffering, a savior who delivered political freedom while leaving the soul in bondage.

The crowd was not wrong to want a king. They were wrong about what kind of king they needed.

And that mistake, wanting the right thing in the wrong way, wanting God's power deployed according to human expectations, is not a first-century problem. It is a perennial problem. It is our problem.

The Wilderness Temptation: The Shortcut That Never Stops Being Offered

To understand why Jesus withdrew from the crown, we need to go back to the beginning of His public ministry and the temptation in the wilderness. Because the temptation the crowd is offering in John 6 is the same temptation Satan offered in Matthew 4. The packaging is different. The substance is identical.

Matthew records three temptations. The first is about provision: turn stones into bread. The second is about spectacle: throw yourself off the temple and let the angels catch you. The third is the one that matters most for our purposes, because it is explicitly political:

"Again, the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. And he said to him, 'All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me.'" —Matthew 4:8–9

Read this carefully. What Satan is offering is not merely temptation to personal sin. It is a theological proposition. It is a competing vision of how the world gets fixed.

Satan is saying: You want to save the world? Here it is. All the kingdoms. All the power. All the political authority. You can have it, right now. No cross. No suffering. No slow, patient, agonizing work of redemption through death and resurrection. No thirty years of obscurity followed by three years of rejection followed by a humiliating public execution. Skip all of that. Take the shortcut. I will give you what you want, just worship me.

And Jesus said no.

"Then Jesus said to him, 'Be gone, Satan! For it is written, "You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve."'" —Matthew 4:10

Jesus did not refuse because political authority is evil. He will later tell His disciples that all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Him (Matthew 28:18). He did not refuse because He was uninterested in the kingdoms of the world. He came precisely to redeem them.

He refused because political authority obtained through compromise with evil, even for good ends, even for redemptive purposes, even to "save the world", is a betrayal of the Father's plan. The shortcut is always a betrayal. The means matter. The method of conquest matters. And God's method is not coercion, not power brokering, not political maneuvering. God's method is a cross.

That temptation did not end in the wilderness. It followed Jesus throughout His entire public ministry. Every time the crowd wanted to crown Him. Every time the disciples argued about who would sit at His right and left hand in the coming kingdom. Every time zealots tried to recruit Him for their cause. Every time the people expected a military Messiah who would throw off Roman oppression and restore Israel to national greatness.

The temptation was always the same: bypass the cross, take the shortcut, use power to accomplish what only suffering can achieve.

And Jesus refused. Every single time.

Now here is the question for the American church: Are we refusing it? Or are we accepting the very deal Jesus turned down?

When we align the church with political power in exchange for protection, influence, and cultural dominance, when we accept the bargain of "all these kingdoms, if you will just support me", are we following Jesus, or are we accepting the offer He rejected?

The question is not comfortable. But it is inescapable.

The Kind of Messiah They Rejected

To understand the first-century rejection of Jesus, we need to understand the first-century expectation. Because the people who rejected Jesus were not godless pagans. Many of them were devout, Scripture-reading, synagogue-attending, prayer-praying believers. They loved God. They believed His promises. They were waiting for the Messiah with genuine hope.

They just had a very specific idea of what the Messiah would do.

The dominant messianic expectation in first-century Judaism was political. The Messiah would be a new David, a warrior king who would restore Israel's political sovereignty, drive out the occupying Romans, reestablish the Davidic throne in Jerusalem, and inaugurate an era of national greatness under God's visible rule. This was not a fringe hope. It was the mainstream expectation, rooted in real biblical texts and reinforced by centuries of foreign occupation, humiliation, and longing for deliverance.

The promises to David in 2 Samuel 7. The victory songs of the psalms. The prophetic visions of a restored kingdom in Isaiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel. The expectation of a "Son of Man" who would receive dominion and an everlasting kingdom. All of these texts were in their Bibles, and all of them could be read, and were read, as predictions of a politically triumphant Messiah.

What they missed, or what they chose not to see, was the other thread. The thread of the Suffering Servant in Isaiah 52–53, who would be "despised and rejected by men," who would bear the iniquities of the people, who would be "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter."

The thread of the humble king in Zechariah 9:9, who would come "riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey", not on a war horse, not in a chariot, not at the head of an army, but on the most humble animal available.

The thread of the shepherd struck down in Zechariah 13:7, whose death would scatter the sheep before God's refining work could begin.

The thread of the psalmist in Psalm 22, who cries out, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?", a psalm that reads like a detailed preview of crucifixion written centuries before crucifixion was invented.

All of these texts were in their Scriptures. But the political messiah was so much more appealing, so much more satisfying to the national ego, so much more aligned with the desire for visible victory, that the suffering thread was minimized, spiritualized, or simply ignored.

When Jesus arrived, He was not what they expected.

He did not raise an army. He did not storm Herod's palace. He did not negotiate with Rome. He did not organize a political movement. Instead, He washed feet. He touched lepers. He ate with tax collectors and prostitutes. He welcomed children. He confronted the religious establishment. He wept over Jerusalem. He told parables about seeds and soil instead of strategy and siege warfare.

He announced that the Kingdom of God was at hand, and then defined that Kingdom in ways that baffled, disturbed, and eventually infuriated everyone who heard Him.

The Kingdom belongs to the poor in spirit. The meek. The mourners. The peacemakers. The persecuted. Not the powerful, the triumphant, the well-connected, the politically astute.

No wonder they were confused. No wonder many of them eventually turned away. No wonder, when the choice was laid before them between Jesus and Barabbas, between the suffering servant and the violent revolutionary, the crowd chose Barabbas.

They chose the strongman over the servant. And they have not stopped choosing.

The Triumphal Entry That Wasn't

Luke 19 records what the church has traditionally called the "Triumphal Entry"—Jesus' arrival in Jerusalem on what we celebrate as Palm Sunday. It is one of the most misunderstood moments in the Gospels, and the misunderstanding reveals everything about the distance between human expectations and divine intention.

The scene looks triumphant. The crowd is ecstatic. They are shouting, "Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord! Peace in heaven and glory in the highest!" They are throwing their cloaks on the road, a gesture of royal honor. The atmosphere is electric with expectation. This is the moment. The Messiah is finally going to take His throne. Jerusalem will be restored. Rome will be humbled. God's people will be free.

But look at what Jesus does as He approaches the city:

"And when he drew near and saw the city, he wept over it, saying, 'Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes. For the days will come when your enemies will set up a barricade around you and surround you and hem you in on every side and tear you down to the ground, you and your children within you. And they will not leave one stone upon another in you, because you did not know the time of your visitation.'" —Luke 19:41–44

The crowd is celebrating. Jesus is weeping.

They think He is coming to conquer. He knows He is coming to die. They believe this entry will lead to a throne. It will lead to a cross. They are shouting about glory. He is grieving about destruction, because He knows that within a generation, Rome will do exactly what He describes: surround the city, tear it down, and leave not one stone upon another. The very political catastrophe the crowd hopes the Messiah will prevent is the catastrophe Jesus sees coming precisely because they have rejected His kind of messiahship.

And the reason He weeps is contained in that devastating phrase: they do not know "the things that make for peace."

They think peace comes through military victory and political restoration. Jesus knows peace comes through repentance, reconciliation, and the self-giving love of God poured out on a cross. They want the peace that comes from defeating your enemies. He offers the peace that comes from loving them. They want the peace of power. He brings the peace of surrender.

The distance between what the crowd wanted and what Jesus came to do is the same distance that exists today between what many American Christians expect from political power and what God actually provides through the gospel.

We keep looking for a triumphal entry, a moment when our side wins, our values prevail, our enemies are humbled, and the kingdom feels like it has arrived.

God keeps offering a crucifixion.

And we keep being confused about why He won't fight the way we want Him to.

Mark 8: The Rebuke That Defines Everything

If there is one passage in the Gospels that exposes the spiritual danger of wanting the wrong kind of Messiah, it is Mark 8:27–38. This passage is the hinge of Mark's entire Gospel, and its implications for the American church are staggering.

The scene begins with a question. Jesus asks His disciples: "Who do people say that I am?" They offer various answers—John the Baptist, Elijah, one of the prophets. Then Jesus makes it personal: "But who do you say that I am?"

Peter answers with the confession that defines Christianity: "You are the Christ." The Messiah. The anointed one. The King we have been waiting for.

It is the right answer. And everything that follows reveals how catastrophically Peter misunderstands what it means.

Because immediately after Peter's confession, Jesus begins to explain what kind of Christ He will be:

"And he began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders and the chief priests and the scribes and be killed, and after three days rise again. And he said this plainly." —Mark 8:31–32

Suffer. Be rejected. Be killed. Rise again. That is the messianic program. That is the plan. Not political conquest. Not military victory. Not cultural dominance. Suffering, rejection, death, and resurrection.

Peter cannot accept it.

"And Peter took him aside and began to rebuke him." —Mark 8:32

Let the audacity of this sink in. Peter has just confessed Jesus as the Christ, the anointed king of Israel, the one sent by God to save the world, and within sixty seconds, he is rebuking Him. Correcting Him. Telling God's Messiah that God's plan is wrong.

Peter is not rejecting Jesus. He is editing Jesus. He is keeping the title "Christ" while insisting on a different job description. He wants a Messiah who wins through strength, who conquers through power, who restores Israel through political and military triumph. A suffering Messiah is a contradiction in terms. A crucified King is an absurdity. A Savior who dies is no savior at all.

And Jesus' response is the most devastating rebuke in the entire Bible:

"But turning and seeing his disciples, he rebuked Peter and said, 'Get behind me, Satan! For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man.'" —Mark 8:33

Jesus calls Peter "Satan."

Not an enemy. A disciple. Not someone who denies Christ. Someone who confesses Christ, and then tries to edit the kind of Christ he is willing to follow.

And the reason for the rebuke is not that Peter is stupid or that his concern is trivial. The reason is that Peter is promoting a theology of power that is satanic in origin. The desire for a Messiah who wins through strength rather than suffering, who conquers through force rather than sacrifice, who delivers the nation through political triumph rather than personal transformation, that desire is not merely mistaken. It is the same offer Satan made in the wilderness. It is the same temptation Jesus has already rejected. And now it is coming from inside the inner circle.

The application to our moment should be unmistakable.

When the American church confesses "Jesus is Lord" but then insists that the way forward is through political power, cultural dominance, legislative control, and the defeat of enemies through coercion rather than love, we are doing exactly what Peter did.

We are confessing the right Christ and demanding the wrong mission.

We are saying "You are the Messiah" and then rebuking Him when His plan does not look like what we wanted.

And Jesus' response has not changed. It is the same today as it was on the road to Caesarea Philippi: "You are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man."

Answering the Objection: "But Jesus Confronts Evil—What About Justice?"

This is an important objection, and it deserves more than a dismissive wave. Because the portrait of Jesus I am painting, a Jesus who rejects political power, who suffers rather than conquers, who dies rather than fights, can be misread as a portrait of passivity. And Jesus is not passive.

Jesus overturns tables in the temple. He calls the Pharisees "whitewashed tombs" and "brood of vipers." He speaks truth to the face of every power structure He encounters, religious, political, economic. He does not avoid conflict. He walks into it. He does not tolerate injustice. He names it.

So the objection is fair: if Jesus confronts evil, why can't we use political power to confront evil too?

The answer is not that Christians should never use political tools to pursue justice. As we established in the previous chapter, political engagement is a legitimate expression of neighbor-love. Laws matter. Policies matter. The vulnerable need protection, and sometimes that protection comes through legislation, courts, and governmental action.

The answer is in how Jesus confronts evil, and what He refuses to do in the process.

Jesus confronts evil without seizing political control. He speaks truth to Pilate without negotiating for a position in the Roman government. He rebukes the Pharisees without organizing a competing religious party. He cleanses the temple without establishing a permanent enforcement mechanism. His confrontation of evil is fierce, direct, and prophetic, but it is never coercive. It never depends on having the political power to enforce compliance.

Jesus confronts evil without dehumanizing His enemies. He calls the Pharisees hypocrites, but He also weeps over Jerusalem. He rebukes Peter, but He also restores him. He is arrested by soldiers, but He heals the ear of the one Peter struck. The confrontation of evil in the Gospels never slides into the contempt, cruelty, or dehumanization that characterizes so much of our political engagement. Jesus can name sin without destroying the sinner. That is a skill the church has largely forgotten.

Jesus confronts evil by absorbing it rather than retaliating against it. This is the heart of the matter. On the cross, Jesus does not defeat evil by exercising superior force. He defeats evil by taking it into Himself, bearing its full weight, and refusing to let it reproduce through Him. He breaks the cycle of violence not by winning the fight but by refusing to fight on evil's terms. "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23).

And the resurrection that follows is God's vindication of that path, not the path of the sword, but the path of self-giving love. The resurrection does not come through military revolt. It comes through dying and being raised. The victory is real. The kingdom is established. Evil is defeated. But the method of defeat is sacrifice, not coercion.

So when we insist that the church needs political power to accomplish justice, we are implicitly saying something about the cross: we are saying it was not enough. We are saying God's strategy of conquering through suffering is insufficient and needs to be supplemented by the strategies of the world. We are saying what Peter said—"Surely this cannot be the plan", and receiving the same rebuke.

Justice in the way of Jesus does not bypass suffering. It passes through it. And any vision of justice that requires the church to become cruel, manipulative, dishonest, or power-hungry in order to achieve its goals has already departed from the way of Christ, no matter how righteous the cause.

Cross-Shaped Courage and Non-Anxious Presence

So what does this mean practically? If the church is not supposed to seize political power to impose righteousness, and if the church is not supposed to withdraw into passivity and irrelevance, what is the church supposed to do?

The answer is cross-shaped courage. And it looks like nothing the world expects.

A cross-shaped church does not need to win every culture war to maintain its identity. Its identity is not derived from cultural dominance but from union with Christ. A cross-shaped church can lose every political battle and still be the church. It can be marginalized, ridiculed, legally constrained, and culturally irrelevant, and still proclaim the resurrection with authority and joy.

Why? Because its confidence was never in its cultural position. Its confidence was in a tomb that is empty.

A cross-shaped church is free, genuinely, radically free, from the tyranny of outcomes. It can work for justice without being destroyed by injustice. It can advocate for truth without being devastated when truth is rejected. It can engage the political process without being defined by the political results. Its future is secured not by electoral victories but by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead.

This is not passivity. It is the most active and most dangerous thing the church can be. Because a church that is not afraid of losing is a church that cannot be controlled. A church that does not need political power is a church that can speak truth to political power without flinching, without hedging, without calculating the political cost.

The reason so many churches cannot speak truth right now is precisely that they are afraid of losing, losing access, losing influence, losing protection, losing tax exemptions, losing cultural centrality, losing the seat at the table. They have become dependent on the chariot. And the chariot's owner always demands cooperation in exchange for rides.

But a church that has already died, a church that has taken up its cross and followed Jesus into death and resurrection, has nothing left to lose. And a church with nothing left to lose is the most powerful force on earth. Not powerful in the way Rome was powerful. Powerful in the way the resurrection is powerful: an authority that does not come from the sword but from the empty tomb.

Edwin Friedman, the family systems therapist, coined a phrase that has become invaluable for understanding leadership in anxious times: "non-anxious presence." The idea is that in a system flooded with anxiety, a family, an organization, a nation, the most transformative thing a leader can do is refuse to be governed by the anxiety. Not to ignore it. Not to dismiss it. But to be present in the middle of it without being controlled by it.

That is what the church is called to be in America right now: a non-anxious presence. Not indifferent to the crises around us. Not naive about the real threats and real suffering in our culture. But refusing to let anxiety set the agenda. Refusing to let fear rewrite our theology. Refusing to let the temperature of the political moment determine the posture of the people of God.

The anxious church grasps for power. The non-anxious church extends open hands.

The anxious church demonizes its enemies. The non-anxious church prays for them.

The anxious church measures its health by political influence. The non-anxious church measures its health by faithfulness to Christ.

The anxious church asks, "Are we winning?" The non-anxious church asks, "Are we faithful?"

Cross-shaped courage means being willing to lose the chariot in order to keep the King. It means choosing the towel over the sword, the cross over the throne, the way of Jesus over the way of the world, even when the way of the world looks like it is winning and the way of Jesus looks like it is losing.

Because the way of Jesus always looks like it is losing right up until Sunday morning. And then everything changes.

Which Messiah Are You Following?

Let me close this chapter with a question that is meant to be uncomfortable.

Which Messiah are you actually following?

Not which Messiah do you say you follow. Not which Messiah appears on your doctrinal statement or your church's website. Which Messiah governs your actual behavior, your actual emotional life, your actual response to the political moment you are living in?

If the Messiah you are following promises political victory, cultural dominance, and the defeat of your enemies through the exercise of power, that is not Jesus. That is the messiah the crowd wanted. That is the messiah Peter demanded. That is the messiah Satan offered in the wilderness.

If the Messiah you are following calls you to suffer for righteousness rather than seize power for convenience; to love your enemies rather than destroy them; to speak truth without cruelty; to pursue justice without becoming unjust in the process; to hold political engagement with open hands and ultimate trust in closed hands around the cross, that is the Jesus of the Gospels. That is the Messiah who was rejected, crucified, and raised on the third day.

The first messiah fills arenas. The second fills tombs, and then empties them.

The first messiah offers you the kingdoms of the world. The second offers you a cross, and then, through the cross, gives you the universe.

The first messiah makes you feel powerful. The second makes you free.

The question is not whether you believe in Jesus. The question is which Jesus you believe in. And the answer is revealed not by your theology but by your behavior, by what you are willing to lose, what you are willing to suffer, and who you become when you do not get what you want.

If losing a political battle destroys your faith, your faith was in the wrong messiah.

If being marginalized makes you cruel, you have been following the wrong king.

If the only future you can imagine for the church requires political power, you have been worshiping at the wrong throne.

The true Messiah does not need your political project to succeed. He needs your faithfulness. And faithfulness looks like a cross, not a crown.

At least, not yet.

*Key Takeaways*

• The crowd in John 6 wanted to make Jesus king by force; He withdrew because they wanted sovereignty without surrender, power without a cross. • Satan's wilderness temptation was a theological proposition: political authority without suffering. Jesus refused the shortcut because the means of victory matter to God. • First-century Israel rejected Jesus not because they hated God but because He did not match their expectation of a politically triumphant Messiah. • The "Triumphal Entry" reveals the distance between human expectation and divine intention: the crowd celebrated while Jesus wept, because they did not know the things that make for peace. • Peter's rebuke of Jesus and Jesus' response ("Get behind me, Satan") reveals that demanding a theology of power over suffering is satanic in origin, even when it comes from a sincere disciple. • Jesus confronts evil fiercely but never coercively—He absorbs evil on the cross rather than retaliating, and the resurrection vindicates this path. • Cross-shaped courage means the church is free from the tyranny of outcomes, able to speak truth without fear because its future is secured by resurrection, not elections. • The question is not whether you believe in Jesus but which Jesus you believe in, the one who offers a crown without a cross, or the one who offers a cross that leads to a crown.

*Reflection Questions*

1\. What kind of Messiah do you actually want, honestly? One who suffers alongside you and calls you to sacrifice, or one who delivers political victory and cultural dominance? 2\. When was the last time you were genuinely uncomfortable with the way Jesus does things? What does that discomfort reveal about the messiah you are functionally following? 3\. How would your church's ministry change if political influence were completely removed tomorrow? Would the mission survive? Would your people know how to be the church without cultural power? 4\. Can you distinguish, in your own life, between confronting injustice and seizing control? Where have you confused the two? Where have you called coercion "justice"? 5\. What would "cross-shaped courage" look like in your specific context this week, in your family, your workplace, your church, your social media presence? 6\. If losing the next election would destroy your confidence in God, what does that reveal about which messiah you have been trusting?

*Practices*

• Personal: Read John 6:1–21 slowly and imagine yourself in the crowd. Be honest: would you have tried to make Jesus king? Would you have been frustrated when He withdrew? What does your answer reveal about your expectations of God? • Personal: Identify one area of your life where you have been trying to get God to act like the Messiah you want rather than the Messiah He is. Write it down. Surrender it in prayer. Ask God for the grace to accept His plan instead of demanding your own. • Family: Read Mark 8:31–38 together at a family meal. Discuss two questions: What does it mean to "take up your cross" in our family's current circumstances? And: Are there ways our family has been following a "power messiah" instead of the suffering servant? • Church: Challenge your congregation or small group to articulate the church's mission in terms that do not depend on political outcomes. What remains when political power is removed? Is that enough? If it is not, why not?
