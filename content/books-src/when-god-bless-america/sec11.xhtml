Chapter Ten

Cross and Sword: Two Theologies of Power

"Propaganda can fill arenas. It cannot raise the dead."

The Question Behind Every Question

We have spent nine chapters diagnosing a disease. We have named it zanah, relocated trust. We have traced it through Israel's alliances, through the crowd's demand for a political messiah, through the domestication of the confession "Jesus is Lord," through the Constantinian bargain, through Germany's catastrophe, through the generational wreckage of hypocrisy. We have watched the same pattern repeat across centuries and continents: fear drives the people of God toward political power, political power promises protection, and the church trades its prophetic soul for a seat at the table.

But beneath every chapter, beneath every historical example and every scriptural text, there has been one question pulsing like a heartbeat:

How does God actually conquer?

That is the question this chapter must answer. Because everything else, every diagnostic marker, every reflection question, every call to repentance, depends on whether we believe God's method of victory is sufficient. If we do not trust the way God conquers, we will inevitably supplement it with the way the world conquers. If we believe that the cross is God's definitive revelation of power, then the entire posture of the church in relation to political authority changes. If we do not, if we secretly believe the cross was a necessary but insufficient step that must be supplemented by legislation, cultural dominance, and the machinery of coercion, then we will keep running back to Egypt no matter how many times the prophet calls us home.

So this is where the argument arrives at its climax: not a political strategy, not a policy recommendation, but a theology of power, specifically the question of whether the church operates by the logic of the cross or the logic of the sword, and what happens when it confuses the two.

Two Kingdoms, Two Logics

The New Testament does not pretend that political authority is irrelevant or illegitimate. Paul's letter to the Romans includes one of the most discussed passages on government in all of Scripture:

"Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God\... For he is God's servant for your good. But if you do wrong, be afraid, for he does not bear the sword in vain. For he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer." —Romans 13:1, 4

This passage is important, and it is routinely abused in two opposite directions. On one side, it is flattened into an unconditional command to obey whatever the government says, a reading that would have made nonsense of the early church's refusal to worship Caesar, of Peter's declaration that "we must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29), and of the entire book of Revelation, which portrays the Roman state as a beast demanding illegitimate worship. On the other side, it is dismissed as situational advice with no enduring theological significance, a reading that ignores Paul's deliberate, theological grounding of political authority in God's ordering of creation.

The passage does something more careful than either extreme allows. It acknowledges that the state has a God-given role: the restraint of evil, the maintenance of order, the protection of the vulnerable through the exercise of coercive force. The state bears the sword. That is its vocation. And within its proper limits, that vocation is legitimate. Christians can affirm this without embarrassment.

But notice what Paul does not say. He does not say the state bears the cross. He does not say the state's method is the church's method. He does not say that because government uses coercion to restrain evil, the church should use coercion to advance the gospel. He does not collapse the distinction between how the state operates and how the church operates.

The state restrains evil through force. The church overcomes evil through witness, service, sacrifice, and the power of the Holy Spirit. These are not the same logic. They are not interchangeable methods. And the confusion of the two, the assumption that the church can or should adopt the state's tools to accomplish the church's mission, is one of the most persistent and destructive errors in the history of Christianity.

Paul himself makes the distinction explicit in his second letter to the Corinthians:

"For though we walk in the flesh, we are not waging war according to the flesh. For the weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh but have divine power to destroy strongholds." —2 Corinthians 10:3–4

The weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh. That sentence should be tattooed on the conscience of every Christian who has ever believed that the church needs the instruments of political coercion to accomplish its mission. Paul is not saying the church does not fight. It fights fiercely. But the weapons are different, the strategy is different, and the entire logic of engagement is different.

The state uses the sword. The church uses the word. The state compels. The church persuades. The state enforces compliance. The church invites transformation. The state punishes wrongdoing. The church absorbs it, and then rises from the dead.

These two logics can coexist. A Christian can affirm the state's legitimate use of force while simultaneously insisting that the church's mode of operation is fundamentally different. You can support the police while refusing to police the conscience. You can affirm the necessity of national defense while insisting that the church does not need military metaphors to describe its mission, at least, not the kind that involve actual weapons.

The problem arises when the church forgets which logic it operates under. When the church begins to believe that it needs the sword to survive, that without legislative protection, cultural dominance, and political influence, the gates of hell will prevail, it has adopted the state's logic and abandoned its own. It has traded the weapons of divine power for the weapons of the flesh. And in doing so, it has implicitly declared that the cross was not enough.

The Cross as God's Definitive Act of Power

This is where we must stop being sentimental about the cross.

In much of American Christianity, the cross has been reduced to a symbol of personal forgiveness, the mechanism by which individual sinners receive a ticket to heaven. And it is that. The cross is the place where the sins of the world were borne by the sinless Son of God, where the penalty that justice demanded was paid in full, where the wrath of God against human rebellion was absorbed by the one person who did not deserve it. That is glorious, and it is true, and it is the heart of the gospel.

But the cross is more than a mechanism of personal salvation. The cross is God's definitive revelation of how He conquers.

Rome used crucifixion to dominate. It was the empire's most eloquent statement of power: "We can do this to you. We can strip you naked, nail you to wood, and leave you hanging at the intersection where the most people will see your agony. We can turn your body into a public service announcement about what happens to those who resist." Crucifixion was not merely execution. It was political theater. It was the state's ultimate argument: comply, or this is what awaits you.

And God took Rome's most powerful instrument of domination and turned it into the instrument of redemption.

The resurrection did not come through military revolt, through political negotiation, or through the careful accumulation of cultural influence. It came through a man hanging on a cross, absorbing the full weight of human evil into Himself, refusing to retaliate, and then walking out of a tomb three days later.

That is how God conquers: not by exercising superior force, not by out-coercing the coercers, not by assembling a more powerful army or winning a more decisive election. God conquers by absorbing evil, bearing its full weight, and refusing to let it reproduce through Him. He breaks the cycle of violence not by winning the fight on evil's terms but by refusing to fight on evil's terms altogether.

Fleming Rutledge, in her magisterial work The Crucifixion, argues that the cross is not God's concession to weakness. It is God's revelation of strength. The power of God is not demonstrated by the avoidance of suffering but by the transformation of suffering into the instrument of redemption. The cross does not display God's inability to fight back. It displays His refusal to fight back on the terms the world offers, because fighting on the world's terms would reproduce the very evil He came to destroy.

Paul understood this with devastating clarity:

"For the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God\... For the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men." —1 Corinthians 1:18, 25

The cross is foolishness to the perishing because it does not look like power. Power, in the world's estimation, looks like Rome, like chariots, like a congressional majority, like cultural dominance and the ability to enforce your will on those who resist. The cross looks like none of those things. The cross looks like defeat, like weakness, like the end.

And that is why it is the power of God. Because it defeats evil in a way that evil cannot understand, cannot anticipate, and cannot replicate. You can out-fight a warrior, out-strategize a politician, out-spend an empire. But you cannot defeat a man who is willing to die rather than become what you are. You cannot conquer a community whose response to persecution is forgiveness, whose response to hatred is love, whose response to death is the unshakable confidence that the grave is not the final word.

The early church understood this. That is why they conquered the Roman Empire, not by out-powering it, but by out-dying it, out-loving it, and out-hoping it. They offered something the empire could not provide: a community where love was stronger than fear, where death had been defeated, where the powers of this world had been relativized by the power of a resurrection.

Revelation 5: The Lamb Who Conquered

The book of Revelation provides the most vivid and theologically dense portrait of Christ's mode of conquest in the entire New Testament. And the portrait is deliberately, shockingly subversive.

In Revelation 5, John sees a scroll sealed with seven seals, a scroll that represents God's plan for the redemption of all things. No one in heaven or on earth is found worthy to open the scroll. John weeps. The plan of God seems stuck, the future locked shut.

Then one of the elders speaks:

"Weep no more; behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered, so that he can open the scroll and its seven seals." —Revelation 5:5

A lion. The imagery is unmistakable: power, ferocity, royal authority, military conquest. The Lion of Judah has conquered. John turns to look, expecting, as anyone in the ancient world would, to see a creature of overwhelming strength.

And what does he see?

"And between the throne and the four living creatures and among the elders I saw a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain." —Revelation 5:6

John hears "Lion." He sees "Lamb." He hears the language of military triumph. He sees the evidence of sacrificial death.

This is not a mistake. It is the most important interpretive key in the book of Revelation, and arguably in the entire New Testament. The Lion is the Lamb. The conqueror is the slain one. The one who has triumphed over every power in the cosmos has done so not by wielding a sword but by being slaughtered. Victory and sacrifice are not opposites. In God's economy, sacrifice is victory. Death is the means of conquest. The Lamb who was slain is the only one worthy to open the scroll and execute God's plan for history.

Richard Bauckham, one of the foremost scholars of Revelation, argues that this Lion-Lamb juxtaposition redefines the concept of power for the entire Christian community. When you hear "power" and expect a lion but see a lamb, the word "power" itself has been transformed. You can no longer use the word the way Rome uses it or the way political movements use it. Power, in the Kingdom of God, looks like a slaughtered Lamb standing in the throne room of the universe.

And notice: the Lamb is standing. It has been slain, but it is not dead. It bears the marks of its suffering, but it is alive. This is the resurrection, the vindication of the path of sacrifice. God did not rescue Jesus from the cross. He vindicated Jesus through the cross. The suffering was not a detour on the way to victory. The suffering was the victory. And the resurrection is the Father's declaration that this is how He conquers, this is how He redeems, this is how He establishes His Kingdom, not through the sword, but through the self-giving love of the Lamb.

Then heaven responds:

"Worthy is the Lamb who was slain, to receive power and wealth and wisdom and might and honor and glory and blessing!" —Revelation 5:12

Power belongs to the Lamb, not to the empire, not to the political machine, not to the cultural movement that promises to restore Christian influence. Power, real, ultimate, universe-shaping power, belongs to the one who was slaughtered. And the elders and the living creatures do not debate this or qualify it or add footnotes about political strategy. They worship.

That is the theology of power the church is called to embody. And it is a theology that the politically captive church has largely forgotten.

How the Saints Conquer: Revelation 12

If Revelation 5 shows how Christ conquers, Revelation 12 shows how His people do. And the mode of victory is the same.

"And they have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they loved not their lives even unto death." —Revelation 12:11

Three weapons. Not one of them is a sword: the blood of the Lamb, the word of their testimony, the willingness to die.

The saints in Revelation do not conquer the dragon by organizing a more effective political coalition, by electing a sympathetic emperor, or by accumulating enough cultural capital to marginalize their opponents. They conquer by holding fast to their confession, "Jesus is Lord", even when that confession costs them everything.

Their political action is worship, their resistance is faithfulness, and their revolution is the refusal to say "Caesar is Lord" even when the alternative is death.

This is not passivity. Revelation 12:11 is a victory statement. "They have conquered." The verb is enikēsan, they overcame, they prevailed, they won. But the manner of their victory is what stuns the imagination. They won by dying. They prevailed by refusing to save themselves. They overcame the dragon by loving faithfulness more than survival.

Now ask yourself: Does that sound like the American church right now? Does the church in America look like a community that has conquered its fear of death? Does it look like a people who love not their lives even unto death? Or does it look like a people who are terrified of losing cultural influence, political access, tax-exempt status, and the comfortable assumption that America is "our" country?

A church that is afraid of losing cannot conquer in the manner of Revelation 12. Because Revelation 12's victory depends on the willingness to lose everything except the confession. And a church that has made political power essential to its survival has, by definition, added a requirement that the New Testament never imposes.

The early church did not need a friendly emperor to be faithful, legislative protection to proclaim the gospel, or cultural dominance to transform lives. It needed the blood of the Lamb, the word of its testimony, and the courage to die. That is still all it needs.

What Happens When the Church Picks Up the Sword

History provides devastating evidence of what happens when the church abandons the logic of the cross and adopts the logic of the sword. We do not need to speculate. We only need to remember.

The Crusades were launched in the name of Christ and drenched the Holy Land in blood. The Inquisition used the instruments of state coercion to enforce theological conformity and tortured those who dissented. The wars of religion that followed the Reformation, Catholic against Protestant, Protestant against Protestant, killed millions in the name of doctrines that were supposed to bring peace. The colonial missions of the sixteenth through nineteenth centuries used the cross as a vanguard for imperial conquest, so that to this day, in vast portions of the global South, Christianity is associated with cultural destruction, slavery, and the theft of land.

Every one of these catastrophes occurred when the church adopted the state's logic, when it decided that the way to advance the Kingdom was through coercion, control, and the exercise of political power. Every one of them represents a betrayal of the Lamb's mode of conquest. And every one of them did incalculable damage to the credibility of the gospel.

The argument is not that every Christian who engages in politics is heading toward the Crusades. That is a caricature, and caricatures do not persuade. The argument is that there is a structural logic at work: when the church treats political power as essential to its mission, the methods of political power inevitably reshape the church's character. You cannot wield the sword without being shaped by the sword. You cannot adopt the state's tools without absorbing the state's instincts.

Jesus warned about this in the most direct terms possible:

"Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword." —Matthew 26:52

He said this to Peter, the same Peter who had confessed Him as the Christ, the same Peter who would later deny Him, the same Peter who would eventually die a martyr's death. Peter reached for the sword because he wanted to protect Jesus. The motive was understandable, the love was genuine, and the instinct was human and even admirable.

And Jesus rebuked it, not because defending the innocent is always wrong, but because in that moment, the sword represented an alternative to the cross. It represented the belief that what God was about to accomplish through suffering could be improved upon by violence. It represented the same temptation that had followed Jesus from the wilderness to Caesarea Philippi to Gethsemane: bypass the cross, take the shortcut, fight the battle on the world's terms.

The church that picks up the sword may win battles. It may secure cultural influence. It may preserve its tax exemptions and its social respectability and its access to the halls of power. But it will lose something far more valuable than anything it gains: it will lose its resemblance to Jesus. And a church that no longer resembles Jesus is no longer the church, regardless of how many seats it holds in Congress.

The Church That Chooses the Cross

So what does it look like, practically, for the church to operate by the logic of the cross rather than the logic of the sword? What does cross-shaped power look like in the real world, in real communities, in real political engagement?

It looks like a church that tells the truth when truth is costly. The politically captive church has learned to be silent when silence is strategic, to overlook the sins of its preferred leaders, to mute its prophetic voice when speaking up might jeopardize access. A cross-shaped church tells the truth to both parties, both movements, both sides of every cultural divide. It speaks with the specificity that makes everyone uncomfortable, because the prophetic tradition does not deal in vague generalities. Nathan did not say to David, "Some people have done some bad things." He said, "You are the man." A church that cannot name specific sins in its own political tribe has been captured by that tribe.

It looks like a church that loves its enemies visibly and concretely: not in theory, not in sermon illustrations, but in practice. It means Christians across the political divide worshiping together, serving together, and refusing to allow political disagreement to become relational destruction. It means caring for people your party has labeled as threats. It means pursuing the good of communities that did not vote the way you did. It means demonstrating, in the flesh, that baptism creates a deeper bond than voter registration.

It looks like a church that serves the vulnerable without using that service for political gain. When the church cares for the poor, visits the prisoner, feeds the hungry, and welcomes the stranger, it must do so because Jesus commanded it, not because it produces good optics, not because it builds a platform, not because it positions the church as a useful partner for whatever administration happens to be in power. Service that is contaminated by political strategy is no longer service. It is marketing. And the watching world can tell the difference.

It looks like a church that suffers without retaliating. This is the hardest and most essential mark of cross-shaped power. When the church is criticized, marginalized, mocked, or legally constrained, its response reveals which theology of power it actually holds. If the response is outrage, litigation, political retaliation, and the mobilization of cultural warfare, the church is operating by the sword's logic. If the response is patient endurance, continued faithfulness, undiminished love, and the quiet confidence that the tomb is empty, that is the logic of the cross.

Peter, the same disciple who once reached for the sword, eventually learned the difference:

"For to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you might follow in his steps. He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth. When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly." —1 Peter 2:21–23

When He was reviled, He did not revile in return. When He suffered, He did not threaten. He entrusted Himself to the one who judges justly. That is cross-shaped power. It is not weakness. It is the most radical form of strength available to human beings: the strength to absorb evil without reproducing it, to endure suffering without becoming the kind of person who inflicts it, to trust that God's judgment is more reliable than your retaliation.

And it is the only kind of power that the resurrection vindicates.

Answering the Objection: "This Sounds Like Surrender"

Let me anticipate the strongest objection to everything I have written in this chapter. Someone is reading this and thinking: "This is naive. This is pie-in-the-sky idealism. This is the theology of people who have never had to protect their families, their churches, or their communities from real threats. In the real world, you need power. In the real world, the cross does not stop legislation. In the real world, you cannot love your enemies into policy change."

I want to take that objection seriously, because it is not trivial. Real evil exists. Real injustice exists. Real threats to religious liberty, to the vulnerable, to the common good, these are not imaginary. And the Bible itself affirms that the state has a role in restraining evil through the exercise of force. Romans 13 is in the canon. It is not going away.

But notice what the objection assumes. It assumes that the only alternative to political power is passivity. It assumes that if the church does not wield the sword, it must be sitting on its hands. It assumes that the options are either political dominance or total withdrawal.

That is a false binary. And it is the false binary that has trapped the American church for decades.

The early church was not passive. It was the most active, most life-changing, most culturally disruptive movement in the history of the ancient world. It fed the hungry and cared for plague victims when Roman elites fled the cities. It welcomed slaves and masters to the same table. It created communities of radical equality, sacrificial generosity, and stubborn hope in the face of imperial power. It told the truth about who was really in charge. And it did all of this without a single seat in the Roman Senate.

The early church did not have political power. It had something better: spiritual authority. And spiritual authority came not from its proximity to the throne but from its proximity to the cross.

So the question is not whether the church should act. Of course it should act. The question is whether the church's action must take the form of political domination in order to be effective. And the New Testament's answer is no. The church's most powerful action has always been its witness, its willingness to live so differently, love so extravagantly, speak so truthfully, and die so courageously that the watching world cannot explain it by any category other than "God is among them."

That is not surrender. That is the most dangerous thing the church can be. Because a church that operates by the logic of the cross cannot be controlled by the logic of the sword. A church that does not need political power cannot be manipulated by the promise of political power. A church that has already died, that has taken up its cross and followed Jesus into death and resurrection, has nothing left to lose. And a church with nothing left to lose is, paradoxically, the most powerful force on earth.

Repent from Trusting, Not from Voting

So here is the call. And it is a call to repentance, not the kind of repentance that withdraws from the world, but the kind that reorders the soul.

Repent from trusting. Not from voting.

Repent from the belief that the church needs political power to survive. It does not. The church was born without it, grew without it, and has repeatedly been purified by losing it.

Repent from the belief that the right president, the right Congress, the right Supreme Court composition can secure what only Christ can secure. They cannot. They never could. Every political arrangement in history has been temporary. The Kingdom of God is not.

Repent from the fear that drives you to compromise your integrity for political outcomes. Fear is not a strategy. Fear is a confession that you have forgotten who is on the throne. And the one on the throne is not sitting in the Oval Office. He is seated at the right hand of God, and every knee will bow to Him, not because the church amassed enough political capital to force compliance, but because He is the risen Lord of the universe.

Repent from the contempt that your political engagement has produced. If your involvement in politics has made you crueler, more tribal, more willing to dehumanize people made in the image of God, then your politics have been discipling you more effectively than your faith. The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. If your political engagement is producing rage, contempt, and self-righteousness, the root is diseased, no matter how righteous the cause.

Repent from the substitution, the quiet, gradual, fear-driven replacement of Christ as the center of your hope with a political project, a political leader, or a political vision. Name it. Be specific. Do not spiritualize it. Say what it is. Say, "I have treated this movement as though it were the Kingdom." Say, "I have defended this leader as though he were anointed in a way that no politician is." Say, "I have made winning feel like salvation and losing feel like death."

And then do what Hosea 14 says:

"Assyria shall not save us; we will not ride on horses; and we will say no more, 'Our God,' to the work of our hands." —Hosea 14:3

Put the idol down. Stop calling it wisdom, realism, responsible leadership. Call it what it is: relocated trust. And come home.

Because the God who named the adultery is the same God who heals it:

"I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely, for my anger has turned from them." —Hosea 14:4

Freely, lavishly, without a probationary period, without a lecture. The God of Hosea does not wait for you to clean yourself up before He welcomes you back. He meets you in your mess, heals what is broken, and loves you back to life.

That is the God we return to: not a policy platform, not a cultural vision, not a political messiah, but the crucified and risen Christ, who has already defeated every power that has ever claimed ultimacy, including the ones you are tempted to trust right now.

What Is Actually Preserved by the Cross

The church's future is not preserved by presidents, congresses, courts, or cultural legislation. The church's future is preserved by holiness, the kind of holiness that makes the watching world curious about the God who produces it. The church's future is preserved by prayer, not the kind of prayer that lobbies heaven for partisan outcomes, but the kind that surrenders to the God who is sovereign over every nation, every election, and every empire that has ever risen or fallen. The church's future is preserved by truth, spoken with courage, with specificity, with consistency to both left and right, and with the willingness to accept the cost of saying what no one wants to hear. The church's future is preserved by the love of enemies, the one command of Jesus that no political movement on earth has ever been willing to endorse, the one practice that has no strategic value whatsoever, and the one thing that most clearly marks a community as belonging to the crucified God. The church's future is preserved by gospel courage, the willingness to proclaim the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ as the decisive event in the history of the world, to call all people to repentance and faith, and to do so without apology, without political calculation, and without the need for any earthly power to validate the message.

These are the weapons of divine power. They are not impressive by the world's standards. They do not fill arenas or trend on social media. They do not produce the rush of adrenaline that comes from political victory. But they raise the dead. And a church that can raise the dead does not need to win an election to prove it is alive.

The Closing Word

Propaganda can fill arenas. It cannot raise the dead. A church that chooses propaganda over resurrection power may keep its influence for a moment. But it will lose its soul.

A church that chooses the cross will lose, in the world's estimation. It will be mocked and marginalized. It will be told it is irrelevant, naive, and weak. It will be tempted, every single day, to pick up the sword and fight the battle on the world's terms. But the way of Jesus always looks like it is losing right up until Sunday morning. And then everything changes.

So put down the sword. Pick up the cross. And trust the Lamb.

Because the one who stands between you and destruction is not riding a chariot, occupying an office, or building a coalition. He is standing in a throne room, bearing the marks of His slaughter, and every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth is singing:

"Worthy is the Lamb who was slain."

That is the power and the victory. That is the only King who will never disappoint, never betray, and never need your political project to succeed.

Trust Him. And let the sword go.

Key Takeaways

- Romans 13 affirms the state's legitimate use of the sword to restrain evil, but Paul never gives the church the sword, the church's weapons are "not of the flesh" but have "divine power to destroy strongholds" (2 Corinthians 10:3–4).

- The cross is not merely the mechanism of personal salvation, it is God's definitive revelation of how He conquers: by absorbing evil rather than retaliating, by suffering rather than coercing.

- Revelation 5 redefines power: John hears "Lion" but sees "Lamb." The conqueror of the cosmos is the slain one. Victory and sacrifice are the same act in God's economy.

- The saints in Revelation 12 conquer by the blood of the Lamb, the word of their testimony, and the willingness to die, not by political coalitions, cultural dominance, or legislative strategy.

- Every historical catastrophe where the church adopted the sword—Crusades, Inquisition, wars of religion, colonial missions, demonstrates that wielding the state's tools reshapes the church's character.

- The alternative to political power is not passivity, it is spiritual authority: the church's most transformative moments have come when it had the least political power and the greatest proximity to the cross.

- Repentance means not withdrawing from political engagement but renouncing political trust as ultimate: "Assyria shall not save us."

- The church's future is preserved by holiness, prayer, truth-telling, enemy-love, and gospel courage, weapons that do not fill arenas but can raise the dead.

Reflection Questions

1. When you think about the church's future, does your confidence rest primarily on the power of the Spirit or on the outcome of the next election? What does your emotional response to political news reveal about which theology of power you actually hold?

2. Can you identify a specific instance where your church or community adopted the logic of the sword, coercion, manipulation, dehumanization of opponents, while claiming to serve the Kingdom of God? What was the fruit?

3. If the church lost all political influence tomorrow, no access, no cultural clout, no legislative leverage, what would remain? Would it be enough? Does the thought fill you with dread or with freedom?

4. Read 1 Peter 2:21–23 slowly. When you are reviled, do you revile in return? When you suffer political loss, do you threaten? Or do you entrust yourself to the one who judges justly? Where is the gap between Peter's description and your actual practice?

5. What would it look like for your church to be the most loving, most truthful, most generous community in your city, without any political advantage whatsoever? Would that mission feel like enough?

6. Has your political engagement over the past decade made you more like the Lamb or more like the beast? Be honest about the fruit: patience or rage, humility or contempt, faithfulness or compromise.

Practices

- Personal: Read Revelation 5 every morning this week. Each day, journal one way the Lion-Lamb vision challenges how you think about power, victory, and the church's mission. Ask: "Where have I been expecting a lion when God is sending a lamb?"

- Personal: Identify one area where you have adopted the logic of the sword, where you have responded to opposition with coercion, contempt, or the desire to dominate rather than serve. Confess it specifically. Ask God to replace the sword with the cross in that area of your life.

- Family: Read Matthew 26:47–56 together—Peter's use of the sword and Jesus' rebuke. Discuss: Where has our family reached for the "sword" (anger, retaliation, political hostility) when Jesus was calling us to a different response? What would it look like to put the sword back?

- Church: Ask your congregation or small group to articulate the church's mission using only the categories of Revelation 12:11, the blood of the Lamb, the word of testimony, and the willingness to suffer. Does the mission survive this filter? What would need to change if it does not?

Hosea 14:1–5—The structure of repentance: confession, renunciation of false saviors, and God's promise to heal apostasy freely. Fleming Rutledge, The Crucifixion (Eerdmans, 2015)—On the cross as God's definitive act of power through weakness. Richard Bauckham, The Theology of the Book of Revelation (Cambridge, 1993)—On the Lion-Lamb juxtaposition and the reinterpretation of messianic victory. John Howard Yoder, The Politics of Jesus (Eerdmans, 1972)—On the political implications of Jesus' rejection of violence and the cross as social ethics. Miroslav Volf, Exclusion and Embrace (Abingdon, 1996)—On the theology of embrace, the refusal of violence, and the cross as the model for engaging the "other."
