Chapter Two

Chariots Aren't Evil—But They Aren't Ultimate

"When legitimate goods become ultimate goods, they become idols."

The Good Gift That Becomes a God Substitute

Every idol begins as a gift.

This is the thing most people miss about idolatry, and it is the reason the Bible's warnings about it are so persistently ignored. We imagine idols as obviously evil things, golden calves, pagan statues, demonic rituals. We picture idolatry as something primitive and absurd, something no sophisticated modern person would fall into. And because we picture it that way, we never see it coming.

But the most dangerous idols are not evil things. They are good things that have been inflated beyond their design capacity.

Money is a gift. It provides for families, funds ministries, builds hospitals, feeds the hungry. Sex is a gift. It binds marriages, produces new life, expresses the deepest human intimacy. Work is a gift. It cultivates creation, serves neighbors, exercises the stewardship mandate that God gave humanity in the garden. Family, friendship, and health are gifts. Even politics, the ordering of common life for the common good, is a gift from the God who cares about justice, order, and human flourishing. None of these things are evil. All of them become destructive when they are treated as ultimate.

Tim Keller articulated this pattern with a clarity that has shaped a generation of theological thinking. In Counterfeit Gods, he argued that an idol is not typically a bad thing. It is a good thing that has been made into an ultimate thing. It is a gift that has been promoted to a position that only the Giver should occupy. When a good thing becomes a god thing, it becomes a destructive thing, not because its nature has changed, but because its position has.

John Calvin said it first, and more bluntly: the human heart is a perpetual factory of idols. We do not need pagan temples to manufacture false gods. We carry the factory with us. And the raw material we use is almost always something good, something God gave us to enjoy, to steward, to use in His service, that we have quietly, imperceptibly, desperately elevated to the place where only God belongs.

This distinction matters enormously when we talk about politics. Because the moment someone raises concerns about the church's relationship to political power, the instinctive response is: "Are you saying politics is evil? Are you saying Christians shouldn't be involved? Are you saying we should just let the world burn?"

No. I am saying that political engagement is a good gift. Voting is a good gift. Advocacy, legislation, civic participation, the pursuit of justice through governmental structures, these are legitimate and even necessary expressions of neighbor-love. A Christian who ignores politics is not holier than one who engages; in many cases, they are simply less compassionate. The Samaritan did not walk past the beaten man and call it "spiritual detachment." He got involved, spent money, and used the systems available to him to help.

The question has never been whether Christians should engage politically. The question is what weight that engagement carries in the life of the soul. And that question, the question of weight, is where everything turns.

The Biblical Architecture: Psalm 20 and the Trust Distinction

Psalm 20 is a royal psalm, a prayer offered for the king of Israel as he prepares for battle. It is not a pacifist text. It does not condemn military action or pretend that warfare is irrelevant or that national defense is unspiritual. It takes seriously the reality that kings go to war, that nations face enemies, and that the survival of a people sometimes depends on the courage and competence of its leaders.

But in the middle of this very practical, very political prayer, the psalmist draws a line that echoes through the rest of Scripture:

"Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the Lord our God. They collapse and fall, but we rise and stand upright." —Psalm 20:7–8

This verse is one of the most important sentences in the Old Testament for understanding the relationship between faith and politics. And it is important because of what it does not say.

It does not say, "Chariots are evil." It does not say, "Only fools use horses." It does not condemn military preparedness as inherently faithless or suggest that Israel should disband its army and wait for miraculous intervention. The psalm is being prayed for a king who is about to fight. The psalmist is not naive about the realities of power.

What the psalmist does is draw a distinction between using and trusting. Chariots can be used, horses ridden, military resources deployed, political structures engaged. But none of these things can be trusted, not in the way that God is trusted, not as the foundation of hope, not as the thing that ultimately determines whether you stand or fall.

That distinction, between use and trust, between engagement and dependence, between the tool and the savior, is the theological key to this entire book.

You can use political structures without trusting them. You can vote without worshiping the outcome. You can advocate for justice without believing that justice depends entirely on your advocacy succeeding. You can engage the political process with passion, intelligence, and conviction without making the political process the foundation of your hope.

But the moment you cross from engagement to dependence, from using the chariot to trusting the chariot, from participating in politics to being emotionally sustained by politics, something has shifted in your soul. And that shift is what the psalmist warns against.

Because notice how the verse ends: "They collapse and fall, but we rise and stand upright." The people who trust chariots eventually discover that chariots are not ultimate. Chariots break, horses stumble, armies lose, political movements fail; every human structure eventually proves insufficient. And when it does, the people who built their hope on it collapse with it.

But the people who trusted in the name of the Lord stand, not because they were passive or did nothing, but because their standing was never dependent on the chariot in the first place.

That is the freedom the gospel offers to politically engaged Christians: you can participate fully without being destroyed by the outcome. You can care deeply without being consumed. You can engage the process without being defined by the results.

But that freedom is only available to people who have made the trust distinction. And making the trust distinction requires a kind of spiritual honesty that most of us find deeply uncomfortable.

Isaiah's Warning: The Egyptian Alliance

Isaiah 31 provides an even more pointed illustration of the trust distinction, and one that carries a devastating irony.

The historical situation is this: the kingdom of Judah faces military threat from Assyria, the superpower of the north. Assyria has already conquered the northern kingdom of Israel. Judah is next. The political establishment in Jerusalem, understandably terrified, wants to form a military alliance with Egypt, the superpower of the south. It is a classic balance-of-power strategy. Egypt has chariots and a massive, professional army, and can provide the military counterweight that tiny Judah cannot muster on its own.

From a purely strategic perspective, the alliance makes sense. It is the kind of decision any reasonable political advisor would recommend. It is pragmatic, realistic, and "responsible."

And God condemns it.

"Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses, who trust in chariots because they are many and in horsemen because they are very strong, but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the Lord!" —Isaiah 31:1

The accusation is specific: they "do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the Lord." The problem is not that Egypt exists or that military alliances are available. The problem is the direction of the gaze, the posture of the heart, the location of trust.

Judah has not consulted God. They have not prayed or sought divine guidance. They have assessed the political situation with their own wisdom, identified the most powerful ally available, and made their decision based on calculation rather than faith. They have looked at the problem with entirely human eyes and reached an entirely human conclusion.

And there is a devastating irony in this particular text that we must not miss. Egypt is where Israel was enslaved. Egypt is where God demonstrated His power to save, through plagues, through the parting of the sea, through the destruction of Pharaoh's army. Egypt is where the chariots of the most powerful nation on earth were drowned in the waters of the Red Sea while Israel walked through on dry ground.

And now, generations later, Israel is running back to Egypt's chariots for protection. They have forgotten the Exodus, forgotten what God did, forgotten who saved them from the very power they are now running to for help.

Does that pattern sound familiar? The American church was forged in a tradition that insisted on the sufficiency of Christ, the power of the Holy Spirit, and the sovereignty of God over nations. The great revivals that shaped American Protestantism were not produced by legislative action. They were produced by prayer, preaching, and the mysterious work of the Spirit. The church grew not because it had political access but because it had spiritual authority.

And now, in our anxiety, in our fear of cultural decline and moral collapse, we are running to the very power structures we were never meant to depend on, as though God's arm is too short to save without Washington's help, as though the Spirit who raised Christ from the dead needs a congressional majority to do His work.

We have forgotten our Exodus. And we are running back to Egypt.

Fear: The Accelerant That Makes the Idol Feel Necessary

Here is the crucial pastoral insight that holds this chapter together: good things do not become ultimate things in a vacuum. The accelerant is almost always fear.

No one sits down in a quiet moment of reflection and calmly, rationally decides, "I am going to make politics my functional savior." That is not how idolatry works. Idolatry happens under pressure and in the dark. It happens when the ground shifts beneath you and everything you value feels like it is under threat. It happens when you watch the news and your stomach tightens and your thoughts race and your blood pressure spikes and you think, with every fiber of your being, "Something has to be done."

That phrase, "something has to be done", is not wrong in itself. Christians should respond to injustice, should act in the face of evil, should work for the good of their neighbors and communities. The instinct to act is a gift from God.

But fear corrupts the instinct. Fear takes the legitimate desire to act and transforms it into a desperate need to control. Fear says not merely "something should be done" but "everything depends on what we do next." Fear removes God from the equation and puts the entire weight of the future on human shoulders, specifically, on the shoulders of whoever promises to carry it.

Fear does not patiently reason its way to conclusions. It does not wait for careful theological reflection or pause to ask, "What does Scripture say about this?" or "What does the history of the church teach us about moments like this?" Fear reads one headline and reorganizes the soul. It takes the hierarchy of your affections, God first, then everything else in its proper order, and scrambles it in an instant. And once fear has reorganized the soul, the idol does not feel like an idol. It feels like the only reasonable option.

This is why fear-driven Christianity is so spiritually dangerous. Not because the fears are always irrational, some of them are legitimate. Moral decline is real. Cultural hostility to Christianity is real. Legal threats to religious liberty are real. The erosion of institutions that once supported family life, community cohesion, and moral formation is real.

But fear bypasses the spiritual discernment that would normally prevent a good thing from becoming an ultimate thing. Under normal circumstances, a healthy Christian can distinguish between caring about an election and worshiping the outcome. But when fear enters the picture, when the stakes feel existential, when the threat feels total, when the enemy feels overwhelming, that distinction collapses.

When you are afraid, your threshold for idolatry drops dramatically. Suddenly the chariot does not look like a useful tool. It looks like the only thing standing between you and destruction. And once something feels like the only thing standing between you and destruction, it has become your functional savior, whether you call it that or not.

That is how political engagement crosses the line into political idolatry: not through a conscious decision to worship power, not through an explicit renunciation of faith, but through the gradual, fear-driven elevation of political outcomes into objects of ultimate concern, so that winning feels like salvation and losing feels like damnation.

And the terrifying thing is that the whole process feels responsible. It feels like stewardship, like "being realistic," like what any good Christian parent, any good pastor, any good citizen would do. Fear always makes the idol feel necessary. That is its most powerful trick.

1 Samuel 8: When Israel Wanted a King Like the Nations

The pattern we are describing is older than Hosea and older than Isaiah. It goes back to one of the most instructive and haunting passages in the entire Old Testament: the moment when the people of Israel demanded a king.

The context matters. Samuel, the last and greatest of the judges, is aging. His sons, Joel and Abijah, have been appointed as judges after him, but they are corrupt, taking bribes, perverting justice, failing to walk in their father's ways. Meanwhile, the Philistines remain a constant military threat. The nation feels vulnerable, leaderless, and afraid.

And so the elders of Israel come to Samuel with a request that carries the weight of a theological revolution:

"Appoint for us a king to judge us like all the nations." —1 Samuel 8:5

"Like all the nations." That phrase is the key to understanding everything that follows. Israel was not meant to be like the nations. Israel was meant to be set apart, a people whose king was invisible, whose protector was divine, whose security came not from military might or political institutions but from the covenant faithfulness of God Himself. Their distinctiveness, their strangeness, their refusal to organize themselves the way other nations did, was supposed to be their witness to the world that there was a God who could be trusted.

And now they want to be like everyone else. They want a visible king, a human protector, a leader they can see, touch, follow, and point to when the Philistines come.

Samuel is distressed, and he prays. And God's response is stunning:

"Obey the voice of the people in all that they say to you, for they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them. According to all the deeds that they have done, from the day I brought them up out of Egypt even to this day, forsaking me and serving other gods, so they are also doing to you." —1 Samuel 8:7–8

Notice: God does not say the request is merely pragmatically unwise. He does not say, "This is a bad political strategy." He says it is a spiritual rejection. The desire for a visible, powerful, human protector, even if that protector is imperfect, even if the request is driven by legitimate fear of legitimate enemies, is itself a rejection of God's invisible kingship.

This is extraordinary. Israel is not asking for a pagan deity. They are not building a golden calf. They are asking for a political institution, a monarchy, like the other nations have. And God identifies this request as idolatry, not because monarchy is inherently evil (God will later establish the Davidic monarchy and call it a covenant blessing) but because the motivation behind the request is fear-driven replacement of divine trust with human security.

God tells Samuel to warn them. And the warning is detailed and devastating:

"These will be the ways of the king who will reign over you: he will take your sons and appoint them to his chariots and to be his horsemen... He will take your daughters to be perfumers and cooks and bakers. He will take the best of your fields and vineyards and olive orchards... He will take the tenth of your flocks, and you shall be his slaves." —1 Samuel 8:11–17

The king they are demanding will take: their sons, their daughters, their land, their wealth, their freedom. The protector will become the predator, and the savior will become the enslaver. The power they trust will eventually consume them.

And then the most devastating line of all:

"And in that day you will cry out because of your king, whom you have chosen for yourselves, but the Lord will not answer you in that day." —1 Samuel 8:18

God grants the request and gives them Saul, and the experiment unfolds exactly as warned. Saul is tall, impressive, initially successful, and ultimately a disaster. He is consumed by jealousy, paranoia, and disobedience. He consults a medium instead of God. He sacrifices what is sacred for what is expedient. He becomes everything God said a fear-chosen king would become.

The pattern is consistent throughout Scripture, and it should terrify every American Christian who has ever thought, "If we just get the right person in office, everything will be okay."

When God's people demand political power as their security, God sometimes gives it to them, not as a blessing, but as a judgment. He lets them have what they asked for. And what they asked for eventually devours them.

That should make us pause, not because political participation is wrong but because the spirit in which we pursue political solutions, the fear, the desperation, the sense that everything depends on this leader or this election, may reveal that we have already made the request Israel made. And God may already be granting it.

Answering the Objection: "Are You Saying Christians Shouldn't Be Involved?"

Let me be as clear as I can, because this objection is the most common one I hear, and it deserves a thorough and honest answer.

No. I am not saying Christians should withdraw from political life. Christianity has never been a quietist religion. The claim that Christians should retreat from public engagement is neither historically accurate nor biblically supported. It is, in fact, a failure of love. If you see injustice and do nothing because you are worried about "mixing faith and politics," you have not achieved spiritual purity. You have abandoned your neighbor.

Abraham Kuyper, the Dutch Reformed theologian who also served as prime minister of the Netherlands, insisted that Christ lays claim to every square inch of existence, including politics. Kuyper saw no conflict between strong Christian conviction and vigorous political participation. He believed that precisely because Jesus is Lord, Christians have a responsibility to bring the implications of that lordship into every sphere of public life.

Augustine engaged the political philosophy of his day at the highest intellectual level. His City of God is not a call to political disengagement. It is a massive, sophisticated work of political theology that insists earthly politics matters, while simultaneously insisting that it is not ultimate. Augustine did not withdraw from Rome. He engaged Rome while relativizing Rome.

Calvin worked to shape civic structures in Geneva. The Reformers did not hide in monasteries and wait for the Second Coming. They built schools, shaped laws, reformed institutions, and insisted that the gospel had public implications.

So the question has never been whether Christians should be involved in politics. The historical and theological consensus is clear: they should.

But, and this is where the conversation must become painfully, uncomfortably honest, none of those great theologians made political power the functional center of their hope. Kuyper did not believe the church's survival depended on his political success. When his party lost elections, he did not treat it as a crisis of faith. Augustine did not panic when Rome fell. He wrote The City of God specifically to argue that the fall of an earthly empire is not the fall of the Kingdom of God. Calvin did not treat Geneva's government as the guarantor of the gospel. He knew the gospel would outlast Geneva.

They engaged politics without worshiping it and used the chariot without trusting it. They participated in civic life without making civic life ultimate, held political convictions with deep seriousness and loose hands, and cared about outcomes without being defined by them. That is the model. And it is a model that requires a level of spiritual maturity, theological clarity, and faith that does not require visible results to remain steady.

The question is not whether we are involved. The question is whether we can be involved without being consumed, whether we can care about politics without making politics the load-bearing wall of our spiritual house, whether we can use the chariot without kneeling before it.

If we can, if our engagement is driven by love rather than fear, by conviction rather than desperation, by hope in Christ rather than hope in a candidate, then political engagement is an expression of faithfulness.

If we cannot, if every election feels like the apocalypse, if every political loss feels like a spiritual crisis, if our engagement is driven more by terror than by love of neighbor, then something has shifted. And the shift needs to be named before it can be healed.

A Good Gift / God Substitute Diagnostic

As a practical tool for discernment, something you can return to regularly, not just once, consider these diagnostic questions about any good thing in your life. They apply to money, career, relationships, reputation, and family. But for the purposes of this book, apply them specifically to your political engagement.

If this were taken away, would I lose my sense of security? Not "would I be disappointed", that is normal. But would I feel existentially unsafe? Would my confidence in God's goodness collapse? If the loss of a political outcome would shake your faith in God, that outcome has been carrying weight that only God should carry.

Do I defend this with more passion than I defend the character of Christ? If you will argue for hours about a political leader but fall silent when Christ's name is mocked, if you will confront a coworker over a policy disagreement but not over a blasphemous joke, your passion reveals your priority.

Does this good thing require me to compromise my integrity to maintain it? This is perhaps the sharpest question. If supporting your political project requires you to excuse dishonesty, cruelty, sexual misconduct, corruption, or the dehumanization of people made in God's image, then the project has demanded a sacrifice that only an idol requires. God never asks you to sin in order to serve Him. Only idols make that demand.

Do I spend more emotional energy on this than on my relationship with God? Measure it honestly. Not in theory, in practice. How much time this week did you spend reading political commentary? How much time in prayer? How much time arguing on social media? How much time in Scripture? The allocation of your emotional energy is the most honest map of your actual priorities.

Would I be willing to lose this for the sake of faithfulness to Jesus? This is the ultimate test. Every good thing should be held with open hands. If you could not imagine remaining faithful to Christ if your political project failed, if the thought of political loss makes following Jesus feel pointless or impossible, then the political project has replaced Christ as the non-negotiable center of your life.

Has my political engagement made me more like Jesus or less? Look at the fruit. Has your involvement in politics made you more patient, more kind, more generous, more truthful, more humble? Or has it made you angrier, more contemptuous, more tribal, more willing to dehumanize people who disagree with you? The fruit reveals the root. If your political engagement is producing the fruit of the Spirit, it is being held properly. If it is producing rage, contempt, and self-righteousness, something has gone wrong at the root level.

These questions are not designed to produce guilt. They are designed to produce clarity. And clarity, the ability to see yourself honestly, without defensiveness or rationalization, is the first gift of repentance.

Holding the Chariot Loosely

So what does it look like to engage politically without making politics ultimate? What does it look like to use the chariot without trusting it?

It looks like a Christian who votes with conviction and then sleeps well regardless of the outcome, not because they do not care, but because their security does not depend on winning. It looks like a believer who advocates passionately for justice but does not demonize people who disagree, because they know that the image of God in their opponent is more fundamental than the policy difference between them. It looks like a church that engages the political process but refuses to let the political process set the emotional temperature of its worship, because the resurrection of Jesus Christ, not the latest poll numbers, determines the mood of the people of God. It looks like a pastor who speaks prophetically about public issues but refuses to endorse candidates or align the church with a party, because the church's prophetic voice depends on its independence from every political faction. It looks like a community of believers who can disagree politically within the same congregation without treating each other as enemies, because they know that baptism is a deeper bond than voter registration. It looks like the early church, which paid taxes to Caesar, prayed for rulers it did not choose, served neighbors it could not control, and refused to grant any earthly power the status of Lord, because that title was taken.

This is not disengagement. It is the most engaged and most free form of political participation available. It is engagement that is liberated from fear, because the person who is not afraid of losing cannot be manipulated by the threat of loss. It is engagement that is liberated from desperation, because the person whose hope is in Christ does not need the next election to vindicate their faith.

The chariot is a useful tool. Use it well and wisely, in service of your neighbor and the common good. But do not trust it or worship it. Do not let anyone convince you that the chariot is what stands between you and destruction, because the One who stands between you and destruction is not riding a chariot. He is hanging on a cross. And three days later, He will be standing in a garden, having defeated every power that has ever claimed ultimacy, including the ones you are tempted to trust right now.

Trust Him. And hold the chariot loosely.

*Key Takeaways*

• Every idol begins as a gift, not an evil thing but a good thing elevated beyond its design capacity. Political engagement is a good gift that becomes destructive only when treated as ultimate. • The trust distinction in Psalm 20 is the theological key: you can use chariots without trusting them, engage politics without worshiping outcomes. • Isaiah's warning about the Egyptian alliance carries a devastating irony: Israel ran back to the chariots of the very nation God had delivered them from. The American church risks the same pattern. • Fear is the accelerant that transforms good gifts into god substitutes. Under pressure, the chariot stops looking like a tool and starts looking like the only thing between you and destruction. • Israel's demand for a king "like the nations" was not pragmatically foolish, it was spiritually idolatrous. God identified it as a rejection of His kingship and granted it as a judgment. • The great Christian political theologians—Kuyper, Augustine, Calvin, engaged politics vigorously without making politics the functional center of their hope. That is the model. • The "Good Gift / God Substitute" diagnostic reveals where trust has been relocated by examining emotional stability, moral consistency, and willingness to lose. • Holding the chariot loosely means engaging politics with conviction and freedom, free from fear, free from desperation, and free from the need to win in order to maintain faith.

*Reflection Questions*

1\. Think of a political issue you care deeply about. Is your engagement driven more by love for your neighbor or by fear of losing something you value? 2\. Can you name a specific time when a political outcome felt like salvation, when winning felt like resurrection and losing felt like death? What does that reveal about the weight you are placing on politics? 3\. Apply the "Good Gift / God Substitute" diagnostic to your political engagement. Which question is most uncomfortable? What does the discomfort reveal? 4\. What would change in your spiritual life if you genuinely, deeply believed that no election result could threaten the Kingdom of God? 5\. Are there political positions you hold that you have never examined theologically, positions you inherited from your tribe rather than derived from Scripture? What would it look like to examine them honestly? 6\. Has your political engagement over the past five years made you more like Jesus or less? Be specific about the evidence.

*Practices*

• Personal: Take the "Good Gift / God Substitute" diagnostic and apply it to your three strongest political convictions. Write down your answers. Do not rationalize. Be honest with yourself and with God. • Personal: Fast from political media for one full week, no news, no commentary, no social media arguments. Replace the time with prayer, Scripture reading, and silence. At the end of the week, journal what you noticed about your anxiety, your peace, and your relationship with God. • Family: At a family dinner this week, ask: "What are the good gifts in our life that might be in danger of becoming ultimate? Where have we started trusting the chariot instead of God?" Let everyone answer without judgment. • Church: Propose a small group study, Sunday school series, or sermon series on idolatry, one that explicitly includes political idolatry on both the right and the left. If your church is unwilling to address political idolatry, ask yourself why.
