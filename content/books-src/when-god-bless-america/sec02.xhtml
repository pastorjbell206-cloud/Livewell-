Chapter One

Zanah: When You Keep the Vows and Give Away the Heart

"Israel did not stop believing in God. They simply stopped believing He was enough."

The Language God Chose

Of all the metaphors God could have used to describe Israel's unfaithfulness, He chose the most intimate and devastating one available: marriage.

Not contract law. Not military desertion. Not commercial fraud. Marriage.

The entire book of Hosea is structured around this metaphor. God tells the prophet Hosea to marry a woman named Gomer, a woman who will be unfaithful to him, as a living parable of what Israel has done to God. The relationship between Hosea and Gomer becomes a prophetic mirror of the relationship between Yahweh and His people. Every act of adultery, every wandering, every return, it is all there, written in the flesh of a real marriage so that Israel cannot look away.

What makes this metaphor so searing is what it implies about the nature of the betrayal. A business partner who breaks a contract is frustrating. A soldier who deserts the army is dangerous. But a spouse who sleeps with someone else while still coming home for dinner, while still saying "I love you," while still occupying the same house and sharing the same table, that is a particular kind of devastation.

It is the devastation of closeness without faithfulness. Of presence without loyalty. Of saying the right words while your heart belongs somewhere else.

That is zanah.

The Hebrew word appears dozens of times in the prophetic literature, and it is almost always translated with sexual language: "playing the whore," "prostituting oneself," "committing adultery." The prophets are not being crude for shock value. They are being precise. They are saying that what Israel has done to Yahweh is not casual negligence or mild forgetfulness. It is relational betrayal of the most intimate kind, the kind that can only happen between two parties who are supposed to be bound to each other in exclusive, covenant love.

And this is the word, not a political term, not a policy label, not an academic category, that best describes what is happening in parts of the American church today. Not atheism. Not apostasy in the classical sense of walking away from the faith entirely. But a quiet, respectable, fear-driven relocation of the heart's deepest trust from God to political power.

The vows are still spoken. The heart has moved.

What Zanah Is Not

This is where we need to slow down, because most people, including most churchgoers, assume that the opposite of faithfulness is atheism. If you are unfaithful to God, you must have stopped believing in Him. If you have wandered from the Lord, you must have rejected His existence. The assumption is that spiritual betrayal looks like a conscious decision to walk out the door, slam it shut, and never come back.

But zanah is not atheism.

It is not the abandonment of faith. It is the supplementation of faith. Israel kept worshiping. They kept the temple open. They kept the festivals on the calendar. They kept the prayers in their mouths. They kept the language of covenant on their lips. They did not deny Yahweh. They did not tear down the altars or burn the scrolls or declare that God was dead.

They simply decided that Yahweh, by Himself, was not enough to guarantee their security.

So they added. They added alliances with Egypt. They added treaties with Assyria. They added the military resources of foreign nations. They added the gods of surrounding peoples, not because they loved those gods, not because they found those religions intellectually compelling, but because they feared their enemies. And fear always demands more insurance than faith alone provides.

Listen to how God describes this in Hosea:

"For they have gone up to Assyria, a wild donkey wandering alone; Ephraim has hired lovers. Though they hire allies among the nations, I will soon gather them up." —Hosea 8:9–10

"Hired lovers."

That phrase should arrest us. God is not describing a nation that has abandoned religion. He is describing a nation that has kept religion while simultaneously pursuing security through political alliances. The temple is still open. The sacrifices continue. The priests are still employed. But the trust, the deep, functional, emotional trust that determines how a people actually live, has shifted.

Israel has not divorced Yahweh. Israel has brought another partner into the covenant bed and called it "strategy."

This is the anatomy of zanah: you do not throw God away. You simply add insurance. You do not deny the covenant. You supplement it. You do not leave the marriage. You bring someone else into the bed and tell yourself it is for the good of the family.

And God's response is not indifference. It is not theological detachment. It is the white-hot grief of a betrayed spouse who has given everything and watched it treated as insufficient.

Why Prophets Use Marital Language

The covenant between God and Israel was not a business arrangement. It was not a political contract with terms and penalties, like a treaty between two nations of equal standing. It was a personal bond patterned on the most intimate human relationship available, marriage. When God brought Israel out of Egypt, He did not merely free a labor force. He wed a bride.

Jeremiah captures this with heartbreaking beauty:

"I remember the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride, how you followed me in the wilderness, in a land not sown." —Jeremiah 2:2

God remembers the early days, when Israel trusted Him in the desert with nothing. No armies. No treaties. No chariots. No backup plan. Just manna falling from the sky each morning and a pillar of cloud leading through the wilderness. That was the honeymoon.

And it was beautiful. Not because the desert was comfortable, but because the dependence was total. Israel had no one to lean on but God, and God proved sufficient. Every time. The water came from the rock. The bread came from heaven. The enemies were defeated not by superior military strategy but by divine intervention. Israel learned, in those early years, what it meant to have God as their only security. And they thrived.

And then came the land.

And with the land came prosperity. And with prosperity came options. And with options came the quiet, reasonable, gradual migration of trust from the God who provided to the means by which provision came. The harvests were bountiful, so they began to credit the soil instead of the Sower. The borders were secure, so they began to trust the walls instead of the Wall-Builder. The economy was stable, so they began to rely on trade networks instead of the Provider who made trade possible.

It is the oldest spiritual pattern in the world. Dependence breeds intimacy. Prosperity breeds independence. Independence breeds forgetfulness. And forgetfulness breeds betrayal.

Deuteronomy 8 warned about this pattern with almost eerie precision:

"Take care lest you forget the Lord your God... lest, when you have eaten and are full and have built good houses and live in them, and when your herds and flocks multiply and your silver and gold is multiplied... then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt." —Deuteronomy 8:11–14

The warning is not against prosperity itself. It is against the spiritual amnesia that prosperity produces. When life is comfortable, we forget who made it so. When the chariots are plentiful, we forget who we used to cry out to when we had none.

The prophets use marital language because nothing else captures the offense. This is not a policy dispute between a government and its advisor. This is a God who created, rescued, sustained, fed, defended, and covenanted with a people, only to watch them hand their security to another while still singing His worship songs on Saturday morning.

That is not a doctrinal error. That is heartbreak. And the marital metaphor is the only container large enough to hold the grief.

Translate to Today: Jesus Plus the Strongman

Now here is where this stops being ancient history and starts being an examination of the American church.

What does zanah look like in twenty-first-century Christianity?

It looks like a church that still preaches the gospel on Sunday but spends the rest of the week convinced that political power is what will actually preserve the faith. It looks like believers who pray fervently but whose emotional stability rises and falls not with their relationship with God but with their party's electoral prospects. It looks like a Christianity that claims Jesus is Lord but acts as if the nation's future depends on the right president occupying the right office at the right time.

It looks like Jesus plus the strongman.

This is not a caricature. I have sat in rooms with sincere, Bible-believing Christians who speak about political leaders with a reverence and desperation that they do not bring to prayer meetings. I have watched congregations weep over election results with more visible emotion than they show during communion. I have heard pastors describe political losses as though the tomb were still sealed, as though Christ had not risen, as though the Spirit had not been poured out, as though the gates of hell had somehow prevailed.

I have watched Christian social media accounts erupt with praise for a political figure using language that should be reserved for the Messiah: "Sent by God." "Chosen for this moment." "Our only hope." "The one who will save us."

None of these people would say they worship a politician. That is the whole point.

Zanah does not require conscious idolatry. It only requires relocated trust. It only requires that something besides God becomes the thing you cannot imagine living without. It does not require a formal declaration of apostasy. It only requires that your functional hope, the thing that actually governs your emotions, your decisions, your fears, and your sense of security, has quietly migrated from the throne of Christ to the platform of a political movement.

Ask yourself: When you think about the future of the church in America, what comes to mind first? Is it the sovereignty of God, the power of the Holy Spirit, the faithfulness of Christ to build His church? Or is it the next election, the composition of the Supreme Court, the outcome of a culture war, the legislative agenda of your preferred party?

I am not asking whether those political realities matter. They do. Policies have consequences. Laws shape cultures. Justice requires engagement.

I am asking which one is ultimate in your emotional life.

Because whatever is ultimate in your emotional life is functionally your god.

And if that god is anything other than Jesus, if the thing you cannot imagine living without, the thing whose loss would devastate you beyond recovery, the thing that occupies the center of your hope, is a political outcome rather than the risen Christ, then you have not necessarily abandoned the faith.

You may have committed zanah.

And you may not even know it yet. Because the whole point of zanah is that it happens while you are still going to church.

Answering the Objection: "Isn't This Judging Motives?"

At this point, someone will push back: "You cannot see my heart. You do not know why I care about politics. You do not know whether my concern is idolatrous or simply responsible. Stop judging my motives."

That is a fair instinct. And Jesus does warn against reckless, hypocritical judgment. He tells us to take the log out of our own eye before examining the speck in someone else's.

But Jesus also says, "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:20). And the prophets did not hesitate to name zanah when they saw it, not because they could read hearts with omniscient precision, but because the behavioral evidence was overwhelming. You do not need to be omniscient to notice when a marriage is in trouble. The signs are visible to anyone willing to look.

When a person says they trust God but panics at every political development, that is evidence.

When a church preaches grace but excuses cruelty in leaders because those leaders protect Christian interests, that is evidence.

When believers cannot distinguish between a threat to their nation and a threat to their faith, that is evidence.

When the emotional temperature of a congregation is set not by the preaching of Scripture but by the latest political headline, that is evidence.

When Christians can tolerate almost any moral failure in a leader as long as that leader delivers the right policy outcomes, that is evidence.

I am not claiming the ability to read anyone's heart. I am saying that the prophetic tradition, the tradition of Hosea, Jeremiah, Isaiah, and Ezekiel, gives us the categories to name what we see. And what we see, in too many corners of the American church, looks remarkably like what Hosea described: a people who keep the worship and give away the trust.

The question is not "Are you political?"

The question is: "When you are afraid, where do you run first?"

If the honest answer is not "to God," then something has shifted. And the first step toward repentance is admitting the shift has happened.

How to Spot Relocated Trust in Your Emotional Reactions

Idolatry is notoriously difficult to detect in yourself because it always feels rational. No one thinks they are worshiping an idol. They think they are being realistic. Responsible. Wise. Strategic. The idol never introduces itself as an idol. It introduces itself as the obvious and necessary thing to do given the circumstances.

But there are diagnostic markers, not designed to condemn, but to invite honest self-examination. Consider these carefully:

Your emotional stability tracks with political outcomes more than spiritual disciplines. If an election result can ruin your week but a missed prayer time barely registers, something has shifted. If you spend more time reading political commentary than reading Scripture, and if the commentary produces more emotion in you than the Scripture does, your heart is telling you where your functional trust resides.

You defend behavior in your preferred leaders that you would condemn in your opponents.* This is perhaps the most visible symptom of zanah in American Christianity. It is the moral double standard that reveals tribal loyalty has replaced principled conviction. When your side's leader lies, it is "strategy." When the other side's leader lies, it is "corruption." When your side's leader is cruel, it is "strength." When the other side's leader is cruel, it is "tyranny." This inconsistency is not merely hypocrisy. It is the fruit of idolatry. When the idol is your tribe, you will protect the tribe's representatives regardless of their character, because the idol demands protection.

You cannot imagine the church thriving without political power. If the thought of Christianity without cultural influence, legal privilege, or political access fills you with dread rather than historical awareness, the early church had none of those things and turned the world upside down, you have attached the church's survival to something other than Christ. The church was born in a manger, grew under persecution, and conquered the Roman Empire not through political leverage but through the power of a resurrection. If you have forgotten that, political power has replaced the Holy Spirit as your functional source of confidence.

You treat criticism of your political position as an attack on your faith. When political allegiance and spiritual identity become fused, any critique of the former feels like blasphemy against the latter. If someone can question your preferred candidate and you experience it as an assault on the gospel, the fusion has already happened. Your politics and your faith have become so entangled that you can no longer tell where one ends and the other begins.

You use apocalyptic language for political events. If every election is "the most important in history," if every cultural shift is "the end of Christianity," if every political opponent is "evil" in an ontological rather than moral sense, the eschatological temperature of your language reveals that politics has become your horizon of hope. When the next election feels more decisive than the return of Christ, your eschatology has been quietly rewritten by your political commitments.

Your prayer life has been colonized by political anxiety. If most of your prayers are about elections, policies, and political enemies rather than about your own sanctification, the needs of your neighbor, and the advance of the gospel, your anxiety has reshaped your communion with God. Prayer has become a form of political lobbying directed at heaven rather than an act of surrender to the God who is sovereign over all nations.

None of these markers are unforgivable. All of them are diagnostic. And they all point to the same underlying condition: trust has been relocated from God to a political project, and the relocation happened so gradually that it felt like faithfulness.

That is the genius of seduction. It does not come as a stranger knocking at the door with a competing religion. It comes dressed as wisdom, wrapped in urgency, and speaking the language of responsibility. It says, "Of course you still believe in God. But be practical."

And "be practical" is the phrase that has undone more spiritual faithfulness than any atheist argument ever conceived.

The God Who Runs Toward the Unfaithful

But here is where the story turns. Because if Hosea only contained accusation, it would be unbearable. If it were only diagnosis without remedy, it would drive us to despair. But Hosea does not end with the accusation of adultery.

It ends with the most extraordinary invitation in the Old Testament.

After all the indictments, after the graphic language of betrayal, after the exposure of every alliance and every false lover, after the naming of every idol and every misplaced trust—God does something that no one in the ancient world would have expected a betrayed spouse to do.

He asks them to come home.

"Return, O Israel, to the Lord your God, for you have stumbled because of your iniquity. Take with you words and return to the Lord; say to him, 'Take away all iniquity; accept what is good, and we will pay with bulls the vows of our lips. Assyria shall not save us; we will not ride on horses; and we will say no more, 'Our God,' to the work of our hands. In you the orphan finds mercy.'" —Hosea 14:1–3

Read that repentance carefully. Notice its structure.

First: "Take away all iniquity." This is a confession that the problem is real. Not exaggerated. Not merely a matter of perception. Real iniquity, real unfaithfulness, that requires real forgiveness.

Second: "Assyria shall not save us." This is the renunciation of the false savior. It is the explicit naming of the political alliance that had functioned as a substitute for God's protection. It is Israel saying, out loud, what they had been refusing to admit: "We looked to Assyria for what only You can provide."

Third: "We will not ride on horses." This is the renunciation of military self-sufficiency. It is the admission that the chariots and horses, the instruments of political and military power, had become objects of trust rather than tools of stewardship.

Fourth: "We will say no more, 'Our God,' to the work of our hands." This is the deepest level of repentance. It is the admission that the things they had built, the alliances, the systems, the structures of human power, had been elevated to divine status in their hearts. They had called the idol "God." And now they are refusing to do so any longer.

That is the repentance this book calls for. Not withdrawal from public life. Not cynical disengagement. Not the abandonment of civic responsibility. But the radical, uncomfortable, soul-level honesty of admitting that we have given our hearts to something other than God, and the courageous act of taking them back.

And look at how God responds:

"I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely, for my anger has turned from them. I will be like the dew to Israel; he shall blossom like the lily; he shall take root like the trees of Lebanon." —Hosea 14:4–5

"I will heal their apostasy." Not "I will punish their apostasy." Not "I will reluctantly tolerate their return." Heal. The word is restorative. It implies that the unfaithfulness was a wound, a wound in the soul of Israel and a wound in the heart of God, and that God's response to the returning spouse is not grudging acceptance but extravagant healing.

"I will love them freely." The Hebrew word translated "freely" carries the sense of generosity, spontaneity, and abundance. God's love for the returning unfaithful is not measured, not cautious, not conditioned on a probationary period. It is free. Lavish. Unreserved.

This is the gospel before the gospel had a name.

This is a God who watches His bride walk away, names the betrayal with devastating honesty, and then, when the bride turns around, runs toward her.

If that sounds familiar, it should. It is the same God who, centuries later, told a story about a father who saw his prodigal son coming from a long way off and ran to meet him (Luke 15:20). It is the same God who, on a cross, looked at the people who had nailed Him there and said, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). It is the same God who, in the risen Christ, appeared to Peter, the disciple who had denied Him three times, and said not "Explain yourself" but "Do you love me?" (John 21:15–17).

The God of Hosea is not a God who waits at the end of a long road of self-improvement for His people to earn their way back. He is a God who meets them in their mess, heals what is broken, and loves them back to life.

That is the God we are called to return to. Not a taskmaster. Not a cold judge. Not a distant sovereign. A jealous Lover who refuses to share His bride but who will not stop pursuing her even when she wanders.

The Way Back Begins Here

The way back begins with naming the idol.

And the idol's name is not "politics." Politics is not the problem. The idol's name is relocated trust. The idol is whatever you cannot imagine living without that is not God. It is the thing that, if taken away, would cause your faith to collapse, because your faith was secretly resting on it rather than on Christ.

For some of us, the idol is a political party. For some, it is a political leader. For some, it is the cultural influence of Christianity. For some, it is the comfort of being in the majority. For some, it is the sense that "our country" and "our faith" are the same thing. For some, it is the feeling of power itself, the intoxicating sensation that we are in control, that we can shape the future, that we can win.

Name it. Be specific. Do not generalize. Do not say, "I trust God too much in politics", that is too vague to repent of. Say what it actually is. Say, "I have treated this leader as if he were anointed by God in a way that no politician is." Say, "I have attached the survival of my faith to the survival of my political party." Say, "I have been more afraid of losing an election than I am of losing my integrity."

Name it. Confess it. And return.

Because the God of Hosea is waiting. Not with folded arms and a lecture. With open arms and a feast.

And the road home is shorter than you think.

It begins with one sentence:

"Assyria shall not save us."

Whatever your Assyria is, name it. Lay it down. And come home.

Because the God who named the adultery is the same God who heals it. And He is not finished with you. He is not finished with the American church. And He has no intention of sharing His bride with a political idol, no matter how respectable that idol may appear.

*Key Takeaways*

• Zanah is not atheism, it is spiritual adultery: keeping God while relocating trust to something else. The vows remain; the heart has moved. • God chose marital language because the covenant is intimate, personal, and exclusive, and betrayal within intimacy is the deepest wound. • Israel did not stop worshiping; they supplemented worship with political alliances driven by fear. They "hired lovers" while keeping the temple open. • The oldest spiritual pattern is: dependence breeds intimacy, prosperity breeds independence, independence breeds forgetfulness, forgetfulness breeds betrayal. • Modern zanah looks like Christians who claim Jesus is Lord but whose emotional stability, moral standards, and deepest hopes are functionally attached to political outcomes. • The diagnostic question is not "Are you political?" but "When you are afraid, where do you run first?" • Biblical repentance from zanah is not withdrawal from public life but the explicit renunciation of political trust as ultimate: "Assyria shall not save us." • The God who accuses Israel of adultery is the same God who invites them home, and promises to heal their apostasy freely, lavishly, and without condition.

*Reflection Questions*

1\. In what areas of your life do you keep the language of faith while functionally trusting something else for your security? 2\. When political events go badly for your preferred side, does your emotional response look more like disappointment or despair? What does the intensity reveal about where your hope is actually placed? 3\. Can you think of a specific time when fear, not conviction, not principle, not love of neighbor, drove you to defend a political position or leader you might otherwise have questioned? 4\. What would it look like for you to say, with Hosea 14, "Assyria shall not save us"? What is your specific "Assyria", the political reality you have functionally trusted to do what only God can do? 5\. How might the church's public witness change if believers genuinely believed that Jesus, not political power, preserves the church? 6\. Which of the diagnostic markers in this chapter do you recognize in yourself? What would it look like to begin addressing them honestly?

*Practices*

• Personal: This week, before reading any political news, spend five minutes in prayer asking God to be your first source of security. Notice how this changes your emotional reaction to the headlines. • Personal: Write a letter to God, not a prayer, a letter, confessing the specific ways you have supplemented your trust in Him with political trust. Be honest. Be detailed. Do not spiritualize it. • Family: Read Hosea 14:1–5 aloud together and discuss: What are the "chariots" and "Assyrias" our family tends to trust instead of God? How does fear shape our family's relationship with politics? • Church: Invite your small group or Sunday school class to discuss one question: "What does relocated trust look like in our congregation, and how would we know if it were happening to us?"
