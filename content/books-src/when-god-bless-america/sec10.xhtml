Chapter Nine

The Generational Cost: When Hypocrisy Makes the Gospel Unbelievable

"Hypocrisy does not merely make the gospel less attractive. It makes the gospel unbelievable."

The Audience That Never Left the Room

There is a generation watching.

They are sitting in the back pews of churches they are not sure they belong to anymore. They are scrolling through social media feeds where pastors they once trusted share content that sounds more like campaign propaganda than the gospel. They are sitting across the dinner table from parents whose faith they once admired but whose political behavior has become indistinguishable from the tribalism they hear condemned from the pulpit every Sunday.

And they are drawing conclusions, not first about politics, not first about culture, not first about which party is right or which candidate deserves their vote, but about God.

This is the part of the conversation that pastors and parents need to hear, and it is the part that makes everyone uncomfortable. Because the generational exodus from the church in America is not primarily a story about secularism winning. It is not primarily a story about universities corrupting the young. It is not primarily a story about social media, pornography, or the seductive power of progressive ideology, though all of those factors are real and should not be dismissed. It is, at its deepest level, a story about credibility.

When the people who taught you that character matters excuse the most visible character failures because the person in question delivers the right political outcomes, you do not merely question their politics. You question their theology. You question whether they ever believed what they said. You question whether the faith they handed you was real or whether it was always, underneath the Sunday language, a vehicle for something else.

And once that question takes root, it does not go away easily. Because the question is no longer "Is this political position right?" The question becomes "Is any of it true?"

That is the generational cost of what we are doing. And the bill is already coming due.

The Data and the Stories Behind It

The research on religious disaffiliation among young adults in America has been extensive, and the findings are consistent across multiple studies and methodologies. The number of Americans under forty who identify as religiously unaffiliated, the so-called "nones", has grown dramatically over the past two decades. The Pew Research Center has documented this trend repeatedly. The General Social Survey has tracked it for even longer. The numbers are not in dispute. What is in dispute is the explanation.

The most common conservative explanation runs something like this: young people are leaving because secular culture is too appealing, because universities undermine faith, because social media exposes them to ideas that erode belief, because the sexual revolution made the church's moral demands inconvenient. In this telling, the problem is entirely external. The culture is corrupting our children. The solution is more insulation, more apologetics, more cultural engagement, more political power to push back against the secular tide.

There is partial truth in that explanation. The cultural pressures on young believers are real. The intellectual challenges are real. The sexual ethics of historic Christianity do create genuine tension with contemporary cultural norms. None of that should be minimized.

But that explanation, by itself, is dangerously incomplete. Because it conveniently ignores the most uncomfortable factor: the behavior of the church itself.

David Kinnaman's research at the Barna Group, published in books like unChristian and You Lost Me, found something that should have alarmed every church leader in America. Young adults who left the church did not primarily describe the faith as intellectually implausible. They described it as hypocritical, as judgmental, as politically entangled, as more interested in power than in love. They described a faith that said one thing and did another, and they found the contradiction intolerable.

The word that came up again and again in the research was not "false." It was "inauthentic."

That distinction matters enormously. A generation that thinks the gospel is intellectually false might be reached with better arguments. A generation that thinks the church is fundamentally inauthentic requires something far more costly: repentance.

What Hypocrisy Actually Communicates

We tend to think of hypocrisy as a personal moral failing, an individual who says one thing and does another. And it is that. But when hypocrisy becomes systemic, when entire communities normalize a gap between their stated values and their actual behavior, it communicates something far more devastating than individual moral weakness. Systemic hypocrisy communicates that the stated values were never real.

When a church preaches that character matters and then rallies behind a leader whose character is visibly, publicly, undeniably deficient, not in the way that all sinners are deficient, but in ways that the church itself would have condemned without hesitation in any other context, the message received by the watching world is not "Christians are imperfect." Everyone already knows that. The message received is: "Character never actually mattered to them. It was always about power."

When a church preaches that truth matters and then tolerates, defends, or celebrates dishonesty because the dishonest person advances their political interests, the message received is not "Christians are finding their way through a complex world." The message received is: "Truth was always negotiable. It was only a weapon they used against other people."

When a church preaches love for enemies and then dehumanizes, mocks, and demonizes political opponents with language that would shame a high school bully, the message received is not "Christians are passionate about justice." The message received is: "The love they preached was conditional. It only applied to people who agreed with them."

When a church preaches humility and then celebrates arrogance, cruelty, and domination as "strength," the message received is not "Christians value strong leadership." The message received is: "Jesus was a mascot. Power was the real god all along."

This is not a progressive critique. This is not a politically motivated complaint from people who simply dislike conservative politics. This is a theological catastrophe of the first order. Because the gospel's power to persuade depends, in part, on the community that proclaims it being recognizably shaped by it. When the community is not recognizably different from the tribalized, fear-driven, power-hungry culture around it, when the only distinguishing mark of the church is which political tribe it belongs to rather than whose image it bears, the gospel does not merely lose its appeal. It loses its plausibility.

Jesus said it plainly:

"By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another." —John 13:35

The world was supposed to recognize Christ's people by their love. Instead, an entire generation now recognizes Christ's people by their politics. And that recognition is not leading them toward faith. It is leading them away.

Jesus on Hypocrisy: The Sin He Condemned Most Fiercely

It is worth pausing to notice something about the Gospels that should unsettle every church leader in America: Jesus reserved His most devastating language not for pagans, not for the sexually immoral, not for tax collectors or prostitutes, but for the religious establishment. Matthew 23 is the longest sustained rebuke in the Gospels. Seven times Jesus pronounces "woe" on the scribes and Pharisees. And the recurring charge is not theological error in the abstract. It is the specific sin of saying one thing and living another:

"The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat, so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice." —Matthew 23:2–3

They preach but do not practice. They bind heavy burdens on others but will not lift a finger themselves. They do everything to be seen. They love the places of honor. They love being called "teacher" and "leader." They tithe mint and cumin but neglect justice, mercy, and faithfulness. They clean the outside of the cup while the inside is full of greed and self-indulgence.

And the metaphor Jesus reaches for is devastating:

"Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs, which outwardly appear beautiful, but within are full of dead people's bones and all uncleanness." —Matthew 23:27

Whitewashed tombs: beautiful on the outside, dead on the inside, religious performance masking spiritual rot.

Now notice: Jesus does not say the Pharisees' theology is entirely wrong. He even tells the crowd to do what they teach. The content of their teaching is largely correct. What is catastrophically wrong is the disconnect between their teaching and their lives. They have the right words but the wrong character, the right doctrine but the wrong spirit, the right positions but the wrong posture.

And Jesus says this about them:

"You travel across sea and land to make a single proselyte, and when he becomes a proselyte, you make him twice as much a child of hell as yourselves." —Matthew 23:15

That verse should terrify every Christian leader in America. Because Jesus is saying that hypocritical religious leadership does not merely fail to produce disciples. It actively produces the opposite. The converts produced by hypocritical leaders are worse off than they were before, not because the theology is false, but because the hypocrisy teaches them that theology is a tool for manipulation rather than a call to transformation.

When the church normalizes the gap between its words and its behavior, it does not produce a generation that simply drifts away. It produces a generation that is actively inoculated against the gospel, because they have seen what they believe to be the gospel lived out, and what they saw was not good news. It was tribal power dressed in religious language.

If we are producing young people who look at the church and see not the beauty of Christ but the ugliness of partisan tribalism, if the fruit of our "engagement" is a generation that finds the gospel unbelievable not because of its claims but because of its claimants, then we need to stop blaming the culture and start examining ourselves.

The Specific Hypocrisies That Are Costing Us

Let me be specific, because vague accusations of hypocrisy are easy to dismiss. The hypocrisies that are driving the next generation away are concrete, visible, and well-documented.

The character reversal. For decades, evangelical churches taught that character is the most important qualification for leadership, in the home, in the church, and in public office. This was not a controversial position. It was the position. Sermons were preached about it, and books were written about it. It was the stated reason for political engagement: we need leaders of character, leaders who reflect biblical values, leaders whose personal integrity matches their public promises.

And then, in the span of a single election cycle, that position was abandoned. Not revised. Not careful. Abandoned. Character stopped being the primary criterion and became an afterthought, something to be excused, explained away, or simply ignored because the political stakes were deemed too high.

The watching generation noticed. And what they concluded was not that the church had made a difficult pragmatic calculation. What they concluded was that the church had never meant it in the first place. The "character matters" conviction was exposed, in their eyes, as a weapon deployed selectively rather than a principle held consistently.

The compassion inversion. The Jesus of the Gospels is consistently portrayed as one who moves toward the vulnerable: the sick, the outcast, the foreigner, the poor, the powerless. The church has historically been at its most beautiful when it has done the same, building hospitals, establishing schools, feeding the hungry, welcoming refugees, visiting prisoners.

But when political alignment demands hostility toward the vulnerable, when immigrants become "invaders," when the poor become "lazy," when refugees become "threats," when compassion is reframed as weakness and cruelty is celebrated as strength, the watching generation sees a faith that has betrayed its own Savior. They do not see a church working through complexity. They see a church that has chosen its political tribe over its theological identity.

The truth crisis. Christianity is built on truth claims. The resurrection is either historically true or it is not. The Scriptures are either trustworthy or they are not. The character of God is either faithful or it is not. The entire apologetic enterprise depends on the claim that truth matters, that reality is knowable, that lies are destructive, that integrity in speech reflects integrity of soul.

And then the watching generation sees churches, and church leaders, tolerating, repeating, and sometimes enthusiastically promoting claims that are demonstrably false. Conspiracy theories are shared without scrutiny, and misinformation is spread without correction. Leaders who lie repeatedly and provably are defended not by arguments for their truthfulness but by arguments for why their lying doesn't matter because the other side is worse.

When the community that claims to serve the God of truth becomes a community that tolerates untruth whenever it is politically convenient, the foundation of every other truth claim, including the gospel itself, is undermined. Because if truth is only valued when it serves the tribe, then truth is not actually valued. It is instrumentalized. And a generation that has watched truth be instrumentalized will not easily believe that the truth claims of the gospel are anything different.

The enemy-love gap. Jesus said, "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). This is not an optional addendum to the faith. It is one of the most distinctive and radical commands in the entire New Testament. It is what sets Christian ethics apart from every other ethical system in the ancient world. It is the command that made pagans stare in astonishment at the early church.

And the watching generation sees a church that has largely abandoned it. Not in theory; no one has officially removed enemy-love from the doctrinal statement. But in practice, political opponents are spoken about with a contempt, mockery, and dehumanization that bears no resemblance to anything Jesus ever modeled. The people we are supposed to love are the people we most visibly hate. And the generation that notices this does not think, "Well, enemy-love is aspirational." They think, "They don't actually believe any of this."

Answering the Objection: "They're Leaving Because of Culture, Not the Church"

This is the objection I hear most frequently from church leaders, and it needs to be addressed with care, because it contains a genuine insight wrapped in a dangerous deflection.

The genuine insight: cultural pressures on young believers are real and significant. The ubiquity of pornography has affected sexual ethics and relational expectations. Social media has created comparison cultures that breed anxiety and dissatisfaction. Universities have, in some cases, created environments hostile to religious conviction. The entertainment industry often portrays Christianity as bigoted, outdated, or irrelevant. The sexual revolution has made the church's teaching on sexuality the most socially costly position a young person can hold. All of this is true.

The dangerous deflection: using these real cultural pressures as a shield against self-examination. Because the logic runs like this: "If young people are leaving because of TikTok and universities, then the church doesn't need to change. The culture needs to change. And the way to change the culture is more political power."

That logic is a closed loop. It starts with the assumption that the church is fine and the culture is the problem, and it ends by demanding more of the very thing, political power and cultural control, that is alienating the next generation in the first place.

The honest truth is that both factors are at work simultaneously. Cultural pressures are pulling young people away from the church. And the church's own behavior is pushing them. The pull factors are real. But the push factors are the ones we can actually do something about, because they are ours to repent of.

Here is what pastors know in their bones but are often afraid to say out loud: a young person who sees the church living out the gospel with integrity, compassion, honesty, and sacrificial love can withstand enormous cultural pressure. The faith is not so fragile that a college philosophy class can destroy it, if it has been formed by a community that embodies what it preaches. What makes the cultural pressures devastating is not their strength. It is the weakness of the church's witness. When the culture says "Christianity is hypocritical" and the young person's lived experience in the church confirms it, the cultural critique sticks. But when the culture says "Christianity is hypocritical" and the young person's lived experience in the church contradicts it, the critique bounces off.

The church's greatest apologetic has never been its arguments. It has been its life. And when the life of the church validates the world's critique rather than refuting it, no amount of apologetics, youth programming, or political activism will keep the next generation.

The Witness of 1 Peter: Beauty in a Hostile World

The New Testament provides a remarkable guide for exactly this situation, a situation where the surrounding culture is hostile, skeptical, and predisposed to think the worst of the church. And the strategy it prescribes is not political power. It is visible, undeniable goodness.

The apostle Peter, writing to Christians scattered across a hostile Roman world, does not tell them to seize political influence, to fight for cultural dominance, or to find a sympathetic Caesar. He tells them to live so beautifully that even their critics will be silenced:

"Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation." —1 Peter 2:12

Peter's strategy is extraordinary. He does not deny that the world will misrepresent Christians. He assumes it. He expects slander, false accusations, and hostility. But his response is not outrage, not political maneuvering, not culture war. His response is: live so well that the accusation becomes absurd. Let your conduct be the rebuttal. Let your love be the apologetic. Let the sheer quality of your life together make the slander unsustainable.

He continues:

"But even if you should suffer for righteousness' sake, you will be blessed. Have no fear of them, nor be troubled, but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect, having a good conscience, so that, when you are slandered, those who revile your good behavior in Christ may be put to shame." —1 Peter 3:14–16

Notice the sequence. First, honor Christ as holy in your hearts, that is, make sure your ultimate allegiance is rightly ordered. Second, be prepared to give a reason for your hope, not for your political platform, not for your cultural agenda, but for your hope. Third, do it with gentleness and respect. Fourth, maintain a good conscience so that the slander has nowhere to land.

This is the early church's strategy for credibility in a hostile world. And it could not be more different from what the American church is currently doing.

We are not giving people a reason for our hope. We are giving them a reason for our outrage. We are not demonstrating gentleness and respect. We are demonstrating contempt and tribal aggression. We are not maintaining a good conscience. We are excusing behavior that violates our stated convictions because the political stakes feel too high.

And the result is exactly what Peter's strategy was designed to prevent: the watching world, including the watching next generation, has credible grounds for dismissing the church, not because the gospel is false but because the community that proclaims it is not living as though it is true.

Titus 2 and the Doctrine That Depends on Living

Paul's letter to Titus provides one of the most striking connections between conduct and doctrine in the entire New Testament. Writing to a young pastor on the island of Crete, a culture that was famously difficult and morally chaotic, Paul instructs Titus to teach sound doctrine. But he ties the teaching to something unexpected: the behavior of the community.

He tells older men to be self-controlled and sound in faith. He tells older women to be reverent in behavior and to teach what is good. He tells younger men to be models of good works. He tells slaves to be trustworthy in every way. And then he gives the reason, and the reason is stunning:

"...so that in everything they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior." —Titus 2:10

Adorn the doctrine. The Greek word is kosmeō, from which we get "cosmetic." It means to make beautiful, to arrange attractively, to display in a way that draws admiration. Paul is saying that the behavior of the community is what makes the doctrine beautiful to the watching world.

The doctrine, by itself, is true. But truth that is not embodied is not compelling. Truth that contradicts the lives of those who proclaim it is not merely unpersuasive, it is repulsive. The doctrine needs to be "adorned", dressed in the clothing of a community that actually lives it, before it becomes attractive to the world.

Now apply this to our moment. What is the American church's conduct currently "adorning"? What does the world see when it looks at us?

If what the world sees is a community defined by political tribalism, moral inconsistency, selective outrage, and a willingness to compromise integrity for political gain, then we are not adorning the doctrine. We are defacing it. We are taking the most beautiful message in the history of the world and making it ugly.

And the generation that sees the ugliness is not wrong to recoil. They are responding to exactly what Paul said they would respond to: the doctrine, when lived badly, repels rather than attracts. The problem is not with the doctrine. The problem is with the community that has made the doctrine unrecognizable.

A Credibility Rebuild Plan

If the diagnosis is credibility loss, then the prescription is not better marketing, more engaging youth programs, or trendier worship services. The prescription is repentance that produces visible, sustained, costly change.

What would a credibility rebuild look like for the American church? It would require at least the following:

Consistency of moral standards regardless of political alignment.

The church must hold the same moral standards for leaders on "its side" as it holds for leaders on the other side. If dishonesty disqualifies an opponent, it must disqualify an ally. If cruelty is condemned in the other party, it must be condemned in ours. If sexual misconduct matters when the other side does it, it must matter when our side does it. This is not political neutrality. It is moral integrity. And it is the bare minimum of prophetic credibility.

Public repentance for specific, named failures. Vague confessions of "imperfection" are not repentance. The watching generation has seen specific behaviors, specific endorsements, specific compromises. They need to hear specific acknowledgments. "We were wrong to excuse this." "We should not have tolerated that." "We compromised our witness here, and we are repenting of it." The specificity of the repentance must match the specificity of the failure, or it will be dismissed as public relations rather than genuine contrition.

A recovery of the church's independence from partisan identification. The church must become a community that no political party can claim. Not because the church has no political convictions, it does, and it should, but because its convictions are derived from Scripture and the lordship of Christ rather than from a party platform. When the church's positions line up with the left on some issues and with the right on others, when it is consistently inconsistent with every earthly faction because its allegiance is to a heavenly King, then and only then will its prophetic voice regain credibility.

Visible, sacrificial love that cannot be explained by self-interest.

The early church gained credibility by caring for people who could not benefit them, plague victims, abandoned infants, widows, prisoners. The watching generation needs to see the same. They need to see a church that serves immigrants regardless of their legal status, that feeds the hungry regardless of their voting record, that visits prisoners regardless of their crimes, that cares for the poor regardless of whether the poor are "deserving." When the church's love is indiscriminate, when it mirrors the God who "sends rain on the just and the unjust" (Matthew 5:45), the gospel becomes visible in a way that no sermon, no book, and no political victory can achieve.

Honest engagement with the generation's questions rather than dismissal of their concerns. The next generation is not asking these questions because they are rebellious, lazy, or corrupted by culture. Many of them are asking because they love Jesus and are grieved by what they see. They deserve to be heard, not lectured. They deserve honest answers, not defensive deflections. They deserve a church that treats their departure as a wound to be healed rather than a betrayal to be punished.

What Is at Stake

Let me be as direct as I can about what is at stake.

We are not merely losing a generation to secularism. We are losing a generation to disillusionment, and disillusionment is far harder to overcome than doubt. A person who doubts the gospel can be reached with evidence, with argument, with testimony, with encounter. A person who is disillusioned with the church has already seen the testimony, and found it wanting. They are not asking "Is this true?" They are saying "I watched people who claimed it was true, and it didn't change them."

That is a different problem, and it requires a different solution. The solution is not more information or better programs. The solution is not political victory that will make the church culturally dominant again so that the next generation has no choice but to participate.

The solution is a church that looks like Jesus: a church that tells the truth even when it is costly, that loves its enemies even when it is ridiculed, that holds moral standards consistently even when it is inconvenient, that cares for the vulnerable even when there is no political reward, that repents publicly even when it is humiliating, a church that would rather lose every political battle than lose its integrity, a church that knows the difference between winning an election and winning a soul.

That kind of church has always been the most powerful apologetic in the world, not because it is perfect (no church is), but because it is honest about its imperfection, relentless in its pursuit of faithfulness, and visibly shaped by the Savior it proclaims.

The next generation is not looking for a perfect church. They are looking for a real one. And "real" means a community where the gap between confession and conduct is acknowledged, lamented, and actively closed, not defended, justified, or dismissed.

Paul told the Corinthians that when an unbeliever walks into a faithful assembly and encounters genuine worship and community, the response should be to fall on their face and declare, "God is really among you" (1 Corinthians 14:25).

When the next generation walks into our assemblies, are they falling on their faces? Or are they walking out the door? The answer depends not on our doctrine, our doctrine is fine. It depends on whether our lives match what our doctrine claims. And if they do not, then the most urgent task before the American church is not to win the next election. It is to become the kind of community that makes the next generation say, against all cultural pressure, "God is really among you."

That is the generational cost of hypocrisy: not merely empty pews, but a generation that has concluded the pews were empty of God long before they were empty of people. And that conclusion, however painful, may be a gift, if we have the courage to hear it as a call to repentance rather than a reason for resentment.

Key Takeaways

• The generational exodus from the church is not primarily a story about secularism winning, it is a story about credibility lost. Young adults describe the church as hypocritical and politically entangled, not merely intellectually implausible. • Systemic hypocrisy communicates that the church's stated values were never real, that character, truth, love, and humility were always tools for tribal power rather than genuine convictions. • Jesus reserved His fiercest condemnation for the Pharisees' hypocrisy, not because their theology was wrong, but because the gap between their teaching and their lives made them factories of disbelief (Matthew 23:15). • The specific hypocrisies driving the next generation away are concrete: the character reversal, the compassion inversion, the truth crisis, and the abandonment of enemy-love in practice. • Blaming culture alone for the exodus is a dangerous deflection, the pull factors are real, but the push factors (the church's own failures) are the ones we can repent of. • Peter's strategy for credibility in a hostile world is not political power but visible, undeniable goodness, conduct that silences slander because it cannot be denied (1 Peter 2:12). • Paul teaches that the community's conduct "adorns" the doctrine (Titus 2:10), making it beautiful or ugly depending on whether the lives match the words. • The most urgent task before the American church is not winning the next election but becoming the kind of community that makes the next generation say, "God is really among you."

Reflection Questions

1\. If your children or grandchildren described the faith based solely on how they have seen you engage with politics over the last decade, what would they say Christianity is about? 2\. Can you name a specific moral standard you applied differently to a political leader you supported versus one you opposed? What does the inconsistency reveal about what you actually value? 3\. When a young person tells you they left the church because of hypocrisy, is your first instinct to listen or to explain why they are wrong? What does your instinct reveal? 4\. Has your political engagement over the past five years made the gospel more or less believable to the people watching your life? Be specific about the evidence. 5\. What would it cost you, relationally, socially, politically, to hold the same moral standards for leaders on your side as you hold for leaders on the other side? Are you willing to pay that cost? 6\. If the next generation could ask the church one question and receive an honest answer, what do you think they would ask? Are you prepared to answer it?

Practices

• Personal: Ask a young adult in your life, your child, a college student, a young coworker, this question: "What does the church's political engagement communicate to you about God?" Listen without defending, correcting, or explaining. Write down what you hear. Pray over it for a week. • Personal: Review your social media activity over the past six months. How much of it reflects the character of Jesus? How much of it reflects political tribalism? Would an unbeliever scrolling through your posts encounter the gospel or a partisan agenda? Delete what does not honor Christ. • Family: At a family meal this week, ask each person: "When you think of our church, do you think of a community shaped by Jesus or a community shaped by politics?" Let everyone answer honestly. Do not argue. Process what you hear together in prayer. • Church: Propose that your church leadership invite a group of young adults, including some who have left, to share honestly about what they have experienced. Do not set the agenda. Do not offer rebuttals. Simply listen, repent of what is true, and commit to specific, visible changes. Let the young adults hold you accountable.
