CHAPTER ELEVEN

Single Parents, Blended Families, and the God Who Holds

Raising children in the faith as a single parent or blended family — discipleship that holds when the household does not match the picture.

I have written the last several chapters with one household in mind — two parents, married, both present, both professing faith — because that is the picture most Christian parenting books assume, and I have assumed it too, since it is the household I live in and the one I know best. But if I stop there I fail a great many of the people reading this, many of whom are not in that picture at all; and the implication, rarely stated outright but always felt, is that a household which does not match the picture has children whose formation is compromised beyond repair.

That implication is a lie, and it needs to be named as one, plainly, so that the parent reading this in a house that does not match the picture can hear what follows without the lie sitting in the way.

Single parents are doing the work of formation in millions of homes — single mothers more often than not, but single fathers too, some through divorce, some through death, some through circumstances they did not choose and would never have chosen. And the message they have received from the Christian world, sometimes spoken but more often delivered through silence, is that their household is deficient: that the absence of a second parent, a father above all, has opened a deficit that cannot be closed, that their children are statistically bound for worse outcomes, that the whole project is, at best, damaged goods.

I want to speak directly to that, because it is half true and entirely unhelpful.

The half that is true is this: two believing parents create a formation environment with structural advantages — more presence, more modeling, more reinforcement — and a child who watches a mother and a father both walk with God has two windows into the character of God. That is real, and I will not pretend otherwise.

The half that is a lie is the leap from advantage to impossibility. The absence of one parent does not make formation impossible, because the God who promises to be Father to the fatherless, who keeps particular watch over the widow and the orphan, has not left the single parent's household without resources; He promised to fill what is missing, and He meant it. I have watched Him do it. I have watched single mothers raise children whose faith went the distance, and single fathers carry a weight that would have flattened most people and still produce homes where Christ was more visible, not less, precisely because the dependence on God was more real — for the parent clinging to God for lack of any other option is modeling a faith the comfortable two-parent home often lacks, and a child who watches a parent depend on God honestly, daily, with no backup plan, is learning something about God that the child in the easy home may never learn.

A Different Household, Not a Broken One

The single-parent home is not a damaged copy of the two-parent home; it is a different household, with different challenges and different graces, and the formation principles in this book apply to it without exception. The four channels of the household catechism still run — what you praise, what you worry about, what you repeat, what you rest in — and they run in a single-parent home exactly as they run anywhere else, because the child is still formed by proximity to the parent's actual life with God, and the parent's faith is still the primary curriculum.

What changes is the weight. The single parent carries more of it — the economic weight, the emotional weight, the logistical weight, the spiritual weight, all of it, and carries it without the partner who was supposed to share the load. That weight is real, and I will not spiritualize it into something lighter than it is. Some days it is crushing; some weeks; some years.

Which is precisely where the church is supposed to come in, as the extended family the single-parent household needs, the additional believing adults the child needs, the uncles and aunts and mentors the home cannot supply on its own. This is not a program. It is the body of Christ working as the body of Christ — and a single mother in a church that knows her name, that feeds her family, that draws her sons into the lives of godly men, that walks with her through the hard years and partners with her instead of pitying her, is a single mother who is not alone, and her children are not fatherless, because the church has fathers in it, and those fathers can extend to her children what the absent one did not.

Let me be direct with the church for a moment. A congregation that praises the family and offers no concrete support to single parents is not a family church; it is a church for a certain kind of family, and the single parent feels the difference — she walks into the mother-daughter event and sits alone, he comes to the men's retreat and never hears his situation named, their children sit in the youth group and no one has asked whether they have a believing adult who is not their surviving parent. The church has to do better, not out of pity, but out of the theology it already confesses: if the body has members carrying more than they can carry alone, the body bears the burden with them. That is not optional. That is Galatians 6.

Faith Formation in a Blended Family

The blended family has its own set of formation challenges, different from the single-parent home and almost never addressed in Christian parenting literature, because it is a household where children are being formed by adults who do not all share the same history. The stepparent enters mid-story, into a child who already has a past with a biological parent the stepparent was never part of, loyalties that compete, and grief over the loss of the original family — so that no matter how loving the new family is, its very presence is a standing reminder of what was lost.

Formation in a blended family is complicated, asking for more patience, more communication, and more grace than a household where everyone has been together from the start. The stepparent who claims full parental authority too quickly will usually be resisted; the biological parent who expects the child to transfer loyalty to the new spouse will usually be disappointed; and the household that pretends the blending is finished when it is not will breed confusion, because the child knows the truth even while the adults perform otherwise.

What I have seen work is a posture of presence without presumption — a stepparent who is present, consistent, and kind, who does not force the relationship but lets it grow at the child's pace rather than the adult's, while the biological parent supports the stepparent's authority in age-appropriate ways without requiring the child to feel about the stepparent the way they feel about their own. The household says openly that this family has a complex history, and that the complexity is not a failure but a reality God is present in.

The formation principles still apply. The household still has a catechism — what it praises, what it worries about, what it repeats, what it rests in — and the added complication is that the child may be absorbing two catechisms, one from each home, and the two may contradict. A child who spends weekdays in a home where Christ is the center and weekends in a home where Christ is irrelevant is receiving a divided inheritance, and that is painful; it is also not fatal, because the home where Christ is the center has something the other home does not, the presence and power of the Holy Spirit, and the Spirit is not bound by a custody arrangement.

So hear this, blended-family parent: you are not at a disadvantage that disqualifies you from the work, but at a disadvantage that requires more of you — more prayer, more patience, more dependence on God, more willingness to hold the tension of a situation that does not resolve neatly. The formation that happens in a blended family is sometimes deeper than the formation in a family that was never broken, precisely because the blended family has no illusions about its own sufficiency; it knows it needs God, and that knowing is itself formative for the children who are watching.

The Costly Hope

The hope in this chapter is for every parent whose household does not match the picture on the cover of the parenting book, because God is not limited by your family structure — not by your marital status, the complexity of your custody arrangement, your ex-spouse's hostility to the faith, your age, your energy, your resources, or your mistakes. He is limited by nothing. He placed your children in your care, in this household, in this configuration, and He intends to use you to form them — not despite the difficulty. Through it.

Let them see a faith that is not decorative but necessary. The lifeline of the household that has nothing else to hold onto.

Let your children see you depend on God; let them see you carry what is too heavy and bring it to the One who carries you. That is real formation, and it is a faith the child will remember.

A Practical Word for Single Christian Parents

You need practical words more than theological ones, because you already know God is with you. What you need is help.

First, accept help. Many single parents resist it, feeling they should manage alone and that to accept help is to admit defeat — but it is not defeat, it is wisdom, because God designed the body of Christ to share burdens. You are not failing when you let someone watch your children so you can rest, when you ask another family to bring your son on their camping trip, when you let the grandmother take the kids for a weekend so you can breathe; you are cooperating with the design.

Second, identify the believing adults your children need, because your child needs role models beyond you. If you are a single mother, your son needs godly men in his life — not a replacement father, a presence, a man who can take him fishing, teach him to change a tire, be the male image of God the household does not currently hold; and if you are a single father, your daughter needs godly women, not a replacement mother, but women who can speak into her life in the ways only women can. Look for these people in your church, ask your pastor to help you find them, and if your church cannot provide them, find a church that can — because your children's formation depends partly on the community around them, and building that community is among the most important things you can do.

Third, do not let guilt drive your parenting. Many single parents compensate for the absent parent by going permissive, avoiding discipline, trying to be both good cop and bad cop and ending up neither, when what your children need is a parent who is clear, consistent, and loving rather than a parent performing compensation. The guilt is understandable; it is not useful. Bring it to God, let Him carry it, and then parent your children the way they need — with clarity, with warmth, and with the confidence of knowing that God put them in your care and equipped you for the task.

Fourth, tell your children the truth about your household, at a level they can receive, because children in single-parent homes carry unspoken questions — Why is my family different? Is something wrong with us? Did I cause this? — and you can answer those questions before the child has to ask. Tell them your family looks different and that God is in this family completely; tell them the absence of one parent is not the absence of God's provision; tell them you are doing this together, and that together includes God. Children can carry more truth than we think. What they cannot carry is silence, because silence breeds shame, while truth, spoken with love, breeds security.

The Mother Who Taught Me the Principle

There is a single mother in my church who has taught me more about formation than most two-parent families I know. She was divorced when her children were young, in circumstances that were painful and that she did not choose, and she found herself raising three children on a teacher's salary, in a small apartment, with no family nearby.

What she did was this: she built a household catechism with the materials she had. She could not afford the Christian school, the retreats, the summer camps — but what she could afford was time, and she spent it lavishly. Every evening she sat at the table and asked her children about their day, not casually but with attention; she prayed with them before bed, not for sixty seconds but for ten minutes, naming each child's specific concerns; she read the Bible to them, not from a curriculum but from wherever she happened to be reading herself, and she told them what it meant to her, in plain language, her own struggles included.

Her children are in their twenties now, and all three are walking with Jesus — and all three credit their mother's faith, not the church's programs, as the reason they believe. One of them told me the most formative thing about his childhood was hearing his mother pray; she prayed, he said, like she was talking to someone who was actually in the room. He believed God was real because his mother so clearly believed God was real, and her belief was neither inherited nor performed. It was forged — in the fire of raising three children alone while trusting a God she could not see to hold what she could not carry.

Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ.

Galatians 6:2

That woman's household was not the model. It was the principle. And the principle worked because the presence was real.
