CHAPTER NINE

The Teenage Years, When Formation Goes Underground

When a teenager pulls away and questions the faith — what the adolescent brain is doing, when it is more than a phase, and how to keep the door open.

There is a day that comes for every parent, and you will not see it coming: the child who told you everything stops telling you things, the child who crawled into your lap during family worship now sits across the room and says nothing, the child who asked you about God at bedtime now reaches for the earbuds the moment the car door shuts. The door that was open is closing, and you did not hear it happen. You only noticed the silence.

Name it for what it is. The teenage years are the season when formation goes underground — and everything you built in the first twelve or thirteen years does not vanish so much as drop below the surface, where you can no longer watch it work. The child who was open becomes private; the child who was eager becomes skeptical; the child who was compliant becomes confrontational, or withdrawn — and the parent, used to a certain kind of access, suddenly has almost none. The adolescent is pulling away, not from the faith, necessarily, but from you, from dependence, from the childhood version of himself, because he is becoming a separate person, and becoming a separate person requires distance from the very people he is separating from, which is you.

This is normal, and it is also agonizing, because from where the parent stands it feels like loss, like drift, like the work has stopped or, worse, come undone — so the parent panics, and the panic runs in one of two directions. Either the parent grips harder, with more control, more surveillance, more pressure to perform the old closeness; or the parent withdraws, reading the distance as rejection and answering distance with distance.

Both responses wound the thing they mean to protect. Grip harder and you produce an adolescent who either submits in resentment or rebels in desperation; withdraw and you produce an adolescent who reads your retreat as proof that his pulling away was wrong, that your love was conditional on his accessibility all along. Neither road ends at a young adult with a mature faith. Both end at a young adult who learned, in the most critical years of his identity, that his parent could not handle who he was becoming.

Why the Test of Faith Happens Where You Cannot See

Reframe it. The adolescent pulling away is not the end of formation but its most critical phase, because everything planted in the first twelve years is now being tested — by the child, by the culture, by the child's own doubt and desire — and the test cannot happen under the parent's supervision. That is the terrible truth: the faith formed in proximity to you must now be tested in distance from you, or it will never become the child's own.

For the first twelve years, the child's faith was held inside yours; he believed because you believed, prayed because you prayed, went to church because you took him, so that his faith was, in a real sense, your faith, borrowed. During adolescence he has to decide whether to keep it — whether the faith he inherited is the faith he will carry — and that decision cannot be made under supervision; it can only be made in the privacy of his own mind and heart. Your job, in this phase, is not to control the decision but to be present enough that he knows there is a safe place to land, whatever the decision turns out to be.

I have five sons. Some have already been through this; others are in it now. From the inside of it I can tell you that nothing in my pastoral training prepared me for watching my own child work out his faith in a way I could not direct — I could preach about it, I could counsel other parents through it, but when it was my son closing the bedroom door, my son answering in monosyllables, my son showing no flicker of interest in the things that used to light him up, I felt the same fear every parent feels, and I had to do the very thing I had counseled others to do, which was keep my hands open instead of clenched.

What the Adolescent Brain Is Actually Doing

The neuroscience matters here, so let me name it plainly: the teenage brain is undergoing a massive reorganization, and the prefrontal cortex — the seat of executive function, long-term planning, impulse control, the capacity to hold competing ideas in tension — is not fully developed until the mid-twenties. Through adolescence the brain is being pruned and rewired, with the emotional centers running at full power while the rational centers are still under construction, so that the adolescent feels everything at maximum intensity while carrying a diminished capacity to process those feelings with an adult's balance.

This is why teenagers are so confusing to their parents: the teenager feels with the intensity of an adult and processes with the capacity of a brain still being built. He is not choosing to be irrational — his brain is in a construction zone — and the faith he is weighing is being weighed by a brain that runs ideas through the emotional centers first, so that his experience of faith is heavily mediated by feeling. If God feels distant, God is distant; if prayer feels empty, prayer is meaningless; if church feels boring, church has nothing for him. The parent who tries to argue a teenager out of his feelings with logic is arguing against a brain not yet wired to receive the argument.

This does not mean you abandon logic; it means you lead with relationship, because the adolescent brain is wired for relationship above nearly everything else. Peer bonds become paramount, but the parental bond does not disappear — it goes underground like everything else. The teenager who will not talk to you at the dinner table is still tracking whether you are present, still tracking whether you notice his absence, whether you care enough to keep trying when he gives you nothing back; he is watching from behind the closed door to see if you are still there.

What I Have Learned, at Cost

Here is what fifteen years of ministry and five sons have taught me about parenting a teenager in the faith. None of it came cheap.

Reduce the number of hills you will die on. When children are small, the parent draws every boundary; when they are teenagers, you must decide which boundaries are essential and which are negotiable — because if you fight over everything, hair and music and friends and curfew and phone and chores and clothing and language and church attendance, you will lose the teenager entirely, since he will conclude that you care more about control than about him. Hold the line on safety, on moral integrity, on respect in the house, and bend on the rest. The teenager with some agency, some room to make choices you would not make, is a teenager learning to be a person, and that learning is messy. It is also the point.

Stay in the room. When he pushes you away, stay in the room — not physically, because he needs the physical space, but relationally: keep asking about his day when all you get is one word back, keep inviting him to things when he keeps saying no, keep telling him you love him when he rolls his eyes, keep making his favorite meal, keep being interested in what interests him even when his interests confuse or concern you. The parent who refuses to withdraw when the teenager withdraws is the parent the teenager comes back to when the phase shifts — and it will shift, it always does, and the parent who was still there when it shifts stands in a wholly different position than the parent who left.

Answer the questions he is not asking out loud. Teenagers ask theological questions with their behavior long before they ask them with their words, so that the one who refuses church is asking whether this faith is real or merely forced, the one who dates someone you disapprove of is asking whether you will love him if he does not do what you want, and the one who plays with ideas that contradict the family's beliefs is asking whether this faith is strong enough to survive examination. These are not rebellions. They are questions wearing the clothing of rebellion — and once you see the question under the behavior, you can answer the question instead of reacting to the behavior, which changes everything.

They are not rebellions. They are questions wearing the clothing of rebellion.

Let him see your faith in its adult complexity. To a small child you give faith in simple terms — God loves you, Jesus died for you, the Bible is true — but to a teenager those terms are no longer enough; he needs to see that faith has an adult version, that it can hold doubt, that it has wrestled hard questions, that it has survived suffering, that it is not naive. If the only faith he has ever seen is the children's version, he will discard it, because he is no longer a child. So give him the adult version: tell him about the hard parts, tell him about the time you nearly lost it, tell him about the questions you still carry, the books that unsettled you, the arguments that made you think. Show him that faith is not something you outgrow. It grows with you, into the hardest parts of an adult life.

And pray more than you lecture. In the teenage years your primary spiritual work for the child shifts from instruction to intercession, because you have said what you needed to say and planted what you could plant; now you pray, and you pray specifically. You pray by name. You pray for his friends, because his friends are shaping him right now more than you are; you pray for his mind, because his mind is meeting ideas that challenge everything you taught him; you pray for his heart, because his heart is being pulled in several directions and you cannot choose which one wins. You pray for the Holy Spirit to do what only the Holy Spirit can do — take the seeds you planted and bring them to life in a soul you can no longer reach directly.

If You Think You Started Discipling Too Late

Many parents of teenagers are terrified they began too late — they did not do the work in the early years, never built the household catechism, outsourced to the church, and now the teenager is pulling away and there is little foundation to stand on. If that is you, let me be honest: it is harder, and starting at fourteen is harder than starting at four. But it is not impossible, because God is not bound by your timeline, and the parent who starts being real with a teenager now — honest about his own failures, asking instead of lecturing, present instead of controlling — can rebuild the relationship into the bridge the teenager crosses when he is ready.

The teenage years are not the years you lose your child; they are the years your child decides whether to keep what you gave him. Your role is not to force the decision but to be the kind of person he wants to come back to once the decision is made. That is enough. It will not feel like enough. It is.

A Story From My Own House

One of my older sons went through a season, around fifteen, when he stopped wanting to go to church. He announced no departure from the faith and gave no speech; he simply started saying he was tired on Sunday mornings, started finding reasons to stay home. He was not hostile. He was distant. And I felt the fear rise in me, the same fear I had counseled dozens of other parents through.

My first instinct was to mandate it — he lives under my roof, he will go to church — but that instinct came from fear, not wisdom. So Susanna and I talked, and we prayed, and we chose a different way. We told him church mattered to our family and that we wanted him there; we also told him we would not force him, because forced worship is not worship, and that if he chose to stay home on a given Sunday, that was his call and we would love him the same either way. And we asked him to tell us honestly what was going on.

He told us. Church felt boring; the sermons did not connect to his life; he felt like he was going through the motions. He was not rejecting God. He was rejecting the experience as he was having it — and those are different things — so that because we listened instead of reacting, we learned something we would never have learned if we had forced him into the car.

So we made changes. I started talking with him after the service about the sermon, not as a quiz but as a conversation; I asked what he disagreed with, asked what confused him, let him push back on things I had said from the pulpit. That was uncomfortable for me. It was essential for him. Over several months he came back — not because we forced him, but because we made the faith a conversation instead of a mandate — and he is engaged now in a way he was not before, and I believe the engagement runs deeper because it was chosen rather than coerced.

Not every story ends that way. Some teenagers pull away and stay away a long time; some never come back to church even if they come back to God. But the point of the story is not the outcome — it is the posture, because the parent who listens, who does not panic, who keeps the relationship above the compliance, gives the teenager the best chance of owning the faith for himself. Whether he takes that chance is not in your hands. The posture is.

When a Teen Doubting Faith Is Not Just a Phase

One word for the parent whose teenager is not merely questioning the faith but is in a genuinely dark place — using substances, in a destructive relationship, harming himself, speaking of hopelessness. That is not the situation I have been describing; that situation calls for intervention, not patience. If your teenager is in danger, get help: talk to a counselor, talk to your pastor, and do not wait for the phase to pass, because some phases do not pass on their own.

The parent who steps into a crisis, who says I love you too much to watch this happen, is not violating his teenager's autonomy — he is exercising parental love at its highest and most costly. Do not confuse the patience I described for ordinary adolescence with passivity in the face of real danger. They are not the same thing.

The Friends, the Other Adults, and the End of the Tunnel

During adolescence the peer group becomes the primary social world, and this is normal — and it is terrifying, because the peer group is shaping your child in ways you cannot control and often cannot see. The friends your teenager chooses are, in a real sense, choosing the catechism he absorbs in the most impressionable years of his life.

That does not mean you choose his friends for him — that level of control breeds rebellion, not formation — but it does mean you should know who they are: invite them over, feed them, talk to them, watch how your child acts around them, because a teenager who becomes a different person in the presence of certain friends is a teenager whose formation is being pulled somewhere you need to understand. I have made it our practice to make our home the gathering place, my sons' friends in our house rather than my sons in houses I do not know, and this is not surveillance but hospitality with a purpose: when the friends are here, I can hear the conversations and see whether they bring out the best in my son or something else, and my son, watching me be kind to his friends, does not feel policed — he feels his world is welcome in ours. There have been friendships that concerned me, and I did not forbid them; I named what I saw, asked him what he thought, and trusted him with the decision while making clear I was watching because I loved him, not because I doubted him. In one case my son told me I was wrong about a friend, and he was right — I had misjudged — and I told him so and thanked him for helping me see it.

Your teenager also needs adults in his life who are not you — adults he respects, who share your faith, whom he can talk to when he cannot talk to you — and this is not a failure of your parenting but a feature of adolescent development, because he is individuating from you, and part of that requires other models of adult faith: a youth pastor, a mentor, a coach, a family friend, an uncle, a grandparent. They do not replace you; they extend you, showing your teenager that the faith you hold is not peculiar to your household but is held by other adults too, in their own way, with their own stories. I have intentionally put my sons in relationship with men in our church I trust — men who will tell them the truth, pray with them, and not let them get away with what they should not get away with — and my sons have conversations with those men they do not have with me. I am not offended by that. I am grateful for it, because the more voices my sons hear the faith from, the harder it is to dismiss as one man's opinion.

And the teenage years will end. I say this to the parent in the middle of them who cannot see the end: the withdrawal will ease, the resistance will soften, and the door that is closed will open again — not on your timeline, on his, but it will open. The parent who was still standing at the door when it opens, who did not walk away, who did not retaliate, who did not answer the teenager's distance with his own, will be the one the young adult comes back to. I have watched it happen enough times to say it with confidence: the parents who stay, who keep loving and praying and keeping the relationship above the rules, see their children come back — not always to church, not always on the parent's terms, but back, back to relationship, back to conversation, back to the faith they tested and found, after testing it, to be real.

The teenage years are a tunnel. It is dark in the tunnel, and the parent cannot see where it goes — but there is light at the far end, and the parent who keeps walking, keeps praying, keeps loving, comes out of it with his child, both of them changed, both of them deeper, connected in a way the easy years of childhood could never have produced.

Trust the process — not the therapeutic slogan, the actual theological one: God is at work in your teenager, and the Holy Spirit is pursuing him with a persistence that exceeds your own. Your job is not to be the Holy Spirit. Your job is to stay close enough that when the Spirit does His work, your teenager has someone safe to tell about it.

Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it.
