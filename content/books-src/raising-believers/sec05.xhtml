CHAPTER FOUR

The Questions Children Actually Ask

How to answer the hard questions kids ask about God — death, suffering, other religions, and doubt — so your home stays the safe place to ask.

Somewhere around age eight, sometimes earlier, a child begins to ask the real questions, and they almost never arrive when you are ready for them; they come in the back of the car, at the dinner table when the conversation was about something else entirely, in the last minute before sleep when the child has worked out that a parent cannot easily walk away. They are plain on the surface and devastating underneath. If God is good, why did my dog die. If God is real, why did grandma die. Is heaven real. What if I do not feel like I love Jesus. Why are there other religions, and how do we know the Bible is true, and why do bad people seem to win, and what happens to my friend who does not believe.

How a parent answers in the first ninety seconds tells the child everything, not about the answer, but about whether the question is welcome. And children are taking notes.

Most of us answer too quickly, reaching for a phrase half remembered from a sermon, or a verse that does not quite fit, or some version of because God said so; we change the subject, we try to shut the question down before it unsettles us, and worse (through tone, through impatience, through the sheer speed of the response), we tell the child that the question itself is the problem. That Christians do not ask this. That faithful children do not doubt. That curiosity is a temptation to be resisted rather than a door to be walked through together.

When we do that, we teach the child something far more damaging than the content of our too-quick answer: we teach them that questions do not belong in this household, and the child learns very early that if there is a question underneath the belief, it has to be hidden from the adults. And because children are not stupid, they keep asking; they just stop asking you. They ask Google, they ask their friends, they ask the algorithm quietly curating the videos they fall asleep to, they ask at two in the morning to themselves, and they build, over years, a parallel life of unresolved doubt that runs alongside their polite church attendance.

The mechanism matters, so trace it slowly. A child asks a question, and the parent's response opens one of two doors. Behind the first, the question is received: the parent's face is open, the parent does not rush, and the child learns that this house is a place where I can think about God honestly; the question gets discussed over days or weeks, and the child absorbs not only the content but the posture, that faith and questions live in the same room.

Behind the second door, the question is deflected; the parent's face is tight, the answer comes too fast, and the child learns that this house is not a place where I can think about God honestly. The question goes underground, and the child learns to perform belief while conducting the real intellectual life elsewhere.

The second door is the one producing the crisis we are in: a generation of young adults raised in Christian homes who will tell you, if you ask, that their first honest conversation about faith happened in college, not because their parents were bad people, but because their parents could not handle the questions, and the children, being loyal, stopped asking.

Eventually one of two things happens. They hit an age, usually between sixteen and twenty-two, when the parallel life overwhelms the church one and they walk away; or they keep performing belief while no longer holding it, because they do not want to disappoint the people who clearly could not bear their doubt. Neither outcome is faith. Both are the result of households that could not tolerate a child's honest question.

So reframe it. A child asking you a hard spiritual question is not drifting from faith, often they are doing the exact opposite, trying to make their faith fit the reality they are seeing, and that is a sign of spiritual seriousness, not spiritual weakness. The child who never asks is not the child who has settled faith; the child who never asks is often the child whose curiosity has already migrated elsewhere because the home did not feel safe for it. So when your child asks you a hard question, be grateful; it means they still trust you with it. Do not blow that trust by answering in ninety seconds.

Three Questions Kids Ask That Are Really a Theology

Here is what I have learned raising five sons and pastoring for twenty years: three sentences will carry you through almost every hard question your child asks, if you mean them, and if you have the courage to say them slowly.

First, that is a really good question, not as filler, but as an honest response, because the question really is a good one and you are welcoming it before you answer it. The child is watching your face while you say it, and everything hangs on what your face does: if it is honest, the conversation breathes; if it is tight, the child already knows this was a mistake.

Second, I have wondered that too, said only if it is true, and in my experience it will almost always be true, because children ask the questions grown adults ask quietly. If you are a Christian who has never wondered whether God is real, whether suffering makes sense, whether other religions might be right, whether the Bible can be trusted, then you are either lying to your child or you are not yet a mature believer; mature faith has sat with hard questions and come through them to confidence, which is a different thing altogether from never having had the questions. When you tell your child you have wondered what they are wondering, you hand them a gift: you tell them doubt is part of faith, not the opposite of it, and you tell them they are not broken for asking.

Third, let us think about it together, not here is the answer, but let us think. That language is slow on purpose. It refuses to perform certainty, it models a believing mind willing to look at the question honestly, it invites the child into the thinking and makes the conversation collaborative rather than corrective, and it often takes more than one conversation, which is the most important thing of all.

Those three sentences work because they are not a technique. They are a theology. The first says your question has dignity; the second says your doubt is human, not sinful; the third says we find truth together, not by decree. All three are theologically loaded, and a Christianity that cannot say them to a child is a Christianity that does not trust its own claims enough to let them be examined, and a Christianity that does not trust its own claims will never convince a child who is watching to see whether the adults actually believe what they say.

Your authority does not come from answering every question in real time. It comes from a God you trust with the things you cannot answer — and a child watching you do it.

The Hard Bible Questions a Parent Cannot Answer

I cannot count the times one of my sons has asked me a question I could not answer well in the moment. A few years ago one of the older ones asked why God commanded the Canaanites to be wiped out in the book of Joshua, and I want to sit with the fact that he had been reading it on his own, not because I made him but because he had watched me read mine, which is itself the whole argument of this book about formation. But the question was real. He was eleven, he was bothered, and I did not have a clean answer in ninety seconds.

What I had was the three sentences. That is a really good question. I have wondered that too. Let us think about it together. And then I told him the truth: that I do not think there is a tidy answer, that Christians have wrestled with this passage for two thousand years, that I hold a tentative way of understanding it, and that I wanted to walk through it with him over the coming weeks. So we came back to it, more than once; we read some commentators together; I did not resolve it for him but let him stay with it, with me. That is what formation looks like on a hard question.

Here is what I did not do. I did not pretend I had a clean answer, I did not give him a Sunday school platitude, I did not fast-talk past the difficulty, I did not act as though the question itself were unfaithful, and I did not walk away feeling like a failure because I had not converted his question into certainty on my own timeline.

When the Question Is Not Really About Information

There is another kind of question entirely, and it asks for a different posture, because it is not really about information at all; it is about pain. A child whose grandfather has died asks if grandpa is in heaven, and he is not asking for a position paper on the intermediate state; he is asking whether grandpa is okay, whether he will see him again, whether death is the end, and underneath even that, whether this world is safe and whether there is someone holding what hurts.

The Lord is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit.

Psalm 34:18

The parent who answers the emotional question with an intellectual answer has missed the child entirely, because the child needed a hug first, and tears, and to hear that grief is okay and that God is near to the brokenhearted. The theology can come later. The presence comes first.

I have made this mistake; I have answered emotional questions with theological precision when what my son needed was his father's arms and his father's honesty, that death is painful, that we grieve with hope but the grief is real and we do not have to pretend it is not. The child who receives a lecture when he needed a human presence learns that faith is cold, which is not what we mean to teach; it is what we teach anyway when we reach for our head before we reach for our heart.

Name the most common mistake, which is believing that every hard question requires an immediate settled answer or you have lost your authority. Not true. Your authority does not come from answering every theological question in real time; it comes from your presence, your integrity, your faithfulness over years, and the plain reality that you love your child. You can hold an unresolved question in front of your child for a whole season and watch your authority grow rather than shrink, because you are modeling what a grown believer looks like: grown believers do not have an answer for everything, but they have a God they trust with the things they cannot answer, and modeling that trust, in real time, in front of your child, is one of the most formative things you will ever do.

There is a subtler mistake, and it is harder to see, answering a question the child did not ask because the question the child did ask scares us. A child asks about hell and we turn it into a lesson on sin; a child asks why a relative died and we turn it into a platitude about heaven; in each case we are answering our own discomfort rather than the child's actual question. Children can tell. They learn not to ask.

One category terrifies many Christian parents above the rest: questions about other religions and other worldviews. A child comes home and says her friend is Muslim, or Hindu, or atheist, and wants to know why the friend believes something different and whether the friend is wrong, and this terrifies the parent who has been taught that exposure to other worldviews is dangerous. But the question is not dangerous. The question is the beginning of apologetics.

Always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you to give the reason for the hope that you have. But do this with gentleness and respect.

1 Peter 3:15

The child is asking you to give a reason for the hope that is in you, and if you cannot do it with gentleness and respect, as Peter commanded, the child will conclude that your faith cannot withstand comparison. That conclusion is devastating, and it is reached not through exposure to other religions but through the parent's own inability to engage them.

So answer the actual question. If you do not know, say so; if it is complicated, say so; if there is more than one Christian position, name it, because you are not required to pretend the tradition is monolithic. Children are impressed, not destabilized, by a parent who says there are faithful Christians who read this passage differently, and here is how your mother and I see it and why; you are giving them a model of thinking, and you are giving them permission to think, which most of them are already doing anyway.

What Fifteen Years of Open Doors Produces

I have watched what happens when a household handles questions well over years, and the child raised in a home where questions are welcome does not stop asking; they ask better ones. At eight it is why did the dog die; at twelve, how do we know the resurrection happened; at sixteen, what do we do about suffering in the world; at nineteen, how do I hold my faith when my roommate's arguments are good. The progression is striking when the door has stayed open, because at every stage the child's faith is being refined by the questions rather than eroded by them.

The questions do to faith what fire does to gold: they burn away what is false and leave what is true. A child whose questions have been welcomed for fifteen years has a faith that has been tested, and a tested faith is a faith that lasts.

I have also watched the opposite. The child raised where questions are unwelcome does not stop having questions; they go underground, they multiply in the dark, they attach to resentment, and when the child finally finds an environment where the questions are welcomed, a college classroom or an online community, that environment feels more honest than home. Not because the professor has better answers, but because the professor did what the parent would not: treated the question as worth asking. The parent lost the child not to better arguments but to a better posture. That is preventable. That is what this chapter is trying to prevent.

So build question-asking into the rhythms of the household, and do not wait for questions to surface at random; make spaces where they are expected. At the dinner table, ask what your children are thinking about, not what they learned at school but what they are thinking about; at bedtime, after the prayer, ask if there is anything they have been wondering about; on car rides, turn off the music for ten minutes and ask. You will get silence at first, and shrugs, but keep asking, because over weeks and months the child begins to believe you mean it, and when they believe you mean it, the questions come. They come to you, which is the whole point.

Start Now, While the Questions Are Still Small

The hope in this chapter is plain and heavy: be the safe person your child can ask the hardest question of their life now, so that ten years from now, when the question is the one that will decide whether they stay in the faith, they are still coming to you with it. Not to Google. Not to the friend who is also confused. To you.

That means starting now, when they are six and eight and ten and the questions are still small enough to practice on, because if you cannot handle a small question now you will not be the one they trust with the large one later, and the large question is coming. It always does.

Some of you are parents of teenagers who have already stopped asking; you feel the loss, you know when the questions went underground, and you wonder if it is too late. It is not too late. But you will have to begin by being honest: that you know you did not handle their questions well when they were smaller, and that you want to try again. You might get silence, you might get an eye roll, you might get a door opening slowly over months; do not rush it, and do not retaliate against the slowness, because a parent willing to earn back the conversation, who does not demand it or grow bitter when it is slow in coming, is a parent doing holy work. Keep doing it.

Your children have questions, thousands of them, and most of them do not yet know what those questions will grow into. You cannot give them certainty. But you can give them a seat at your table while they work it out, with a Bible open between you and the Holy Spirit doing what He does best, which is drawing people to Christ in the slow and honest way.

The questions children ask change as they grow, and the parent who handles a six-year-old well can be caught off guard by a twelve-year-old. Young children ask concrete questions (where is heaven, what does God look like, can God see me in the dark) questions that are spatial, physical, imagination-level, because they are trying to locate God in the world they can see. The parent's job here is to be concrete in return: God is everywhere, but He is especially close to you when you pray; God does not have a body like ours, but Jesus did, and Jesus looks at you with love; yes, God can see you in the dark, and He is watching over you right now. Direct, and warm. The child is not looking for sophistication; the child is looking for assurance that God is real and God is near.

Older children ask moral questions (why did God let the dog die, why are there poor people if God loves everyone, why did my friend's parents divorce) and these are justice questions, the questions of a child developing a moral sense and testing whether God fits inside it. The parent who answers with theological precision but no emotional engagement loses the child's trust, because the child is not asking for a theodicy lecture; the child is asking whether God is good in a world that does not always look good. So hold both truths together: the world is broken, and the breaking causes real pain, and God is in the middle of the pain doing something about it even when we cannot see what He is doing. That answer is not neat. It is honest. And honest answers at this stage lay a foundation for the harder questions coming.

Teenagers ask identity questions (how do I know God is real and not just something I believe because my parents do, why should the Bible have authority over my life when other smart people call it mythology, what makes Christianity different from any other religion that claims to be true) and these are existential questions. The teenager is not trying to be difficult; the teenager is trying to own their beliefs rather than borrow yours, and the parent who reacts with fear or anger only confirms the teenager's suspicion that the faith cannot survive examination.

I have seen parents panic at this stage, hearing their teenager voice doubt and treating it as a crisis: they call the youth pastor, they ground the child, they add to the Bible reading schedule, and every one of those responses says the same thing, that your honest questions are dangerous and the adults in your life are afraid of them. That is not formation. That is suppression. And suppression yields one of two ends: either the child hides the questions, which means they fester underground, or the child escalates them into rebellion, because rebellion becomes the only way left to make the adults take them seriously.

The Year My Son Questioned His Faith

The better response is to welcome the question and honor the questioner. When my son asked me, at fourteen, how he could know God was real and not just something he believed because his father was a pastor, I felt pride before I felt anything else, because that question meant he was doing the work. He was not content to carry a borrowed faith. He wanted his own. And getting his own required testing the one he had been given.

I told him several things: that his question was one of the best a person can ask, and that I was glad he was asking it; that I had asked the same question when I was not much older than him, and that my faith had survived the asking; that there are good reasons to believe God is real and Christianity true, reasons articulated by some of the brightest minds in human history, and that I would gladly walk through some of them with him; and that faith is not the absence of doubt but trust in the face of doubt, because the God of the Bible is not threatened by honest questions; He invites them.

We spent the next several months reading together, and not the Bible at first. We read parts of C. S. Lewis, we read parts of Tim Keller, we watched some lectures, we talked about what he found persuasive and what he did not, and somewhere in those months, something shifted. He moved from testing a borrowed faith to building his own. The content was much the same; the ownership was different. And that difference is everything.

Some questions come without words. A child who starts lying is asking a question about safety (am I safe to tell the truth in this house, or will the truth get me hurt; a child who starts withdrawing is asking about belonging) do I still belong here if I am not who you want me to be; a child who starts acting out is asking about attention: does anyone in this house notice me when I am not performing.

These behavioral questions are harder to hear than spoken ones, because the behavior triggers the parent's frustration rather than their curiosity: the parent sees the lie and responds to the lie, sees the withdrawal and takes it personally, sees the acting out and reaches for discipline, and in each case the parent is answering the surface behavior rather than the question underneath it.

The parent who can look past the behavior to the question is the parent who can actually form the child, which does not mean the behavior goes unaddressed, but that the behavior is addressed after the question has been heard. You lied to me, and we need to talk about that; but first I want to understand something: are you afraid of what happens when you tell me the truth, because if you are, that is something I need to change about how I respond to you. That kind of response forms a child. It teaches them that the adults in their life are paying attention to the person, not managing the performance.

Underneath all of this lies another issue, one that shapes whether the child is even asking for truth or asking for permission. That is the next chapter. Obedience without gospel.
