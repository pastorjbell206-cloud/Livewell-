CHAPTER THREE

When the Church Replaces the Parent

Why Sunday school, youth group, and children's ministry cannot form a child's faith alone — and how parents become the primary disciple-makers again.

I am a pastor; I lead a local church, and I have given my adult life to a particular congregation in a small Michigan town because I love what gathers there. I say all of that first because of what I am about to argue: that if you have handed the spiritual formation of your children to the church you attend, you have made a quiet mistake, and that the church, for its part, has too often been happy to receive what it was never built to carry.

Over the last fifty years, American evangelicalism restructured itself on a single assumption, that the spiritual formation of children is primarily the church's job, and then it built an entire infrastructure to do that job: age-graded Sunday school, children's church, Vacation Bible School, Awana, mid-week kids programs, youth group, Christian summer camp, retreats, mission trips. Every one of those is good in the right proportion, and the mistake was never in building them. The mistake was in building them so thoroughly that parents came to believe the infrastructure was the formation, and that their own role was to deliver their children to it on time.

I watch parents do exactly this every week. They drop off a kindergartner on Sunday morning like a dry-cleaning ticket, pick up a fifth grader at the end of Wednesday night Awana and ask only what he earned, and sign a youth-retreat permission slip without ever once asking what the retreat is covering, investing in their children's spiritual life by paying the registration fees and running the carpool. And when those same children hit eighteen, twenty, twenty-two and step away from the faith, the parents are devastated, and they ask what they did wrong. The honest answer is the hard one. You did everything you were told to do; what you were told to do was never going to produce what you were hoping for.

How Parents Became the Church's Delivery System

This did not happen by accident. The explosion of children's and youth ministry in the second half of the twentieth century was driven by good intentions running alongside market logic: the good intention was to reach young people with the gospel, and the market logic held that specialized, age-appropriate programming would do this better than general worship services. Both are partially true; young people do need to hear the gospel in language they can receive, and age-appropriate teaching does have value. But the market logic carried an assumption nobody stopped to challenge, namely that professional ministry workers, armed with training and curricula, could form children more effectively than parents could. So the parent became the delivery system, and the professional became the formator.

Consider what that arrangement communicated to the average Christian parent across three full generations: that spiritual formation is specialized work; that it requires training the parent does not have; that the parent's role is logistical rather than formational. Get the child to the program, the message ran, and the program will do the work: the parent supporting the program by volunteering, by giving, by reinforcing at home what the program already teaches. The program sat at the center, and the parent was reassigned to the support staff.

That is backwards, and it has always been backwards. We are now living in the wreckage of a fifty-year experiment that proved it, because the generation formed primarily in church programs rather than in homes is the very generation leaving the faith at historically unprecedented rates, not because the programs were bad, but because the programs were carrying a weight they were never designed to carry. A youth pastor with forty students, two hours a week, and a six-year window cannot undo or redirect sixteen years of whatever the home has been quietly teaching. The math does not work. It never did.

The Math No One Does: Church Hours vs Home Hours

Here is the math most Christian parents have never sat down to do. Add up every hour a child spends in church programming from birth to age eighteen, and be generous about it: Sunday morning service plus Sunday school, two hours; mid-week program, two more; VBS one week a year, fifteen hours; a youth retreat, forty-eight; camp, a hundred. Stack all of it across eighteen years and you arrive somewhere between two thousand and four thousand hours, and that is only if the family is extremely consistent, which most are not. Call it three thousand on the high end.

That sounds like a great deal until you work the other side of the equation and ask how many hours a child is in the home from birth to eighteen, not merely present in the house, but awake and in proximity to a parent. Call it thirty-five thousand hours on the low end, and realistically more. A well-attended child is receiving roughly ten times as much formation at home as at church, which is not a comparison so much as a structural reality: the home is the main event, and the church is a complement to it, never a replacement for it.

Most of you already know this in your bones; the problem is that you have been trained out of acting on it. You have been led, patiently, over years, to believe you are not qualified, that the qualified people are the youth pastor, the children's director, the small group leader, the curriculum writer, and that you are merely a parent whose task is to hand your children to the qualified people so the qualified people can form them. That is the model, and it is a bad one, because it assumes the child forms primarily in programmatic environments rather than in the ordinary, unglamorous weight of a home.

I want to name what I have seen from the pastoral side, because parents rarely get to see it. Year after year I have watched gifted children's workers burn out, not because the work was too hard, but because the expectation laid on them was impossible: the unspoken but very real demand that they would produce spiritually mature children out of homes that were not doing the primary work. A children's ministry leader once told me, with tears in her eyes, that she could not build in two hours what went unbuilt the other hundred and sixty-six hours of the week. She was right. She was carrying a burden that belonged to the parents.

I have watched youth pastors absorb the blame, too, when a student walked away, the parents looking hard at the youth pastor, the youth pastor looking back at the parents, and neither of them saying the true thing aloud: that the student's faith had been eroding at home for years, that the youth group had been the one place it was still alive, and that when the student finally left the group the faith left with it, because it had no roots anywhere outside the program. The program had been the faith. The home had been something else.

The Church as Partner, Not Vendor

The church is not the primary catechist of your child. The church is the assembly of believers, including you, gathered around word and sacrament and mission, and your child genuinely belongs in that assembly, learns from it, and is shaped by it. But the assembly is not a parenting replacement; it is a parenting context. The congregation is the extended family within which you do the primary work, not the contractor you hired to do that work on your behalf.

When parents finally understand this, two things change. The first is that they stop expecting the youth pastor to do the impossible, because a good youth pastor has forty students, forty different family contexts, maybe two hours a week with each one, and a narrow window before they graduate, and you have only to ask whether two hours a week for six years could ever redirect sixteen previous years of family-formed assumptions about what life is for. It cannot, and it never could. To expect it is to be unfair to the youth pastor, and unfair to your child, who is now carrying an expectation you yourself communicated, that formation happens here, in this room, on these two hours. It does not. And when it fails to produce what you hoped, you blame the church, and the church quietly wonders why the Christian home has stopped functioning as a Christian home.

The second thing that changes is that parents begin to see the church not as a vendor but as a partner, bringing their children into the life of the congregation not to outsource but to reinforce what is already happening at home. You want them near other believing adults, not because you are delegating the teaching, but because you want them to see that your house is not the only house where Christ is Lord; you want them in a room with other people singing, so they hear that your faith is not some private family quirk; you want them around older believers, because your mother-in-law has walked with God for forty years and you have walked with Him for eight, and the older saints are a gift your household can borrow from. You are not outsourcing. You are integrating.

The church, in other words, is the community in which your home's catechism is confirmed. Your child hears you pray at the kitchen table and then watches the congregation pray together; watches you serve and then sees other adults serving for the same reasons; hears you talk about grace at home and then hears the pastor preach grace from the pulpit, and so the message becomes larger than one household. The church takes the small, private catechism of the home and sets it inside a larger story, showing the child that the faith is not a family tradition but a global, historical, living reality their family happens to be part of. That confirmation is essential, and the home cannot provide it alone; the church can. That is the partnership.

Stop treating the youth group as the load-bearing wall of your child's faith. It is not. Your home is.

Faith Is Not a Department

I want to be careful here, because I have no wish to swing too far the other way. There is a family-worship subculture that has overcorrected against the outsourcing model by pulling children out of age-graded ministry entirely, keeping them in the adult service, running rigorous family devotions, and treating the programmatic side of the church as unnecessary or even corrupting, a subculture that has correctly named a real problem and then answered it with a posture that sometimes hardens into isolation and pride. That is not where I am pointing. I am not asking you to withdraw your child from the youth group; I am asking you to stop treating it as the load-bearing wall of your child's faith.

If your home is teaching a true faith and the children are forming near it, a mediocre youth group will not undo that: I have seen it with my own eyes, kids whose youth group was not much and whose parents were quietly serious about Jesus, and those kids grew into men and women whose faith went the distance. I have also seen the exact opposite: top-shelf youth ministry, engaging teaching, great relationships, and parents whose actual life at home communicated that God is not really the center. Those kids aged out of the youth group, and the faith did not stick, because the youth group had been carrying a load the home refused to carry. The youth group cannot win that fight. Nothing can win it except a change at home.

There is one more thing the outsourcing model quietly does to children, and it is rarely discussed: it teaches them that faith is compartmentalized: something that happens in certain buildings, with certain people, at certain times, while the rest of life is something else entirely. Church is church, home is home, school is school, and faith lives in the church compartment; so when the child eventually leaves that compartment, the faith stays behind, like a coat left hanging in a closet. The child has learned, through the very structure of the model, that faith is not integrated into ordinary life. It is a department. And departments can be closed.

A home doing its own formation work teaches the opposite. Faith is not a department; it is the operating system, running in every room, through every conversation, behind every decision, and the child raised in that home does not know how to separate faith from life, because in his experience the two were never separate to begin with. When he leaves for college, the faith goes with him, because the faith was never located in a building or a program but in the way he sees the world. It is in his marrow. He cannot leave it behind.

The Costly Hope

You get back what you were told you could give away. You are the primary catechist of your children, and you do not need a degree or a curriculum certification to be it; you need a life with Christ, the willingness to let your children see that life up close, and the maturity to bring them into the life of a congregation where that shared life is reinforced and expanded. That is a weight, and it does not feel like relief; it feels, instead, like the responsibility you thought you had safely handed off has quietly landed back on your own shoulders.

It has. Because it was always there, and it was never the church's to take. The church's role is to come alongside you: to preach the word to the whole household, to teach your children in ways that complement what you are already teaching, to surround you with other families trying to do the same difficult thing. The church is there for the help you cannot provide alone. It is not there to do what only a parent can do.

If you are a pastor or a children's worker reading this, you already know what I am saying is true. You have been sold to parents as a service, and you have sometimes believed the sales copy; but you have also watched families for years, and you know which ones are forming children and which ones are merely delegating, and it is not the family that is more religiously active in the church, but the family whose home is running a real catechism. Those children track. Those children come back even when they wander. Those children carry something in them that was planted somewhere other than your classroom.

You cannot do their parents' job; you can only do your own, which is to come alongside the parent: to equip them, encourage them, love them, and serve their children as a partner in what the parent is primarily carrying. Your job is not to be the parent, and it never was; when you try, both of you lose, and the child loses worst of all. The church we have built often incentivizes the outsourcing anyway, because it is easier to run a program than to challenge a household, and because a child at every event looks, on the annual report, like an engaged family. We should be measuring formation, not attendance. I am not asking us to dismantle children's ministry. I am asking us to reorient it toward the household, in service of the parents, as a supplement to the life a family is already living rather than a substitute for the life it is not.

What You Can Do at Home Tonight

I know that many parents reading this are now feeling the guilt rise: you have been dropping your children at church programs and hoping the programs would do the work, and now the weight of it is settling in. Here is what I want to say to that guilt: do not stay there. Guilt that produces change is useful; guilt that produces paralysis is not. If this chapter has shown you something about your household that needs to change, then change it, and do not wallow in what you should have done. Start instead with what you can actually do tonight.

Tonight you can sit at the table and ask your children a real question; tonight you can pray out loud in a room where they can hear you; tonight you can say the blessing over them before bed; tonight you can turn off the television and read a passage of Scripture, not because you are performing a devotional, but because you want to be near God and you want them to see you wanting it. Those are small things, and they are also the entire argument of this book: small things, done consistently, in a household where the adults are actually walking with Christ, over years, in ordinary rooms. That is the curriculum. You already have everything you need to start it tonight.

These words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise.

Deuteronomy 6:6-7

The church gives community, corporate worship, teaching from many voices, the experience of belonging to something larger than one family; the parent gives proximity, daily formation, honest answers, emotional safety, and the lived demonstration of what it looks like to follow Jesus when nobody is watching. Together those two forces produce a formed child, and apart each is incomplete: the church without the home producing a child who knows about God but has no lived reference for walking with Him in the ordinary, the home without the church producing a child who may know God but has no experience of corporate worship and no connection to the broader body of Christ. You need both. Your child needs both.

So this is the chapter where we stop pretending the church is where this happens. The church is where it is reinforced, celebrated, expanded, and held in common with other families; where it happens, primarily, is the place no one else can see, where the parent is the one being watched. That is your house. That is your Tuesday at six thirty in the evening. That is the fifteen minutes before your children go to bed. The goal is not to pull your child out of church programs; it is to make sure that when your child walks through those doors on Sunday morning, he walks in from a household where the faith is already alive, and not from a household where the faith is on hold until the professionals take over.

Now we turn to what those children are quietly asking while they watch you.
