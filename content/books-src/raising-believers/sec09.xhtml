CHAPTER EIGHT

Marriage as the First Classroom

How your marriage shapes your child's faith — why the relationship your children watch teaches them what covenant, love, and God are.

Before your child watched you pray, they watched you love your spouse; before they heard you read Scripture, they heard you argue; and before they ever saw you worship on a Sunday morning, they saw you at the breakfast table, where they learned whether the person across from you was met with tenderness or with indifference. Your marriage is the first classroom your child ever attended, and they enrolled the day they were born.

Most Christian parents keep marriage and parenting in separate rooms, as though the relationship between two adults and the work of raising children were different enterprises that touch only at the practical seams — scheduling, discipline, money — while the marriage itself goes on undisturbed in its own quarters. It does not work that way. The marriage is rarely understood as a place where children are formed, but it is, and it is the primary one in the house, because it teaches the child, far below the level of any argument, what love is, what covenant looks like, and whether God's promises can be trusted.

A child watching a marriage is learning theology — a theology of love built from the questions the marriage answers without ever stating them. Is love patient, or does it erupt the moment it is inconvenienced; is it sacrificial, or does it keep a running scoreboard of who has given more; is it honest, or does it perform in public and deteriorate in private; is it durable, or does it fracture under pressure and never get repaired? The child is not thinking in these categories. The child is absorbing them, and they are being written into the child's deepest assumptions about what human relationships are, what God's relationship with His people might look like, and whether the kind of love the Bible describes actually exists or is only aspirational fiction.

I have watched this play out for two decades, and the pattern has been almost embarrassingly consistent: the families whose children's faith proved most durable were not the ones with the heaviest investment in children's ministry, but the ones where the marriage was alive — where the parents were kind to each other in the kitchen, where the father looked at the mother with something in his eyes that the children could see, where conflict happened but repair happened too, and the children watched both. Those children grew up believing covenant is real, because they had seen it; they grew up with an intuitive trust in the faithfulness of God, because the first covenant they ever observed, the marriage of their parents, was a covenant that held.

The reverse is also true, and I have watched it just as often: children raised in homes where the marriage was not violent, not dramatic, but simply cold — where the parents functioned as competent coworkers who managed the household, slept in the same bed, and lived in separate emotional worlds. Those children grew up with a theology of love that was functional rather than intimate. God is useful, God provides, God manages the world, but God is not close; God does not know you, does not delight in you. The children absorbed the temperature of the marriage and projected it onto God, and the god they ended up with was a manager, not a father.

Your marriage is not a background condition to the work of forming your children; it is the foreground. It is the primary text the child is reading, and every other text — the Bible, the sermon, the devotional — is read against it, confirmed by it when the marriage says love is real and undermined by it when the marriage says love is only a performance. You cannot teach your child about the love of God while living inside a marriage that contradicts it. The child will believe the marriage.

The Invisible Lessons Your Marriage Teaches

There are specific things a child learns from watching a marriage — the lessons no curriculum teaches and no program can replace.

The child learns about conflict, because every marriage has it, and the only real question is what the conflict teaches. Where it is avoided, the child learns that disagreement is dangerous, that peace means the absence of honest conversation, that love cannot survive a hard truth; where it is explosive, the child learns that love is volatile, that anger is power, that words said in heat are normal, and that you can never be sure when the safe person will become unsafe. But where conflict happens honestly and is repaired faithfully, the child learns something rare — that two people can disagree, can even wound each other, and can still come back together; that repair is real; that love is not the absence of conflict but the commitment to stay in the room. That is a theology. It is the theology of a God who does not abandon His people when they fail Him, and the child learns it from watching their parents fight and recover.

I owe the argument something about my own marriage. Susanna and I have had seasons where it was not teaching our sons what we wanted it to teach. There was a year, not long ago, when ministry demands and sheer exhaustion had pushed us into a functional partnership rather than an intimate marriage; we were efficient, and we were not close. And one of our boys, the one with the eyes that miss nothing, said to Susanna one evening, you and Dad do not laugh together anymore. He was not criticizing. He was observing — and his observation was devastating in its accuracy, because we had in fact stopped laughing, stopped looking at each other with the thing the children needed to see, and become coworkers in a household that was running while the marriage inside it was not alive. The children knew it.

That observation turned us. We decided, together, to invest in the marriage as a spiritual act on behalf of our children — not because they needed to see a perfect marriage, but because they needed to see one that was being tended, where the adults were choosing each other and the kindness was deliberate. So we started dating again, not as a formula but as a need, and we started praying together again as a couple — not in front of the children, but for each other — because a shared prayer life between a husband and wife creates a warmth in the home that the children can feel even when they cannot name it.

The child learns about affection. Physical affection between a husband and a wife, in front of the children, teaches the child that love is not an abstraction — that it has a body, that it is given and received. I kiss Susanna in the kitchen; I hold her hand at the table; I hug her when she walks in the door. I am not performing. I am doing what married people who love each other do, and my sons are watching, learning what a man who loves a woman looks like — that tenderness is not weakness, that affection is not embarrassing, that a marriage with physical warmth is a marriage that is alive. Some of you have stopped touching your spouse in front of your children, whether because the marriage has grown cold or because the gesture now feels uncomfortable, and your children have noticed; they learn from the absence of affection as surely as they would learn from its presence.

The child learns about respect, because how a husband speaks to his wife, and how a wife speaks to her husband, is one of the most formative things in any household. A husband who dismisses his wife, who corrects her in front of the children, who treats her opinions as less weighty than his own, is teaching his sons how to treat women and teaching his daughters what to accept from men; a wife who belittles her husband, who rolls her eyes at his ideas, is teaching the same lessons in reverse. Children catalog every interaction, building their reference for what marriage is, and whatever lives inside that reference will feel normal to them — because it is simply what they grew up with.

The child learns about forgiveness, and this may be the most important lesson of all. Your child needs to see you forgive your spouse — not in general but specifically, in the aftermath of a conflict they witnessed — needs to hear you say, I was wrong, I am sorry, will you forgive me, and then needs to hear the other person say, yes. That exchange, repeated over years, teaches the child more about the gospel than any sermon can; for the gospel is the story of a God who forgives, and the child who has watched forgiveness in action, in the most important relationship in the house, carries a frame for receiving and giving it that the child who has never seen it simply does not have.

Covenant the Child Can See

Some of you are reading this in marriages that are not well. The marriage is strained. The affection is gone. The conflict is constant, or the silence is total. And you are reading with a growing weight, because you know what your marriage is teaching your children and you do not know how to change it.

Hear me: I am not writing this to add guilt, but to name the thing honestly, because naming it is the first step toward changing it. If your marriage is not teaching your children what you want it to teach, the answer is not to perform a better marriage in front of them — they will see through the performance — but to actually tend the marriage: to get help, to go to a counselor, to talk to your pastor, to begin the repair that has been deferred for years. That is not a distraction from raising your children. It is the work of raising your children, because the marriage is the first classroom, and you cannot reform the classroom while the curriculum stays broken.

Consider what the marriage teaches about covenant. We live in a culture that treats commitment as provisional — you commit until you no longer want to, you stay until staying costs more than leaving — and children absorb that message from every direction. The marriage in the home is the counter-message. It is the place where the child sees two people who made a promise and are keeping it: not because keeping it is easy, not because every day is good, but because the promise is real and the vows are load-bearing.

When your child sees you choose your spouse on a hard day, choose to be kind when you are tired, choose to stay when staying is difficult, you are teaching them that covenant is not a contract. A contract has exit clauses; a covenant has steadfastness. And the God of the Bible is a covenant God who does not exit and does not abandon — so your marriage, kept in faithfulness over decades, becomes the child's first evidence that this is true.

You cannot reform the classroom while the curriculum stays broken.

It is not too late to change what your marriage is teaching, even if the children are older, even if the damage has already been done. A marriage that begins to heal in front of the children, that begins to warm after a cold season, teaches them something about restoration that changes the very air in a home — that dead things can come alive, that cold things can be warmed, that broken covenants can be repaired. And if that is true in a marriage, then maybe it is true with God; maybe restoration is not a theory but the way God actually works. The children will believe it if they see it.

So tend your marriage. Not for the marriage alone, but for the children who are watching it, learning from it, and building their theology of love from what they see in it every single day.

Guard What Is Alive

If you are in a marriage that is alive, guard it. The forces working against a Christian marriage are relentless. Exhaustion, distraction, unresolved conflict, the slow drift of two people who stop choosing each other because the logistics of life have replaced the intimacy.

Make time for each other that is not about the children; talk about something other than the schedule; pray together even when you do not feel like it; confess to each other, forgive each other, touch each other, laugh together. Your children need you to do these things — not because they are watching a performance, but because they are absorbing a reality, and a marriage that is being tended teaches them what God's faithfulness looks like.

If you are in a marriage that is not alive, do not resign yourself to it. Talk to each other first — not about the children, not about the schedule, but about what has gone cold between you and why. Many marriages die not from a single catastrophe but from a thousand small neglects: the conversation skipped because you were tired, the touch that stopped because the affection felt forced, the prayer you used to pray together and abandoned because it felt awkward after a fight you never resolved. Those neglects accumulate, and they eventually produce a marriage where two people share a house but not a life.

I have counseled couples who could not remember the last time they had a conversation that was not about logistics, and when I asked what they used to talk about when they were dating, they both got quiet — and then they remembered: their dreams, their fears, their faith, what they wanted their life to look like, what they believed about God. Those conversations had been replaced, gradually, by exchanges about who was picking up the children and whether the mortgage payment had cleared — not deliberately, but erosionally. Life eroded the intimacy, and nobody noticed until the river had cut through to bare rock.

The repair begins with a decision, not a feeling. A decision: we are going to talk to each other again about things that matter, we are going to pray together again, we are going to touch each other with tenderness even when it does not come naturally — because discipline precedes desire, and the tenderness will follow the decision if we sustain it. That decision is not romantic. It is covenantal. And covenantal decisions are the ones that save marriages.

The Pastor's Marriage

I want to speak to the dynamic I have seen in ministry marriages, because I live inside it. The pastor's marriage carries a weight other marriages do not. Not because the pastor is more important. Because the pastor's marriage is more visible, more scrutinized, more subject to the demands of a community that does not always understand what it is asking. The pastor gives emotional energy to the congregation all week. They expect him to be available, present, steady. And the person who absorbs the cost of that expenditure is the spouse.

Susanna has told me, more than once, that she sometimes feels like she gets whatever is left over after the church has taken what it needs — and she is not wrong. There have been seasons where I gave the best of my energy to the congregation and came home with the scraps, where I was patient with church members and impatient with her, where I listened carefully to a counseling appointment and then dismissed her concerns at the dinner table. That is not pastoral faithfulness. That is pastoral idolatry — making the church the center rather than the God of the church, sacrificing the marriage on the altar of ministry productivity.

If you are in ministry, hear me: your marriage is not an obstacle to your ministry, it is your first ministry. If you are preaching about the love of God on Sunday and your spouse does not feel loved by you on Tuesday, something is broken, and it is not the sermon schedule. It is you. Fix the Tuesday before you preach the Sunday.

I have had to learn this the hard way, more than once, and each time the lesson has been the same: the congregation can survive a week without my best, but Susanna should not have to, and the children should not have to. The household is the first call on my energy, and when I get that order wrong, everything else suffers — including the very ministry I was prioritizing over the household.

A word about physical intimacy, because it matters to the argument even though most Christian parenting books leave it alone. Your children are not aware of the details of your physical relationship with your spouse, but they are profoundly aware of its effects, because a marriage where physical intimacy is alive produces a different emotional climate than one where it has gone dormant. The warmth between the parents, the ease of touch, the look that passes between them in the kitchen — all of it is visible to the children even when the intimacy itself is private. The child does not know why the house feels warm. The child only feels that it is warm. And that warmth is part of the formation environment.

When physical intimacy dies in a marriage, it takes the emotional warmth with it: the parents become roommates, and the children feel the chill. I am not reducing marriage to the bedroom; I am saying that the bedroom affects the kitchen, and the kitchen is where the children eat, and the meals they eat in a warm kitchen form them differently than the meals they eat in a cold one. So guard the whole marriage — not only the parts the children see, but the whole thing — because the children are formed by the whole thing, whether they can name what they sense or not.

Seek help. Talk to your pastor; find a counselor; read the books that have helped others — not because a dead marriage is a personal failure, though it may involve confession, but because the children in your house are building their theology of love from what they see, and what they see right now is not what you want them to carry. The repair of a marriage, done in front of children over months and years, teaches them that dead things can be raised. That is a resurrection theology. It is the most Christian thing a married couple can do.

When the Marriage Has Ended

And if your marriage has ended, through death or divorce, hear this. The formation of your children is not over. The household looks different now. The catechism has changed. But the four channels are still running. What you praise, what you worry about, what you repeat, what you rest in. Those channels are still forming your children. And the way you handle the grief, the transition, the rebuilding, is itself formation.

A parent who grieves honestly, who refuses to become bitter, who keeps loving the children through the upheaval, is teaching a theology of resilience that the child in the intact marriage may never learn. Your household is not disqualified. It is different. And God is in different the same way He is in whole.

Let me speak directly to the parent walking through divorce, because the formation challenges are particular, and your children are watching all of them: whether you speak of their other parent with bitterness or with restraint, whether you use them as messengers or as weapons or as people, whether the faith you profess survives the worst thing that has happened to your family.

I have counseled parents who handled divorce in ways that devastated their children's faith — not through the divorce itself, but through the behavior during and after it. The parent who badmouthed the other parent in front of the children; the parent who used the children as a bargaining chip; the parent so consumed by their own pain that the children's pain became invisible. In each case the child's theology of love, covenant, and faithfulness was damaged not by the divorce but by the conduct of the parents inside it.

I have also counseled parents who handled it in ways that preserved and even deepened their children's faith — the parent who said, your father and I could not make our marriage work, and that is a grief I carry, but he is still your father and I will not speak against him in this house; the parent who said, I am angry and I am hurt, and I am bringing those things to God because they are too big to carry alone; the parent who prioritized the children's stability over their own need to process publicly. Those parents taught their children something about grace under pressure that no sermon could have taught, and they taught it by living it — at the hardest moment of their lives, in front of the people who needed to see it most.

If you are in that situation, hear me: your conduct during and after the divorce is one of the most significant formation moments in your children's lives. You did not choose the situation, but you can choose the conduct, and it will shape your children's understanding of what it means to follow Jesus when everything falls apart. So choose well — not perfectly, but well — with as much grace and restraint and honesty as you can gather, and with the confidence that God is forming your children through this, even through this, if you will let Him.

Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her.
