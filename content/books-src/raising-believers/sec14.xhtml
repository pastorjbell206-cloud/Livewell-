CHAPTER THIRTEEN

When They Walk Away

When an adult child walks away from the faith — how to keep the door open, what to pray, and the hope for parents of a prodigal child.

There is a grief Christian parents carry in public because they have to, while it quietly breaks them in private — the grief of a child who has walked away from the faith. I have sat with parents in the middle of it, holding their hands across my office desk, and I have watched a mother's face collapse as she said out loud, for the first time in months, that she no longer knows whether her son believes anything at all; I have watched fathers go quiet for a long stretch and then admit, almost helplessly, that they do not know what they are supposed to do with this. I approach no chapter of this book with more care than this one, because the people reading it are sometimes bleeding, and what bleeding people need is not a platitude.

So let me name it plainly. Some of your children are going to walk away from the faith; some of them already have; and some are walking away right now, inches at a time, in a way you can feel but cannot yet bring yourself to say. This has been true in every generation, though it is more pronounced in ours, and it does not always mean you did something wrong any more than it always means you did something right. What it means, most of the time, is that you have a child who is becoming their own person before God — and that becoming runs on no timeline of yours, and sometimes it carries them through places you would have given anything to keep them out of. Pretending the whole thing is rare, or laying it entirely at the feet of parental failure, helps no parent who is actually living it; it only adds guilt to grief. That is not Christian. That is cruel.

Name What You Are Actually Looking At

I need to draw a few distinctions here, because the distinctions change how you pray and how you respond — and because these children, who can feel identical to a frightened parent, are not the same at all.

There is the child in active rebellion, pushing against everything they were raised with and rejecting it loudly, often through choices you cannot endorse. This child is the easiest to identify and the hardest to answer, because the rebellion trips a parent's fear and a parent's anger in the same instant.

There is the child in honest questioning, who is not rebelling but thinking — who has met ideas that press hard against what they were taught and is genuinely working through them. They may skip church for a season, read authors you disagree with, say things that sound like unbelief but are in fact the sound of a mind in the middle of examining what it was handed. This child needs space rather than pressure, and a parent who is not terrified — because a parent's terror tells the child, more loudly than any argument, that the faith cannot survive being examined.

There is the child who has concluded, on the merits, that Christianity is not true. This one has arrived somewhere; they have thought it through and made their peace, and they are neither angry nor rebellious. This child needs a parent who can respect the dignity of their thinking while holding firmly to his own convictions, and who does not insult that thinking by treating the conclusion as a phase to be waited out.

There is the child wounded by the church — the one for whom something happened in a church context, a leader's failure, a community's hypocrisy, an injury inflicted by people who claimed to speak for God. Their departure is not from God; it is from the people who spoke for him. This child needs a parent who can separate the church's failures from God's character without ever defending the indefensible.

There is the child using unbelief as a permission slip — the one who has not examined the faith and found it wanting, but found it inconvenient and built a rationale for setting it aside. That is not an intellectual departure but a moral one, and because the root is different, the response must be different too.

And there is the child who is unsure and ashamed, the most common of all and the least visible — the one who has not walked away so much as drifted, who does not know what they believe and is afraid to say so, afraid above all of disappointing you. They perform faith while the faith quietly evaporates behind the performance. This child needs a parent who creates room for honesty, a parent who can say: you can tell me where you really are, and I will not fall apart. The first pastoral work, before prayer and before speech, is to discern as honestly as you can which of these you are actually looking at, because that discernment changes how you pray, how you speak, and how you wait.

A Prodigal Child Is a Chapter, Not a Verdict

A child walking away is not the end of the story; it is a chapter — one that may run long, that may run the rest of your earthly life, but a chapter nonetheless and not the verdict. God is not finished simply because you have passed the age at which you could control the outcome.

The whole New Testament is crowded with people who came to Christ long after their mothers had stopped being able to make them, and Augustine is one of them — Monica prayed for him for decades, decades, and he came home. Some of your children may take exactly that long, which means you will have to do the hardest thing a parent of faith is ever asked to do: entrust them to the Father across years of not knowing, without curdling into bitterness on one side or collapsing into indulgence on the other.

And hear this carefully, because everything turns on it: your child's walking away is not a verdict on your parenting. It may expose things that need confession, or it may not. Many of the parents I have walked with through this grief were faithful — they did the work, they loved their children, they prayed over them, they made the hard calls — and the child still walked. Children are not products; they are persons, and persons have agency, and a child made in the image of God will, at some point, make their own response to God that no parent can make for them. You can only raise them in a home where that response is invited; whether they make it is, in the end, not within your control. It is a painful truth. It is also, if you will let it be, a freeing one.

What Keeps the Door Open, and What Slams It Shut

I have seen things that work and things that destroy families, so let me go in both directions and be specific about each, because vagueness is no kindness to a parent trying to decide what to do tonight.

What works is the open door — keep the relationship, keep the phone calls, keep inviting them to dinner, keep showing up for the grandchildren if there are grandchildren. Do not lecture them every time you see them, do not turn every conversation into an evangelistic appeal, do not treat them as a project; treat them, instead, as a person you love, who is loved by Christ, and whom Christ is pursuing in ways you will never see. The parent who keeps the door open is the parent the child is most likely to come back to when the season finally changes. It does not guarantee the return. It is what makes the return possible.

What destroys is the exact opposite, and it wears many faces: making your love conditional on their belief, withholding affection until they return, weaponizing Scripture in every conversation, grilling them about their spiritual life the moment they walk through the door, shaming them in public, triangulating other family members, refusing to attend their wedding because they married someone you disapprove of. These are not Christian responses; they are the responses of a parent who has made the child's belief into an idol and is now willing to harm the child for failing to feed it. It is common, and it is never pastoral, and it never leads back. It only hardens.

This will bother the stricter corners of the Christian parenting world, but I will say it anyway: tough love, in the way that phrase has been used over the last thirty years, has destroyed more relationships with prodigal children than it has ever restored. Look at the father in Luke 15, who did not chase his son down and drag him home, but who also did not cut him off in bitterness.

While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him.

Luke 15:20

That is the pattern, and in its structure it is not complicated — let go, watch, and run to them when they turn — but in its execution it is brutal, because the watching is what breaks you. The years of watching and not knowing are the suffering, and God does not promise to remove that suffering; he promises to carry you through it.

The Sins That Find Grieving Parents

There is a sin pattern among grieving Christian parents that I have watched destroy people, and I want to name it, because it hides itself as faithfulness — the spiral of self-blame.

If I had done this differently; if I had not sent her to that school; if I had held on tighter; if I had let go sooner; if I had prayed more, or prayed less, or not been so strict, so lenient — you can replay every parental decision you ever made, forever, and never once find the single one you can hold up as the cause. The grief wants a cause because a cause is manageable and mystery is not; stay with the mystery anyway. Confess where you were actually, specifically wrong, but do not confess a general indictment against yourself, because a general indictment is not repentance at all — it is self-punishment wearing repentance's clothes. Repent of what you did, and entrust what lies beyond that to God.

The grief wants a cause because a cause is manageable. Mystery is not. Stay with the mystery anyway.

There is a second pattern, and it happens more often than I can say: the weaponizing of the grief, in which the child's unbelief becomes a cudgel against your spouse. Two parents grieve differently — one wants to push harder, the other to back off — and the disagreement becomes blame, and the blame cracks the marriage. But the marriage is not supposed to be the casualty of the child's wandering; it is supposed to be the shared place where the two of you carry the weight together. Guard it. Pray together for the child, and refuse to turn against each other, because your unity under this cross is itself part of what God can use to draw the child home — a home unified in prayer and love is magnetic even to a child running from it.

What Is Actually in Your Hands: Prayer and Presence

You need some practical counsel, so here it is, beginning with the plainest thing: do not go silent with your child, and keep the ordinary rhythms of relationship running even when everything in you wants to retreat into them being a project.

Text them about things that have nothing to do with faith — ask about work, ask about the dog, send a photo of something that made you think of them — and tell them you love them, often, without strings attached. Invite them over, and when they come, feed them well and let the conversation be about whatever they want it to be about. If they raise something spiritual, listen far more than you speak; if they ask what you think, answer honestly, briefly, and without escalating; and whatever you do, do not turn every encounter into a summit. Most of the time, just be their parent.

And pray — pray specifically and often, pray by name, pray the passages that match the exact place they are in. Pray with your spouse, pray with other parents living the same grief, and do not walk this alone, because the enemy wants you isolated for the simple reason that isolation is where parents fall apart. Find two or three believing parents and pray together for one another's children; do it weekly, and do it for years. I have seen children come home out of those prayer rooms.

The hope in this chapter is slow and heavy. It is the hope that God is not finished, that you do not know what he is doing, and that the very child you are grieving may be being drawn, right now, in ways you cannot perceive, by a Father who loves them more than you do. Your role was never to secure their return; it is to be the kind of parent they would come home to, if they came home. Be that parent. That is what is in your hand. The rest is not.

Prepare yourself, too, for the possibility that you may not see the return in this life — it is a hard thing to say, and I say it only because it is true. Some children come home when you are old, some come home after you are gone, some come home in the last hour in a way that only God ever sees, and some, if we are going to be honest with one another, may not come home at all. This is the dark edge of the hope: you live in hope, and yet you do not know the outcome and you do not control it, so you entrust it to a Father who is both just and merciful — and whose mercy I would bet on, in the end, over my own reading of my child's trajectory any day. God is not a terrified parent. He is a steady one. Let him teach you his steadiness in the waiting.

To the Parents Whose Prodigal Came Home

I want to close with a word to the parents whose children did come back, because I have watched it happen after five years and after twenty, at thirty and at forty and at fifty — watched them come back quietly, without announcement, sometimes without telling their parents until months later, returning through a crisis, through a spouse, through the birth of their own child, through an illness that finally brought them to their knees.

The patterns of return are as varied as the patterns of departure, but a few threads run through nearly all of them, and the first is this: the child who comes back almost always says it was the parent's steady love they returned to. They do not say the parent's arguments convinced them. They say the love outlasted the rebellion, they say the door was always open, and they say the prayers were felt even when they pretended not to feel them.

They also often say that the faith they returned to is not the faith they left — that it is deeper, more honest, tested by the departure and refined by the return. They do not come back to a childhood faith; they come back to an adult faith forged in the furnace of their own doubt and their own failure, and that faith is frequently stronger than the faith of the child who never left, precisely because it was earned at greater cost.

If your child has come back, or is coming back, receive them — and do not make them perform gratitude for your faithfulness, do not say you told them so, do not require them to affirm every doctrine before you will welcome them. Welcome them the way the father in Luke 15 welcomed his son: with a ring and a robe and a feast, with extravagant joy, with the tears you have been holding for years, with the simple and devastating sentence that you were lost, and now you are found.

That is the gospel, and that is the hope, and that is what we have been building toward in every chapter of this book and every year of our children's lives — not a guarantee, but a hope, costly and earned and grief-tested and prayer-soaked, that God is the Father of our children, and that he is working even when we cannot see it, even when the years are long, even when the silence is deafening.

And if you are reading this in the middle of the grief, hear one last thing: you are loved by Christ, who sees your grief and is not angry with you for it but near to you in it. The children of God grieve with hope, which means you are not grieving wrong — you are grieving because you love. Keep loving. Keep praying. Keep the door open. And let the Father do what only a Father can do. He is working. Trust him.
