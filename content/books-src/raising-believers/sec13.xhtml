CHAPTER TWELVE

Grandparents and the Long Memory of Faith

How grandparents pass on faith to grandchildren — the long memory, the family story, and the generational role Scripture gives them.

Most parents read the seventy-eighth psalm as a parenting verse, and most grandparents have never once been told it is about them. Listen to how far it actually reaches:

We will tell the next generation the praiseworthy deeds of the Lord, his power, and the wonders he has done. He commanded our ancestors to teach their children, so the next generation would know them, even the children yet to be born, and they in turn would tell their children.

Psalm 78:4-6

Count the generations as the psalmist names them — ancestors, their children, the next generation, the children not yet born, and the children of those children — and you arrive at five, which is the first clue that this was never a one-generation project. What the psalmist describes is a relay, and a relay depends on every runner who carries the baton, not only on the parents holding it in the middle of the race; it depends on the grandparents, and on the great-grandparents, and on the old man in the back of the congregation who has walked with God for sixty years and whom, in all that time, nobody has thought to ask for his story.

The church has largely sidelined the grandparents from this conversation, and you can see it the moment you walk the parenting shelf at any bookstore, where you will find books for parents of toddlers and parents of teenagers and parents of prodigals, and almost nothing written for the grandparent. That silence is strange, because a grandparent is among the most formative presences a child will ever know — and the absence on that shelf is a loss the church has never bothered to measure.

What Christian Grandparents Carry

Grandparents carry something parents simply do not have. Not schedule time. Deep time — the memory of decades, the accumulated evidence of God's faithfulness over a span of years the child's own parents have not yet lived long enough to witness.

A grandmother who has walked with God for fifty years carries fifty years of answered prayer, fifty years of sustained faith, fifty years of watching God work in rooms that everyone else had given up on, and that memory is not sentimental at all — it is evidentiary, the testimony of a life lived long enough to confirm what Scripture only promises. When a child hears it from someone they trust, in an ordinary conversation that asks nothing of them, the child receives what no curriculum was ever built to hand over.

I think about my own congregation, where the most formative people in the lives of the young families are not the young families' peers but the older saints who have outlived their own fear. There is the woman in her seventies who has buried a husband, survived cancer, and raised four children, and who still weeps when she sings How Great Thou Art; there is the man in his eighties who fought in a war, lost a child, and rebuilt a marriage, and who will tell you with steady eyes that God has never once abandoned him. Their lives are the evidence — and in too many churches they sit in the back row, unasked, while the children's ministry combs the directory for twenty-five-year-old volunteers.

The grandparent's role is not supplementary; it is essential, because it fills the one gap a parent cannot fill — the gap between claimed faith and proven faith. A parent can tell a child that God is faithful, but a grandparent can tell a child that God has been faithful for sixty years, can point to the specific evidence and say I bet my whole life on it, I am still here, and He is still good. The child needs both voices. But the grandparent's testimony carries a weight the parent's claim has not yet earned, for the simple reason that the grandparent has lived long enough to prove it.

Four Things Only a Grandparent Can Give

Let me be specific about what a grandparent can do that no one else in the child's life can. There are four of them, they are not interchangeable, and each one does something the others cannot.

The first is story. Every family has a faith story, and in more families than anyone admits that story is dying in silence, because the only person who knows how the family came to faith — who prayed, who sacrificed, what the hard years actually cost, which prayers were answered and which miracles were small and which were not — is the grandparent who has never been asked to tell it. That story is the child's inheritance; it is the narrative in which the child's own faith finally makes sense. A child who knows that their grandmother came to Christ as a teenager in a small church in Alabama, that their grandfather came home from a war and built a life of faith out of the rubble, that their great-aunt prayed forty years for a wayward brother who came home in the end — that child has been set inside something old. The faith is not new. It has roots, it has tested itself against real years and held, and the child is simply the next chapter.

So tell it — at the dinner table, at Thanksgiving, in the car, while making cookies, while walking the dog. Tell it when the grandchild opens the door with a question, and tell it just as readily when they do not, because sometimes the story turns out to be the invitation they did not yet know they needed. The informality of the telling makes it more real, not less; the child who hears a conversion story over a bowl of ice cream has been handed something that will surface years later, at the exact moment they need it most.

The second is prayer. A parent's prayer is urgent, shaped by the daily crisis of the formation project and the fear that runs underneath it, but a grandparent's prayer is long — the grandparent has learned across decades that God works slowly, that what looks like unanswered prayer is not unanswered at all, that years of quiet intercession bear fruit on no human timeline. The one who prays for a grandchild by name, daily, for years, is laying a foundation the child will not understand until adulthood, and some of the most striking conversions I have ever witnessed were traced back, by the convert himself, to a grandmother who prayed.

Monica prayed for Augustine for decades while he ran in every direction away from her — brilliant, rebellious, dissolute, lost — and she prayed, and she wept, and she followed him across the Mediterranean, and she prayed more, and in the end he came home. The greatest theologian in the history of the Western church was, before he was anything else, a mother's prayer project carried across years. Do not tell me prayer does not work. It works on a timeline longer than ours.

A parent can tell a child that God is faithful. A grandparent can tell a child that God has been faithful for sixty years, and here is the evidence.

The third is stability. There are seasons when the parents are simply overwhelmed — by illness, by money, by a marriage under strain, a job lost, a ministry burning down — and in those seasons the children feel the ground shift beneath them and feel the parent's fear before anyone names it. A grandparent who is present can be the ground that does not shift: the steady hand, the calm voice, the reminder that this family has come through hard things before and that God carried them through every one. A child who has a grandparent for an anchor in a storm learns something he will keep for life — that faith does not depend on circumstances but on God, and that God does not move.

The fourth is love without the weight of the parenting role. The parent must enforce the rules and make the hard calls, but the grandparent gets to love without an agenda — to delight in the child rather than shape him, to be present without the pressure of the project. A child who has an adult who loves him that way, who is enjoyed rather than managed, celebrated rather than corrected, welcomed without having to perform first, carries a felt picture of the unconditional love of God that the parent, who must also discipline, cannot always provide. That delight is not indulgence. It is theology — it forms the child's capacity to receive the love of God at all.

A Word to Christian Grandparents

Some of you feel sidelined — feel that the young parents do not want your input, that the culture has informed you your season is over, that you no longer understand the world your grandchildren are growing up in and therefore have nothing left to offer them.

None of it is true. You have a life behind you, and you have testimony, prayer, and presence still in your hands. You have a patience the young parents lack because they have not yet lived long enough to learn it, and a perspective they lack because they are still inside the very thing you can now see from a distance. All of it is formative. All of it is needed.

So do not wait for an invitation; offer yourself. Call them. Write them letters — actual letters, on paper, the kind they can hold and keep and read again when they are thirty and you are gone. Tell them what you believe and why you believe it, tell them what God has done across the length of your life and what you pray for them now, tell them the specific things you see in them that remind you of His work. Do not assume they already know any of it. Tell them.

Many of you carry a harder tension — the one between wanting to guide and not wanting to overstep — because you see a grandchild raised in a way you would not have chosen, and you face the choice between speaking up and risking the relationship or staying silent and carrying the worry alone. There is no formula for it, but there is a principle: your primary gift is presence, not correction. If you must choose between being right and being present, choose presence, because a grandchild who has you — who knows you love him, who has heard your testimony and been prayed for by name — has a resource he can draw on later, even if the parenting you disagreed with produces the very crisis you feared. You will be there when it does. That matters more than winning an argument about bedtimes or screens.

The one exception is moral or physical danger; if you see genuine harm, you speak — clearly, and with love. But most of parenting lives in the gray, and in the gray your gift is patience, prayer, and presence. Not control.

When the Family Faith Story Is Missing or Broken

I think about my own sons, and about the fact that they do not have grandparents who can tell them stories of multigenerational faith, because my mother came to faith later in life.

That long arc of generational faithfulness — the kind in which a grandparent can say, my grandmother prayed for me before I was born, and her mother prayed for her, and now I am praying for you — is not an arc I can give my sons, and it is something I grieve, quietly, precisely because I know the power of that kind of story and I know my sons do not have it. So if you can tell that story, understand that you are carrying something rare, and do not keep it to yourself. Tell it at Thanksgiving and tell it on ordinary Tuesdays when you are babysitting. The child will not remember every detail; he will remember that his grandparent had a story with God, and that he himself is part of it.

I want to speak to the grandparent who is raising grandchildren, and there are far more of you than anyone bothers to count. You stepped in when the parents could not or would not, and you are raising children at the age you thought you would finally rest; you are tired, you are afraid you are not enough, and you wonder whether the gap between your generation and theirs is simply too wide, whether they resent you for not being their parents. Hear this plainly: you are doing holy work. You are the stable ground under a child whose ground was taken away, the face of God's faithfulness to a child who has every reason to believe people leave. You do not need to be perfect. You need to be there — and you are. That is enough.

And I want to speak to the grandparent estranged from grandchildren, where a son or a daughter has pulled away, the relationship is broken, and you are not permitted into their lives the way you long to be — a grief that is among the most painful things a person can carry. I cannot fix it in a chapter, but I can tell you this: pray anyway, because your prayers are not restricted by geography or distance; write the letters even when they go unanswered, and send the gifts even when they go unacknowledged; let them know, by every means still left to you, that your love has not changed and that you are waiting. Some of the most striking reunions I have ever witnessed came only after years of patient waiting — the grandparent who kept praying, who kept sending the birthday card long after it stopped being acknowledged. The wait was long. The faithfulness was costly. And the reunion, when at last it came, was a resurrection.

A Word to Churches

Honor your elderly. They are not a demographic to manage; they are the long memory of the faith, and they carry the very stories the young families need most.

So create intergenerational space, and create it on purpose: let the old saints pray over the young families, let the grandmothers hold the babies while the young mothers breathe, let the grandfathers sit with the teenage boys and tell them what manhood has actually looked like across sixty years. Stop segregating the church by age group as though the generations had nothing to give one another, because they have everything to give one another — the relay in Psalm 78 requires the handoff, the handoff requires proximity, and proximity is the one thing our programs keep engineering away. Put the generations in the same room and watch what God does.

And to the grandparent who has read this far: your role is not finished. It may, in fact, be entering its most important phase, because the prayers you pray now may be the prayers that bring your grandchildren home twenty years from now, the stories you tell now may be the ones they remember when everything else has been forgotten, and the love you give now may be the picture of God's love that surfaces at the moment of their greatest need. You are not retired from the work of formation. You are in the deep end of it. So stay there — pray, tell the stories, be present, and let the long memory of your faith become the foundation of theirs.

You are living in a culture that worships youth and discards age, and the church has too often followed it down that road, building its programs around the young while the elders sit in the pews with decades of tested faith and nobody asks them to testify. Resist that marginalization, because you have what the young do not — you have lived proof. So tell the stories, and not once but many times over, because the embedding requires repetition: tell the story of how you came to faith, the story of the hardest year of your life and how God carried you through it, the story of the prayer that took twenty years to answer, the marriage that almost broke and was rebuilt, the child who wandered and came home. Your grandchild may not seem the least bit interested. Tell it anyway — because ten years from now, or twenty, or thirty, in a moment of crisis you cannot foresee, the story will surface, and the memory of your faith will be the thing that holds when everything else gives way.

The next chapter is the hardest one in the book. It is about what happens when they walk away.
