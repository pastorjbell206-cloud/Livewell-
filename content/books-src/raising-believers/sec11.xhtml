CHAPTER TEN

Fathers, Presence, and the Weight of Blessing

The Christian father as the spiritual leader of the home — why a father's presence and blessing form a child's faith, and a word to men raised without one.

I grew up without a father, and I have said it once already in this book and will say it again before the end, because it is the place everything in me starts. I know what absence does to a boy, and I have watched it do its slow work in the lives of the men I have pastored for twenty years, which is how I came to know something the church seems to have forgotten: that the conversation about Christian fatherhood has grown so sentimental it no longer lands on the men who most need to hear it. So this chapter will be blunt. If you are a father, I am asking you to stand inside the indictment with me, because I am already in it.

Name it plainly: American Christian fatherhood has become largely ornamental. In a great many professing Christian homes the father is physically present and spiritually peripheral; he provides, he appears for the big moments, he takes his family to church, and he is not absent in any legal sense, yet he is absent in the sense that matters most for the formation of his children, because his interior life before God is not visible to them and therefore is not shaping them. He is around. He is not present.

This kind of absence has a signature, and you can trace it with a few questions. Ask a child in a Christian home who prays for them by name at night, and where the mother does it, God bless her, she is often doing it alone; ask who reads Scripture with them, and the answer is often the mother again; ask who walks them through the hard conversations of purpose and sin and sex and failure and calling, and it is often the mother, or no one; ask who blesses them when they leave the house, and often the honest answer is no one. The father is at work, or on his phone, or decompressing, or simply not thinking about it. He is not hostile to the faith. He is just not carrying the weight.

The Spiritual Weight a Father Carries

The weight is the word, and the biblical picture of a father is a man who carries weight on behalf of his household, the weight of provision, yes, but also the weight of prayer, of blessing, of correction delivered in love, the weight of deciding which direction the household will face, the weight of being the first one to repent when he has been wrong, the weight of being home when his people need him home. A father who carries only the economic weight is not carrying the biblical weight; a father who carries the biblical weight is doing something his children are shaped by for life, whether or not they can name it at the time.

I have a theory about why so many men carry only the lighter weight, and it goes deeper than laziness: most Christian men were not fathered well themselves. Their own fathers, if present at all, modeled a fatherhood that was economic at best and absent at worst: men who provided and protected but did not pray with their children, or bless them, or speak over them, because no one had done the intimate, exposed work of spiritual fatherhood for them and the culture told them that intimacy was the mother's domain. The current generation inherited the void. They know something is missing, but they do not know what it is, and lacking a model they default to the only version they know: present, employed, attending, and absent at exactly the level where formation actually happens.

The Bible's understanding of fatherhood is not cultural but covenantal, and the father represents something that cannot be outsourced: however imperfectly, he represents the Fatherhood of God to his children, the first picture they get of what a Father is like. That is an enormous thing to lay on a man, and it is meant to be, meant to drive him to his knees, meant to send him to the actual Father, daily, for the grace he needs to represent Him at all. A Christian father is not a man who has mastered the old patriarchal script; he is a man who has become small enough before God to be used as a window, the window through which his children get their first glimpse of the Father's face.

This is why the stakes are not abstract. If the father is angry, the children will struggle for decades to believe that God is not angry; if the father is absent, they will struggle to believe that God is not absent; if the father is forever disappointed, they will carry the quiet sense that they have disappointed God. Theology in the head competes with theology in the marrow, and the theology in the marrow, the one planted by the father, usually wins.

The Hollow Place

A man in my congregation came to me about three years ago, in his late thirties, with two sons, eight and six. He had been raised in a Christian home by a father who was doctrinally precise and emotionally unavailable (a man who could explain substitutionary atonement and had never once told his son he loved him) and the man now sitting in my office was carrying a wound he had never named, a wound that was leaking into his own fathering. He was repeating the pattern. He knew it. He did not know how to stop.

I asked him when he had last blessed his sons by name, and he looked at me as though I were speaking a foreign language, so I explained: lay your hand on your son's head, tell him who he is, tell him who God is making him, speak a future over him, and use the Aaronic blessing if you do not have your own words yet. He said he had never done it, that his father had never done it, and then he started crying, because he understood in that moment that the hollow place inside him was the place where a father's blessing was supposed to be.

He went home that night and blessed his eight-year-old for the first time, and he told me later that the boy looked up at him with wide eyes and said, Dad, do that again. The child was hungry for it. He did not know what it was called. He only knew that it was the thing his soul had been waiting for.

A generation of men is walking around with a hollow place where a father's blessing was supposed to be. Do not let your children become those men.

This is why a Christian father must take his own formation seriously, because you cannot offer your children a God you do not know, and you cannot represent a Father whose face you rarely see. Your children will absorb your actual relationship with God, not the one you wish you had: if that relationship is thin, their inheritance is a thin God, and if it is alive, their inheritance is a living one. This is the most important pastoral work any man ever does, and also the work he is usually least equipped for, because the church has rarely discipled men to do it and the culture has handed him no categories beyond breadwinner and occasional disciplinarian.

Three Things a Father Can Start This Week

Let me be practical, because there are three things a father can begin this week that change the spiritual climate of a home more than almost anything else I can name.

The first is to bless your children by name, out loud, regularly, not as a performance but as a pattern. Lay your hand on the child's head and speak words over them: you are my son, I love you, I am proud of the way God is building you. The oldest words for it are the best ones, and you can borrow them until your own arrive:

The Lord bless you and keep you; the Lord make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you; the Lord lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace.

Numbers 6:24-26

Some of you are reading that and finding it strange, even foreign, and I understand, because the first time I did it with one of my sons I was self-conscious, and I kept doing it anyway, until it became one of the most load-bearing practices in my home. My sons expect it now; they ask for it when I forget. There is something in a child designed to receive a father's blessing, and it starves without it; starving is not too strong a word. Bless them now, while they are small, and keep blessing them when they are grown.

The second is to pray with your children, out loud, often, not for them, with them. Let them hear you thank God for specific things by name, let them hear you ask for mercy when you were wrong, let them hear you intercede for people outside the family, including the people who are hard for you to love, and let them hear you carry your worries to God rather than to the television. Prayer practiced in front of children catechizes them in what God is for and how He is to be approached; you cannot outsource this to a grace before meals that nobody means. Your children should hear you pray often enough that they begin to pray the same way when you are not in the room. That is the mark.

The third is to repent in front of your children, and this one is harder, which is why most men avoid it. They believe that authority in the home is held by never showing weakness, but that is a cultural script, not a Christian one, and a father who is never wrong in front of his children is teaching them that Christianity is a performance of rightness, which is the opposite of the gospel. A father who turns to his child and says, I was wrong, I was short with you, that came out of my own stuff, I have asked God's forgiveness and now I am asking yours; that father is teaching something profoundly Christian, and the authority does not shrink but grows. The child learns that authority in this house is not brittle. It is weighty. It is honest. It is rooted in God, not in the father's ability to pretend.

I have had to do this many times, more than I would like. I have snapped at my sons, I have been withdrawn when I should have been present, I have come home from ministry with nothing left and spent the nothing on the people I most love, and every time I have gone back, admitted it, asked forgiveness, and prayed with the child about it, the relationship has strengthened rather than weakened. Children do not need an unimpeachable father. They need an honest one: a father who is being sanctified in front of them, not one pretending he has already arrived.

Sons, Daughters, and a Father's First Word

To fathers of sons: your boys are watching you to learn what a man is, and the culture will not tell them, the schools often will not, and the internet will offer them a thousand broken versions of manhood and hope to leave them either ashamed or bitter. You get the first word. Speak it.

A man is a servant of God who carries weight for the sake of others, a man who is not afraid to kneel before God and not afraid to apologize, strong enough to be gentle; he works hard, protects what God has put under his care, is patient with his wife and tender with his children, and tells the truth when it costs him. Let your sons see it; do not lecture them, let them see it. They become what they watch, not what they hear.

To fathers of daughters: your daughters are watching you to learn what a man is like, and to learn how they should expect to be treated by one, which means the way you love their mother is a template, the way you speak to her is a template, the way you listen, or do not, is a template. One day a man will come to win her, and whether she can tell a true one from a false one will have been shaped, in large part, by you. Give her so much evidence of what love looks like that she can spot the counterfeit at a hundred yards.

The Busy Father and the Discipline of Presence

I want to add something I have not often seen in books on fatherhood, because I know it from the inside: the ministry father gives his best hours, his best energy, his best emotional availability to the congregation, and then, having been present all day for other people's families, he comes home spent, with nothing left for his own. His wife sees it. His children see it. They learn that other people's needs come first, that God's work as the church represents it matters more than God's work in this home, that the pastor's family is the sacrifice the pastor makes for the ministry.

That is a lie, and it has destroyed more pastoral families than I can count, and I have been in this sin. There were seasons when I gave my church my best and gave my family my empty, and the result was predictable: my sons pulled away, not from God but from me, because they had learned that their father's attention was a scarce resource other people consumed before they could reach it. The correction has been painful and is ongoing. I have had to learn to leave the office, turn off the phone, tell a congregant that the evening belongs to my family, and resist the savior complex that says I must be available to everyone at all hours. The church will survive without me for an evening; my sons will not survive without a father who is present. If you are a pastor reading this, hear me: your family is your first ministry, not your second, not your backup; get that order wrong and you may build a great ministry and lose your children.

But the deeper problem is not unique to pastors; it belongs to every father who is here but not present: the one who comes home every evening, eats at the table, attends the events, and whose mind is somewhere else, his phone in his hand, his work following him home in his head where he cannot set it down. You are a body in a chair, and your children have learned to tell the difference between a father who is in the room and a father who is with them.

Presence is not proximity. I can sit three feet from my son and be a thousand miles away: at the dinner table, turning over a sermon or a budget meeting or a hard phone call, while he tells me about his day and feels the distance as plainly as if I had left. He stops talking. He looks down at his plate. He learns that his voice is not interesting enough to hold his father's attention, and that lesson, absorbed over a thousand dinners, forms something in him that no bedtime blessing can undo.

The discipline of presence is one of the hardest disciplines of fatherhood, because it asks you to set things down (not the phone only, but the worry, the planning, the rehearsal of tomorrow's problems) and to be in the room you are in, with the people who are in it, giving them the thing they most need from you, which is not your provision and not your protection but your attention. I practice this imperfectly, but I practice it: when I sit down for dinner the phone goes in another room, and when a son is talking to me I stop and turn my body toward him, not because I am naturally attentive, but because I have learned that the moments I am tempted to half-listen are the very moments he is most likely to say the thing I need to hear. Children do not schedule their vulnerability. They tell you in the car, while you are making sandwiches, at nine-thirty at night when you are ready for bed, and miss those unscheduled moments and you miss the conversation that mattered most.

To the Man Who Grew Up Without a Father

I have saved a word for last, and it is for the men like me, the men who did not have a father, or had one whose presence wounded them. Many of you are feeling the old grief as you read, and I know it, because I have lived in it; and I know the particular difficulty it creates when you sit down to form children of your own.

When you had no model, you are building the plane while flying it: there is no template, no voice in your memory that says, this is what a good father sounds like, only a silence where that voice should be, and the silence breeds two opposite errors. Either you try too hard, becoming an overwhelming presence that smothers rather than forms, or you withdraw, afraid you will repeat patterns you cannot even name, because the patterns are absences rather than actions. And how do you keep yourself from repeating an absence?

I have lived in both errors. In the early years I compensated (at every game, every practice, every school event, present in body to a degree that exhausted me, because I was terrified of being absent the way my father was absent) and my presence, driven by fear instead of love, had an edge my sons could feel. Fear-driven presence is a form of control; it looks like devotion and feels like surveillance. Then I swung the other way and pulled back, telling myself I was giving them space when I was really protecting myself from an intensity of fatherhood I did not know how to regulate. Children always feel the difference between a father who gives them room and a father who is retreating.

The correction came, as it often does, through my wife. Susanna sat me down one evening and said the thing that changed the course of my fathering: you are either trying to be the father you did not have, she said, or running from the fear that you will fail the way he failed, and neither of those is you. Find the father God made you to be. It took me years to unwrap it, but the core was simple and hard: I could not parent from a wound, I had to parent from a healed place, and healing the father wound meant taking the wound to God rather than to my children. My sons are not responsible for healing my father's absence; God is. As He has done that healing, slowly, across years, the fathering that comes out of me has grown less driven by fear and more rooted in love.

So if you are a father without a father, hear me: you are not disqualified, and you are not destined to repeat what was done to you, or what was never done, but the wound will define your fathering if you leave it untreated, because untreated wounds leak into every relationship, and the one they leak into most is the relationship with the small person who looks up and calls you Dad. Take it to God, and to people who can help you carry it: a counselor, a mentor, a friend who will listen without flinching. You do not need a father who modeled it in order to become one, because God is the Father, and He is faithful to give you, over time, what no earthly father gave. I am living evidence. I did not have it. He has been giving it to me for twenty-five years, slowly, and He has let me hand pieces of it to my sons, and the pieces are holding. The past did not finish you. Go to the Father. Let Him father you. Then go home, and father them.
