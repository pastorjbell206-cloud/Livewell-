INTRODUCTION

The Faith You Cannot Transmit

Why so many children raised in Christian homes leave the faith — and what bedtime devotions, Sunday school, and youth group were never built to do.

I have five sons, and I have been a pastor long enough to have baptized children I once held as infants, which means I have sat, over the years, in two kinds of living rooms that have never stopped haunting me. In the first sit parents in their sixties whose grown children no longer claim the faith; in the second, parents in their thirties, watching their own toddlers and terrified of ending up in the first room. Something has broken in the way Christian households form children, and no curriculum, however good, is going to fix it.

This is a book for the parents who already sense that, who have done the bedtime devotionals and the Sunday school drop-offs and the years of youth group volunteering and still carry a quiet suspicion that something is not taking root. They did, faithfully, what they were told to do; and they are watching children they love lose interest, or harden, or disappear into a phone that catechizes them in a different gospel every seven seconds.

Let me be plain about what this book is not. It is not a program, and it is not thirteen steps to raise godly children; the shelf is already crowded with those, and most of them sell the same quiet lie: that if you run the right process on your child, you will get the right output. You will not, because children are not processes and faith is not an output, and the parent who believes otherwise will be devastated either by a child who performs compliance without conviction or by a child who, sooner or later, stops performing at all.

What this book argues is slower and far more uncomfortable, because faith forms in children the way it forms in anyone: through the weight of a life lived near other lives that actually believe, through the presence of adults who are not pretending, through questions answered rather than deflected, and through a household whose ordinary rhythms say, over years and without announcing it, that God is real and that following Him is the most serious thing we do.

That kind of formation cannot be outsourced; it cannot be scheduled into thirty-minute devotions everyone dreads, it cannot be purchased in a curriculum, and it cannot be handed off to the youth pastor, God bless the youth pastor. Faith forms in the soil of a home, and where the soil is thin, nothing grows, no matter how good the seeds are.

The Kitchen Where This Began

Here is the part I have to say up front, because it is the part that keeps me honest: I am inside this indictment. I have been, in different seasons, the distracted father and the anxious father, the father who outsourced what he should have carried, the father who answered too quickly because he could not bring himself to admit he did not know; I have performed spirituality in front of my sons when what they needed was a father who was actually praying, and I have told them the right things while my life quietly taught them something else. If that sounds familiar, then understand you are not reading a man who has figured it out; you are reading a man who has been chastened enough to stop pretending.

This book did not start in a study. It started in my kitchen, about six years ago, when one of my older boys looked at me in the middle of a family devotional and asked, Dad, do you actually want to be doing this right now. He was not being disrespectful; he was being observant. He had watched me check my phone twice during the reading, watched my eyes glaze over my own questions, watched me rush the prayer because I still had emails waiting, and so he was not questioning the faith at all. He was questioning me, asking whether the thing we were doing mattered to the person who kept insisting it mattered.

I wish I could tell you I handled it well. I gave a defensive little speech about the importance of family devotions, and he did not respond, because he did not need to; his point settled into me over the following weeks like a stone dropping slowly through water. He was right. The devotional had become a performance; I was checking a box, my sons could see it, and they were drawing the only correct conclusion: that this faith was important enough to perform but not important enough to be real. That evening began the slow dismantling of the model I had been running. Not the model I preached. The model I lived. They were not the same model, and my sons knew the difference long before I did.

So I want to be careful about the diagnosis, because it is not that Christian parents are failing their children. It is that Christian parents were sold a model of formation that was never going to work, and many of them have run that model with great faithfulness and are now grieving the results. If that is you, I am not here to add guilt; I am here to name what was wrong, and to point toward something older and slower and harder and, I believe, more honest.

The Pastor's Chair: Why Christian Kids Leave the Church

The view from this chair is different from the view from the pew. I watch families doing everything right by every metric the evangelical world has handed them (regular attendance, small group, missions giving, Bible memory) and I watch their children leave, not at the margins but at the center. The most committed families in my church are sometimes the ones whose children walk away with the most resolve, because those children got the full version of the model and found it wanting.

I also watch families nobody would ever put on a poster (families that are messy, that miss Sundays, that have never finished a single study) and their kids are walking with Jesus in their twenties and thirties. When you ask those kids why, they tell you the same thing, almost word for word: my parents were real. They were not impressive; they were real. The faith in our house was never a program. It was simply the way my mom and dad actually lived.

That observation, repeated across twenty years of ministry, is where this book comes from, not a theory but a pattern, consistent enough that I am now willing to call it a principle: faith forms in proximity to a real life, not in exposure to a right program. And the implications are uncomfortable for a generation of parents who invested heavily in programs and lightly in their own interior lives.

Some of you came to this book because of the statistics. The numbers shift depending on who counts and how they define their terms, but the picture is clear enough: somewhere between forty and sixty percent of young adults raised in evangelical homes are walking away from the church by their mid-twenties. Not all of them abandon faith entirely (some move to other traditions, some to a private spirituality with no community, some to nothing at all) but the exodus is real, it is large, and it has been growing for three decades.

The common evangelical response has been to build better programs (better youth ministries, better apologetics, better college transition strategies, better worship engineered to keep young people engaged) and none of these is wrong in itself, yet all of them miss the point. The programs were not bad; the programs were simply being asked to do what only a household can do, while the households behind all that program attendance were teaching a different catechism than the one the programs taught.

This is not a comfortable diagnosis, but it is the one I believe is true, and I have staked the book on it: if I am wrong, the rest of it falls apart, and if I am right, then the most important decision a Christian parent makes is not which church to attend or which school to choose or which curriculum to follow. The most important decision is what kind of person the parent is going to be, in their own home, when no one from the church is watching.

The Fortress and the Presence

The old model runs roughly like this. Christian formation is primarily cognitive, so the task is to get the child to believe the right things by stacking the content (Sunday school, Awana, VBS, youth group, Christian school if you can manage it) and then to build the behavioral guardrails around it: no this, no that, yes to this, layered in with the right music, the right friends, the right media. You hold the line until eighteen, then release the child into the world and hope.

I call this the fortress model. You build the walls and fill the interior with the right content, you patrol the perimeter to keep the bad out and the good in, and when you finally release the child you hope the fortress held long enough that they will carry the architecture out on their own. The problem is threefold.

First, the fortress is permeable, and it always was. In 1985 the fortress had cracks; in 2026 it has a smartphone-sized hole in the wall that connects to every idea, image, and ideology our species has ever produced. The wall cannot hold. It was only ever built to hold until the next Sunday, and even that is optimistic now.

Second, the fortress produces a child who knows what is inside the walls and what is outside them but who has never actually been taught to think. The right answers were inside the fortress, so the child knows them; the wrong answers were outside, so the child has never been allowed to engage them, and when the wrong answers finally arrive, at college, at work, online, the child has no structure for processing them, having been protected from the questions and never equipped to handle them. The questions, when they come, do not feel like an attack on the faith. They feel like the first honest conversation the child has ever had about whether any of this is true.

Third, and worst, the fortress treats the child as a container to be filled rather than a person to be formed: good content in, bad content out, while the child's own curiosity, doubt, desire, and interior life are not part of the equation at all. They are not trusted; they are managed. And a child whose interior life has been managed rather than formed will, at the first opportunity, seize the interior life that was never given to him. From the parent's chair that seizure usually looks like rebellion. It is not rebellion. It is a person becoming a person, and the tragedy is only that it happens outside the faith, because it was never permitted to happen inside it.

I have a different model to offer, and it is not new; it is very old. It is the way faith was actually passed down for most of Christian history, before the programmatic era, before age-graded infrastructure, before we assumed formation was something you do to a child rather than something that happens near one. The model is presence: formed presence, over time, in ordinary rooms.

It works like this. A parent who is being formed by Christ, who is honest about that, who has a real interior life with God and lets that life show in ordinary moments, who answers hard questions instead of deflecting them, who lets his failures be visible and his repentance be real, who treats the faith not as a performance staged for the children but as the animating reality of the whole house; that parent is the primary formation environment. Not the curriculum, not the church, not the school: the parent.

This does not mean the parent must be impressive; it means the parent must be real, because a parent whose faith is small but honest does more for formation than a parent whose faith is large but performed. Children do not need a parent who has all the answers. They need a parent whose life makes the questions worth asking.

The old model has many problems, and the chapters ahead work through them in order: it treats faith as a transferable body of information rather than a formed way of being; it casts the parent as a manager of inputs rather than the primary curriculum; it outsources the part of formation that cannot be outsourced; it meets a child's real questions with a performance of certainty the child can usually see through by middle school; it mistakes compliance for conviction, and never reckons with how discipline either forms or deforms a child's image of God. It ignores what screens are doing to the attention of anyone under eighteen, forgets that a child's first formation environment is the marriage and not the family devotional, and has no theology for the teenage years, when formation goes underground and the parent must learn an entirely different posture. It has let fathers become ornamental, has no word for the single parent or the blended family or the grandparent raising grandchildren, no place for the role grandparents play in intergenerational faith, and no theology at all for what you do when a child walks away.

All of that is in the pages that follow. But underneath every chapter, hold this one sentence in front of you, because everything turns on it.

You cannot give your children a faith you do not have.

If your own faith is thin, if it runs on performance, if it has shrunk to attendance and correct opinions, if you have not wrestled with God in a long time and have quietly stopped being hungry for His presence, then what you pass on will be thin too, not because your children are rejecting anything, but because there is nothing substantial there for them to reject.

I learned this the hard way, not from a book but from watching my own children absorb not what I said but what I was. They did not absorb my sermons; they absorbed my Monday mornings. They did not absorb my theology of prayer; they absorbed whether I actually prayed when no one was performing. They did not absorb my lectures about Scripture; they absorbed whether the Bible on my nightstand had been opened that week or whether it was simply furniture.

Children are the most honest theologians in the house. They evaluate the adults every day, and they are not evaluating what the adults say; they are evaluating the gap between what the adults say and what the adults are. When the gap is small, faith forms; when the gap is wide, something else forms, something that looks like faith on the outside and is hollow inside, something that holds only until the child is old enough to name what he has been watching all along.

This is the hope, and it is also the cost. The hope is that the most important thing you can do for your children's faith is to attend to your own; you do not need a better curriculum, you need a deeper life. The cost is that there is no shortcut: you cannot outsource this, and you cannot catch up at fourteen after twelve years of practical absence. The work begins in the parent's interior life, works outward through the texture of a home, and compounds quietly over years.

For the Christian Parent Who Feels Disqualified

I want to say a word to the parents who need this book most, because they are the ones most likely to feel disqualified by it. You have already failed, and I do not mean that as a judgment, I mean it as a fact. You have already said the wrong thing at the wrong moment, already lost your temper when your child needed your steadiness, already chosen the screen over the conversation, the schedule over the person, the shortcut over the investment. You have failed. So have I. The question is not whether you have failed; the question is whether the failure is the last word.

It is not, and the reason has nothing to do with your competence. It has to do with the grace of a God who has been forming people through imperfect parents since the garden. Abraham lied, Isaac played favorites, Jacob manipulated, Moses lost his temper, David was absent from his sons in the years that mattered most, and God kept forming a people through every one of them, not because they were adequate but because He was. That does not excuse negligence; it does mean the door to faithful parenting is not locked to people who have already blown it. It has always been open, and the key is not perfection. The key is presence: imperfect, honest, repentant, faithful presence, sustained over years.

I once counseled a father in his late fifties who had spent his career building a business and his evenings in front of the television. His children were raised in church but had never seen their father pray outside a meal, never heard him say what God meant to him, never watched him open a Bible that was not in a pew rack; his faith was real but invisible, and his children, now in their thirties, had concluded it was a social convention rather than a living reality. He came to me because his youngest daughter had told him, in a conversation that shook him, that she had stopped believing in God, not because of any argument, but because she had never seen any evidence that her father actually believed.

He asked me what he could do, and I told him three things: become the man you wish you had been, not as a strategy to win her back but as obedience to the God who is still forming you; tell her the truth, that your faith was real but you hid it, and that hiding it was wrong; and pray for her every single day, by name, and do not stop. He did all three. His daughter has not returned to the faith as I write this, but the father is a different man, and the door has been open now for three years, and the daughter knows it is open, because the father has become the kind of man who keeps it open.

This book is for that father as much as for the mother of a toddler, because the principles in these chapters are not age-locked; they work in the home with a newborn and in the conversation with a thirty-year-old, since formation is not a program that expires but a way of being that God can use at any point in the story. It is for the parent of young children who wants to start well, and the parent of teenagers who feels like they are starting over. It is for the single mother who is doing this alone and has been told, usually by implication, that her household is incomplete; it is not. It is for the father present in body and absent in spirit who is ready to change; for the grandparent given a second chance at formation; for the stepparent forming faith in children they did not raise from birth; for the parent whose child has already walked away and needs to know that God is not finished; and for the married couple whose marriage is the first classroom their children attend and who have never once thought of it that way. I have tried to see all of you. If I miss you in places, that is my limitation, not yours.

One last thing I have to be honest about: this book is not a guarantee. I cannot promise that if you follow every principle in these pages your children will walk with Jesus into adulthood, because I know families who did everything right, or close to it, and watched their children walk away, and I know families who did almost nothing deliberate and whose children are among the most faithful believers I have ever met. The sovereignty of God is real, and it means the outcome is not entirely in your hands. What is in your hands is the soil.

You can tend the soil, you can pull the weeds, you can water the ground, but whether the seed sprouts and grows to its full height is between the seed and the God who made it, and that should be both humbling and liberating. Humbling, because you cannot guarantee the result; liberating, because you do not have to. Your job is faithfulness. The harvest is God's. And the best thing you can do for that harvest is to become the kind of soil where faith has its best chance of taking root.

So turn the page, if you would rather have the truth than the comfortable lie that you can give your children what you have not yet let God give you. This book does not end in a neat resolution, and it cannot, because the work of raising believing children does not resolve; it continues past their childhood, past their leaving home, past your ability to control any outcome, into the years when your only tools are prayer and presence and the open door. I will prepare you for as much of that road as I can see from where I stand. The rest belongs to God, and His vision is longer than mine. We have rooms to walk through.
