CONCLUSION

The Long Obedience of Raising Believers

The long, ordinary work of raising believers — what faithful Christian parenting looks like on a Tuesday when no one is watching.

I will not restate the book. You read it, and a summary at this point would only insult your attention. What I owe you instead is a word about how to carry any of it into an ordinary Tuesday — because a Tuesday, when no one is watching and nothing feels significant, is where parenting actually happens. Every book ever written on this subject is finally worth only as much as it can be lived out on the kind of afternoon that leaves no record.

So let me close the way the book began: in my own house, with my own children, where the principles I believe were tested against the life I was actually living — and where I learned that the testing is the only proof that matters.

A Year in Luke With My Son

One of my sons came to me a few years ago, at an age when I had long stopped expecting it, and asked whether the two of us might read through a book of the Bible together. He picked Luke, and we spent the better part of a year in it, chapter by chapter — not every night, and never the same way twice. Some nights were short and some were long; some wandered off the page entirely; and on some of them he asked a question I did not expect from someone his age.

On one of those nights he asked whether I thought Jesus actually rose from the dead. I told him I did, he asked why, and we spent an hour there. I did not hand him a canned answer. I walked him through what I actually believed — the historical evidence, the alternatives I had weighed, the reasons the resurrection has held for me under every alternative I have ever considered — and he listened, and then he was quiet for a long while. Finally he said, I think I believe it too, Dad. Not a declaration. A small report, at the end of a year of reading together.

That conversation did not happen because I had a program. It happened because we had built, over months, the slow practice of reading and talking; because the shape of my own life had given him reason to think the question was worth bringing to me; and because, when he brought it, I answered him honestly instead of defensively. That is formation. That is the thing I hope this book has been moving you toward, in whatever season you happen to be in with your own children.

From the Valley, Not the Summit

I began writing during a week when one of my sons was struggling — I will not name the son or the nature of it, because his story is his to tell — but the timing was not accidental. I started writing about the formation of children in the faith at the exact moment my own formation of my own child felt most fragile. I was writing about the household catechism while my household felt like it was running the wrong one; I was writing about answering a child's questions while my son was asking ones I did not know how to answer.

That is the honest context of this book. It was not written from a summit of parental achievement; it was written from the valley, from the place where the principles I believe in were being tested against the reality I was living in. And what I found, in the writing and in the living both, was that the principles held — not because they produced immediate results, because they did not, but because they were true. True things hold you even when you cannot feel them holding.

Formation is proximity to a formed life, and that held: even as my son pulled away, he stayed near enough to watch what I did with my own fear. He saw me pray about it rather than rage about it; he saw me stay in the room rather than walk out of it. I do not know what he made of the watching. I know that the watching was formation, whether it felt like it at the time or not.

And then the hardest thing held — that a child's faith is, in the end, between him and God. I had to let go: not of my parenting, not of my prayers, not of my presence, but of the outcome. I had to hand my son to God and say, he is Yours before he is mine, You love him more than I do, and I trust You with him even though I cannot see what You are doing. That surrender is the hardest act a Christian parent ever performs, and it is also the final act of formation — because before it forms the child, it forms the parent.

I am still in the story. I do not know how it ends for my sons. They are still being formed. They are still choosing. And I am still in the room.

The Soil: Planting Faith and Trusting the Harvest

I think about my mother. She raised me without a father, without faith, and without a model for any of the things I have written about in these pages. She did not form me in the Christian faith, because she did not have it to give — but she formed me in something the faith would later fill. She formed me in honesty: she told me the truth about our situation and refused to pretend we were fine when we were not. She worked two jobs, and cried in the kitchen when she thought I was asleep, and got up the next morning and did it again. What I absorbed from her was not theology. It was the refusal to quit.

When I came to faith in college, everything she had formed in me found its home. The honesty became confession; the endurance became perseverance; the refusal to quit became faithfulness. She did not give me the faith. But she gave me the soil — and the soil received the seed, and the seed grew.

That is what you are doing. You are preparing soil: pulling weeds, watering ground, working with the tools you have in the time you are given. The seed is not yours to manufacture, because the seed belongs to God; your work is only to make the ground as ready as you can. And the time is shorter than you think. Your children will be grown before you are ready for it. Use the years. Not perfectly. Faithfully.

This is the long obedience of raising believers. It is not dramatic, and it does not make for good social media; it is slow and hidden and mostly unnoticed, done in kitchens and cars and at bedsides, over decades, by parents who decided quietly that they would rather be faithful than impressive. It is the work of being near your children, for a long time, with a life actually turned toward God — in a house where grace lands the first word and truth is never softened — trusting that God will meet them in ways you could never orchestrate.

I will be a Father to the fatherless, and a husband to the widow. He sets the lonely in families.

Psalm 68:5-6

So go. Into the kitchen. Into the car. Into the bedroom where the child is waiting for the blessing. Go with the confidence that the small thing you do tonight is the thing God will use — not because it is impressive, but because it is faithful. And faithfulness, in the economy of God, is the only currency that never loses its value. Go. Raise them.
