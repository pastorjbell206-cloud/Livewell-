CHAPTER TWO

The Home Is the Primary Catechism

How the Christian home disciples children every day — the rhythms, words, and habits that teach a child who God is before any lesson does.

There is an old word Christian parents should recover, and the word is catechism. Most Protestants have forgotten it, and the ones who keep it tend to treat it as a document, a book of questions and answers a child learns by repetition. That is one meaning, and it is not the deepest one. The deepest meaning is the shaping of a person, through the rhythms of teaching and life together, into what they believe at the level below argument. In that older and heavier sense, every home is a catechism. The only question is which one.

Your children are being catechized from the day they are born, and if they live in your home, you are the primary catechist, not the only one, but the first. The culture catechizes them, their friends catechize them, the algorithms they scroll through catechize them with a great and frictionless efficiency; but before any of those, for the critical early years, the catechist is the household. Your house teaches your children something every single day about what reality is, what it costs, what it rewards, and the only question worth asking is whether what it teaches them is true.

Let me be concrete, because this is the chapter where we start naming specifics. A household's catechism runs on four channels, all the time, whether you intend them or not: what the family praises, what the family worries about, what the family repeats, and what the family rests in. None of them announce themselves. All of them are teaching.

The Four Channels Your Home Disciples Through

Children notice what their parents praise, and you can watch it happen; watch a child's eyes when a parent lights up, because in that moment the child is learning what is worth lighting up about. If the parent lights up about money, the child learns that money is the thing; if the parent lights up about academic performance, the child learns that performance is the thing; but if the parent lights up when the child is kind, when the child is honest, when the child is brave for the sake of someone weaker, the child learns something else entirely. I am not saying you should never celebrate a good grade. I am saying that the pattern of your celebration is teaching, day after day, and that your praise is itself a catechism.

I have watched this run in both directions. I knew a family in one of my earlier churches where the father was a successful businessman, and every achievement the children brought home was measured against his standard of excellence, which was high; the praise flowed when they finished at the top of the class, won the game, got the lead in the play. He was not cruel; he was enthusiastic; he loved his children. And his enthusiasm, sustained over years, catechized them into a theology of performance in which God loved you when you performed and was pleased when you achieved. So when one of those children hit a season of failure in their twenties, their faith collapsed, because the god they had been taught to worship rewarded performance and they had stopped performing. The father never said any of that. His house said it for him.

I knew another family where the mother praised character. When her daughter told the truth about something that would have been easier to hide, the mother stopped what she was doing, knelt to the girl's level, and said, that is the kind of person God is making you into, and I am proud of you. That was not a formula; it was a reflex, and the reflex catechized the child, day after day, in a theology that had nothing to do with output and everything to do with who she was becoming. That girl is in her late twenties now, still walking with Jesus, and she will tell you that her mother's voice is still the loudest in the room when she has to make a hard decision.

Children also read what their parents worry about, and they are more attuned to parental anxiety than parents ever realize. A child knows when you are stressed about the mortgage even if you have never said the word in front of them; a child knows when you are anxious about a social situation, when you fear a particular person's opinion, when you are quietly obsessed with how your life looks to others. And children assume, rightly, that the things their parents worry about are the things worth worrying about, so that the content of the worry becomes the content of the child's imagined threat. If the home worries about appearance, children grow up afraid of being seen wrong; if the home worries about status, children grow up afraid of being passed over; but if the home quietly entrusts these things to God, children learn there is a Father who holds what the world told them to grip.

Here is what worry actually catechizes: it catechizes the nature of God. A worried household teaches, at the catechism level and below argument, that God is not trustworthy, that the world is finally in the hands of forces God cannot control, that the parent must grip tighter because letting go is too dangerous. A child raised in that home may learn all the right verses about trusting God, and they will believe the worry, because the worry was the catechism that ran all day and the verses were the correction that ran for fifteen minutes at bedtime.

This does not mean you must never worry. It means you must bring the worry somewhere your children can see. When you are afraid and you take that fear to God out loud, in the kitchen, where your child can hear you say, Lord, I am anxious about this, and I am bringing it to You because I cannot carry it; you are catechizing your child in the nature of God, teaching them below argument that there is a Father who receives fear and does something with it. That catechism will run longer and louder than the anxiety, if you practice it.

Children remember what the family repeats, not what it says once, but what it says a thousand times: the prayer over the food that stumbles out the same way every evening, the verse the mother quotes when something breaks, the phrase the father uses when he apologizes, the song that plays in the car, the way Sunday morning starts. These are the liturgies of a household, and they do their work below conscious memory. My own sons will repeat phrases I have said for decades without knowing they are mine; the words will come out of their mouths under pressure, and they will not remember where they heard them. That is catechism, not content delivery, but repetition that has become reflex.

James K. A. Smith calls these liturgies, and the word is exactly right, because a liturgy is a repeated practice that forms desire, and the church has understood this for two thousand years. When you pray the Lord's Prayer weekly, you are not delivering information; you are forming a people whose desires are being shaped by the prayer even when they are not paying full attention. The same thing happens in a home: when you speak a blessing over your children before bed, you are forming a child whose sense of identity is shaped by the blessing even when they are half asleep; when you sing the same hymn in the car on the way to church, you are forming their emotional association with worship before they understand what worship is.

The danger of repetition is that it goes empty: the grace before meals that nobody means, the bedtime prayer recited with the parent's foot already out the door. Children know when the repetition has gone hollow, and they are not fooled by it; they are catechized by it, and what they learn from hollow repetition is that the words do not matter, that the rituals are performances, that the adults do not believe what they are saying. The correction is not to stop repeating. It is to mean what you repeat. If the blessing before the meal has gone rote, stop one night and say it fresh: look your child in the eye and thank God, specifically, for the food, for the family, for the particular mercy that was visible that afternoon, and let the child see that the words connect to something real. That, too, is catechism. The words are the same; the life behind them makes them empty or full.

And children learn what is trustworthy by watching what their parents rest in. When the parent is exhausted, what does the parent turn to; when the parent is afraid, what does the parent reach for; when the parent needs comfort, where does the parent go. Children are not fooled by stated values, so they watch the parent's rest, because rest reveals the true god of a household. If the parent rests in scrolling, in wine, in the next purchase, in the praise of a certain person, the child receives a theology of rest; if the parent rests in prayer, in the presence of God, in Scripture, in the company of people who genuinely love them, the child receives a different one.

A household's resting point tells you more about its actual theology than its stated theology ever will.

I want to press here, because this is the most revealing of the four. The parent who collapses into a screen every evening is not bad; they are tired. But the child is watching where the tired parent goes, and the child is learning where to go when they are tired. The parent who collapses into prayer (even briefly, even imperfectly, even while exhausted) is teaching the child where the weight goes. I have told my sons, more than once, I am too tired tonight to do much, but I am going to take this to God before I sleep. That is not impressive; it is honest. And honesty at the point of exhaustion is catechism.

So a household catechism is built from four materials (what you praise, what you worry about, what you repeat, what you rest in) and the child is writing a book inside them whose chapters are constructed from those four. The book the child finishes is the book they will carry into adulthood.

The Two Catechisms Forming Your Child

Most Christian parents have tried to add content to the catechism without ever touching the four channels. They sit their children down for devotions at seven in the evening while the household runs all day on anxiety, appearance, and performance, and the child receives a mixed signal: Mom praises grades and mentions Jesus, Dad worries about work and says God is in control, the family repeats platitudes the family does not live and rests in the phone, and then at seven everyone opens a Bible together for fifteen minutes. The children receive the data, but the catechism has already been written.

This is why so many adults leave the faith of their childhood and say they do not know why. They know why. They were catechized by a household running two catechisms at once (one explicit, one implicit; the explicit one said God is central, the implicit one said God is peripheral) and because both could not be true, when the child grew up they had to pick which one had been real. They usually picked the implicit one, because implicit lessons feel more honest than explicit ones. That is not rebellion. It is integrity. They believed what the household actually taught.

I have seen this in my own home. There was a period, several years ago, when Susanna and I were running hard: ministry was demanding, the boys were at ages where everything required logistics, and we were physically present and spiritually depleted. We kept doing the family devotional, we kept saying the prayers, but the boys could tell something had changed, not in the devotional, in us. We were more irritable, more distracted, quicker to snap and slower to repair, and the implicit catechism was quietly drowning the explicit one. Our oldest, who has a gift for directness I suspect he got from me, said one night at dinner: you guys seem different lately, not bad, tired. That was the kindest version of what he could have said. What he meant was: the thing you say is real does not seem to be reaching you right now, and I can tell.

That was a turning point. The next week Susanna and I made a decision that changed more in our home than any curriculum ever could; we would tend our own souls first, before the family devotional, before the children's ministry, before the church calendar, getting our own lives with God back into a sustainable, honest place. We started praying together again, as a couple, not for the family but for ourselves; we started reading Scripture again, not to prepare for anything but to be near God. And as that happened, the four channels began to shift, not because we added a program, but because the adults in the house changed what they were resting in.

Reading Your Own House: What It Teaches About God

A parent can rewrite the household catechism, slowly, not by adding a program but by paying attention to the four channels and beginning to align them with the thing the household says it believes. The first work is honesty about what the house is currently teaching, and that work startles you. When I sat down and watched my own four channels for a week, I found that I praised achievement far more than character; that I worried aloud about money and time more than I trusted aloud in God's provision; that the phrases I repeated most were hurry up and not now and we will talk about it later; and that when I was spent at the end of the day, I reached for the screen before I reached for anything else. None of that was what I would have said my household catechism was. It was what it was.

Susanna and I sat with that for a while, and we did not overhaul the household overnight; we chose one channel at a time. We started with praise, deciding that every day, at least once, we would name character in front of the boys rather than performance, when one of them was kind, when one of them was honest, when one of them was brave in a moment where silence would have been easier. That one shift, sustained over months, changed the feel of the house. Not a new program. A channel that had been running on autopilot, turned on purpose.

Worry was harder, because worry is reflexive; you do not decide to worry aloud, it escapes. But when one of us caught ourselves worrying in front of the boys, we learned to follow it with a turn: I am worried about this, and I am bringing it to God because He is the one who holds what I cannot. Not a formula; a discipline. The boys heard it enough that it became part of the household's vocabulary, and one of my sons, at age ten, said to his younger brother during a thunderstorm: it is okay to be scared, Dad says you bring the scared thing to God. He had absorbed the catechism.

Repetition was the easiest to change, because it was the most concrete, so we chose phrases we wanted to become load-bearing: the blessing over the boys before bed, the old words from Numbers:

The Lord bless you and keep you.

Numbers 6:24

There was a question at dinner, where did you see God today (not every night, but often enough that they expected it) and a sentence Susanna began saying to each boy in the morning, God has a plan for you today. These repetitions, sustained over years, are embedded in my sons now, and they will carry those phrases into their own households one day whether they know it or not. That is catechism.

Rest is the channel we are still working on, and it is the hardest, because it touches the deepest part of us. Where do I go when I am depleted, if the honest answer is the phone, the television, or the pantry, then that is what my children are learning is the resting place. Changing that answer requires me to change myself, and that is the deepest work of all.

The Catechism That Grows

The catechism of a household is not static; it changes when the family hits a crisis, when a parent loses a job, when someone gets sick, when a child enters a new stage and the dynamics shift. The catechism you ran when your children were toddlers is not the one you run when they are twelve, and the parent who pays attention can adapt the four channels to the new reality instead of running the old catechism on autopilot into a situation it no longer fits.

I noticed this when our boys hit adolescence, and the practices that had worked when they were small (the bedtime blessing, the dinner question, the Saturday morning Scripture reading) began to feel different. Not wrong. Different. The boys were changing; their capacity for depth was growing while their tolerance for routine was shrinking, so we made changes. The bedtime blessing stayed, because it was non-negotiable in our house, but it got shorter and more personal, one sentence tailored to what that boy was carrying that day. The dinner question moved from where did you see God today to something more probing (what was the hardest thing about today, what are you afraid of right now, what made you angry this week) and those opened doors the simpler question had stopped opening. And the Saturday reading became a conversation: I stopped reading to them and started reading with them, asking what they thought, letting them disagree, letting the text be alive rather than recited.

The four channels are permanent (what you praise, what you worry about, what you repeat, what you rest in, those run for the life of the household), but the content that flows through them must mature. I have seen families lose their teenagers because the parents refused to let it, because the practices that formed the children at six were enforced at sixteen with no modification, and the teenager who needed depth and conversation and room to question was handed the same simple routine they had outgrown years ago. The teenager did not reject the faith. They rejected the version of the faith that was too small for who they were becoming. And the parents, who could not tell the difference, treated the rejection of the small version as a rejection of God.

The House That Implicates You

The reason most parents resist all of this is that it implicates the parent. You cannot change what the household praises without changing what you praise; you cannot change what it worries about without changing what you worry about; you cannot change what it rests in without changing where you go for rest. Every channel reveals the interior life of the adults, and that is the thing most of us do not want revealed.

The house will catechize the child either way; the only question is whether it catechizes them toward Christ or away from Him, and that is not a question of content but a question of alignment, one that points inward before it points outward. I am not writing as a man who has nailed this. I am writing as a man who has been confronted, again and again, with the gap between the catechism my sons say we have and the catechism my weekly life actually teaches them. When the gap narrows, formation follows; when it widens, nothing I say from a pulpit can fix it, and nothing a Sunday school teacher says can fix it either.

The household is the first and most honest seminary a Christian will ever attend, and most of us graduated before we could speak. It is worth asking, slowly, what the curriculum was, and then, with as much honesty as you can bear, what the curriculum is in your own house now.
