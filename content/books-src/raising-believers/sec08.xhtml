CHAPTER SEVEN

Screens, Algorithms, and the Catechesis of Attention

Kids, screens, and faith — how the smartphone disciples a child's attention, and what Christian parents can do about screen time that still works.

You cannot write a book about forming faith in children in 2026 and pretend the old questions are the only ones, because for many hours of most days your child's primary catechist is not you; it is an algorithm trained to hold their attention by any means available, and whatever it is forming in them competes directly with whatever you are trying to form. You should know what you are up against.

A smartphone is not a television, not a radio, not a video game; those are older technologies, and a generation of Christian parents has tried to govern the new one with the old rules (limit screen time, use parental controls, put the computer in a central room) rules that worked, imperfectly, in the old world and are simply insufficient in this one, because the smartphone is a different kind of object altogether. It is not a thing you watch. It is a system that watches you back, studying your child, learning their attention signature, adjusting in real time, and delivering precisely the content that will keep them scrolling longest. It is adversarial to their interior life by design, and it is doing its job.

If that sounds like overstatement, read the public testimony of the engineers who built these systems, many of whom have admitted that they do not let their own children use the platforms they made, and they know what those platforms do, because they built them to do exactly that. These are not neutral tools your child uses for homework and occasionally for fun; they are formation environments with a theology of the human person, and their theology is that the person is a consumer of stimulus whose attention is a resource to be extracted.

That theology stands in direct opposition to the Christian one, which says the person is a soul made for communion with God, whose attention is a gift to be given back to the Creator in worship and to the neighbor in love. The phone's theology says your attention is yours only in the legal sense, that it exists to be harvested, and both cannot shape your child at the same rate, so one will win. If your child receives forty-five minutes of the Christian theology on Sunday morning and six hours a day of the other one, the math is not complicated.

What the Smartphone Is Actually Doing to Your Child

Most parents think the danger is content; they worry about what their child is seeing, and they should. But the deeper danger is structural; it is what the device is doing to their child's capacity for the kind of attention that faith requires.

Jonathan Haidt has documented this at length: the adolescent mind is being reshaped by a platform-based childhood in ways that produce unprecedented anxiety, depression, and attention fragmentation, and these are not incidental side effects but the natural result of training a developing brain on intermittent variable reinforcement, the same mechanism that makes slot machines addictive. The platform delivers a reward, withholds a reward, delivers a larger one, then withholds again until the brain learns to chase, and the chasing itself becomes the settled state of mind. The child is not choosing to be distracted; the child's brain has been trained to be distracted, and now the distraction is the default.

This carries spiritual consequences most Christian parents have not considered, because prayer requires sustained attention, and so does Scripture, and so does listening to a sermon, sitting in silence with God, confessing, repenting: every practice of the Christian life depends on a capacity the smartphone is systematically dismantling in your child. You are trying to form a child in a faith that requires slow, focused, interior attention while the device in their pocket trains their brain in the exact opposite direction, and the device is winning. Not because it is smarter than you. Because it has more hours.

I am not saying no phones, and I am not saying throw out the internet; I am a pastor who communicates daily through these tools, my sons have earned graduated levels of device access over time, and I am no Luddite. What I am saying is narrower and harder to escape: if you do not have a deliberate theology of attention for your household, the platforms have one for you, and they are winning. Name it. Act on it. Or accept what you are accepting.

Why Attention Is a Spiritual Matter

The historic Christian tradition has understood this for centuries, treating the capacity to attend to God, to the neighbor, to the word of Scripture, and to one's own soul as the very substrate of the Christian life. The saints trained that capacity, treating attention as a muscle that strengthens through use and atrophies through neglect, because they knew a scattered mind cannot easily pray, cannot easily love, cannot easily meet God in Scripture. They did not have our devices, but they knew what a disordered soul does when it has no disciplines of attention: it becomes shallow, it becomes frantic, it becomes incapable of the long, slow, quiet attention God requires.

Simone Weil wrote that prayer is attention, and she meant it technically: the capacity to hold one's mind before God, without distraction, without chasing the next stimulus, is the very thing prayer is. A child raised on a device that delivers a new stimulus every few seconds is being trained, all day, in the opposite direction, formed in a rhythm that runs short, shorter, shortest, stimulus after stimulus after stimulus, with no silence, no boredom, no waiting. That is catechesis. The child who cannot tolerate five minutes of silence cannot be still before God; the child who cannot tolerate a slow story cannot read a Gospel; the child whose reward circuits have been trained by infinite scroll cannot easily accept that faith is a long obedience. You are not working against a neutral baseline. You are working against a trained one.

And the device is not only training attention; it is training desire, because the algorithm learns what your child wants and gives them more of it, until over time the child's wanting is shaped by what the algorithm has taught them to want. The child does not want God, because the child has been trained to want stimulus; the child does not want Scripture, because the child has been trained to want content that confirms what they already believe; the child does not want the hard, slow work of growth, because the child has been trained to want immediate satisfaction. You may have worried the platform corrupted your child's morals; more quietly, it has corrupted your child's desires, and desire is the engine of the spiritual life. A child who desires God will grow toward God through difficulty. A child whose desires have been trained elsewhere will not, no matter how many programs you run them through.

You stand between your child and a system built to harvest them. Stand there. It is one of the most pastoral things a parent can do.

Christian Screen-Time Rules That Work, and What They Cost

Make peace with the fact that this will be harder than you wanted, because you will not solve it with a weekend family conversation; you will have to take a stand that costs you, and your child will resent you for a while; you are asking them to do what their peers are not, and peer conformity at age twelve has its own gravity. Take the loss. Take the hostility. It is the cost of what you are trying to do, and here is what I would do in practice, none of it easy.

Delay, and delay hard, because the research on adolescent phone use is not ambiguous, and delaying a personal smartphone into the teenage years is one of the most load-bearing decisions you will make. A dumb phone or a watch for safety is fine; a smartphone with social media and infinite access is not, so hold it back until your child is old enough to have some chance of using it with formed resistance, which is not twelve, not thirteen, and in most cases older than you would like. You will be in a minority, but you are already in a minority by pursuing serious Christian formation, and this is one of the places the minority is right.

Keep devices out of bedrooms (every device, including yours) because the bedroom is a sanctuary, and nothing that wants a piece of your child's attention at two in the morning gets into that room. This rule is among the hardest to keep, and it is the parents who break it: if you want your children to leave their phones downstairs at nine, you will leave yours there too, since your phone is catechizing you at the same hours it is catechizing them. The rule applies all the way up.

Replace before you remove, because taking screens away without offering something that meets the human need the screen was meeting is a setup for failure; a bored child will find a way back, since nature abhors a vacuum. What a child needs is embodied life (physical work outside, time with animals, time with older people and younger ones, projects that take weeks, real books, long walks, hand tools, sports played for love rather than trophies, instruments, chores that matter, failure that is not life-ending, conversation at a table with adults who are not performing) all of which is formation, and all of which is displaced by a scrolling device. A home full of embodied life has less need for screen-time enforcement, because the screen is no longer the most interesting thing in the house.

Model it, because you will not win any of this if your own attention is captured. Your children watch you reach for the phone at the first break in conversation, watch you disappear into it at dinner, watch you fill every quiet moment with input, and they are being catechized by your relationship with your device as much as by their own. So put phones in a basket during dinner, keep yours out of the bedroom, take one day a week of severely limited use, and sit with your own Bible, in paper, at a table, where the children can see you doing it. You cannot hand them a relationship with God while modeling a relationship with your phone that is more intimate than your relationship with God. They will read the real one.

And talk about it openly, because your children are not blind and often know more than you do about what the platforms are doing to them. Come to them in humility (I want us, as a family, to think carefully about how these devices are forming us, and I want your help figuring out what is wise) and you will get a more honest conversation than you expect, because children resent rules imposed from outside but respond to an invitation to think together about a real problem. Let some of the rules come from them. They will keep rules they helped design longer than ones that came down from above.

The costly hope is that attention can be retrained; it is slow and it is difficult, but a child whose attention has been fed for years on the platforms can, with time and discipline and help, recover the capacity for slower, deeper attention. I have seen it happen. It requires the parent to be in the process with the child, it requires the household to change, and it cannot be done to the child; it has to be done with them.

The Phone as a Rival Worship Device

There is one more dimension to this, because it touches worship in a way most parents have not considered. Worship is not a Sunday activity; it is the orientation of a person's deepest attention toward whatever they regard as most worthy, and by that measure the phone is a worship device, training attention, directing desire, forming what the user regards as most worthy of their time, their emotion, their energy. When a child spends four hours a day on a device, they are being formed in worship. The only question is what they are worshiping.

The algorithm does not care about your child's soul; it cares about engagement, measuring what your child watches longest, clicks most, and responds to most strongly, then feeding them more of it, until over time it learns your child better than your child knows themselves (their fears, their insecurities, their triggers) and exploits all of them. Not because the algorithm is evil in a moral sense, but because it is designed to capture attention, and captured attention is the raw material of profit.

When Paul wrote that we should take every thought captive to Christ, he was describing what happens when a person's attention is rightly oriented; the phone does the opposite, taking every thought captive to the feed, and a child whose thoughts are captive to the feed is a child whose worship is being redirected without their knowledge or consent. If they are scrolling social media, they are worshiping the opinion of their peers; if they are watching content about their bodies, they are worshiping appearance; if they are consuming outrage, they are worshiping the feeling of being right; if they are gaming for hours, they are worshiping achievement in a world that rewards faster than the real one. None of these are neutral. All of them are forming what your child considers worthy of their deepest attention, and whatever has your child's deepest attention has your child's worship.

The Christian answer is not to ban all technology, which is neither possible nor wise; the answer is to teach your child to think about attention the way a disciple thinks about worship, where is your attention going, what is it forming in you, is the thing that has your attention worthy of it, is it making you more like Christ or less. Those questions, asked regularly, in a household where the parents are asking them about their own attention, produce a child who can use technology without being used by it. That distinction, between use and mastery, is the one Paul makes:

All things are lawful for me, but I will not be mastered by anything.

1 Corinthians 6:12

When I see my sons on their phones for a long stretch, I have started asking a different question, not how long have you been on that, but what is that doing to you. The first question is about time; the second is about formation, and it invites the child to consider what is happening inside them rather than what they owe me in compliance. They do not always like it, but they have started asking it of themselves, and a child who can ask what is this doing to me is a child learning to steward their own attention: one of the most important skills a young person can develop in this generation, because the forces competing for that attention are fierce and growing, and the only defense against a captured attention is a trained one.

To the Parent Who Already Gave the Phone

I want to close with a word to the parent who has already given the phone over and is now trying to get it back, because undoing a decision is harder than making one, and the parents living inside the regret of premature screen access need more than guilt.

You are not the only one; you are not even in the minority. Most parents I counsel gave their children a device earlier than they now wish, and they did it because the child was the last in the friend group without one, because the school required it, because the child was bored and the parent was tired and the screen was the easiest answer, because they did not know what the device would do to a child's attention, sleep, social development, and spiritual life. Now they know, and the knowing feels like an indictment.

Let me offer something other than guilt: you made a decision with incomplete information. The research on adolescent mental health and smartphone use has moved fast in the last several years, so that what we know now we did not know five years ago, and what little we did know was not being communicated clearly by the people selling the devices. You are not a bad parent for that decision; you are a parent who now has better information and needs a strategy for acting on it. Do not give up. The battle for your child's attention is the battle for your child's soul, and you fight it with patience, with honesty, with your own example, and with the confidence that the God who formed your child's mind is greater than the algorithm trying to capture it.

There is no Christian formation without trained attention, and no trained attention without protection from the forces actively training it the other way, and that protection is the parent's job, because nobody else will do it. The schools will not. The church cannot. The broader culture is the one doing the harm. And when you fall short of this, which you will, go back to the principle we learned two chapters ago: repair. Admit what you are doing, tell your children you are trying to get better at it too, let them see you struggle with it the way they struggle with it, and do not pretend you have it under control if you do not. The honesty will go further than the rules.

The next chapter is about the thing your child watched before they ever watched your phone, the thing they have been watching since the day they were born: your marriage, and what it is teaching them about God, love, and the possibility of covenant. For now, teach them that attention is the currency of the soul. Then teach them, by your own example, to spend it on what is worthy.
