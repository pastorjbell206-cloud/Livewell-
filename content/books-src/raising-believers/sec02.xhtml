CHAPTER ONE

What We Mean When We Say Formation

What spiritual formation actually is — how a child's faith is shaped by years lived near believing adults, not by curriculum or quizzes.

There is a word we throw around in Christian parenting circles, and most of the time we are using it to mean something it does not mean: formation. We have made it a dressed-up synonym for teaching, or for discipline, or for the whole package of Sunday school and family devotions and good habits — when formation is in fact older and stranger than any of that. If we are going to raise children in the faith, we have to recover what it actually is.

Formation is what happens to a human being who spends years near a particular kind of presence; it is slower than teaching and deeper than behavior, and it does not produce outputs on a predictable timeline. You cannot measure it in a catechism quiz. You recognize it later, when you notice that a person thinks a certain way, responds a certain way, carries a certain weight — and you realize they did not get any of that from a single lesson but from a life lived near other lives, over years, in a particular direction.

Most Christian parents are running an education strategy and calling it formation, and the strategy goes like this: get the right content into the child, protect the child from the wrong content, structure enough repetition to make the content stick, and hope the content becomes conviction. That is education. It is not formation. It can be part of formation, but it is never the whole of it, and when it is treated as the whole, it produces children who know the right answers and have no interior connection to the God those answers describe.

Somewhere in the second half of the twentieth century, American evangelicalism made a decisive turn toward information as the primary vehicle of spiritual growth — and there were reasons. The fundamentalist-modernist controversy of the early 1900s had produced a Christianity suspicious of experience, suspicious of mystery, suspicious of anything that could not be printed on a doctrinal statement; the way you knew someone was Christian was that they believed the right propositions, and the way you formed a Christian child was that you got the right propositions into them early enough and often enough that they stuck.

This was not entirely wrong. Doctrine matters, content matters, and a child who grows up with no biblical knowledge is not formed in the faith. But the emphasis on information did something its advocates did not intend: it reduced the Christian life to a set of ideas to be believed rather than a way of being to be inhabited. And once the Christian life is a set of ideas, you can deliver it through programs, because programs are how you deliver ideas — you can age-grade it, put it in a workbook, test for comprehension, measure it. The entire infrastructure of children's ministry in the American church was built on that assumption: give them the right information at the right developmental stage, and formation will follow.

Formation did not follow. Not because the information was wrong. Because information is not formation.

I grew up without a father, and I did not grow up Christian; I came to faith in college, through a roommate who did not lecture me and a visitor to our apartment who answered my actual questions rather than the questions he wished I were asking. What shaped my early faith was not a curriculum but being near people who were believing in a way I had not encountered before — I watched how they handled disappointment, how they treated women, how they talked about money, how they prayed when they thought I was not listening. That was formation. The content came later. The formation came first.

The Catechumenate: How the Early Church Formed Faith

The early church understood this. Before there were catechism books, before there were Sunday school curricula, before there were children's Bibles with cartoon illustrations, there was the catechumenate — the process by which a person was formed into the faith over one to three years. It included instruction, but the instruction was embedded in a life: the catechumen lived among believers, watched them pray, watched them fast, watched them serve the poor, watched them suffer, watched them die well. The formation happened in the watching, and the instruction named what the watching had already been teaching.

When Paul wrote to Timothy, he did not say, I taught you the right content and therefore you are formed. He wrote something else:

You have followed my teaching, my conduct, my aim in life, my faith, my patience, my love, my steadfastness, my persecutions and sufferings.

2 Timothy 3:10-11

That is a formation inventory. Teaching is first on the list, but the list does not stop there — it includes conduct, aim, faith, patience, love, steadfastness, and it includes suffering. Timothy was formed by proximity to a life; the teaching was part of the formation, not the formation itself.

Now consider how most Christian households operate. The parents have the content — the Bibles, the podcasts, the sermons, the small group material — and the children are exposed to it, but the children are not forming near believing parents so much as near parents who attend Christian events, and there is a difference. A parent can be in Christian spaces every week and have no formed interior life, and a child raised near that parent receives the externals without the center — which is precisely the recipe for what we used to call pharisaism and now call church culture.

I have watched this play out for twenty years in pastoral ministry, and the families where the children's faith has gone the distance are not the families that attended the most programs; they are the families where the parents had an actual life with God that was visible in ordinary moments — where the father prayed and the children could hear it, where the mother opened her Bible on a Tuesday morning because she wanted to and not because anyone was watching, where a crisis was met with real faith rather than Christian platitudes and a failure with real confession rather than blame-shifting, where the home had a center that was not anxiety or performance or appearance but Christ, in the mundane operations of a household.

Proximity to a Life: How Children Catch Faith

Formation is not something you do to a child; it is something that happens in proximity — and proximity to what? Proximity to a life that is actually being lived under the lordship of Christ. Your kitchen is a formation space, and so is your car on the way to school, and so is the way you treat your spouse at seven fifteen in the morning, and so is the way you respond to the email from your boss that you did not want to receive. Your child is reading all of it. The child reads what you actually are, not what you say you are, because the child has been watching you long enough to know the difference.

That is why formation is terrifying: you cannot fake it over the timeline a child has with you. You can fake it on Sunday morning in front of people who see you two hours a week, but you cannot fake it in front of a seven-year-old who has watched you for two thousand five hundred days. The child knows. The child knows whether prayer in your home is a ritual that adults perform for the child's benefit or whether the adults in this house actually pray; whether the Bible is a reference book that gets pulled out for arguments or a book the parent is being shaped by; whether church is performance for you or whether it is where you go because something in you is hungry for God.

A parent whose faith is small but honest does more for formation than a parent whose faith is extensive but performative.

This does not mean your interior life has to be impressive; it means it has to be real. The child is not impressed by theological vocabulary — the child is watching for alignment. When the words the parent says about God line up with the way the parent is actually living, something forms; when they do not line up, something also forms, and it is the thing we are all grieving in the generation leaving the church right now. They did not leave because the teaching was too demanding. They left because the teaching and the life did not match, and they noticed.

Formation is happening at all times, in all directions; you are not choosing whether to form your children, you are choosing in which direction. A home that runs on anxiety is forming anxious children, a home that runs on performance is forming performers, a home that runs on conflict avoidance is forming people who will not name truth when it costs them, and a home that runs on consumption is forming consumers — and none of those parents intended any of it. They did not set out to teach anxiety or performance or avoidance; they did not have to, because the household was the curriculum, and the children absorbed it the way they absorbed language, without effort, without choice, by immersion.

The Formation of the Parent Comes First

This is why the formation of a child begins with the formation of the parent — and I will say it a dozen times in this book because it cannot be said too often. The parent's own walk with Christ is not a private devotional matter; it is the soil in which the child's faith will grow or will not. A parent being sanctified, slowly and honestly and visibly, creates a different household than a parent performing sanctification, and the child does not know the word sanctification — the child only knows whether the parent is changing.

Here is the part I have to say because I need to hear it myself: you cannot curriculum your way out of your own lack of life with God. You can buy better books, enroll in better schools, join better churches, and none of it touches the problem if the problem is that your own walk with Jesus has flattened out. Children form around the center of a parent's actual life — if the center is work, they will form around work; if the center is appearance, they will form around appearance; if the center is Christ, they will form around Christ. The center is not what you say it is. It is what your week actually shows.

I have to name a particular failure I have seen in pastors' homes, including my own. Pastors are professionally Christian — their work is Christ, their calendar is church, their social network is believers — and it is easy for a pastor's child to conclude that the faith is a vocation rather than a life. The child watches the father prepare sermons, lead Bible studies, counsel the hurting, pray over the sick, and the child asks, perhaps not aloud, does Dad believe this, or is this what Dad does for a living? The distinction is everything, which is why I have had to work, deliberately, to let my sons see me as a believer and not only as a professional believer. They need to see me pray when I am not preparing to lead a meeting, to read Scripture when I am not preparing a sermon, to repent when the only person I have wronged is their mother. They need to see a man who follows Jesus at home, on a Tuesday, when the collar is off and the office is closed.

The hope in this chapter is small and heavy: it is that formation in your children is doable — not easy, not quick, not guaranteed, but doable, because the lever is not external to you. It is the thing you already have direct access to, which is your own life before God. You cannot control what your children will choose when they are grown; you can only be the kind of person near whom formation in a Christlike direction is more likely than not — honest instead of performative, hungry instead of settled, present instead of distracted, slow to anger when the children press on the places your flesh wants to hide. That is what formation is. Proximity to a formed life. Over years. In ordinary rooms. With a thousand small honesties along the way.

What a Thousand Ordinary Mornings Do

Because the concept can feel abstract, let me describe two mornings in two different homes — both Christian, both with children under ten.

In the first home, the alarm goes off and the household immediately enters management mode: the parent is on the phone before their feet touch the floor, breakfast is functional, and the conversation, such as it is, is about logistics — who needs to be where, what needs to be signed. The parent is tense because they are late, and the children absorb the tension; nobody prays, nobody mentions God, and the Bible on the counter is under a stack of mail. The parent drops the children at school with a hurried goodbye and drives to work listening to a podcast about productivity. The morning has catechized the children in a theology of urgency — the world is demanding, there is not enough time, the most important thing is efficiency, and God is not in the morning but somewhere else, for later.

In the second home, the household wakes up at roughly the same speed. The parent is tired, the morning is not serene, and there is still cereal to pour and permission slips to find — but somewhere in the first fifteen minutes the parent pauses, not for long, for ninety seconds, and says, out loud, at the table: Lord, thank you for this day. Be with these boys today. Help me be patient. The children hear it. They do not respond, because they are eating cereal, but they hear it. The parent is not performing a devotional; the parent is doing what the parent does every morning, taking thirty seconds to orient the household toward God before the logistics take over. On the drive to school, the parent asks the child about something the child mentioned the night before — not school, something personal — and the child, surprised at being remembered, opens up for two minutes while the parent listens. The morning has catechized the children in a different theology: God is in the morning, the parent is paying attention, my interior life matters to the adult.

Neither morning is dramatic, and neither will be remembered by the child as a specific event — but multiply each one by a thousand, and you have two entirely different formation environments. The first produces a child who sees God as an addition to an already full life; the second produces a child who sees God as the orienting reality of the household. Formation is what a thousand mornings do. It is not what one sermon says. It is the accumulated weight of a life lived in a particular direction, and the child, who is in the room for all of it, absorbs the direction even when they are not paying attention.

Parents tell me they do not have time for formation, that life is too busy, that the schedule does not allow for it — and I have felt it myself, many times. But the objection misunderstands what formation is. Formation is not an additional activity you add to the schedule; it is the character of the activities already in it. It is not thirty minutes of devotions bolted onto an otherwise secular day, but the way you are present in the day you already have. You are already forming your children every hour they are with you. The only question is in which direction.

I am not saying that teaching does not matter — it does, content matters, Scripture matters, doctrine matters. What I am saying is that teaching without formation produces a child who can defend a position they do not actually hold, and formation without teaching produces a child with a warm sense of spirituality and no structure to carry them when the world presses hard. The two belong together. But if I had to choose which comes first, I would say formation, because you can add teaching to a formed child later — you cannot add formation to a person who has been content-fed all their life without it. That person has to start over as an adult, and most of them do not.

A child raised on formation without content is like a tree that grows strong but does not know its own species: it needs instruction, it needs to learn the name and shape of the thing it already is, and when that instruction comes it confirms what the child already knew in their bones. A child raised on content without formation is like a blueprint without a house — the plans are precise, the specs are correct, but nobody built anything; the child has all the information and no structure to live in, and when the wind comes, the blueprint shelters no one. Build the house. Let the blueprint serve the house. Let the formation come first, and let the content fill the space the formation has opened.

The Comparison Trap Every Christian Parent Knows

There is one thing I see in nearly every conversation I have with Christian parents about formation, and it is the comparison trap. You look at another family and see what appears to be a seamlessly formed household — the children are polite, they quote Scripture, they lead worship at youth group, the parents seem calm, the home seems ordered — and then you look at your own household, where the morning was chaos and the evening ended in tears and the Bible reading lasted four minutes before someone spilled milk on the table, and you think, we are failing.

You are not failing. You are comparing your interior to their exterior, your Tuesday morning to their Sunday morning, your actual life to the curated version of theirs you are permitted to see — and the comparison is poisoning your ability to see what God is actually doing in your own home.

I have been inside both kinds of homes, the ones that look formed from the outside and the ones that feel chaotic from the inside, and from twenty years of pastoral observation I can tell you the correlation between external polish and internal formation is weaker than you think. Some of the most polished Christian families I have known produced children who walked away from the faith the moment they left home, because the formation was cosmetic — it was behavior management dressed in spiritual language. And some of the most chaotic, struggling, imperfect households I have known produced children whose faith is as deep as bedrock, because the formation was real: it was messy and inconsistent and often interrupted by spilled milk, but the center held, and the center was not the parents' competence — it was Christ, actually present, in the middle of the mess.

Stop comparing your home to the home that looks better from the outside, and start asking the questions that matter. Is Christ the center of this household, not in theory but in practice? Do my children see me turn to God when I am afraid? Do my children hear me pray when nothing is wrong? Do my children know that they are loved with a love that is not contingent on their performance? Those are the formation questions, and you can answer them honestly without looking at anyone else's home.

There is something else that blocks formation before it starts, and it is fear — specifically, the fear that you will do it wrong, that you will say the wrong thing about God, mishandle a theological question, that your own doubts disqualify you from forming your children's faith. That fear is understandable. It is also paralyzing, and it is based on a misunderstanding of what formation requires: formation does not require theological expertise, it requires theological honesty. Your child does not need a parent who has every answer; your child needs a parent who is honest about what they know, honest about what they do not know, and honest about where they go when the questions outrun the answers. A parent who says, I do not know, but let us find out together, is forming a child who believes that God is big enough for hard questions; a parent who pretends to know everything is forming a child who will eventually discover the pretense and wonder what else was fake.

When my second son was eight, he asked me if God could make a rock so big that He could not lift it; he had heard the question somewhere and it fascinated him. I could have given him the standard philosophical answer about the logical coherence of omnipotence, and I did not. I said, that is a question philosophers have asked for a long time, and the short answer is that the question itself is built wrong, like asking if God can make a square circle — but here is what I want you to know: the fact that you are asking hard questions about God means your mind is working the way God designed it to work, so keep asking, and I will always try to answer, and when I cannot, we will look for the answer together. He does not remember that conversation. I remember it, because it was one of the moments when I chose honesty over performance — I let him see that his father thinks about hard things and does not pretend they are easy. That was formation. Not the content of the answer. The posture of the answerer.

James K.A. Smith has spent a career arguing that we are not first thinking things but desiring, loving, liturgical animals, formed less by what we are taught than by what we are repeatedly made to love — and he is right, and the home is where the first liturgies are written. Your home is a formation space; it became one the day your child entered it, and the only question is what it is forming, with the answer to that question beginning in you, the parent, and the God you are actually walking with when no one is watching. Let us go into the rooms where that walking happens. The catechism room is first.
