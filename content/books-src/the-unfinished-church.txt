TITLE: The Unfinished Church
SUBTITLE: Calling, Vision, and the Future God Is Building
PILLAR: After Christendom
BLURB: The church is not a monument to defend or a method to chase. It is a building still rising, founded on a promise older than any of us and aimed at a city we have not yet seen. This book is about what it means to belong to something God is building and we are not finishing.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: A Building Still Rising
SUMMARY: The church is unfinished by design, not by failure, and the promise that it will be built belongs to Christ, not to us. To see it rightly we have to stop measuring it against either its imagined past or its anxious future.
VERDICT: He said he would build it. He did not say he was done.
REFLECT: Where in your life do you treat the church as a thing to preserve rather than a thing being built? | What would change if you believed the future of the church were a promise rather than a problem? | What unfinished work has God given you that you keep trying to finish on your own?
BODY:
There is a sentence in Matthew that we have domesticated almost beyond recognition. Jesus, at Caesarea Philippi, in the shadow of a rock face carved with the niches of dead gods, says to a fisherman who has just confessed him: "I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18). We quote it on cornerstones. We engrave it over doors. We have made it sound like a guarantee of permanence, as if the point were that the building would stand forever.

But that is not what the sentence says. It says he is building. Present tense reaching into future. A thing underway.

Let me put you in the place where Jesus said it, because the location is half the meaning. Caesarea Philippi sat at the northern edge of the country, at the foot of Mount Hermon, around a spring that fed the Jordan. There was a cliff there, and in that cliff people had carved niches and set the idols of a dozen gods, and at the base of it was a deep cave the locals called the gate of the underworld, the mouth of Hades, the place where the dead were thought to pass down. Pan was worshiped there; Caesar was worshiped there. It was a tourist trap of dead religions and living empire, the most pagan square mile a Jewish rabbi could have chosen to stand in. And it is there, with his back practically to the gate of hell itself, that Jesus turns to a fisherman and says he is going to build something the gate cannot stop. He picked the spot. He always picks the spot.

I have spent fifteen years inside the institution of the church, and I have watched pastors and members alike treat it as a finished structure that needs guarding. We protect the carpet. We defend the order of service. We talk about the church the way a family talks about a house their grandparents built — with reverence, with grief, with the quiet assumption that the best work was done before we arrived and our task is mostly maintenance. We are curators. We dust the relics. We keep the lights on.

I sat in a deacons' meeting once, early on, where we spent forty minutes on the color of the new carpet in the foyer and four minutes on a family in the church whose marriage was coming apart in front of everyone. I am not telling that story to shame anybody. I am telling it because I was in the room, and I let the agenda go that way, and I went home and could not sleep. Forty minutes on carpet. Four on a marriage. That is what it looks like when you have quietly decided the building is finished and your job is upkeep. You start to care more about the surfaces than the stones.

That is not what we were handed.

The earliest image the New Testament gives us for the church is not a fortress and not a museum. It is a construction site. Peter — the same Peter who heard the promise at Caesarea Philippi — writes years later that we are "living stones," being "built up a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5). The verb is passive and ongoing. We are being built. We do not lay ourselves. Paul says the same thing to the Ephesians: the household of God is "built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ himself being the chief corner stone," and in him "the building fitly framed together groweth unto an holy temple in the Lord" (Ephesians 2:20-21). Groweth. A temple that is still going up. Paul could have reached for a finished image — he knew the great temple in Jerusalem, all polished stone and gold — and instead he reached for a verb that means it is not done yet.

If you have ever lived near a building under construction you know the feeling of it. Scaffolding everywhere. Mud where the garden will be. Wires hanging out of walls that will one day hold paintings. There is a half-built house two streets over from mine, and for the better part of a year it was the ugliest thing on the block — a skeleton of studs, blue tarps snapping in the wind, a portable toilet by the curb, a dumpster full of the offcuts of somebody's future kitchen. My youngest asked me once why anybody would want to live there. He could not see the house. He could only see the mess. And I told him the mess was the house. The mess was the house, caught in the middle of becoming the house. A construction site is not a beautiful thing to look at, and anyone who walks past and judges the architecture by the present state of the rebar has misunderstood what they are seeing.

I think most of our despair about the church comes from forgetting this. We look at the divisions, the scandals, the empty pews, the embarrassing posts, the slow leak of the young out the back door, and we conclude the project is failing. And it is true that there is real failure in the church. I am not going to pretend otherwise; I have contributed to it. I have preached sermons I should have repented of instead of preaching. I have protected my own reputation when I should have protected a wounded person. There is real rot in the church, and pretending there is not is its own kind of lie. But there is a difference between a building that is collapsing and a building that is unfinished, and to the untrained eye they can look the same. Both have exposed beams. Both have gaps where walls should be. A man who has never built anything cannot tell the difference between a demolition and a construction; he just sees rubble and assumes the worst. It takes a builder to look at the same site and say, no, this is going up, not coming down.

The question that decides everything is this: whose project is it?

If the church is our project, then its future depends on our competence, our strategy, our ability to read the culture and adjust. And if that is true, then we have every reason to be afraid, because the evidence of the last century is not encouraging. We have tried program after program. We have chased relevance and chased rigor and chased the next conference promising the next method, and the building has not risen the way the brochures said it would. If it is our project, we should be terrified. I have met pastors who are terrified. I have been one of them. You can see it on a man at a conference, working the hallways, collecting techniques like a drowning man collecting driftwood, because somewhere underneath the networking he believes the whole thing rests on him and he knows he is not enough to hold it up. He is right that he is not enough. He is wrong about whose job it is.

Now, somebody is going to push back here, and the push is fair, so let me put it on the table. If it is all God's project, the objection runs, then why does it matter what I do? Why plan, why work, why grieve the decline at all? If he is going to build it regardless, I might as well go fishing. I will answer that objection more than once in this book, because it never fully goes away, but the short answer is this: the same God who promised to build the church chose to build it through laborers, and a laborer who decides his labor does not matter has misread both his smallness and his dignity. You are not the architect. But you are not a spectator either. The mason who lays a wall does not design the cathedral and does not guarantee its completion, and yet the wall does not lay itself, and the cathedral is genuinely made of his actual work. Sovereignty does not cancel labor. It frees it from terror.

Because it is not our project. "I will build my church." The pronoun is the whole sermon. He is the builder. We are the stones, and the laborers, and somehow at the same time the material being shaped by the work. Augustine, writing as the Roman world fell apart around him, made this the spine of his great book The City of God. He watched Rome sacked in the year 410 — the first time the eternal city had fallen to an enemy in eight hundred years, an event so traumatic that pagans blamed it on the Christians for angering the old gods — and Augustine refused to grieve it as the end of anything that finally mattered, because the city he belonged to was not built by Roman hands and would not fall with Roman walls. There is a city, he said, being built across all of history, and its architect is God, and its completion is certain because its builder does not abandon his projects. Augustine wrote that book over more than a decade, finishing it around 426, while the lights of his civilization went out one by one. He died in the year 430 with the Vandals literally besieging his own city of Hippo, the enemy at the wall. And one of the last things on his desk was a defense of the unfinished, unfailing city of God. The man wrote about a building that could not fall while the building he lived in fell around him. That is not denial. That is sight.

That is the posture I want for us. Not the curator's reverence for a finished past. Not the strategist's anxiety about an uncertain future. Something older and steadier. The bricklayer's confidence that the architect knows what the building will be, even on the day when all you can see is mud and scaffolding and your own small course of bricks.

There is a freedom in this that I did not expect when I first understood it. When I believed the church was mine to finish, every problem was a verdict on me. Every decline was my failure. Every conflict was a wall I had to hold up with my own back, and I was exhausted, and I was bitter, and I was the kind of pastor who controls everything because he is convinced that if he lets go the whole thing falls. I would lie awake doing math on the offering. I would read another church's growth numbers and feel something close to despair, which is a strange thing to feel about your brothers. I have repented of that more than once. I will probably repent of it again before this book is done. The man who thinks he is holding up the temple has confused himself with the cornerstone, and there is only one cornerstone, and it is not me.

It is not you, either. That is meant as relief, not insult. I know how it can land as insult — you have given your life to this, your evenings and your marriage's margins and your kids' patience, and now somebody tells you it does not finally rest on you. But sit with it a second longer and feel which way it falls. The weight you have been carrying was never yours to carry. You picked it up because you love the thing, and love makes you want to be responsible for it, and there is something good in that. But there is a love that crushes the lover because it claims a responsibility God never assigned. Lay it down. The building is his.

So this is a book about hope, but I want to be careful about what kind of hope. There are two cheap versions on offer, and both will let you down. One says the church's best days are behind it and our job is to defend the ruins of a Christian past — a hope that is really nostalgia, a hope facing backward. The other says the church's best days are ahead if only we find the right strategy, the right brand, the right technology — a hope that is really pragmatism, a hope that depends on us. Two whole chapters of this book are spent dismantling those two, one each, because they are the two ditches every thinking Christian falls into, and I have spent time in both. I want to refuse both. The hope I am after faces forward and depends on God. It is the hope of a building still rising under the hands of a builder who has never once failed to finish what he started.

The church is unfinished. That is not the bad news. That is the gospel of an ongoing God who said, at the foot of a rock full of dead gods, with the gate of hell at his back, that he would build something the grave itself could not stop. He is still building it. He is building it through people like you, who feel like rubble more days than stone. He has not changed his mind. He has not walked off the site. The scaffolding is up because the work is going on, and the work is going on because the builder is faithful, and the builder is faithful because he is who he is and not because we have given him reason.

He said he would build it. He did not say he was done.

TITLE: Between the Garden and the City
SUMMARY: Scripture begins in a garden and ends in a city, and the church lives in the long middle, called not merely to recover Eden but to participate in something Eden never was. Understanding our place on this arc reorders both our nostalgia and our ambition.
VERDICT: We are not going back to the garden. We are going on to the city.
REFLECT: Do you imagine the goal of faith as a return to innocence or as arrival at something new? | What does it mean that the Bible ends in a city rather than a garden? | How does knowing where the story is going change how you live in the middle of it?
BODY:
The Bible opens in a garden and closes in a city, and almost nobody notices the distance between those two facts.

We are sentimental about the garden. We sing about it. We imagine that the whole arc of redemption is a long road back to Eden, a recovery of what was lost, a return to the simple innocence of the beginning before everything went wrong. And there is truth buried in that instinct. Something was lost. Something does need recovering. But if you read the last two chapters of Revelation carefully, you will notice that God does not put humanity back in the garden. He does not run the film in reverse. There is no scene where the flaming sword is sheathed and Adam and Eve walk back through the gate of Eden to take up where they left off. The end of the story is not Genesis 2 restored. It is something Genesis 2 never was.

John sees "the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven" (Revelation 21:2). A city. With walls and gates and streets, with the nations walking by its light, with the kings of the earth bringing their glory into it (Revelation 21:24). The garden had two people in it. The city has multitudes. The garden was raw potential, unworked, innocent. The city is cultivated, gathered, fulfilled. And here is the thing I want you to see and not skip past, because it is easy to skip past: there is a river there, and the tree of life is there (Revelation 22:1-2). The very tree from the garden, the one guarded by the angel with the sword, shows up again in the middle of the city. The garden is not abandoned. It is taken up into something larger and set in the middle of a civilization. The end contains the beginning and exceeds it. That is the shape of the whole Bible, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

Think about what that means. God's original command to the first humans was not "stay here and keep things as they are." It was to be fruitful, to fill, to work the ground, to make something of the raw world he had given them (Genesis 1:28, Genesis 2:15). The garden was a starting point, not the destination. Even before the fall, even before anything went wrong, the direction of the human calling was forward, toward cultivation, toward building, toward a fullness the garden only contained in seed. The fall did not change the direction. It corrupted the work and added the long detour of redemption. But the arrow was always pointing from garden to city, from raw to cultivated, from beginning to fullness. We did not lose a destination in Eden. We were given one.

This matters enormously for how we think about the church, because the church lives in the middle. We are after the garden and before the city. We are the people of the long between. And a people who do not know where they are on a map will make every decision wrong. If you think you are supposed to be heading back, you will read every change as loss. If you think you have already arrived, you will defend the present as if it were final. Only the people who know they are in the middle — past the garden, not yet at the city — can hold the present rightly, as a stretch of road, neither home nor exile, but the way.

N.T. Wright has spent much of his career trying to wake the church up to this, especially in his book Surprised by Hope, where he argues that the Christian future is not the disembodied escape we have so often preached but the renewal of all things, heaven and earth joined, a new creation that fulfills creation rather than discarding it. He points out, rightly, that our hymns and our funerals are full of leaving — going up, flying away, the soul departing for a better shore, this world is not my home I'm just passing through — when the actual hope of the New Testament is God coming down, the city descending, resurrection, embodiment, a world made right. We have been singing the wrong direction. I have sung the wrong direction at gravesides, more times than I can count, with the family weeping and the casket lowering and the song promising that the dead had flown away to a better place far from here, when the truth I should have been preaching is that the dead will be raised, here, in a body, in a world made new. The hope is not evacuation. It is restoration that surpasses the original.

I find this clarifying for the church's vocation, because it means we are not maintenance workers on a decaying world, waiting to be rescued off it before it sinks. We are early laborers on a project God intends to complete. The garden was given. The city is coming. And between them stretches all of human history, including these particular years that we have been given to live in, the 2020s and whatever comes after, and the church is the community that knows where the story is going and lives now in light of where it ends.

I think a great deal of the church's confusion in this generation comes from losing our place on the arc. When we forget the city ahead, we cling to a garden behind. We turn the church into a project of recovery — recover the 1950s, recover Christendom, recover the culture we used to have, recover the town where everything closed on Sunday. And so we become reactionary, grieving, suspicious, our energy spent on holding ground rather than building forward. We become the people who, told to march toward a city we have never seen, keep turning around to weep over a garden we can never reach. I have a friend who pastors a church in a town that has changed underneath him over thirty years. The factory closed. The young people moved away. The downtown he remembers is half empty storefronts now. And his preaching, I have noticed, has slowly become a kind of grieving — eulogy after eulogy for a world that is gone — and his people grieve with him, and the church has become a place you go to mourn, not a place you go to be sent. He is not wrong that something was lost. He is wrong about which direction the church is supposed to face.

The Israelites did this in the wilderness, and their story is in the Bible precisely so we will recognize ourselves in it. Brought out of Egypt toward a promised land they had never laid eyes on, they spent a remarkable amount of energy romanticizing Egypt — the fish they ate freely, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks and the onions and the garlic (Numbers 11:5). Egypt, where they had been slaves. Egypt, where the previous chapter of their own scriptures says their lives were made bitter with hard bondage and their baby boys were thrown in the river. The memory edited out the whips and kept the cucumbers. That is what nostalgia does, and I will spend a whole chapter on it later because it is that dangerous. It is not memory; it is memory with the suffering removed, and it is one of the most powerful forces working against the church's vocation, because it tells us the best thing God could do is take us backward into a bondage we have repainted as a golden age.

But the wilderness generation had a second temptation, and so do we, and this is the one we talk about less. Some of them did not want to go back; they wanted to seize the land by their own strength, on their own timeline, before the promise was ready. When the spies came back with their report and the people refused to enter, God said that generation would not see the land — and then, hearing that, one faction grabbed their weapons and charged up into the hill country to take it anyway, without God, without the ark, without the word, and they were beaten and chased and crushed (Numbers 14:44-45). Read those verses slowly. They had just been told to wait, and they decided that waiting was for cowards, and they went to take the promise by force, and the promise broke them. Nostalgia and presumption. The two ditches. One faction wept for Egypt; the other charged the hill. One wants to go back; the other wants to seize the future before its time. Both refuse the one thing God actually asked for, which was the slow, faithful, forward walk of a people led by him toward a place they had not yet seen, at his pace, on his timeline.

The church is meant to walk that walk. Eugene Peterson called the Christian life "a long obedience in the same direction," and he borrowed the phrase from Nietzsche of all people, an atheist who used it to describe what makes anything worthwhile, and Peterson saw that it described discipleship better than most things written by Christians. The direction matters as much as the obedience. You can obey in circles. You can be very disciplined about going nowhere. We are not walking in a circle back to Eden. We are walking a straight line, however winding it feels underfoot, toward a city.

So what does this mean on an actual Tuesday, in an actual church, with an actual budget shortfall and an actual teenager who has stopped coming? It means the work you do in the church now is not preservation of a fading thing. It is construction toward a coming thing. When you teach a seven-year-old the books of the Bible in a half-empty Sunday school room, when you sit with a widow who tells you the same three stories every visit, when you forgive the man on the finance committee who undercut you, when you plant a congregation in a county everyone is leaving, you are not slowing the decline of something doomed. You are laying courses of brick in a wall whose blueprint is the New Jerusalem. None of it is wasted. Paul ends his great resurrection chapter with exactly this logic, and the logic is airtight: because the dead are raised, because the future is secured by the resurrection of Christ, "be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Therefore. Because the city is real, the labor is not in vain. The connective tissue between the resurrection and the widow's living room is the word therefore.

Now somebody will say: that is a nice way to make a discouraged person feel better, but how do you actually know the widow's living room matters to the city? You do not see the brick go into the wall. You see the widow forget your name and tell the same story again. Fair. I do not see the wall rise either. But I notice that God consistently asks his people to do things whose results they cannot trace, and he consistently tells them the results are real anyway, and the whole structure of faith is the willingness to act on his accounting rather than your eyesight. I cannot prove the widow's living room is a course of brick. I can tell you the one who promised to build the church told me my labor in him is not in vain, and I have decided to take him at his word, and if I am wrong I have lost nothing but an afternoon, and if I am right I have spent that afternoon building the eternal city.

I confess I am a man more tempted by the second ditch than the first. I do not pine for the garden so much as I want to storm the city, to see it finished in my lifetime, to make it happen by strategy and effort and the sheer force of my own will. I have started ministries because I could not bear to wait. I have pushed a congregation faster than the Spirit was moving it because the slowness felt like failure to me. That is its own kind of unbelief, and it is harder to spot than nostalgia because it looks like zeal. It treats the future as a thing I build rather than a thing God gives. And it always ends the way the charge up the hill country ended — in exhaustion and defeat, because I tried to seize by force what could only be received as promise. The men who charged the hill were not lazy. They were brave. They were just disobedient in the direction of action, and disobedience in the direction of action still gets you killed on the hill.

So here is the discipline of the middle, and it is harder than either ditch because both ditches are forms of escape and the middle is not. We refuse to go back, and we refuse to charge ahead alone. We walk. We build the courses we are given, at the pace we are given, in the place we are given. We trust the architect for the building we will not live to see finished. And we keep our eyes on the city, because the people who know where the road ends walk it differently than the people who are lost. A man walking home walks differently than a man wandering, even if you film them from behind and their steps look the same. The difference is invisible and it is everything. It is the difference between a church that is dying and a church that is on the way.

We are not going back to the garden. We are going on to the city.

TITLE: The Fire That Started It
SUMMARY: Pentecost is the church's beginning and its blueprint, the moment the Spirit launched a community that was never meant to be self-sustaining. The church runs on a fire it did not light and cannot replace with strategy.
VERDICT: The church began with a fire we did not light, and it will not run on fuel we manufacture.
REFLECT: Are you depending on the Spirit's power or on your own competence to sustain the work God has given you? | What in your church life is running on borrowed momentum rather than present fire? | When did you last ask God to do what only God can do?
BODY:
The church did not begin with a strategy. It began with a fire.

Read the opening of Acts and notice how little of it is human planning. A hundred and twenty people in an upper room, told to wait — not to act, not to organize, not to launch, but to wait "for the promise of the Father" (Acts 1:4). Sit with how strange that command is. The risen Lord of all creation, about to ascend, with the whole mission of the church in front of him, and his instruction to the leadership team is: do nothing. Wait. They have no building. They have no budget. They have a roster of leaders that includes a man who recently denied Jesus three times to a servant girl by a fire, and a group that scattered into the dark the moment the soldiers came. By any reasonable assessment this is not a movement poised to outlast the week, let alone the Roman Empire. If you were consulting for them you would not be optimistic. And then, on the day of Pentecost, fifty days after the resurrection, "there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind," and "cloven tongues like as of fire" sat on each of them, "and they were all filled with the Holy Ghost" (Acts 2:2-4).

Everything the church has ever been, it became in that moment. Not because the disciples finally got their act together. Because God came down.

I keep returning to this because I think the modern church has quietly stopped believing it, and I include myself in the indictment. We have become extraordinarily good at the parts of Acts that do not require the Spirit. We can organize. We can market. We can build systems and platforms and brands and email funnels. We have conferences on every conceivable competence — leadership, communication, assimilation, the science of the first-time guest. And there is nothing wrong with competence in itself; Acts is full of organization. They appoint seven men to handle the food distribution so the widows are not neglected (Acts 6:1-6). They collect money for the famine in Judea. They convene a council in Jerusalem to settle a doctrinal fight (Acts 15). The early church was not allergic to structure. But underneath all of it runs a fire that no committee produced, and when we forget the fire, all our competence becomes machinery running on momentum — a flywheel that keeps spinning for a while after the motor that drove it has shut off, so that you can stand there watching it turn and believe the power is still on when actually you are just watching stored energy bleed out.

Let me make that concrete, because I have watched it happen. I once consulted, informally, with a church that on paper was thriving. Good attendance, solid budget, a slick service, a staff that ran like a machine. And the longer I was around them the more I felt a coldness I could not name at first. The services were excellent and dead. The prayers were articulate and dead. Nobody was being converted; people were being moved around — transferring in from other churches, attracted by the quality. The flywheel was spinning beautifully. But somewhere years back the fire had gone out, and nobody had noticed, because the machinery was so good it kept turning on its own momentum. They were running on the stored energy of a revival that had happened under a previous pastor a generation earlier. The motor was off and the wheel was still turning and they thought that was life.

Lesslie Newbigin saw this dynamic with painful clarity at the level of a whole civilization. He spent nearly forty years as a missionary in India, much of it in the south, and then came home to England in the 1970s as an old man and found a church that had forgotten it was a missionary church at all — a church that assumed the surrounding culture was basically Christian, that ran on the borrowed momentum of a Christendom that no longer actually existed. He started saying that the West itself was now a mission field, perhaps the hardest one on earth, and this scandalized people, because the English had thought of themselves as the senders of missionaries, the ones who brought the gospel to India, not the ones who needed it brought back. But Newbigin understood that a church living on cultural momentum rather than the present power of the Spirit is a church burning through fuel it is not replacing. Eventually the tank runs dry, and you discover, too late, whether there was ever a fire or only a flywheel. He wrote books about this in the 1980s — The Gospel in a Pluralist Society and others — that read now like they were written about my own country this morning.

I have pastored long enough to feel this in my own ministry, and not as a theory. There are seasons when the machinery runs and you mistake the running for life. The services happen on schedule. The programs fill the calendar. The reports look fine, the giving holds, the building is full enough. And then one ordinary Thursday afternoon you are at your desk and you realize you have not prayed — not really prayed, not the desperate asking that admits with your whole body that you cannot do this and never could — in longer than you want to admit. You have said grace. You have prayed over meetings. You have prayed in public, professionally, the way a man does a thing he is good at. But you have not gotten on the floor of your study and begged God to come, because somewhere along the way you stopped believing you needed him to, because the flywheel was turning and the turning felt like enough. And in that moment you understand that you have been coasting on competence, and the fire is somewhere behind you, and you are not even sure when you left it.

The disciples could not have manufactured Pentecost. That is the point I cannot get past, and I want to press on it because everything in me resists it. They did not work themselves up into it with worship sets and atmosphere. They did not generate the wind by their fervor. The text is careful, almost insistent, to make the whole thing arrive from outside them, from heaven, given. The sound comes from heaven. The fire descends. They are filled — passive verb, something done to them. The church is, at its root, a receiving community before it is a doing community. We receive the Spirit. We do not produce him. We cannot summon him, schedule him, or replicate him by getting the lighting right. And every single generation of the church faces the temptation to forget this and to start running on what it can produce, until the day the produced thing fails and we are reminded, often through humiliation, that we were never the source.

Now here is a real objection, and it is one I have heard from good people. Are you not, the objection goes, just giving lazy and incompetent churches an excuse? "We don't need to plan or work or improve, we just need to wait for the Spirit" — has that not been the cover for a thousand badly run, dying congregations that mistook their decline for spirituality? Yes. Absolutely yes. That abuse is real, and I have seen it, and it is its own form of unbelief, which I will get to. But notice that the answer to one ditch is not to jump into the opposite one. The fact that some people use the Spirit as an excuse for sloth does not make the Spirit optional for the diligent. A church can be both diligent and dead. In fact the deadest churches I have known were not the lazy ones; they were the excellent ones who had quietly replaced the fire with their own excellence. The cure for laziness is not self-reliance. The cure for both laziness and self-reliance is dependence — a dependence that prays and then works hard, that raises the sail and then mans the rigging.

Jonathan Edwards understood the alternation here better than anyone in our tradition. He pastored in Northampton, Massachusetts, and he lived through the revivals of the 1730s and 40s, the awakening that swept New England and the colonies. He watched whole towns turn to God in a matter of weeks in 1734 and 1735 — taverns emptying, lawsuits dropped, the young gathering to pray, the whole texture of a town changed. And then he watched the fire recede and the coldness creep back in. He did not conclude from the recession that the fire had been false; that is what a cynic concludes, and Edwards was no cynic. He concluded that the church lives by outpourings it cannot schedule, that revival is sovereignly given and not humanly engineered, and that the right posture is therefore not control but readiness — to pray, to wait, to seek, to preach faithfully, and to receive gratefully when God pours out and to keep faithful when he does not. Edwards was a man of enormous intellect, one of the finest minds the American church has produced, and he nonetheless built his whole understanding of the church on the conviction that it runs on a power it does not own and cannot manage. The smartest man in the room knew the room could not generate its own fire.

This is humbling for those of us who like to be useful, and I am one of them. I am a builder by temperament. I want to do something, fix something, launch something, see a result by Friday. And the gospel keeps telling me that the single most important thing about the church is something I cannot do and did not start and cannot sustain by effort. The Spirit "bloweth where it listeth," Jesus told Nicodemus in the dark, "and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth" (John 3:8). You cannot command the wind. You cannot bottle it or schedule it for the second service. You can only raise the sail and be found ready when it blows.

But notice what this does not mean, because this is where people fall off the other side. It does not mean passivity. The disciples waited, but read how they waited — they waited together, in one accord, in prayer, in obedience, even attending to the practical business of replacing Judas (Acts 1:14-26). They did not wait by scattering to their boats. They waited by gathering and praying and staying ready. When the fire fell, they were positioned to catch it; they were in the room. There is a false spirituality that uses the sovereignty of the Spirit as a permission slip for doing nothing — well, if it is all up to God, I will sit here and let him sort it out. That is not the posture of Acts 1. The posture of Acts 1 is the sail raised in the still air, the boat pointed in the right direction, the crew at their stations, the lookout watching the horizon for a wind they cannot summon but fully intend to be ready for the instant it comes. Waiting on God is the most strenuous passivity there is. It takes more discipline to pray and wait than to run around being busy.

So what does it actually look like, on the ground, in a real church, to depend on a fire we cannot light? It looks like prayer meetings that are not a formality — gatherings where people actually ask God to do what no program can do, where you can feel that the people in the room believe they are asking for something they cannot get any other way. It looks like preaching that aims past technique at the conviction only the Spirit can give, so that the preacher prepares with everything he has and then mounts the steps knowing that all his preparation will accomplish exactly nothing unless God breathes on it. It looks like planning that holds its plans with an open hand, genuinely ready to have them overruled by a wind from heaven that blows the agenda off the table. And it looks like a church that measures itself not by the smoothness of its machinery but by its hunger for the presence of God — because that hunger is itself the first sign the fire has not gone out. A satisfied church is in more danger than a hungry one. The hungry one at least knows what it is missing.

Pentecost was not the church's launch event, a one-time ignition we now run on for two thousand years like a coasting flywheel. It was the revelation of how the church always runs, in every century: on the present power of a present God, given and not earned, received and not produced. The same Spirit who fell in the upper room in roughly the year 30 is the Spirit your church needs this Sunday morning, no less, no substitute available. And the temptation in every generation, including ours, including mine, is to replace him with our own competence, our own momentum, our own well-oiled machinery — and to keep the lights on and the wheel turning for years after the fire went out, not even noticing the cold, because the room is bright and the schedule is full and the reports look fine.

The church began with a fire we did not light, and it will not run on fuel we manufacture.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: When the Empire Adopted Us
SUMMARY: The conversion of the empire and the long age of Christendom gave the church power and respectability at the cost of forgetting how to live as a minority witness. Its passing is loss and gift at once, and the church must learn again what it was before the empire adopted it.
VERDICT: We are not losing the church. We are losing the empire's version of it.
REFLECT: How much of what you assume about the church is actually a habit of Christendom rather than the gospel? | What might the church recover now that it can no longer assume cultural power? | Where are you grieving the loss of influence as if it were the loss of faith?
BODY:
In the year 312, a Roman general saw something in the sky before a battle and changed the history of the church for the next seventeen centuries.

Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge, credited the God of the Christians, and within a decade the faith that the empire had been feeding to lions was the faith the emperor protected. By 380, under Theodosius, Christianity was the official religion of Rome. The catacombs emptied. The persecuted became the established. And the church, which had spent three hundred years learning to be a minority — gathered in homes, suspect, powerless, occasionally hunted — suddenly had to learn how to be a majority, with property and prestige and the sword of the state somewhere nearby.

Historians call the long age that followed Christendom: the arrangement in which the church and the surrounding culture were assumed to be one thing, in which to be born in a Christian country was to be assumed Christian, in which the church set the calendar, blessed the king, and occupied the center of public life. For most of us who were raised in the church in the West, Christendom is the only church we have ever known, and we have mistaken its habits for the gospel itself.

That arrangement is ending. It has been ending for a long time, but in the last few decades the ending has accelerated, and you can feel it in any congregation. The culture no longer assumes the faith. The church no longer occupies the center. The young no longer drift in by default. Christianity in the West is becoming, again, a minority position — sometimes tolerated, sometimes mocked, increasingly strange. And a great deal of the church's anxiety is really grief over this, the grief of a community losing a status it held so long it forgot the status was ever given.

I want to say something that will sound like betrayal to some and like relief to others. The passing of Christendom is not the death of the church. It may be one of the better things to happen to the church in a thousand years.

Here is why. Christendom gave the church power, and power is a dangerous gift. The same arrangement that filled the cathedrals also produced the crusades, the inquisitions, the slow fusion of cross and sword and crown until people could no longer tell where the gospel ended and the empire began. When the church sits at the center of power, it stops needing to persuade and starts being able to coerce. It stops being a witness and starts being an establishment. The early church converted the empire from below, by the strange power of love and martyrdom and a community that cared for the poor and the plague-stricken when no one else would. The established church too often maintained itself from above, by law and pressure and the assumption that everyone was already in.

Kierkegaard saw this in nineteenth-century Denmark and raged against it — a whole nation of baptized people, official Christians, where, he said, being a Christian cost nothing and therefore meant nothing. When everyone is a Christian, no one is. The faith that the early church died for had become, in Christendom, a thing you were simply born into, like a nationality, and Kierkegaard thought this had emptied it of everything that made it the gospel. He was not gentle about it. He thought Christendom had to die for Christianity to live.

I do not romanticize persecution. I am not eager to lose what the church has had. But I notice that the church grows most vital not at the center of power but at the edge of it, and that the New Testament was written entirely from the edge — by a persecuted minority with no political power, no cultural assumption, no establishment to lean on. The letters of Paul assume a church that is strange to its neighbors, that has to explain itself, that wins people by the visible difference of its life. That is the church we are becoming again. And we have, in the New Testament, a manual for exactly this situation, written by people who never knew Christendom and would not have recognized it.

The danger in this moment is that we will spend our energy trying to recover the empire's version of the church. We will fight to get the center back — the laws, the prayers in schools, the cultural deference, the assumption of belonging. We will mistake the recovery of influence for the recovery of faith. And I understand the impulse; I feel it. But a church that organizes itself around regaining power has fundamentally misunderstood its founder, who was offered all the kingdoms of the world and refused them (Matthew 4:8-10), who told Pilate "my kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36), who built his church not on a throne but on a cross.

Bonhoeffer learned this in the hardest possible school. He watched the German church, the established church of a Christian nation, fold itself almost entirely into the Nazi state in the 1930s — Christendom's final, monstrous logic, the cross fused to the swastika. And out of that catastrophe he came to believe that the church must be the church, distinct, costly, a community that follows Christ even against the nation, even against the culture, even against the empire that had adopted it. He paid for that conviction with his life in 1945. But he left the church a vision of itself stripped of establishment — the church as a costly minority that belongs to Christ before it belongs to any nation.

That is the recovery actually on offer in this moment. Not the recovery of the empire's church, but the recovery of the church beneath it — the gathered, peculiar, persuading, serving, costly community that existed before Constantine and will exist after the last Christian nation forgets it was ever Christian.

So I refuse the grief that treats the loss of cultural power as the loss of the faith. We are not losing the church. The church was never the cathedral at the center of the city or the prayer at the start of the legislative session or the assumption that the neighbors share our creed. The church was always the body of Christ, the living stones, the people of the fire. All of that survives the end of Christendom intact. What dies is the arrangement — the comfortable fusion that let us mistake a culture for a faith and a status for a calling.

Let it die. Grieve it if you must; I do, some days. But do not confuse the funeral of Christendom with the funeral of the church. One is an empire's borrowed clothing. The other is a building God is still raising, and no empire ever held the deed.

We are not losing the church. We are losing the empire's version of it.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: The Sin of Nostalgia
SUMMARY: Longing for a golden age of the church is not faithfulness but a refusal to trust God with the present, and it edits the past into a fiction. The church is called forward, and nostalgia is a way of facing backward while pretending to be devout.
VERDICT: Nostalgia is unbelief wearing the costume of reverence.
REFLECT: What golden age of the church or your own faith do you secretly believe you have lost? | What suffering does your nostalgia conveniently edit out? | Is God less present in the church now than he was then, or only less obvious to you?
BODY:
Every church I have ever served has had a golden age, and it is always just out of reach.

For some it was the 1950s, when the pews were full and the culture agreed with us. For others it was a particular pastor, a particular building program, a particular revival in a particular year, after which everything was somehow less. The details change but the structure is identical: there was a time when the church was what it should be, and we have fallen from it, and our task now is to grieve the fall and, if possible, return. I have heard this in churches that are objectively healthier than they have ever been. The golden age is not a fact about the past. It is a feeling about the present, projected backward.

I have come to believe that nostalgia is one of the most spiritually destructive forces in the church, and one of the least examined, because it does not look like a sin. It looks like love. It looks like reverence for what God has done. It wears the costume of devotion. But underneath, nostalgia is a quiet accusation against the God of the present — a way of saying that the best of him is behind us, that he was more himself then than now, that the building he is raising today is a lesser thing than the building he raised before.

The Bible is unsentimental about this. When the second temple was being rebuilt after the exile, the old men who had seen Solomon's temple wept at the sight of the new foundation, because it was smaller, poorer, less glorious than what they remembered (Ezra 3:12). And God's word to them through Haggai was not comfort for their grief but a rebuke of it: "Who is left among you that saw this house in her first glory? and how do ye see it now? is it not in your eyes in comparison of it as nothing? Yet now be strong... for I am with you" (Haggai 2:3-4). And then the staggering promise: "The glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former" (Haggai 2:9). The old men were weeping over a building God said would surpass the one they mourned. Their nostalgia had them grieving at the exact moment they should have been hoping.

That is what nostalgia does. It positions you to weep over the better thing because it does not match the remembered thing.

And the remembered thing is usually a fiction. This is what I have learned from pastoring people through their own golden ages: the memory has been edited. The 1950s church that people pine for was full in part because the culture made church attendance a social requirement, and a great deal of that fullness was nominal, the Christendom attendance that meant nothing, the pews crowded with people who had never been converted. The remembered revival left out the conflicts that followed it. The beloved pastor had failures his admirers have forgotten. Nostalgia is memory with the suffering surgically removed, and it produces a past that never existed, against which the present can never compete.

The Israelites in the wilderness remembered Egypt as a land of fish and cucumbers (Numbers 11:5). They left out that Egypt was the house of slavery. The edit is always the same: keep the comfort, delete the bondage. And the spiritual effect is always the same too — it makes you want to go back to a place God brought you out of.

I think this is why Scripture so consistently commands remembrance of a particular, disciplined kind, and so consistently warns against the undisciplined kind. Israel was told to remember the exodus — but to remember it rightly, as deliverance from slavery, as evidence of God's faithfulness, as fuel for trusting him forward. That is memory in service of hope. Nostalgia is memory in service of despair. The same act — looking back — can do either, depending on whether it sends you forward into trust or backward into grief.

Brueggemann, who has thought as deeply as anyone about the prophetic imagination, argued that the prophet's task is precisely to break the grip of a frozen past and a foreclosed future — to crack open the present so the people can imagine God doing a new thing. He drew on Isaiah, where God says: "Remember ye not the former things, neither consider the things of old. Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:18-19). Notice that God says this to people in exile, people with every reason to romanticize the past, people who had lost the temple and the land and the monarchy and everything that had made them who they were. To those people, God says: stop looking back. I am about to do something you have not seen. The prophetic word is almost always a word against nostalgia, because nostalgia is the chief obstacle to hope. You cannot watch for the new thing while you are weeping over the old one.

Here is the part that implicates me, because I am not above this. I have golden ages of my own. There was a season early in ministry when everything felt charged with God's presence, when prayer was easy and the work was joy and I was certain of my calling, and there have been long stretches since when I have wanted that back, when I have measured the present against that remembered fire and found the present wanting. And I have had to learn, slowly, that this is unbelief. It assumes God was more present then than now. It assumes the dryness I feel is his absence rather than his hiddenness, his withdrawal rather than his deeper work. The God of my golden age is the God of this gray Tuesday, and to pine for the one while resenting the other is to refuse the God who is actually here.

The church does not move forward by recovering its golden age. There is no golden age to recover; there is only a remembered fiction that keeps us facing the wrong direction. The church moves forward by trusting that the God who built the former house is building the latter, and that the latter will be greater — not because we are better than our forebears, but because the building is rising, course by course, toward a glory that makes every former glory look like a foundation.

Be strong, the prophet says to the weeping old men. The latter glory will exceed the former. Stop grieving the foundation and help raise the house.

Nostalgia is unbelief wearing the costume of reverence.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: The Sin of Pragmatism
SUMMARY: The opposite error to nostalgia is the frantic pursuit of the next method that will save the church, a faith in technique that quietly replaces faith in God. The church cannot be engineered into the future; it can only be entrusted to the one who builds it.
VERDICT: You cannot strategize your way into a future that only God can give.
REFLECT: What method or model have you secretly hoped would save your church? | Where has anxiety about the future driven you to act faster than you have trusted? | What would faithful patience look like in the part of the church's work you find most discouraging?
BODY:
If nostalgia faces backward, pragmatism faces forward — and they are the same sin in opposite costumes.

I have spent years inside the conference economy of the evangelical church, and I want to describe it honestly because I have profited from it and been deformed by it. Every year there is a new method. A new model of doing church that will reach the people the old model lost. A new book, a new framework, a new set of practices that some growing church somewhere has discovered, packaged, and is now selling to the anxious pastors of declining churches. The seeker model. The attractional model. The missional model. The organic model. The multisite model. The model after that. Each arrives promising to be the thing that finally works, and each is adopted by thousands of churches who are quietly terrified that without it they will die.

I do not say all of this is worthless. There is wisdom in studying what God seems to bless, in learning from healthy churches, in being willing to change methods that no longer serve. The early church changed methods constantly — went from temple courts to homes, from Jerusalem to the nations, adapted to every new context it entered. Adaptation is not the sin. The sin is the underlying belief, rarely stated but everywhere assumed, that the right method will save us. That if we can just find the correct technique, the future of the church is in our hands.

It is not in our hands. That is the whole point of the book, and pragmatism is the precise way the church denies it while sounding industrious.

There is a story in the Old Testament that has haunted me for years. David, near the end of his reign, decides to take a census of Israel — to count the fighting men, to measure the strength of the nation (2 Samuel 24:1-10). It seems harmless. It seems responsible, even, the kind of thing a good administrator does. But it provokes God's anger, and David himself, afterward, is struck with conviction, and the whole episode ends in plague. For years I did not understand why counting was a sin. Then I understood. David was measuring the kingdom's strength as if the kingdom's strength were in its numbers — as if the future of Israel depended on how many swords it could field rather than on the God who had brought it from nothing. The census was unbelief expressed as arithmetic. It moved the trust from God to the metrics.

The church does this constantly. We count. We measure attendance, giving, baptisms, engagement, reach. And there is nothing wrong with counting itself — Acts counts, three thousand added at Pentecost (Acts 2:41). The wrong is in where the counting moves our trust. When the numbers become the thing we depend on, when our sense of the church's future rises and falls with the metrics, when we adopt methods chiefly because they move the numbers — we have made David's mistake. We have located the kingdom's strength in something we can measure and manage, which is to say, in ourselves.

Keller wrote helpfully about this, warning against making methods into idols while also insisting that thoughtfulness about ministry matters. He held a hard balance: be wise about how you do ministry, study your city, think carefully, but never confuse your wisdom with the power that actually changes anyone. The conversion of a single soul is a work of God that no method produces. You can fill a building with technique. You cannot make a single dead heart alive with all the strategy in the world. "Paul planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6). Planting and watering are method, and they matter, and we are commanded to do them well. But the increase is not on the menu of things we control. The increase is given.

I think pragmatism is, at root, a failure of patience. The future feels uncertain, the decline feels real, the anxiety is unbearable, and a method offers something the soul desperately wants: control. If I just do this, then that will follow. It converts the agonizing trust of faith into the manageable equation of technique. And it always disappoints, because the church was never an equation. The man who plants a field cannot make it grow by anxiety, however much he paces the rows at night. "The earth bringeth forth fruit of herself," Jesus said, while the farmer sleeps and rises night and day and "knoweth not how" (Mark 4:27-28). The growth happens in a hiddenness the farmer cannot penetrate. His job is to plant faithfully and wait. The anxious farmer who digs up the seed every morning to check on it kills the very thing his anxiety is trying to save.

I have been that farmer. I have, in seasons of fear about my church's future, chased methods the way a sick man chases cures, certain that the next one would be the one, growing more frantic and less faithful with each disappointment. And what God has had to teach me, slowly and against my temperament, is that the future of the church is not a problem for me to solve by superior technique. It is a promise for me to trust while I plant. The difference between those two postures is the difference between exhaustion and rest, between control and faith, between the census-taker counting his swords and the shepherd-king trusting the God who made him from a boy with a sling.

Brueggemann had a phrase for the alternative — he spoke of the prophetic imagination as the capacity to live out of God's promised future rather than the present's foreclosed despair or the technician's managed control. The pragmatist and the nostalgic share a single unbelief: both think the future is up to human management, one by recovering the past and one by engineering the future. The prophet refuses both. The prophet plants in faith and watches for the increase that only God can give.

So I am not against method. I am against the idol that method becomes when it replaces trust. Use the best wisdom you can find. Study, plan, adapt, count if you must — and then lay it all down at the feet of the only one who gives the increase, and sleep, and rise, and not know how, and trust the God who makes the earth bring forth fruit of herself while the farmer rests.

You cannot strategize your way into a future that only God can give.

TITLE: Living Stones, Not Finished Statues
SUMMARY: The church is built of people who are themselves unfinished, and its imperfection is not a betrayal of the vision but the condition of the work. To demand a perfected church now is to refuse the patient grace that is building it.
VERDICT: A perfect church would have no room for you, and that should make you grateful.
REFLECT: What imperfection in your church do you treat as proof of its failure rather than evidence of its construction? | How patient is God being with you, and how patient are you being with others? | Are you waiting to belong until you are finished, or letting the belonging finish you?
BODY:
Peter calls us living stones (1 Peter 2:5), and I do not think we have taken the adjective seriously enough.

A stone is hard, finished, set. You lay it and it stays. But a living stone is something else — a stone that is still changing, still being shaped, still growing into its place in the wall. The metaphor is deliberately unstable. We are stones, so we are real building material, load-bearing, part of the structure. But we are living, so we are not yet what we will be, and the wall we form is made of material that is itself under construction. The church is not built of finished statues, set and perfect. It is built of people who are themselves unfinished, and the building rises out of their shared incompleteness.

This reframes almost everything about how we relate to the church's flaws.

We are scandalized by the imperfection of the church. The hypocrisy, the conflict, the pettiness, the failures of leaders, the slowness to change, the people who should know better and do not. And there is real sin in the church that deserves to be named and grieved, and I am not minimizing it. But a great deal of our scandal comes from a hidden expectation that the church should already be finished — that a building God is raising should look like a building God has completed. We walk into the construction site and are appalled by the scaffolding. We see the exposed beams and conclude the architect has failed.

The exposed beams are the work. A church full of unfinished people will look exactly like a church full of unfinished people: it will have conflict, because unfinished people clash; it will have hypocrisy, because unfinished people are inconsistent; it will have slowness and failure and a thousand disappointments, because that is what it looks like when grace is in the middle of doing its long work on a community of sinners. The imperfection is not evidence that the vision is false. It is evidence that the vision is honest — that the church is what it claims to be, a hospital for the sick rather than a museum for the cured.

I have watched people leave the church over its imperfection, and I understand it, and I grieve it, but I have come to see that beneath much of that leaving is a demand the gospel never makes: the demand that the church be finished now. Show me the perfect community, and I will join it. But a perfect community would have no room for me, and no room for the one demanding it. A perfected church, here and now, would be a church with the door shut to every unfinished person — which is to say, every person. The imperfection that scandalizes us is the same imperfection that lets us in.

Bonhoeffer wrote about this with surgical precision in *Life Together*. He warned that the greatest enemy of real Christian community is the dream of ideal community — the vision in our heads of what the church should be, against which we measure and condemn the actual church in front of us. He said that the person in love with their dream of community will destroy the actual community, because they demand of it a perfection it cannot have, and they will leave or embitter the moment it disappoints them. But the person who receives the actual community as a gift of grace, imperfections and all, will be able to love it and build in it and let God do his patient work through it. The dream is the enemy. The gift is the reality. Bonhoeffer wrote that out of a community of seminarians the Nazi state would eventually scatter and kill, so he was not romanticizing; he knew exactly how disappointing real Christians could be, and he chose to receive them as gift anyway.

This is the patience the church requires, and it runs in two directions.

First, it asks us to be patient with the church, the way God is patient with us. I cannot demand of the body a completion that God has not demanded of me. I am an unfinished stone. The work on me is visibly incomplete; ask anyone who lives with me. If God were as impatient with me as I am tempted to be with the church, I would have been discarded long ago. "He which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6) — note the future tense, the until, the long road still to walk before the work is done. God is not finished with me, and his patience with my unfinishedness is the only reason I am still in the wall at all. That patience is the model for how I must treat the unfinished people around me.

Second, it asks us to let the belonging do its work. Here is something I got wrong for years: I thought I had to be finished before I could fully belong. I thought I needed to get my life in order, my doubts resolved, my sins managed, and then I could take my place as a proper stone in the wall. But that is backward. The stone is shaped in the wall, not before it. It is by belonging — by being among the people, under the word, in the friction and the forgiveness and the long ordinary faithfulness of a real congregation — that the shaping happens. You do not get finished and then join. You join, and the joining finishes you. The community is not the reward for completion; it is the means of it. Iron sharpens iron (Proverbs 27:17), and you cannot be sharpened by iron you are keeping at a distance.

I came to faith out of atheism, and I came alone, intellectually, through argument and crisis. But I was not made into anything resembling a Christian alone. I was made by being among Christians — disappointing, ordinary, unfinished Christians who put up with my unfinishedness while God did his slow work on all of us together. The man I am, such as he is, was built in a wall, not chiseled in isolation. I am still being built there.

The church will not be finished in your lifetime or mine. The scaffolding will be up the whole time we are here. And the temptation will always be to read the scaffolding as failure and walk away looking for the finished building that does not exist this side of the city. Resist it. The unfinished church is the only church there is, because it is made of unfinished people, and you are one of them, and the same grace that bears with the church's imperfection is the grace bearing with yours.

A perfect church would have no room for you, and that should make you grateful.

TITLE: The Hope That Pulls
SUMMARY: Christian hope is not optimism about trends but confidence in a promised future that reaches back into the present to shape it. The future God has guaranteed becomes the engine of present faithfulness rather than a distant consolation.
VERDICT: Hope is not a guess about the future. It is the future arriving early to change the present.
REFLECT: Is your hope based on what you can see happening, or on what God has promised regardless of what you see? | How would living from a guaranteed future change what you do this week? | Where have you settled for optimism when God offers you hope?
BODY:
There is a difference between optimism and hope, and almost everything depends on which one you are living by.

Optimism is a reading of the trends. It looks at the data, weighs the probabilities, and concludes that things will likely improve. It is a posture toward the future based on what can be seen and projected, and it is therefore at the mercy of the evidence. When the numbers are good, the optimist hopes; when the numbers are bad, the optimist despairs. Optimism about the church in the West is hard to sustain right now, because the trends, read honestly, are not encouraging — declining attendance, declining belief, a culture drifting steadily away. If your confidence in the church's future is optimism, the data will eventually crush it.

Hope is something else entirely. Hope is not a reading of the trends. It is confidence in a promise. It looks not at what can be seen and projected but at what God has said he will do, and it holds that against all the contrary evidence, because its ground is not the data but the character of the one who promised. "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1). The evidence of things not seen — which means hope sees something the trends cannot show, and stakes everything on it.

Jürgen Moltmann wrote the book that named this for the modern church. *Theology of Hope*, published in 1964, argued that Christian faith is, from beginning to end, oriented toward the future God has promised — that the resurrection of Christ is not merely a past event to believe but a future breaking into the present, a guarantee of the new creation that pulls all of history toward itself. Moltmann had been a prisoner of war, captured as a young German soldier, held in camps for years, and it was in that captivity, with no earthly future to hope in, that he encountered the God of hope and found that hope is not based on circumstances at all. He came home and built a whole theology on the conviction that the church lives forward, drawn by a promised future rather than driven by a remembered past or a measured present.

This is the crucial move, and I want to make sure it lands. The Christian future is not merely a destination we are heading toward. It is a power reaching back into the present to shape it. The future God has guaranteed is not passive, sitting at the end of the road waiting for us to arrive. It is active, pulling us, drawing the present into conformity with itself. Paul prays that we would know "what is the hope of his calling" (Ephesians 1:18), because the hope of the calling is meant to do something now — to reorganize how we live this Tuesday in light of a city we will not reach for years.

Think of how a guaranteed future changes present behavior. The runner who knows the race ends at the finish line spends himself differently than the one who fears the road goes on forever. The soldier who knows the war is already won fights differently than the one who fears defeat. The whole logic of the New Testament's ethics runs on this: because the future is secure, live now in its light. "Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him... And every man that hath this hope in him purifieth himself" (1 John 3:2-3). The hope purifies. The guaranteed future reaches back and changes the man who holds it.

This is why hope is not passive, and why it has nothing to do with sitting around waiting for heaven. The most hopeful Christians I have known were also the most active, the most willing to give themselves to lost causes, the most able to labor at things they would not see completed. Because they were not working to secure an uncertain future by their effort — they were working in joyful response to a future already secured by God. That changes the whole quality of the work. The pragmatist works in fear, trying to engineer a future he is afraid will not come. The hopeful Christian works in freedom, building toward a future he knows is coming regardless, and therefore building without the frantic exhaustion of the man who thinks it all depends on him.

I have buried people whose hope humbled me. People dying of things that would not be cured, who faced the end not with optimism — there was no good reading of their trends — but with hope, with the steady confidence of those who knew that what they could see was not the whole story. I sat with a man once, near the end, who had every reason to despair and did not, and when I asked him how, he did not give me a strategy or a positive outlook. He said, more or less, that he had read the last chapter and knew how the story ended, and that the present was hard but the present was not the end. He was living, in that hospital bed, from the future. The city was pulling him forward through a present that had nothing left to offer his optimism but everything still to offer his hope.

That is the posture I want for the church in this generation. Not the optimism that rises and falls with the attendance numbers. Not the despair that reads the cultural trends and concludes the building is collapsing. But the hope that holds the promise against the evidence, that lives from a future God has guaranteed, that labors in freedom because the outcome is already secure. The trends may be against us for a long time. The hope does not depend on the trends. It depends on the one who said he would build his church and has never failed to finish what he began.

Read the last chapter. The city comes down. The dwelling of God is with men. Every tear is wiped away (Revelation 21:3-4). That is not optimism — there is no trend that predicts it. It is hope, the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen, and it is meant to reach back into this gray and difficult present and pull you forward into a faithfulness you could not sustain on the evidence alone.

Hope is not a guess about the future. It is the future arriving early to change the present.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: What We Are Building Toward
SUMMARY: The end of the story is not escape from the world but the marriage of heaven and earth in the New Jerusalem, a future so concrete that it reshapes what the church should value now. We build toward a city, and the city tells us what matters.
VERDICT: The end is not a rescue from the world but the healing of it, and that changes what we build.
REFLECT: Does your vision of heaven involve escaping the world or the world being made right? | If the end is a city full of gathered human work, what does that say about the things you are building now? | What would it mean to build, this week, something fit for the New Jerusalem?
BODY:
We have spent generations preaching the wrong ending, and the wrong ending has produced the wrong church.

The ending most Christians imagine is escape. The soul leaves the body, the world is left behind, and we go up to a disembodied heaven of clouds and harps, eternally elsewhere. It is the ending of a thousand hymns and ten thousand funeral sermons. And it is not the ending the Bible actually describes. The Bible's ending is the opposite direction: not us going up to heaven but heaven coming down to earth. "And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven" (Revelation 21:2). The movement is downward, toward the world, not upward, away from it. The voice from the throne does not say the redeemed have escaped to be with God; it says, "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them" (Revelation 21:3). God moves in. Heaven and earth, long separated, are married at last.

This is not a minor correction. It changes what the church is building toward, and therefore what the church should build now.

N.T. Wright has labored to recover this against centuries of escapist preaching, and his argument in *Surprised by Hope* is simple and devastating: if the Christian hope is escape from the world, then the world does not ultimately matter, and the church's work in the world is just rearranging deck chairs on a sinking ship until the rescue helicopter comes. But if the Christian hope is the renewal of the world — resurrection, new creation, heaven and earth joined — then everything done in faith in this world matters eternally, because this world is not going to be discarded but healed. The body is not a prison the soul escapes; the body will be raised. The earth is not a doomed husk we flee; the earth will be renewed. The end is not evacuation. It is restoration.

And notice what the restored end actually contains. It is a city — which means it is full of culture, architecture, gathered human life, the works of human hands taken up and made glorious. "The kings of the earth do bring their glory and honour into it" (Revelation 21:24). Something of human civilization, purified, is carried into the New Jerusalem. The labor of human history is not erased but gathered, redeemed, fulfilled. The garden was raw nature; the city is nature plus everything good that humanity made of it across all the centuries, healed of its sin and offered to God.

If that is the ending, then the church's work now is not waiting-room activity. It is foundation-laying for the city. When you build something good and true and beautiful in faith — a marriage, a craft, a just institution, a faithful congregation, a healed relationship, a work of mercy — you are not killing time until the rescue. You are building something that, in ways you cannot trace, will be gathered into the city. Paul says it plainly: your labor "is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Not in vain — which means it carries forward, somehow, into the end. The good you build does not evaporate when the world is renewed. It is taken up.

This rescues ordinary faithfulness from meaninglessness. I have stood beside people who spent their whole lives in obscure, unglamorous service — the woman who taught children's Sunday school for fifty years, the man who quietly paid the bills no one else would, the saints whose names will never appear in any history of the church — and the escapist ending makes their work look small, a way of passing the time before heaven. But the biblical ending makes their work look enormous, because it is the kind of work the city is made of. The New Jerusalem is not built of grand gestures. It is built of ten thousand acts of faithful, ordinary love, each one taken up and made permanent in a world that no longer dies.

This is also, I think, what saves the church from two opposite errors about its work in the world. The first error says the world does not matter, so the church should retreat into a spiritual ghetto and wait for the end, ignoring justice, beauty, culture, and the suffering of the body. The escapist ending produces this. The second error says only the world matters, so the church should become a program of social improvement and forget about God altogether. The merely earthly hope produces this. But the biblical ending refuses both. It says the world matters because God is going to redeem it, not replace it — so the church works for the good of the world as foretaste of the city, while never confusing the foretaste with the feast, never pretending we can build the New Jerusalem ourselves. We build toward it. We do not build it. The city comes down from God; we do not construct it up from earth. But what we build in faith is gathered into what he brings down.

Augustine held these two cities in his great book — the city of man and the city of God, intermingled in history, distinguishable only at the end. He never thought the church could build the city of God on earth by its own effort; that was the error he most feared, the confusion of the two cities. But he also never thought the Christian's work in this world was meaningless, because the city of God was being built by God through that work, gathered out of history toward its completion. We labor in the city of man, citizens of the city of God, building toward a completion only God will bring.

So when people ask me what the church is for, I have stopped giving them the small answers — community, comfort, moral instruction, a place to belong. Those are true but they are not the size of it. The church is the community that knows where history is going and lives now in light of the end. The church is the people who have read the last chapter — heaven and earth married, the city descended, the dwelling of God with men, every tear wiped away (Revelation 21:4) — and who therefore build, in this present darkness, things fit for that city. Marriages fit for the city. Mercy fit for the city. Beauty and justice and faithfulness fit for the city. None of it wasted. All of it gathered.

The end is not a rescue from the world but the healing of it, and that changes what we build.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: The Church Across the Earth
SUMMARY: The future God is building is global, and the church's center of gravity has already shifted away from the West that imagines itself central. The unfinished church is rising fastest in the very places the old Christendom thought of as its mission field.
VERDICT: The church is not dying. It is moving, and we mistook our corner of it for the whole.
REFLECT: When you imagine the global church, do you picture the West or the world? | How does the explosive growth of the church elsewhere reframe the decline you see at home? | What would humility look like for the Western church in a world where it is no longer the center?
BODY:
When Western Christians despair about the future of the church, they are almost always making a geographical error. They mistake their corner of the church for the whole of it.

The story most of us absorbed goes like this: Christianity is a Western religion, born in the Mediterranean, carried by Europe, exported by missionaries to the rest of the world, and now declining in its heartland as secularism advances. The faith is shrinking, we assume, because it is shrinking here, in Europe and North America, where we happen to live and where we happen to look. But "here" is not the world. And the actual map of global Christianity tells a story so different from our despair that it should reorder how we think about the church's future entirely.

The center of gravity of the Christian church has moved. For most of the last two thousand years, the typical Christian was a European. Today the typical Christian is more likely to be a Nigerian, a Brazilian, a Chinese house-church believer, a Filipino, a Kenyan, a Korean. The faith that is declining in Europe is exploding across the global South and the East. There are more practicing Christians in China than members of the Communist Party, gathered in churches the state does not authorize and cannot count. The church in sub-Saharan Africa has grown from a small minority a century ago to hundreds of millions today. The scholar Philip Jenkins documented this shift in his work on what he called the next Christendom — the simple, staggering fact that the future of Christianity is not Western and increasingly is not even mostly Western, and that the church's center has moved south and east while the West was busy writing its obituary.

This is exactly what we should have expected, if we had been reading the right ending. The promise was never confined to one continent. "I will build my church" was spoken over a global project from the start. The risen Christ told his disciples they would be his witnesses "unto the uttermost part of the earth" (Acts 1:8), and the vision John sees at the end is explicitly every-national: "a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues" (Revelation 7:9). The New Jerusalem has the glory and honor of the nations carried into it (Revelation 21:26), plural nations, the whole earth's peoples gathered. A church that was only ever Western would be a failure of the vision. A church that is becoming global is the vision coming true.

I find this both humbling and thrilling, and I want to be honest about the humbling part, because the Western church needs it.

For centuries we assumed we were the center. We were the senders; others were the sent-to. We were the teachers; others were the taught. We exported the faith with one hand and, too often, exported our culture, our politics, and our colonial assumptions with the other, until the gospel and the Western package were hard to separate. And now the situation has reversed in ways we are barely able to process. Churches in Africa and Asia and Latin America are sending missionaries to Europe and North America, to the post-Christian West that has become, in Newbigin's phrase, a mission field again. The taught have become the teachers. The sent-to have become the senders. The center has moved, and we are no longer it, and learning to receive the faith from those we once condescended to teach is going to require a humility the Western church has rarely practiced.

But there is glory in this for those who can see past their wounded centrality. Because what it reveals is that the church was never a Western institution with a Western future. It was always God's global project, and the contraction we feel in our particular corner is happening at the very moment the building is rising faster than ever in corners we were not looking at. The scaffolding came down on one wing of the construction site, and we concluded the whole building was being abandoned, when in fact the crews had simply moved to where the work was now most alive.

This should do something specific to our despair. The decline we see at home is real, and I am not denying it, and there is grief in it and lessons in it. But it is local. It is not the verdict on the church; it is the verdict on the church in one place, in one season, under conditions we have already examined — the end of Christendom, the long deformation of nominal faith. To read the global future of the church off the European attendance numbers is like reading the global weather off your own backyard. It is raining here. It is not raining everywhere. In much of the world the church is in a noonday of growth that the West has not seen for centuries.

There is a passage in Isaiah where God promises the servant that it is "a light thing" that he should merely restore Israel — God will make him "a light to the Gentiles," that his salvation may reach "unto the end of the earth" (Isaiah 49:6). It was always too small a thing for the church to be one people's possession. The scope was always the end of the earth. And the church we are watching emerge in this generation — global, multiplying, sending in every direction at once, no longer organized around a single Western center — is closer to that promised scope than the Christendom we are mourning ever was.

So when a discouraged believer in a half-empty Western sanctuary asks me whether the church has a future, I want to widen the question before I answer it. The church you can see from that pew may be in a hard season. The church God can see from his throne is a multitude no man can number, drawn from every nation, and it is rising. The building did not stop. We were just standing in a quiet wing of it, mistaking the quiet for the whole.

The church is not dying. It is moving, and we mistook our corner of it for the whole.
===CHAPTER===
TITLE: The Day the Scaffolding Comes Down
SUMMARY: One day the church will be finished, the scaffolding removed, the building revealed as the bride and the city it was always becoming. This final completion is the weight the whole vision rests on, and it gives the present labor its meaning and its end.
VERDICT: We will not see the building finished. But it will be finished, and we will be in it.
REFLECT: Do you live as though the work depends on you finishing it, or as though God will complete what you only contribute to? | What course of brick has God given you to lay, knowing you will not see the building done? | When the scaffolding comes down, will you be found laboring or merely lamenting?
BODY:
There will be a day when the scaffolding comes down.

I have built this whole book on the image of an unfinished building, a church under construction, a project still rising. And I want to end by insisting on the part that makes the unfinishedness bearable: it does not go on forever. The construction is not eternal. There is a day, fixed in the purpose of God, when the last stone is set, the scaffolding is struck, the dust is washed away, and the building stands revealed as what it was always becoming. The church is unfinished now. It will not be unfinished then.

John sees that day. After the long apocalypse, after the wars and the plagues and the fall of every false city, he sees the church revealed in its finished glory: "the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2). The bride. The city. Two images for one reality — the church completed, the building finished, the long construction over at last. And the voice from the throne declares what the finished building is for: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God" (Revelation 21:3). The whole point of the building, from the first stone Peter spoke of to the last, was this — a dwelling place for God among his people. That is what we have been building toward. That is what the scaffolding was always for.

And then the sentence that breaks me every time: "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away" (Revelation 21:4). The former things. All of it — the conflict in the church, the failures of its leaders, the scandals and the splits and the slow leak of the young out the back, the decline and the despair, the funerals, the doubts, the long gray Tuesdays of faithfulness with nothing visible to show for it. The former things. Passed away. The scaffolding struck and hauled off and the building standing in a glory that makes the whole long, mud-spattered construction make sense at last.

I will not see that day from here. Neither will you. This is the hard, clarifying truth that I want to leave you with, because it is the one that orders everything. The building will not be finished in our lifetimes. We will lay our courses of brick and then we will die, as every generation of the church has laid its courses and died, and the work will pass to others, and to others after them, until the day God himself completes it. We are not the generation that finishes the church. There is no such generation among the living. We are one crew among the centuries of crews, and our task is not to see the building done but to lay our few courses faithfully and hand the work on.

That sounds like loss, and there is loss in it. But I have come to find it the deepest freedom the gospel offers the laborer. Because if it were my job to finish the church, I would have to despair — I cannot finish it, no one can, the thing is too large and I am too small and my time is too short. But it is not my job to finish it. It is my job to lay my courses well. The finishing belongs to the one who said he would build it, and his arm is not shortened, and his timeline is not mine, and the building will stand long after my name is forgotten. "He which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6). He will perform it. Not I. He will strike the scaffolding, on the day he has appointed, and the building will be his, finished, perfect, the dwelling of God with men.

I think of the medieval cathedral builders, the ones who started cathedrals they knew they would never see completed — buildings that took two hundred years, three hundred, so that the man who laid the foundation stone and the man who set the final spire were separated by ten generations and never knew each other's names. The foundation-layer worked in faith, building toward a glory he would see only from the grave, trusting that those who came after would carry on the work and that one day, long after him, the thing would stand finished. That is the church. We are foundation-layers in a cathedral whose spire we will not see, laboring in faith toward a completion God has guaranteed and will bring about in his time, not ours.

And here is the final weight, the thing I most want you to carry out of this book. We will not see the building finished from here. But we will be in it when it is. That is the part the cathedral image misses, and that the gospel supplies. The medieval mason who died with the cathedral half-built never saw it finished and never entered it complete. But we will. Because the church is not only the building we are building — it is the people who are raised. The living stones do not merely contribute to the temple and then perish. The living stones are raised, and gathered, and present on the day the scaffolding comes down. The same resurrection that completes the building includes the builders. You will not see the finished church from this life. But you will see it. You will stand in it. You will be a stone in the wall on the day the dust is washed away and God moves in and every tear is wiped from every eye.

So lay your courses. Lay them in the dark, in the mud, in the long ordinary faithfulness that shows no visible progress, in the seasons when the building seems to be coming down rather than going up. Lay them without the despair of the man who thinks he must finish, and without the nostalgia of the man who wants to go back, and without the frantic pragmatism of the man who thinks the future is his to engineer. Lay them in hope — the hope that holds the promise against the evidence, the hope that lives from a guaranteed future, the hope of the building still rising under the hands of a builder who has never once failed to finish what he began.

He said he would build his church. He is building it now, through people like you, who feel like rubble more days than stone. And one day — a day he has fixed and we cannot hasten — he will strike the scaffolding, and the dust will settle, and the city will stand, and God will be with his people, and every tear will be wiped away, and the long unfinished work of two thousand years will be revealed as what it always was: a dwelling place for God, built by his own hand, finished at last.

We will not see the building finished. But it will be finished, and we will be in it.
