INTRODUCTION

The Case for a Reasonable Faith

Why faith is not believing without evidence, and how the case for God is built for skeptics, doubters, and seekers.

A child can ask the question that no seminary degree fully answers: how do you know God is real and not pretend? The honest believer hesitates there, not for lack of replies but for a surplus of them: the rehearsed answer, the apologetics answer, the inspirational answer, the kind offered when the aim is to win rather than to tell the truth. But a child is not asking anyone to win; a child is asking for honesty, and so is the skeptic, and so is the believer whose faith has begun to crack under the weight of a question it was never taught to carry. This book is written to be honest before it is written to be persuasive.

That aim places it inside one of the oldest disciplines of Christian thought. The New Testament itself asks believers to be ready to give a reason, and the word it uses in 1 Peter 3:15, apologia, is the word for a defense offered in a court of law. It does not mean an apology in the modern sense, and it never meant a performance of certainty. It meant an account given in public, to people who had every right to ask for one, with gentleness and respect. Everything in the following pages is an attempt to give that account to a reader in the twenty-first century, who has been taught, often with good reason, to distrust anyone who claims to have one.

A Defense With a Long History

Christians were making arguments for their faith long before they had the legal right to exist. Justin Martyr, a philosopher who had tried the Stoics, the Pythagoreans and the Platonists before he became a Christian, addressed his First Apology to the emperor Antoninus Pius around the middle of the second century, arguing that the faith was neither atheism nor sedition and that the best of Greek philosophy had been reaching toward the Logos who became flesh. Origen answered the pagan critic Celsus point by point in the third century, taking the strongest objections of his day seriously enough to quote them at length before replying. Augustine wrote The City of God after the sack of Rome in 410, when educated pagans blamed the Christians for the collapse of the world, and in answering them he produced the first great Christian philosophy of history.

The tradition did not end with the fathers. Thomas Aquinas wrote the Summa contra Gentiles in the thirteenth century for readers who did not share his Scripture, and he set out in the Summa Theologiae five ways of arguing from the world to God that philosophers still argue about. Blaise Pascal, a mathematician of the first rank, left at his death the notes published in 1670 as the Pensées, fragments of an apology addressed to the bored and the indifferent rather than to the hostile. Joseph Butler's Analogy of Religion (1736) answered the deists of the English Enlightenment by arguing from probability, and William Paley's Natural Theology (1802) gave the design argument the form most people still know. In the twentieth century C. S. Lewis turned a series of wartime BBC broadcasts into Mere Christianity (1952), and in the decades that followed analytic philosophy of religion experienced a revival few predicted, in the work of Alvin Plantinga, Richard Swinburne, William Lane Craig and others, so that the arguments this book rehearses are argued in the journals of the academy today and not only in church basements.

Two things follow from that history. The first is humility: nothing in this book is original in the sense of being new, and it would be suspect if it were, because a faith that needed a novel argument in the twenty-first century to be reasonable would not have been reasonable in the second. The second is confidence of a particular kind. The questions a modern skeptic asks are, with few exceptions, questions the church has heard before, from people at least as intelligent, and has answered in public, in writing, at cost. The reader is not being asked to trust a new idea. The reader is being invited into a very old conversation.

One Argument: The Reasonable Case for Christian Faith

The book began as a preaching series titled BELIEVE: Rational Answers to a Reasonable Faith, not an easier faith, not a louder faith, but the kind that survives contact with the intellect of a grown adult. Beside it sat a second project under the working title Secular Saints: The Gospel of Unbelief and the God It Cannot Escape, written to name a truth most moderns refuse to admit: unbelief is not the absence of a religion. It is a religion, with its own creeds, its own saints, its own funerals, and its own deep confessions of meaninglessness. The two projects were always the same argument, and they are folded here into one, the preaching cadence of the first carrying the polemical edge of the second.

The whole of it can be stated in a single breath. The universe began, and beginnings require beginners; it is fine-tuned in a way no honest physicist denies; the Bible is the best-attested document from antiquity by several orders of magnitude; and a Jewish rabbi named Jesus of Nazareth made claims about himself that no rabbi had made before and none has made credibly since, and he was killed for them. His closest followers, who had every reason to run, testified under torture that they had seen him alive again, and on the strength of that testimony they turned a carpenter's execution into a movement that outlasted the empire that ordered it.

The strongest objections (suffering, hell, science, other religions, hiddenness, hypocrisy) have already been answered by better thinkers than most readers have yet met, and the answers hold; meanwhile a godless universe cannot sustain what the human heart insists on wanting, the meaning and hope and love and identity and justice, the stubborn sense that a life matters and that the people one loves are not merely matter in motion. Christianity has answers. Atheism has attitudes. And at the center of the Christian answer stands not a concept but a Person who says:

Come to me.

Matthew 11:28

Christianity has answers. Atheism has attitudes.

The Method: A Cumulative Case

It matters how an argument like this is supposed to work, because much of the confusion between believers and skeptics is a confusion about method. Few serious defenders of Christianity have ever claimed that a single argument proves God the way a theorem is proved in geometry. The case made here is what the Oxford philosopher Basil Mitchell, in The Justification of Religious Belief (1973), called a cumulative case: many lines of evidence, none of which settles the question alone, which together make one explanation better than its rivals. John Henry Newman described the same habit of mind a century earlier in An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent (1870), where he observed that most of our firmest convictions in history, law and ordinary life rest not on demonstration but on the convergence of probabilities that point the same way.

Philosophers of science call the underlying form of reasoning inference to the best explanation. It is how a physician diagnoses, how a historian reconstructs, and how a jury reaches a verdict: by asking which account explains the most evidence with the fewest strained assumptions. That is why the structure of this book is a courtroom, and why it stands in a line that includes the Harvard jurist Simon Greenleaf, whose Testimony of the Evangelists (1846) examined the Gospels by the rules of legal evidence. The reader should judge the case the same way. The question at each stage is not whether a given argument compels assent by itself, but whether the evidence taken together is better explained if Christianity is true than if it is false.

The method also sets limits on what the book claims. A cumulative case can be strong without being coercive, and it leaves room for a reasonable person to weigh the evidence differently. It is also open to correction: if a line of evidence fails, the case is weakened at that point, and an honest apologist says so. That is the standard to which these pages hold themselves, and the standard by which the reader is asked to hold them.

Faith and Reason: What This Book Does Not Assume

A great deal of modern unbelief rests on a definition of faith that the Christian tradition has never accepted: faith as believing without evidence, or against it. The New Testament word pistis means trust, the kind of confidence a person places in someone who has proved reliable, and the tradition that runs from Augustine through Anselm described the Christian life as faith seeking understanding. Faith in that sense is not the opposite of reason. It is what reason does when the evidence is good enough to act on and not good enough to remove every doubt, which is the situation of every human being in every important decision of life.

Nor does this book assume that a person needs arguments before belief in God can be rational. Alvin Plantinga and the school of Reformed epistemology have argued that belief in God can be properly basic, rational in the way that belief in other minds or in the reality of the past is rational, without being inferred from anything more certain. That position deserves a hearing, and many believers who have never read an apologetics book are not irrational for lacking one. But the reader this book has in mind has asked for reasons, and so reasons are what it offers. It treats fideism, the view that faith needs no reasons at all, as a retreat the church has no need to make, and it treats rationalism, the view that only what can be demonstrated may be believed, as a standard no one actually lives by.

The Setting: Belief in a Secular Age

The case is made in a particular cultural moment, and it is worth naming. The philosopher Charles Taylor, in A Secular Age (2007), argued that the deepest change of the modern West is not that fewer people believe in God, though that is true, but that belief has become one option among others, and an option that has to be defended, in a way it did not have to be in 1500. Most modern people, believers included, live inside what Taylor calls the immanent frame, a picture of the world in which nature is a closed system of causes and meaning is something human beings make rather than receive. The questions of this book are asked from inside that frame, and part of its work is to show that the frame itself is an assumption and not a discovery.

That is where the earlier project, Secular Saints, still speaks. Secularity did not end the human need for a creed, a cause, a community and a hope; it moved them. The modern world has its own doctrines of the self, its own saints and martyrs, its own rituals of confession and exclusion, and its own eschatology of progress. To see that unbelief is a faith of its own is not to score a point against it. It is to level the field, so that Christianity and its rivals can be compared as what they are: rival accounts of what is real, what is good, and what a human being is for.

Who This Book Is For: Skeptics, Doubting Christians, and Seekers

Three readers are in view. The first is the skeptic, told explicitly or otherwise that faith requires checking one's brain at the door, and right to have refused, because no reader here is asked to hold opinions at odds with the evidence; the aim is to show what the evidence actually is. Disagreement is welcome, and there will be places the skeptic judges to be wrong, but the single request is to hear the case rather than the caricature, to steel-man Christianity exactly as this book will steel-man the best version of the objections raised against it.

The second is the Christian under pressure, the one who has believed for years, taken it on faith in the best sense of that phrase, and then felt it begin to crumble after a conversation, a lecture, a hard loss, or a question from a child. Such a reader wants neither to lose the faith nor to keep it dishonestly, and the purpose here is to give what is already believed something solid to stand on: not a feeling, and not a tradition alone, but a case.

The third is the searcher, who would claim neither skepticism nor belief but suspects there is something real behind the claims of Christianity, tired of the cheap versions, tired of the hostile versions, and willing to meet serious apologetics without the sales pitch. To all three, the same welcome, and the same promise: a case made with the reader, never at the reader.

How the Case for God Is Built, Step by Step

Part One establishes the foundation, because before Jesus, the Bible, or the resurrection comes the prior question: does God exist at all? If not, the rest is decoration; if so, what should such a God be expected to do? So Part One deals with the cosmos and the canon: why there is something rather than nothing, and why the Bible is the most reasonable place to look for what that Something wants to say.

Part Two turns to the person. A rabbi walked Galilee two thousand years ago, made astonishing claims about himself, and died in a way that should have buried those claims forever, and it did not. Part Two examines who Jesus said he was and whether the event on which he staked everything actually happened.

Part Three faces the hard questions, the ones that keep honest people awake at night and push honest people out of the church (suffering, hell, science, the people who never heard), and none of them is dodged; each meets the best answers the Christian tradition has produced alongside the best steel-manned version of the objection that can be written against it. Part Four then delivers the verdict: why Christianity and not other religions, why atheism cannot sustain what the human heart requires, every remaining objection answered. And then comes the one question no author can answer for the reader, because the verdict belongs to the jury: what does God actually want from you?

What an Honest Apologetic Owes the Reader

The reader is owed honesty about what cannot be known. Some questions in Christian theology will not be settled inside one book, and some not inside a lifetime (the mechanics of providence and human freedom, the precise metaphysics of the atonement, the exact timeline of eschatology), and these are held here with conviction and with humility, so that where the answer is unknown, the text will say so plainly.

The reader is owed fair play with the other side. The atheist writers gathered here (Russell, Nietzsche, Camus, Sartre, Hitchens, Dawkins) are given their best arguments rather than their worst, quoted in context and never caricatured so that the Christian case might look stronger by comparison, because a case that needs that kind of help is not worth making. And the reader is owed the cost of the claim, for Christianity does not offer a better mood or a more manageable life; it offers a cross before it offers a resurrection, and to hide that would be to sell something other than what Jesus was selling.

In return, only this: read the whole thing before deciding. Do not stop at the chapter that unsettles, or at the chapter that comforts, but let the case build, because the witnesses do not testify in isolation; they testify together. Bring the question you have been afraid to ask, the one you would not raise in Bible study for fear of how it would sound, the one you tried to talk yourself out of, because nothing in these pages crumbles when pressed honestly, and if it would crumble, it is not worth believing.

Read slowly. An apologetics book is not a devotional, but it is not an engineering manual either, and some of it will need to sit for a day before it moves; give it the day. To the doubting Christian reading from inside the house rather than outside it, where the sermons stopped landing and the prayers felt like talking to the ceiling, one distinction is worth more than any reassurance: doubt is not the opposite of faith. Certainty is. Faith is what one does without certainty, deciding to move anyway, and Thomas did it, and David did it in the Psalms of lament, and Habakkuk did it, and Mother Teresa did it in her dark night. None of them had their doubts answered before they kept going; they kept going, and the answers found them in pieces along the way. This book is not an attempt to erase doubt. It is an attempt to give doubt better food.

To the skeptic, no trick is intended and no emotional trapdoor will be sprung, only an argument, with claims stated and reasons offered and the reader invited to weigh whether the reasons hold. Disagree with the conclusions, by all means, but disagree after hearing them rather than before, because what has moved more than one person off of atheism was not a bad day or a tragedy but a question their framework could not answer, put to a Christian who answered it without blinking and without trying to convert. This book hopes to be that Christian for you.

The witnesses are ready, the documents are in evidence, and the objections will be steel-manned before they are answered. The verdict, at the end, will be yours. The argument is waiting on the next page.
