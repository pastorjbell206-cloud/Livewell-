Conclusion

The Table Is Bigger Than the Argument

Why the gospel refuses to reduce people to issues — belonging, transformation, and costly hope.

The table is bigger than your brokenness. The table is bigger than your questions. The table is bigger than your failures.

Belonging Comes Before Transformation

I grew up without a father. That is important to know as I close this book, because my story shapes how I see the kingdom of God. I know what it means to not belong; I know what it means to look for love in the wrong places because I did not have it at home; I know what it means to build yourself around an absence. And I know, because someone showed me, what it means to be welcomed into a community that has no obligation to welcome you but does anyway.

The gospel met me in that place of not belonging and said: You have a Father. You have a family. You belong at this table. The table is set for you. That is the message of the gospel. Not: you need to get your theology right before you belong. Not: there are certain people we cannot accept here. Not: belonging is conditional on your agreement with us. But: you belong. Come. Sit. The table is bigger than your brokenness, bigger than your questions, bigger than your failures.

Now, the gospel also says something else. It says that belonging to this table comes with cost, that following Jesus requires transformation, that the gospel is not just acceptance but reformation: that there are patterns for living that lead to flourishing and patterns that lead to death. But those two things are not in conflict. You can belong to the table and still be challenged to change; you can be welcomed completely and still be called to something harder. In fact, that is what grace is: the offer of belonging without condition, paired with the invitation to transformation.

The church has gotten confused about this. We have made people pass moral tests before they belong, saying first agree with us, first prove you are serious, first show you are changing, and then you can come in. That is not how grace works.

Where the Church Got It Wrong

Let me be specific about where the church has gone wrong on LGBTQ+ issues. We have made sexual behavior a marker of belonging. You can get divorced three times and still be a pastor; you can commit adultery, face a few months of discipline, and return to leadership. But if you acknowledge same-sex attraction, if you pursue a same-sex relationship, the church often responds with permanent exile. That is selective morality. That is not justice. That is tribal preference dressed up as biblical conviction.

We have been cruel. Not all of us, and not always, but the church has been cruel. We have said terrible things about LGBTQ+ people; we have preached their damnation from our pulpits; we have laughed at jokes about them; we have told their children they were disgusting, and we have done it in Jesus' name. I cannot apologize enough for that, and I cannot separate myself from it. The church is us. We have done this, not just leaders, not just pastors. We have done it. And it was wrong.

We have weaponized Scripture, taking texts that are complex and contextual and hammering them flat to say what we wanted them to say, treating people as problems to be solved rather than as image-bearers to be loved. And we have done all of this while claiming to follow Jesus, who was known for being kind to the marginalized, for eating with sinners, for challenging the religious authorities on their judgmental hardness.

What the Gospel Actually Says

But here is what the gospel actually says, not what the church has said in its worst moments, not what we have filtered through our culture and our prejudice, but what Jesus actually says. Jesus says the kingdom of God is at hand; it is available to you; it is not in the future; it is now. Jesus says the last will be first, the excluded will be included, those the world despises will be honored in the kingdom. Jesus says: I did not come to judge the world, I came to save it. Jesus says: love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you. Jesus says: as often as you did it to the least of these, you did it to me.

Come to me, all you who are weary and heavy-laden, and I will give you rest.

Matthew 11:28

Does that sound like judgment? Does that sound like exclusion? Does that sound like a boundary-setting, tribal-defending Messiah? No. It sounds like someone who is subversively inclusive, someone who eats with sinners, who touches lepers, who talks to women who should be beneath his notice, who welcomes the stranger and the marginal and the rejected. Now, Jesus also said things about repentance. He said things about being born again, about taking up your cross. He called for transformation. But he called for it from within belonging, not as a condition for belonging. He said, "Come," not "Become, and then come."

What I hope you have learned in reading this book is several things. First, Scripture is complex; the texts that address same-sex behavior do not speak with one voice; they require careful exegesis, attention to context, theological reflection. Smart, faithful people have read these texts and come to different conclusions, not because they do not respect Scripture, but because Scripture is genuinely complex. Second, the traditional case for sexual ethics is defensible; you can read Scripture carefully, attend to its teaching, and conclude that sex belongs within male-female marriage. That is a legitimate reading. But it is not the only reading, and it is not as self-evident as some have claimed.

Third, the church has failed LGBTQ+ people, not just disagreed with them, but failed them, harmed them. This is historical fact, and it demands repentance. Fourth, LGBTQ+ people can be Christian; there is no such thing as a person too broken or too identified by the wrong desire for Jesus to love. The gospel is for everyone, and LGBTQ+ Christians are part of the body of Christ. Fifth, the culture war frame has destroyed our credibility. We have made this issue a tribal marker, made it political, made it into a battle, and in doing so we have betrayed the gospel.

Costly Hope, Not Cheap Comfort

This book has tried to offer costly hope. Not cheap comfort. Not easy answers. Not a way to avoid the hard questions. Costly hope that says the gospel is true, Scripture is authoritative, Jesus is Lord, and all of that is big enough to hold LGBTQ+ people in it, not as a problem to be solved but as beloved children of God who are part of the family.

Costly hope that says you may not get what you want; you may be called to sacrifices you did not expect; life with Jesus is not a life of comfort. But it is a life of meaning, a life in community, a life where your suffering is not wasted. Costly hope that says the church is going to hurt you sometimes, the church is going to be stupid sometimes, the church is going to fail, but do not let the church's failure make you cynical about the gospel, because the gospel is bigger than the church.

Costly hope that says there is room at the table for people who disagree. There is room for those who hold traditional theology and those who hold affirming theology; there is room for your questions and your doubts and your confusion. That room is not comfortable. But it is real.

An Invitation to Every Reader

This conclusion is also an invitation. If you hold traditional theology, I am inviting you to hold it with conviction and with kindness simultaneously. Do not let the culture's acceptance of same-sex behavior make you afraid of your conviction, but do not let your conviction make you cruel. Love LGBTQ+ people as yourself. Create community that supports those who are faithfully following Jesus within a traditional sexual ethic. Build a church where people can disagree on this issue while remaining united in Christ.

If you hold affirming theology, I am inviting you to engage Scripture seriously and to not pretend it says things it does not say. Do not dismiss the traditional reading as obviously wrong; engage with it, wrestle with it, make your case carefully, and hold your view with humility about the fact that you are interpreting Scripture in a way the church has not done for most of its history.

If you are LGBTQ+ and trying to figure out what it means to be Christian, I want you to know that you belong. Not when you have resolved everything. Not when you have gotten your theology right. Not when you have proven yourself worthy. You belong now. The gospel is for you. The table has a place with your name on it. And if you love someone LGBTQ+ and you do not know what to do, I want you to know that love comes first, always first; theology later, community later, but love first. If you can get that right, you will figure out the rest.

The Table Is Set

The gospel is a scandal. It is scandalous that God would become human, scandalous that God would die, scandalous that God would welcome sinners, scandalous that the least and the lost and the left-behind get priority in the kingdom of God. If the gospel is not scandalous, then it has been domesticated: made safe, made respectable, and it has lost its power.

The church's response to LGBTQ+ people has sometimes been scandalously harsh, sometimes scandalously accommodating. But the scandal we should be offering is the scandal of the gospel itself: the thorough inclusion, the endless grace, the invitation that says the table is bigger than your theology, bigger than your sin, bigger than your fear. Come. That is the gospel. That is the only message worth offering.

The debates about biblical interpretation matter. The questions about what Scripture teaches are important. The theology is not unimportant. But if in the end we get the theology right and the love wrong, we have missed the whole point.

By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another.

John 13:35

Not "if you get the doctrine right." Not "if you defend the traditional position." Not "if you stand against the culture." If you love one another. That is the scandal of the gospel. That is the scandal of grace. And that is the only scandal worth living for. The table is set. Your name is written in. Come.
