Chapter 4

Romans 1:26-27 Explained

Paul, natural law, and the strongest text in the debate — read carefully, including the trap Paul sets in Romans 2.

Romans 1 is serious theological argument, and the work cannot be done in a slogan.

The Traditional Reading of Romans 1

Romans 1 is the most important passage in the entire biblical discussion of same-sex relationships, because it is where Paul does serious theological work. This is not a law code; it is an apostle thinking out the implications of idolatry and disobedience.

Let me state the traditional reading clearly: Paul describes same-sex sexual behavior as a consequence of idolatry. Those who reject God are given over to dishonorable passions; women exchange natural relations for those contrary to nature, and men do the same, receiving the due penalty for their error. This is presented not as cultural observation but as theological truth grounded in God's judgment and in the created order.

That reading has serious exegetical support. It is not a weak argument. It is the strongest argument in the entire biblical case for a traditional sexual ethic, and it is also the passage where the most sophisticated affirming arguments are made. This is where the real conversation happens.

For this reason God gave them up to dishonorable passions. For their women exchanged natural relations for those that are contrary to nature; and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women and were consumed with passion for one another, men committing what is shameful with men and receiving in themselves the due penalty for their error.

Romans 1:26-27

In the traditional reading, "nature" refers to the created order. God made humanity male and female, and designed sexuality for procreation within the male-female covenant; to exchange that design for same-sex relations is to act contrary to nature, that is, contrary to God's created design. The behavior is presented as disordered, as evidence of humanity's rebellion against the Creator. This interpretation assumes Paul is making a statement about the essential nature of same-sex relations: that all such relations are contrary to created order and therefore sinful.

What "Contrary to Nature" (Para Phusin) Meant to Paul

The key phrase is "contrary to nature." The Greek is para phusin. What does "nature" mean here? That is the entire question, and it is where the real exegetical work happens, both for Paul and for the audience who first heard him.

Paul's phrase appears elsewhere in his writings. In 1 Corinthians 11:14 he says it is shameful for a man to have long hair, because nature teaches that this is not fitting. By modern standards, long hair on men is not contrary to nature at all; men can have long hair, and many cultures have emphasized long hair as masculine. So what did Paul mean by "nature"? In that context, he means cultural norms: what your culture understands as natural and fitting. By that reading, "contrary to nature" in Romans 1 might mean "contrary to what your culture understands as natural," not "contrary to the created order."

But there is a stronger reading. The Stoics, whose philosophy shaped Paul's thinking, had a concept of natural law grounded in cosmic order; to act para phusin was to act against the fundamental structure of reality. The Stoics believed in a rational order built into the universe; everything had a purpose, everything had a proper function, and to act against that purpose was to act irrationally and in the end destructively. For them, procreation was a primary purpose of sexuality, and the complementarity of male and female was part of that design. Same-sex behavior did not serve that purpose, and therefore was para phusin, against nature.

Did Paul embrace Stoic natural law thinking? To some extent, yes. He uses the concept of nature several times, and in 1 Corinthians 11:14 he appeals to nature to argue that long hair is unbecoming for men, assuming that nature teaches what is fitting. Yet what is he really saying there? He is appealing to cultural norms: in Paul's culture, long hair on men was considered feminine and socially unacceptable, so nature, understood as cultural custom, teaches that this is unfitting.

Does that mean when Paul appeals to nature in Romans 1 he is making the same kind of argument: that cultural custom teaches same-sex relations are unfitting? Or is he appealing to something deeper, to cosmic design and created order? The text does not tell us explicitly; we infer it from Paul's theological commitments elsewhere. Paul does appeal to creation, Genesis 1-2, in discussing sexual ethics in other passages, and in 1 Corinthians 6 he grounds sexual ethics in the created body and its purpose. So it is reasonable to infer that when Paul says same-sex relations are para phusin, he is making a claim about creation design, not just cultural preference.

Idolatry, Judgment, and the Structure of Paul's Argument

Consider the structure of Paul's argument. Romans 1 is not primarily about cataloguing sexual sin; it is about establishing that all humanity, both Gentiles and Jews, stands under God's judgment. Paul is working through the logic of idolatry: when people reject God, they are given over to spiritual blindness, and that blindness manifests in perverted behavior. The sexual behavior Paul describes is presented as evidence of this spiritual blindness, not as the primary sin itself. Same-sex behavior functions as a sign that something else has gone wrong; people have rejected God, and therefore they have been given over to dishonorable passions, one of which is same-sex desire.

Now the question. Is Paul saying that same-sex desire itself is evidence of rejecting God, or that when people reject God they may be given over to various disordered desires, including same-sex desire? There is a difference. The traditional answer is the former: same-sex desire is inherently contrary to nature, and engaging in it is evidence of spiritual rebellion; the behavior itself violates the created order. The affirming answer is more careful: what if some people have a same-sex orientation that is not the result of rejecting God but is part of their given nature, and what if Paul is not addressing orientation at all, but addressing people who are acting contrary to their own nature?

Consider, too, what Paul says about the penalty: "receiving in themselves the due penalty for their error." The Greek is literally "receiving in themselves the recompense of their deception"; Paul is saying they experience consequences for their disobedience. What consequences? Some have interpreted this as referring to AIDS or other sexually transmitted infections that follow promiscuous behavior, but that reading is historically anachronistic; Paul is not thinking in those categories. More likely he is referring to the spiritual and social consequences of sin: the emptiness, the alienation, the spiritual blindness that comes from rejecting God. That is the recompense. And crucially, Paul is not saying everyone who experiences same-sex desire is experiencing God's judgment; he is saying that when people reject God and are given over to disordered passions, one manifestation of that may be same-sex behavior.

Step back from the sexual specifics and the theological move is clear: Paul's point is about idolatry and judgment. When humans turn away from God, they are given over to disordered desires; the specific desires vary, but they all flow from the fundamental disorder of rejecting the Creator. In that frame, same-sex behavior is presented as one manifestation of spiritual blindness. What is clear is that Paul opposes the behavior he describes, presenting it as contrary to nature and as resulting from idolatry. What is less clear is whether he is making a universal claim about all same-sex relations or a specific claim about the sexual behavior he observed in the context of pagan idolatry.

The Orientation Question Paul Did Not Face

The affirming interpretation relies on the assumption that sexual orientation is a fixed, given reality for some people, and that assumption requires a modern category Paul did not have. Paul lived in a world where same-sex behavior was understood as a choice, something you did, not something you were. He did not have our understanding of sexual orientation. So when he uses the language of "contrary to nature," what is he referencing? If he did not understand sexual orientation as a fixed reality, was he referring to some other kind of "nature"?

This is where the conversation becomes genuinely complex. The traditional interpretation assumes Paul is making a universal statement grounded in creation theology: the created order is male-female complementarity, and same-sex relations violate that order. The affirming interpretation suggests Paul is making a culturally specific observation about people acting against their own experienced nature: people assumed to have a heterosexual orientation, as all people were then assumed to have, who were choosing to act against it. Which interpretation is correct depends partly on exegetical precision and partly on theological assumptions.

If all same-sex desire is inherently disordered, if it is always a consequence of rejecting God, then Paul's argument is that same-sex orientation itself is evidence of spiritual rebellion, and you cannot have a same-sex orientation that is not the result of turning away from God. But what if some people have a same-sex orientation that is not the result of spiritual rebellion, but is simply how they are created? Then Paul's argument might not apply to them in the same way. He would be describing people acting against their orientation; we would be describing people acting according to their orientation.

The traditional interpretation says this does not matter: the created design for sexuality stands regardless of how individuals experience desire. Whether someone is naturally drawn to the same sex or not, the created order is male-female, and that design is not suspended by individual experience. The affirming interpretation says it matters significantly: if Paul did not have the category of fixed sexual orientation, he might not be addressing the question of LGBTQ+ people in committed relationships at all, but a specific historical phenomenon that does not directly parallel modern same-sex relationships.

Greco-Roman Sexual Practice and Pederasty

The affirming challenge, in its most sophisticated form, runs like this: Paul is describing what happened in the specific historical context of the ancient world, where same-sex behavior involved exploitation, pederasty, and the violation of social norms. He is condemning that specific behavior, not making a universal statement about all same-sex relations, especially not about modern relationships characterized by mutual love and commitment. He is not addressing people who understand themselves as having a fixed orientation toward the same sex; he is addressing people who have abandoned their natural orientation and are acting contrary to their own nature. It is not that Paul approves of same-sex behavior; it is that he is addressing a specific cultural phenomenon in response to idolatry, and we cannot extrapolate from that to all same-sex relationships in all contexts.

The Greco-Roman world had forms of same-sex behavior that were culturally sanctioned. Pederasty, the relationship between an older man and a younger adolescent male, was common, often framed as educational and mentoring even though it involved sex. It was exploitative by modern standards; the young man, often the son of another man, could not meaningfully consent. There was also same-sex behavior between adult men, though Greek culture viewed this with more suspicion and the Romans were somewhat more permissive. But across the ancient world, same-sex behavior was generally understood as behavior, not as identity. There was no category of "homosexual person"; there were behaviors people engaged in, often in specific contexts: pederasty, prostitution, the worship of fertility gods.

Paul would have been aware of pederasty. It was common enough that it appears in the Sibylline Oracles, the Jewish warnings against the sexual practices of Gentiles. He would have known that when he condemned same-sex sexual behavior, he was condemning behavior understood as inherently exploitative or as idolatrous. The question is whether Paul thought the same condemnation applied to same-sex relationships that were consensual and between adults. We have no evidence he ever encountered such a thing; that was not a category available in his world.

The Creation-Order Argument and Its Limits

The traditional interpretation has real strength. If Paul is appealing to created design (if he believes the complementarity of male and female is fundamental, if he believes sexuality has a design purpose) then his argument applies universally. It is not culture-bound; it is grounded in the nature of creation. This is a serious claim about how God designed sexuality, and it cannot be dismissed as merely cultural preference.

But the argument also has limits. First, if Paul is appealing to nature as cultural custom, then the argument only applies to Paul's culture, and what his culture viewed as unnatural might not be unnatural elsewhere. Second, if Paul did not have a category of sexual orientation, he might not be addressing people with a fixed same-sex orientation seeking committed relationships, but people acting against their own natural orientation, and that is different. Third, the appeal to nature in other cases, like long hair, seems to reference cultural norms rather than cosmic design, so how do we know which kind of nature Paul is appealing to in Romans 1? Fourth, even if Paul is appealing to creation design, the question remains what the purpose of sexuality is: if it is primarily procreative, same-sex relations violate that purpose, but if sexuality has multiple purposes (intimacy, mutual flourishing, covenant), the question becomes more complex.

Honest exegesis acknowledges several things at once. Paul opposes same-sex sexual behavior, presenting it as contrary to nature and as a consequence of idolatry; that is clear. His argument appears to appeal to creation and nature in a way that suggests a universal claim rather than mere cultural observation; that is defensible. Yet the nature of "nature" in Paul's thinking is complex, because he appeals to both cosmic design and cultural norms, and determining which he means requires inference. Even if Paul is making a universal claim about the sinfulness of same-sex relations, the question of how that claim applies to modern people with same-sex orientation seeking committed relationships is not directly addressed by him.

The honest assessment is this. Romans 1:26-27 is most naturally read as Paul making a theological claim about same-sex behavior rooted in creation theology; the phrase "contrary to nature" suggests an appeal to the created order, not merely to individual choice or cultural norm, and if you read the text carefully, the traditional interpretation has the weight of the language on its side. The traditional position is stronger than some affirming scholars admit: Paul is not simply making a cultural observation, but a theological claim grounded in nature and creation. But the affirming interpretation is not biblically indefensible; it requires careful argument about historical context and the limits of Paul's knowledge, yet it is not a misreading of the text. What neither side can do is pretend this is simpler than it is. Romans 1 is serious theological argument that deserves serious exegetical work, and the work cannot be done in a slogan. Honest people, reading carefully, will sometimes disagree about what Paul is finally asserting.

How Romans 2:1 Reverses Romans 1

Romans 1 is often read in isolation. Someone reads Paul's condemnation of same-sex behavior in chapter 1, and that becomes the foundation for their theology. But if you read Paul carefully, if you understand his rhetorical strategy, you discover something stunning: Paul sets a trap, and most readers of chapter 1 walk right into it. That is the structure.

Romans 1:18-32 describes Gentiles who have rejected God; they worship idols and exchange the truth for a lie, and the consequence is that God gives them over to dishonorable passions, including same-sex sexual behavior. The passage is dense with judgment. Paul begins with the Gentiles, and the audience, both Jewish and Gentile Christians in Rome, agrees with the condemnation. Of course God's judgment falls on idolatry. Of course sexual immorality is a sign of that judgment. Read chapter 1 in isolation, and it seems like a straightforward condemnation of same-sex behavior. Then Paul moves to chapter 2, and everything shifts.

Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, are doing the same things.

Romans 2:1

Who is Paul talking to? He is talking to the person who has just agreed with him, the reader who has assented to the judgment in chapter 1. By the end of chapter 1 the reader has said: Yes, Paul, I agree; those people (the idolaters, the sexually immoral) deserve God's judgment, and I am right to condemn them. Then Paul turns and says: But you, the one judging, are guilty of the same thing. He has gotten the reader to position themselves as the judge, then made clear that by judging, the reader has condemned themselves. The reader expects more condemnation of the Gentiles; instead, the argument turns, and the judge becomes the judged.

The specific sexual behaviors described in chapter 1 are illustrations of a larger point about human judgment and human hypocrisy. The point is not primarily about same-sex behavior; it is about the human tendency to judge others while ignoring your own failures. Same-sex behavior is one manifestation of spiritual disorder, but it is not the only one; the passage also mentions greed, envy, murder, strife, deceit, slander. Sin, for Paul, is at its core about relationship with God, not about the specific behaviors. By chapter 2 the point becomes clearer: the sin is not the behavior alone but the judgment itself, the self-righteous stance of condemning others while remaining blind to your own failure. That is why Romans 2:1 is so important; it shows that Paul's focus in Romans 1 is not primarily on the sexual behaviors, but on setting up an argument about judgment.

If Paul's rhetorical strategy is what matters, if the point is about judgment and hypocrisy, then using Romans 1 as a straightforward condemnation of same-sex behavior misses Paul's argument. You cannot cite Romans 1:26-27 to condemn same-sex behavior without also hearing Romans 2:1, and if you hear Romans 2:1, the application becomes different. Paul is not giving you ammunition to condemn others; he is setting a trap that catches anyone who tries to use Scripture to condemn others. By condemning, you condemn yourself. This is deeply uncomfortable, because it suggests that the traditional use of Romans 1 in arguments against same-sex behavior might itself be the kind of self-righteous judgment Paul was criticizing. It does not mean Paul is affirming same-sex behavior, and it does not mean the traditional interpretation is obviously wrong, but it does mean Paul's rhetorical strategy is more complex than proof-texting suggests.

The traditionalist might respond fairly: you are right that Romans 2:1 matters, but Paul is addressing different sins. In chapter 1 he condemns the Gentiles for idolatry and immorality; in chapter 2 he condemns the Jews for breaking the law while remaining self-righteous. These are different sins, and acknowledging that the Jewish reader is also a sinner does not invalidate the condemnation of the Gentiles. Paul can condemn both idolatry and self-righteous judgment; the fact that the judge is also a sinner does not make the sin of the judged acceptable. But what changes is the use of the passage; once you recognize that Paul is critiquing judgment itself, you become more careful about using Romans 1 as a basis for your own judgment of others.

The pastoral implication is significant. If you are someone who has used Romans 1 to condemn LGBTQ+ people, Romans 2:1 is directly applicable to you; you have been the judge, and Paul is saying you stand under the same judgment. This does not tell you whether traditional or affirming theology is correct, but it tells you something about the posture you should take, not moral superiority, but humility, a recognition that you stand under judgment too. The person who condemns same-sex behavior must also be prepared to be condemned for their own failures. You can hold traditional theology about sexuality, but you must hold it knowing that Romans 2:1 applies to you: you are not in a position of moral superiority but a sinner standing under grace, just as the LGBTQ+ person is a sinner standing under grace.

The real question is not whether Paul opposes the same-sex behavior he describes in Romans 1. He clearly does. The question is what we conclude from that about same-sex relationships characterized by mutual love, commitment, and covenant, relationships Paul almost certainly never encountered and could not have imagined in his first-century context. That is the question each Christian must answer, in submission to Scripture, in conversation with the church, and with genuine love for the people involved. That does not resolve the theological question, but it reframes it. And the reframing matters.
