Chapter 8

The Church's Catastrophic Failure

How Christians have harmed LGBTQ+ people — conversion therapy, family rejection, and the case for real repentance.

Before we continue with the theological conversation, something has to be said that the church has avoided saying for decades. We have sinned against LGBTQ+ people. Not just disagreed. Not just held a different theological position. We have sinned, we have harmed, we have used our theology as a weapon, and we have driven people away from Christ by making Jesus look small and cruel. We have blood on our hands.

I am not making a theological argument here. I am making a pastoral observation.

I need to be clear about what this is. I am speaking from twenty years of ministry, from conversations with people whose lives have been fractured by the church's response to their sexuality, and what follows is not a verdict on which theology is correct but an accounting of what the church has done to real people in the name of Jesus.

Conversion Therapy and Spiritual Violence

Let me start with the most explicit form of harm. For decades the church endorsed, and in many cases ran, programs that claimed to change a person's sexual orientation; these programs rested on the assumption that same-sex attraction was a disorder that could be cured. The evidence now is overwhelming and undeniable. Conversion therapy does not work. It does not change sexual orientation. What it does is cause deep psychological damage; it increases rates of depression, anxiety, and suicide, it teaches people to hate themselves, and it teaches them that their core identity is broken, that they are at their core unlovable, that God rejects them.

The American Psychological Association, the American Medical Association, the American Psychiatric Association, and virtually every credible mental health organization has concluded that conversion therapy is harmful and should not be practiced. It is banned in many states, and many churches have repudiated it. But repudiation without full accountability is not enough. We have to name what happened: the church taught parents to take their gay children to people who would psychologically harm them in the name of Jesus. We did that. We said we were saving souls, but we were actually breaking them.

There are people alive right now whose mental health is damaged because their church sent them to conversion therapy. There are people who attempted suicide because they believed the church's message about their sexuality, and some of them did not survive. Those deaths are on us, not in an abstract way, but in a specific, real way.

Selective Morality and the Divorce Double Standard

Conversion therapy is only the most explicit form of harm. Less explicit but far more common is the simple message that lands on a person before any program ever does: you are not welcome here, you are broken, you are an abomination, and if you do not change you cannot be part of this community. I have counseled teenagers whose parents threw them out of the house when they came out. I have watched families implode because a church taught parents that their child's homosexuality was a spiritual emergency. I have known young people who decided they would rather die than live with that rejection.

The church did not always intend that outcome, but intent does not change the reality. We created an environment in which LGBTQ+ teenagers in Christian homes face suicide rates multiple times higher than their peers. That is not accidental. That is the result of our teaching.

We have also been selective about our moral outrage. A straight pastor divorces his wife, remarries, and continues in ministry with a few words of repentance and the support of his congregation; a teenager comes out as gay, and families are shattered; a gay couple seeks to marry, and the church mobilizes its full moral machinery, but a divorced pastor is not an issue. That selective morality is not accidental either. It is hidden homophobia dressed up as biblical faithfulness, one standard for the sexual sins we are comfortable with and another for the ones that make us uncomfortable.

Some of the harm is theological. We have built entire systems designed to make LGBTQ+ people feel unwelcome, preaching that their identity is incompatible with Christianity and suggesting they must choose between their sexuality and their faith. The person who holds traditional theology might argue that the theology is true and therefore teaching it is not violence. But there is a difference between teaching a conviction and wielding it as a weapon. There is a way to teach traditional sexual ethics with humility, with love, with honest acknowledgment of the cost it asks of people; and there is a way to teach it with cruelty: to emphasize judgment, to express disgust, to make clear that you view LGBTQ+ people as less than. We have done both, sometimes in the same church, and the cruel version has dominated. The consequence is that LGBTQ+ people have learned that Christianity is not for them, that Jesus is not for them, that the gospel is not for them. That is a failure. Not a theological failure. A pastoral failure, a failure of love.

The Cost of Family Rejection

One more thing has to be named: we have told parents to reject their children. Not always explicitly, but the message has been clear, if your child is gay, you are responsible for getting them to repent, and if they refuse, you may need to separate from them to maintain your witness. I have sat with parents who followed that instruction, and I have watched them grow old in the grief of having severed their relationship with their child because they believed it was the Christian thing to do. And then I have sat with those adult children and felt the weight of their pain: the loss of family, the loss of belonging, the loss of the unconditional love that ought to characterize a parent's relationship with a child. That is not faithful theology being applied. That is theology being weaponized. That is the church bearing responsibility for fracturing families.

Maybe the most damaging thing is what we have not said. We have not said loudly and clearly: we were wrong, we harmed people, we used our theology as a weapon, we are sorry, and we are committed to repenting of this, which means changing not just our rhetoric but our actual stance. Instead we have offered soft statements about loving the sinner while hating the sin; we have offered pastoral tone while maintaining harsh theology; we have offered to welcome LGBTQ+ people while making clear that they need to change. That is not welcome. That is a bait-and-switch. Real repentance would mean acknowledging that we have sinned, expressing genuine sorrow for the harm caused, and making concrete changes in how we relate to LGBTQ+ people: providing support for Side B Christians living out traditional theology, and making sure our LGBTQ+ members know they are fully welcome, that they are not projects to be fixed, that their identity is not a problem to be solved.

Exodus International and Institutional Accountability

Exodus International was the largest and most visible conversion therapy organization in America. It was founded by Frank Worthen and Michael Bussee, who later came out and affirmed same-sex relationships, and for decades it claimed to help people change their sexual orientation. In 2012 Exodus International dissolved, and its president, Alan Chambers, issued an apology: he said conversion therapy does not work, and he apologized for the harm caused to the hundreds of people who had gone through Exodus programs and been damaged by them. That apology mattered. It was a public acknowledgment of harm. But an apology cannot undo the damage.

Conversion therapy was built on a simple claim: same-sex attraction is a disorder caused by family dysfunction, trauma, or rejection by God, and change is possible: through prayer, through counseling, through Christian discipline, orientation can shift. The evidence now is overwhelming that the claim was false. Sexual orientation does not change through prayer or therapy; what changes is the person's willingness to hide, to suppress, to perform heterosexuality. The damage is well documented. The American Psychological Association concluded that conversion therapy increases rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality, that it teaches people to hate themselves, that it teaches them their core identity is broken and that God rejects them as they are.

I have sat with people who went through it. I have listened to them describe the psychological damage and watched them try to rebuild their sense of self-worth after being taught that their desires were disgusting, that their identity was sinful, that change was possible but they were somehow failing to change. One woman I counseled spent fifteen years in a conversion therapy program: fifteen years of prayer, counseling, and group meetings, all premised on the idea that if she just tried hard enough her attraction to women would change. It did not. At some point she had to face the reality that the problem was not with her; the problem was with the premise that she needed to change. She left the program, and she also left the faith that had supported it. Years later she said something I have never forgotten: "The church did not just tell me I was wrong. They told me I was broken. That is not something you recover from quickly."

The church bears responsibility not only for the programs but for the theology that made them possible: the theology that said same-sex attraction is sinful, that change is possible, that if you have enough faith you will change. Not every pastor endorsed conversion therapy, but many taught the theology that made it seem necessary. And when people went through it and it did not work, the church's response was often to shame them: you did not try hard enough, you did not pray enough, your faith is not strong enough. The problem shifted from the practice to the person.

Exodus International is gone, but the theology that supported it persists. Many evangelical churches still teach that same-sex attraction is a temptation to be resisted, that change is possible, that the goal is heterosexual marriage or celibacy. That theology is not inevitably harmful (you can hold traditional theology while refusing conversion therapy) but the church must be clear about what it is asking and what it is not. If the church says that same-sex attraction is not in itself sinful, that the desire is not the problem, that what matters is how a person responds to it, then celibacy becomes a real option: you can accept yourself while choosing not to act on your desire. But if the church says that same-sex attraction is itself sinful and must be resisted, that the desire is evidence of spiritual disorder, then celibacy becomes just another form of conversion therapy, about suppression and self-denial and the message that something is wrong with you.

The Trevor Project and Suicide Data

The Trevor Project has collected extensive data on LGBTQ+ youth and mental health, and the data is sobering: LGBTQ+ youth who are rejected by their families or who come from religious communities that reject them have significantly higher rates of suicide attempts. The correlation is clear: religious rejection increases suicidality. That is not opinion. That is data.

The church has caused these deaths. Not always intentionally, but the effect is the same. We have taught parents to reject their children, we have preached messages of condemnation, we have made clear that LGBTQ+ youth are not welcome, and some of them have died as a result. That is a hard truth. But it is true.

Real accountability for this harm would look like four things. First, acknowledgment, not vague language about "those who have been hurt" but the specific admission that we taught conversion therapy is possible, that we taught same-sex attraction is sinful, that we taught parents to reject their children, that we made young people contemplate suicide. Second, remorse, not "I'm sorry if anyone was offended" but "We were wrong, we caused harm, we regret it." Third, structural change, not just different rhetoric but different systems, different preaching, different pastoral care, different support for LGBTQ+ people and families. Fourth, reparation: for conversion therapy survivors, resources to address trauma; for families separated by church teaching, support for reconciliation; for LGBTQ+ youth who lost their faith, the acknowledgment that the church did this. Most churches have not done this work. We have moved on, we have changed our rhetoric slightly, but we have not at the core reckoned with the harm we caused. Accountability is harder than moving on; it requires staying in the room with the pain we caused, changing systems that benefit those in power, spending money and resources and vulnerability. That is why most churches avoid it. But accountability is the only foundation for trust.

The Global South, Persecution, and Colonial Law

The conversation about LGBTQ+ people and Christianity in America happens in a bubble; it is a relatively privileged conversation. In many parts of the world LGBTQ+ people face legal persecution, violence, and death, and the American church has sometimes been complicit in that. In many African countries same-sex behavior is criminalized, and in some cases it is a capital offense. In Uganda, an Anti-Homosexuality Act prescribes long prison sentences for same-sex behavior; in Nigeria, penalties can include flogging or death under Sharia law.

Where is the American church in this? Some American evangelical organizations have supported these laws, some have sent missionaries who promoted them, some have provided theological justification for criminalization. I am not talking about churches that hold traditional theology; I am talking about churches and organizations that actively supported anti-gay legislation in the Global South. Scott Lively, an American evangelical pastor, is credibly accused of going to Uganda and encouraging the most severe anti-gay legislation, and others have provided theological support for criminalization. How is this consistent with the gospel? How is it anything other than betrayal? The church should be advocating for the rights and safety of LGBTQ+ people globally; instead, some parts of the church have actively promoted their persecution.

The church's role in colonialism is relevant here. Colonial powers brought Christianity to the Global South, and in many cases they also brought Western sexual ethics and taboos. The criminalization of same-sex behavior in many African countries is not traditional African law; it is British colonial law imposed during colonial rule. When American churches support the continuation of these laws, they are perpetuating colonial structures, saying, in effect, that the laws against same-sex people, which came from our colonization of these countries, should remain. That is a massive betrayal. The irony is cruel: colonial powers imposed the laws, post-colonial nations now maintain them, and Western churches support keeping them. We have no right.

Some argue that because traditional theology is the majority position in the Global South, supporting that theology means supporting anti-gay laws. That is a false equivalence. You can hold traditional theology about sexuality without supporting criminalization; you can believe same-sex behavior is sinful and still believe that people should not be imprisoned or killed for it. The question is not whether traditional theology exists globally (it does) but whether the church will support the legal persecution of people for their sexuality. The answer should be no. Unambiguously, no.

For American churches, that means several things. Acknowledge that the American church has contributed to this problem; we are not neutral observers, we have influence and responsibility. Refuse to support organizations that promote anti-gay legislation globally, if a denomination partners with organizations that support criminalization, withdraw support. Actively support organizations protecting LGBTQ+ people globally, using our resources, our platforms, our voices. And center the voices of LGBTQ+ people in the Global South, not American ideas about what they need, but their own leadership and their own strategy. The global persecution of LGBTQ+ people is one of the most serious human rights crises occurring, and the American church should be at the forefront of addressing it. Instead, some of us have been complicit in it. That is a deep failure, and repentance is long overdue.

What Real Repentance Looks Like

The church cannot move forward on this issue without repentance, not rhetorical repentance, but actual, costly repentance. That means acknowledging specific harm rather than general, vague language about "those who have been hurt": conversion therapy, family rejection, bullying, exclusion from ministry, the use of our theology as a weapon. It means expressing genuine remorse, not "I'm sorry if anyone was offended," which is not an apology, but "We were wrong, we harmed people, and we regret it." It means making restitution where possible, which is the hardest part, because you cannot undo harm, but you can provide real support, create genuine welcome, and honor people's identities even while you maintain theological disagreement about behavior. And it means committing to change: looking honestly at the language we use from the pulpit, examining whether our actions match our theology, and being willing to hold traditional theology while refusing the cruelty that has so often accompanied it.

None of this depends on whether you hold traditional or affirming theology. Any Christian position on this issue requires pastoral integrity, and that integrity has been lacking. The church has failed LGBTQ+ people. That is not debatable. That is historical fact. People have been harmed in the name of Jesus, and we who call ourselves followers of Jesus bear responsibility for that harm. Until we acknowledge it, repent of it, and change our actual behavior, we will not be able to speak credibly about what the Bible teaches on this subject; we will just be weaponizing Scripture again. The first word on this subject has to be repentance. Everything else follows from that.
