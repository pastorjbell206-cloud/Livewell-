Chapter 7

The Traditional Theology Case

The historic Christian sexual ethic, the Side B path of celibacy, and the support the church owes the people it asks to live it.

The strength of the traditional case depends partly on the theology. But it depends equally on whether the church is willing to actually pastor people in the light of that theology.

Now we reverse the angle and examine the traditional case. This is the position held by most Christian traditions throughout history; it is not the position held by the broadest culture, but it is the one the church has held for two thousand years, and it deserves to be understood fairly and presented in its strongest form. The traditional position rests on several interconnected claims, and to take it seriously means taking each of them in turn.

The Creation, Canonical, and Church-History Arguments

The first claim is the creation theology argument. Genesis 1 grounds the case not in feeling but in design, in what the text says God actually made.

So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them. And God blessed them and said to them, 'Be fruitful and multiply.'

Genesis 1:27-28

The traditional reading is that God created humanity as male and female, and that the complementarity of male and female is fundamental to the created design. Sexuality is designed for procreation and for the deepening of the covenant between husband and wife, and same-sex sexual relations violate that design. This is not arbitrary; it is grounded in what the text explicitly says God created. Male and female. The union of male and female within covenant produces both the fruit of children and the deepening bond of marriage, and that, the argument says, is the design.

This argument is further supported by Ephesians 5, where Paul describes marriage as the primary human relationship besides our relationship with God. He grounds it in Genesis 2, where God creates woman as a helper for the man, and then draws the conclusion forward.

Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.

Genesis 2:24

The design is male-female. That is the claim. The affirming response is that this reading assumes a heterosexual world and does not account for people with same-sex orientation; the traditional counter is that the argument was never about individual feeling or orientation in the first place; it is about what God created and designed, and the created order stands regardless of how individuals experience desire.

The second claim is the canonical argument, which examines the entire biblical witness on sexuality. Throughout Scripture the sexual relationship assumed is male-female; marriage is between man and woman, and sexual sin is defined against that norm. There is no scenario in all of Scripture, not one, where same-sex sexual relations are presented positively. This is a significant observation, because if same-sex relationships were acceptable you would expect to find at least one example, at least one affirmation, at least one scenario where they are held up as good; the traditional interpretation reads that consistent silence as evidence that such relations fall outside the boundaries God designed for human sexuality.

The affirming response is that absence of mention is not the same as condemnation: the Bible also does not mention electricity or technology, yet we do not conclude those things are sinful, and it does not mention committed same-sex relationships because that category did not exist in the ancient world. The traditional counter is that we have every reason to believe Scripture would reflect such relationships if they were within God's design; the unanimous witness against same-sex relations is not accidental, but reflects an underlying theological conviction about human sexuality.

The third claim is the church history argument. For two thousand years (across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions, across cultures and continents), the church has consistently taught that sex belongs within male-female marriage, and no Christian tradition affirmed same-sex relationships until very recently. This is not strictly a biblical argument, but it is significant, because the church's historic consensus carries weight and shows that the church has interpreted Scripture this way consistently across contexts.

The affirming response is that church history is also the history of the church being wrong about many things: the church defended slavery, oppressed women, and persecuted Jews, and the fact that it taught something for a long time does not make it right. That is a fair point. But the traditional counter draws a distinction between the church changing its mind on an application of principle and the church contradicting its foundational teaching. The move away from slavery was consistent with Scripture's trajectory toward the dignity of all image-bearers; the move toward affirming women in ministry is, arguably, consistent with Galatians 3:28 and other texts about equality; but the recent affirmation of same-sex relationships, the traditional position holds, represents a break from the basic teaching about the design of sexuality. Is that a problem? The affirming tradition says no: sometimes the church has to repent of its misreading. The traditional position says yes: this is a break from the canonical witness and the church's consistent teaching.

Complementarity and Natural Law

The fourth argument is complementarity. It holds that there is something unique about the male-female union: that men and women bring different gifts, different strengths, different perspectives to the relationship, and that the complementarity is not just biological but psychological and spiritual. The union of complementary difference, on this view, is part of what makes the sexual union meaningful and rich. This argument appeals to intuition and experience; many people feel that male-female relationships have something distinctive about them. The affirming position responds that this romanticizes difference, and that same-sex relationships have their own forms of difference and complementarity.

The fifth argument is based on natural law. The claim is that the body itself testifies to God's design: that the body of a man and the body of a woman are designed to fit together in a way that is unique, and that same-sex relations require the use of the body in ways contrary to its natural design. This is an older argument, sometimes rooted in natural law theory rather than biblical theology, and it has less purchase in our current context, where we are more skeptical of claims about "natural" design; still, it carries weight for some. The affirming response is that this argument proves too much: bodies have natural limitations, but we do not consider other uses of bodies to be immoral based on how they were designed, and that argument's edge gets more complicated the more you think about it.

What Side B Christianity Is

There is one more element of the traditional position worth special attention: Side B. Side B Christianity is a movement of LGBTQ+ Christians who hold traditional theology but do not believe that requires them to marry opposite-sex partners. They affirm traditional sexual ethics (that sex belongs within male-female marriage), yet they argue that for some people the call is to celibacy; they are gay or lesbian Christians who have chosen to be single and celibate in order to remain faithful to Scripture's teaching.

This position matters because it demonstrates that you can hold traditional theology while simultaneously affirming LGBTQ+ people. Side B Christians like Wesley Hill and Eve Tushnet argue that the church has a responsibility to honor and support celibate gay Christians, who need community, fellowship, meaningful roles in church, and spiritual companionship. The question the traditional church then has to face is direct: if you believe Side B celibacy is the right path, are you providing the communal support that makes celibacy sustainable, or are you telling people to embrace a solitary path while leaving them isolated? The traditional position can only be maintained with integrity if the church actually lives out its commitment to support celibate Christians; to tell LGBTQ+ people to remain single and then provide no real community or role for single people is not honest.

The traditional case for sexual ethics is not weak. It is grounded in creation theology, in the canonical witness of Scripture, in the consistent teaching of the church, and in an argument about human design and flourishing. But it requires something it has not always provided: real support for people who live out the sexual ethic the church teaches. The church can say "sex belongs in male-female marriage" and leave it at that, or it can recognize that this ethic is challenging for everyone (married people struggling with temptation, single people dealing with loneliness, LGBTQ+ people working out questions of identity) and provide real pastoral care for everyone living a life of faithfulness. The strength of the traditional case depends partly on the theology. It depends equally on whether the church is willing to actually pastor people in the light of that theology.

The Cost of Celibacy and the Spiritual Friendship Project

Wesley Hill, Eve Tushnet, and other Side B Christians have written honestly about what it means to hold traditional theology while having same-sex attraction, and their work deserves serious attention because it shows that you can maintain traditional theology while refusing cruelty toward LGBTQ+ people. Hill, in "Washed and Waiting," is a married man who also experiences same-sex attraction; he married a woman, and he does not present his marriage as a way to "cure" his attraction, which remains, even as he holds a traditional theology that says sexual expression belongs within male-female marriage.

Hill's honesty is refreshing. He does not pretend this is easy, does not claim that faith eliminates the difficulty, and acknowledges the loneliness, the struggle, the ongoing tension of desiring what his theology says he should not have. What he demonstrates is that people can live faithfully within traditional theology without self-hatred, acknowledging their experience honestly while maintaining theological conviction, belonging to the church without pretending their experience matches heterosexual norms. But Hill also names something crucial: the church has not provided real support for people like him. He needed community, mentorship, friendship, and belonging, and too often the church preaches celibacy without providing the infrastructure that makes celibacy sustainable.

Eve Tushnet is a lesbian who has embraced celibacy within Catholic theology, and her work is valuable because it comes from a different Christian tradition yet reaches similar conclusions. She emphasizes that celibacy has a long history in Christianity; Paul valued it, and celibate saints have been honored. The problem is not that the church calls some people to celibacy; the problem is that it has not created real community for celibate people. In a healthy parish, celibate people would have deep friendships with others who understand the calling, mentors who have walked this path before them, meaningful roles and leadership opportunities, financial support if necessary, and a community that does not treat singleness as a default waiting state until marriage. Most churches do not provide this; we preach celibacy while leaving celibate people isolated, telling them to make the sacrifice while offering nothing in return. Tushnet's challenge to traditional churches is blunt: you cannot demand celibacy from LGBTQ+ people while offering no real support. That is not theology. That is cruelty.

A group of Side B writers, including Hill, launched the Spiritual Friendship project to explore what genuine friendship and community look like for celibate LGBTQ+ Christians. They argue for deep, non-sexual intimacy between same-sex pairs, and that the church should honor these friendships as covenantal relationships even where they are not sexual. This is a significant proposal: affirming traditional sexual ethics while refusing the conclusion that LGBTQ+ people should live in isolation, holding out the possibility of non-sexual covenantal relationships that provide the intimacy and belonging humans need. The question is whether churches will actually support it; the rhetoric around Spiritual Friendship is rich, but the lived reality in most traditional churches is that single people, including celibate gay Christians, remain marginalized.

The Revoice Controversy

In 2018, the Revoice Conference brought together LGBTQ+ Christians holding traditional theology, with presentations on spiritual friendship, on identity and orientation, on building community. The conference was controversial, and many traditional Christian leaders condemned it. Why? Because some presenters questioned whether rejecting a "gay" identity was necessary for faithfulness, some argued that you could be both gay and Christian without that necessarily being sinful, and some presented affirming theology alongside traditional theology as legitimate options.

The controversy revealed something important: many traditional Christian leaders are not comfortable with Side B Christianity at all. They do not want celibate gay Christians naming their experience; they want them to be silent. That is not a theological position. That is a silencing. If traditional theology is genuinely what the church should hold, then the church should welcome Side B Christians as living witnesses to the possibility of faithful celibacy. Instead, many traditional churches are uncomfortable with them precisely because they name the difficulty and demand that the church provide real support.

Side B Christianity demonstrates that traditional theology can be held with integrity and love: that you do not have to be cruel to hold traditional convictions, that you can acknowledge pain, demand that the church do better, and call for real community and real support. But it also reveals a problem: the church is not actually set up to support celibacy. Our entire institutional structure is built around marriage and nuclear families. Small groups are for married couples; children's ministries exist for families; youth groups focus on dating and purity; our language constantly assumes marriage as the norm and goal. For a Side B Christian, this structure is isolating: called to celibacy in a church designed for the married, called to a sacrificial path while the community that should support that sacrifice is not built for it.

What the Church Owes Celibate LGBTQ+ Christians

If the traditional position is correct, then some LGBTQ+ Christians should embrace celibacy. But here is the problem: the church does not actually support celibacy. We preach it while leaving celibate people isolated. This is not unique to LGBTQ+ Christians (the church has marginalized single people for decades), but it is especially acute for LGBTQ+ Christians called to celibacy.

What would genuine support require? First, celibacy would be honored as a calling. In Catholic tradition, priests and religious communities are supported in celibacy; they do not live alone but in community, with meaningful roles, their commitment recognized and respected. In Protestant churches we have no equivalent; a celibate person is just single, waiting for a marriage that may never come, and if it does not come, a perpetual problem. Second, it would require deep friendships and intimate relationships outside of marriage; if someone is not going to marry, they need other forms of intimate connection, people who know them, people they can be vulnerable with, people who will walk with them through life. Most churches do not facilitate this, because we are built for nuclear families, deep friendships are viewed with suspicion, and non-marital intimacy is treated as a substitute for marriage; but the connection celibate people need is not sexual. It is intimate. Deep. Real.

Third, it would require meaningful work and role. A celibate person needs to contribute, to lead, to feel that their presence matters; yet many churches assign meaningful roles only to married people or to those in professional ministry, leaving the single LGBTQ+ person without a clear place. Fourth, it would require financial support, because celibacy sometimes makes earning a living harder (you might not be able to relocate for career reasons, you might be caring for others, you might be sacrificing earning potential) and a community that calls people to celibacy should provide support when necessary. Fifth, it would require honoring the sacrifice: not treating celibacy as a burden someone carries but as a calling someone lives out, recognizing the cost, the grace, the sacrifice.

Paul describes the gift of celibacy in 1 Corinthians 7, suggesting that some people are called to remain unmarried for the sake of the gospel; but even Paul recognizes this as a gift not everyone has, and he does not address how to support people in this calling. What would supporting them look like? It would look like a community of celibate Christians, both straight and LGBTQ+, intentional about life together, sharing meals, praying together, knowing each other, walking through life together. It would look like mentoring relationships between experienced celibate Christians and newer ones. It would look like intentional residential communities where celibate people live together, not modern at all, since monasteries did this for centuries before the modern church abandoned the practice. It would look like leadership roles for celibate people: teaching, pastoral, counseling roles not reserved for the married. It would look like financial support when needed: scholarships, support for career transition, support for caring for aging parents, support for any situation where celibacy creates hardship. And it would look like acknowledging the loneliness. Celibacy is lonely. It should not be lonely in the way a single person is lonely in a couple-centered church, but it is lonely in the way any sacrificial calling is lonely; that loneliness is real, it is not shameful, and it is the cost of the calling.

Here is what needs to be said clearly. If the traditional position is correct, if LGBTQ+ Christians are called to celibacy, the church is asking them to give up something deep: the possibility of sexual relationship, of marriage, of building a life with another person. That is a real cost. It is not a small thing, and for most people it is not a sustainable calling without community. The church has no right to ask that cost without providing real support, not theoretically, but actually, concretely: money, community, roles, recognition. Most churches do not provide that. We preach celibacy while leaving celibate people alone. That is not theology. That is cruelty.

So if you hold traditional theology and you are asking LGBTQ+ people to embrace celibacy, you owe them six things. One, genuine community, not casual friendship but deep, intentional, covenantal community. Two, meaningful roles: leadership, teaching, ministry, the ability to contribute fully. Three, financial support when necessary; do not make celibacy a poverty vow unless they choose that. Four, mentors who have walked this path, who understand the calling and can guide. Five, acknowledgment of the cost; do not minimize it or pretend it is easy, but honor the sacrifice. Six, public recognition, not private welcome but public affirmation; do not hide celibate Christians but celebrate them, showing that celibacy is a viable calling. If the traditional church cannot provide these things, it has no right to ask people to embrace celibacy, and most traditional churches cannot and have not provided them. That is a problem, and until the church addresses it, the traditional call to celibacy will remain a beautiful but largely impossible calling for most LGBTQ+ people to live out.

Preston Sprinkle and the Third Way

Preston Sprinkle occupies dangerous ground. He refuses to move to either pole (the affirming side that erases conviction, the condemning side that erases love) and this refusal is not cowardice. It is the hardest theological posture available. His book "People to Be Loved" does something the evangelical conversation has largely abandoned: it holds two realities without collapsing them into false peace. Sprinkle argues for traditional sexual ethics (the historic Christian position that sexual expression belongs within heterosexual marriage, which is not negotiable in his work), but he argues this position while treating LGBTQ+ people as beloved, dignified, full participants in the church. Not in spite of his convictions. Through his convictions.

The book matters because it models what the binary of affirming-versus-non-affirming obscures: there is theological ground between the poles. You can believe that homosexual behavior is contrary to Scripture's design and still believe that LGBTQ+ people deserve genuine friendship, full inclusion in Christian community, and a church that does not treat them as theological projects; you can hold both without one dissolving the other. This distinction will not satisfy the activists on either side. The affirming camp sees Sprinkle's sexual ethics as inherently oppressive; the belief itself is the harm. The traditionalist camp often sees the pastoral warmth as dilution of doctrine. Both miss what he is doing: refusing to let his sexual ethics dictate the full scope of his moral response. He is not softening his convictions. He is expanding his compassion.

That expansion is costly. It means sitting with people whose pain he cannot resolve by changing his theology; maintaining friendships across deep disagreement on a question that touches everything: identity, meaning, belonging, body, hope; acknowledging that even if his theology is correct, the church has failed LGBTQ+ people in brutal ways, and that correction of doctrine does not erase the damage done under its banner. The church has gotten this wrong in multiple directions. The fundamentalist right treated LGBTQ+ people as demons to be cast out; the progressive left treated them as victims requiring only affirmation, collapsing every complexity into a single narrative of oppression. Both approaches are exhausting: one in its cruelty, the other in its demand that you never name the cost of your own convictions. Sprinkle's third way names the cost. He does not pretend that holding traditional sexual ethics is painless if you actually love gay people, nor that affirming LGBTQ+ identity is without cost if you believe Scripture matters. He refuses both the false comfort of the right and the false simplicity of the left.

The church can learn what this looks like practically. It looks like the congregation that does not affirm homosexual behavior but grieves when a gay teenager stops coming to church. It looks like the pastor who maintains his convictions about sexual ethics while fighting for his gay friend's dignity in every room he enters. It looks like a body that says: we believe this about sexuality, and we believe you belong here, and we will do the hard work of holding both. The Side B Christians (those who experience same-sex attraction but choose celibacy to align with traditional teaching) need this system more than anyone. They are living theology, not debate points; they are humans who have made an extraordinarily costly choice to belong to a church they love while affirming convictions they believe are true, and the least the church can offer them is genuine community, not a consolation prize.

What the binary cannot hold is the reality of the teenager who kept showing up to youth group even when the preaching hurt, who kept praying, kept serving, loved Jesus more than she feared the church's confusion about her. What the binary cannot hold is the gay man in his sixties who, after decades away, is rediscovering his faith not because someone changed the doctrine but because someone changed the tone, and the tone finally matched the doctrine that Jesus loved him enough to die for him. The third way does not resolve the theological disagreement; it refuses to let that disagreement become an excuse for anything less than full pastoral presence, presence that is not sympathy from a distance but willingness to be challenged by the lives of LGBTQ+ people who are taking Scripture seriously in ways the straight church has often failed to do.

Sprinkle's work matters because it gives language to what many evangelical pastors experience but cannot articulate: the collision between their theological convictions and their pastoral responsibility to actual people. He shows that you do not have to choose between them; you have to integrate them, and the integration is harder than either option alone. The church that reads Sprinkle seriously will find itself with less comfort, not more. It will find itself unable to dismiss LGBTQ+ people as disobedient (the straight church's disobedience to its own call to love is often more flagrant) and unable to pretend that affirming behavior requires no theological cost, since there is a cost to every position and pretending otherwise is dishonesty dressed as compassion. What remains is the only thing that ever remains: the call to love your neighbor as yourself, the neighbor you actually disagree with, whose future you cannot control, whose choice you cannot fully understand. Not love that bypasses conviction. Love that is informed by conviction but not imprisoned by it. Sprinkle occupies that ground, and the evangelical conversation needs more voices willing to stand there.
