Introduction

The Conversation the Church Keeps Getting Wrong

Why the homosexuality and Scripture debate needs more than slogans — and what both sides keep getting wrong.

People are not issues. They are image-bearers.

The False Choice Between Truth and Love

The church is drowning in a conversation it refuses to have honestly. On one side, pastors stand with arms crossed, quoting verses like weapons, defending the "traditional position" with a certainty that has curdled into cruelty; people who are LGBTQ+ are told they are broken, that their desires are disordered, that they must choose between their sexuality and their faith. The message is delivered as truth-telling. It feels like rejection.

On the other side, churches have abandoned the text entirely in the name of love. They have decided that compassion requires capitulation, that being kind to LGBTQ+ people means affirming everything about how they understand themselves, that Scripture should be rewritten to match the culture rather than allowed to challenge it. The message is delivered as inclusion. It sounds like approval.

Both responses betray the gospel. The failure is not that we have disagreed about theology, because disagreement is what thinking people do; the failure is that we have made this a tribal issue, turned complex biblical exegesis into slogans, and weaponized Scripture until it became impossible for LGBTQ+ people to experience genuine Christian love without abandoning their integrity, and impossible for Christians who hold traditional theology to be heard without sounding cruel. The gospel refuses this false choice.

I write this introduction after twenty years of pastoral ministry, having sat across from more people than I can count whose lives have been fractured by both responses. I have counseled a young man whose father told him that his homosexuality made him a disappointment to God; I have also counseled a pastor's daughter who was told by affirming Christian leaders that she was a bigot for holding traditional theology. I have watched families implode. I have watched genuine seekers walk away from faith because the church seemed more interested in defending a position than in telling the truth with love.

Here is what I know: people are not issues. They are image-bearers. When we reduce any human being to their sexuality — when we treat their identity as a problem to be solved or a position to defend — we have already failed the gospel, and it does not matter whether we fail while being harsh or while being affirming. The failure is identical.

What This Book Is and Is Not

This book exists because the church needs a different conversation. Not a compromise conversation, not a "we can all get along if we just tone it down" conversation — that is not what I am offering. I am offering a conversation where Scripture is taken seriously, where people are treated with dignity, where the tension between truth and love is held rather than resolved, and where the gospel's refusal to fit into any tribe is taken at face value.

Let me be clear about what this book is not. It is not a weapon to wield against LGBTQ+ people. If you picked this up hoping for theological ammunition to defend why gay people should be excluded from church, you have picked up the wrong book. I hold a traditional sexual ethic, but I hold an equally strong conviction that the church's response to LGBTQ+ people has been catastrophic. We have sinned against them. We have used our theology as a bludgeon. We have made Jesus look small and petty. That has to end.

This is also not a book that will help you affirm everything without biblical constraint. If you picked it up hoping to prove that Scripture supports same-sex marriage, you will find some material here that resonates, but you will also find exegesis that challenges that conclusion. The Bible is not infinitely plastic; it can be interpreted, but it cannot mean anything we want it to mean. Honest engagement with Scripture means being willing to follow the text where it leads, even when the culture is heading a different direction.

What this book is, then, is an honest examination of what Scripture actually says — not what your tribal tradition told you it says. I am going to walk you through every biblical text that addresses homosexuality; not the texts people claim address it indirectly, but the ones that actually do. I am going to give you the Hebrew and the Greek, walk you through the interpretive debates, and present the strongest case for same-sex affirming theology alongside the strongest case for traditional theology. You deserve better than slogans.

And then I am going to ask a question the church rarely asks anymore. What does it mean to hold a biblical conviction about sexuality while refusing to dehumanize people? What does it look like to say that Scripture's teaching on sex is serious while treating LGBTQ+ people as beloved? What would it mean if the church became the safest place in the world to be LGBTQ+, even for those of us who hold traditional theology? That is not impossible, but it requires something the church has not done: repentance for how we have handled this issue.

When People Become Issues Instead of Image-Bearers

I have watched gay teenagers in Christian homes contemplate suicide because they believed their parents' Christianity and their own identity could not coexist. I have listened to pastors describe the loneliness of holding theological convictions their denominations abandoned without ever asking whether those convictions were biblically grounded. I have sat with women who left the church because they were treated as second-class citizens while their male pastors' divorces were met with prayer and grace. I have encountered people who love Jesus, who take Scripture seriously, who affirm traditional sexuality and have chosen celibacy in response — and who are completely abandoned by the churches that claim to honor their sacrifice.

The conversation we need is not about whether you will agree with me; it is about whether you are willing to examine what Scripture actually says, to hold your convictions honestly, and to love people without condition. Those three things are not mutually exclusive. They have been made mutually exclusive by a church that has refused to think carefully.

This book demands that we think differently. It means asking difficult exegetical questions, reading the voices we disagree with and understanding their strongest claims rather than their weakest, acknowledging that the affirming case has legitimate biblical arguments and that rejecting those arguments requires serious theological work rather than tribal repetition, and acknowledging that people have experienced genuine harm in traditional churches — harm we cannot pretend away and remain faithful to the gospel.

It also means something harder: holding the text of Scripture seriously even when the culture is moving the opposite direction. It means saying no to the god that American culture has invented, being willing to lose people in your congregation who want the Bible to say what they have already decided it must say, and choosing truth over belonging. But here is what it never means. It never means being cruel. It never means being dismissive. It never means treating LGBTQ+ people as less than image-bearers of God. It never means pretending the church has not sinned against them, and it never means defending the indefensible — and we have done a great deal that is indefensible.

I write as a pastor who has spent decades trying to tell the truth about sex. I have preached that sex within the covenant of male-female marriage is a gift from God and that sexuality outside that covenant is sinful; I have also preached against a divorce culture in the church that treats divorces as consequences without much consequence. I have preached that lust is serious while refusing to shame people for normal human sexuality. I have tried to hold all of it at once — that Scripture takes sexuality seriously, that the church has often been hypocritical about it, and that the answer to hypocrisy is not to lower the standard but to actually live by it. This book is an extension of that work.

Three Counseling Conversations From the Pastoral Room

I have sat across from more people than I can count. Let me be specific about what I mean. There was a mother who came to my office on a Tuesday afternoon in the fall; her son was sixteen, he had come out as gay six months earlier, and she was trying to hold together a marriage that was fracturing over it. Her husband had moved into an apartment. Their church had made it clear that her son was welcomed "in love" if he would embrace celibacy, but the message landed as rejection dressed in pastoral language.

She did not come asking me to "fix" him. She came asking whether it was possible to love both Jesus and her son without choosing between them. She knew her theology, she knew the traditional teaching, and she also knew that something had gone wrong in how that theology was being lived out. Her son was not broken. What was broken was the system for holding him while maintaining conviction.

I told her that the people selling her a simple answer — Jesus says no to homosexuality, so your response is clear — were selling her a lie. This is complex. Her love for her son was not in tension with Scripture; her refusal to shame him was not in tension with biblical teaching. What was in tension was the cruelty that had wrapped itself around traditional theology, and she had every right to reject that cruelty while maintaining conviction about sexual ethics. She wept — not because I gave her what she wanted, but because someone finally said both things can be true: your son is welcome at your table, and your theology about sexuality can stand. You do not have to choose. Six months later, she and her husband reconciled — not because we solved the theological question, but because they decided together that their son mattered more than the answer, that his presence at the table was non-negotiable. The theology could be worked out. The love could not be negotiated.

There was also a young man, twenty-three, who had walked away from the faith he grew up in. He had not come out at first because he was afraid, and by the time he told his parents he had already built a life outside the church, in a long-term relationship — his partner known to his extended family, welcomed at family events, accepted by everyone except his parents. They still loved him; they visited, they texted, but there was a wall, because they could not reconcile his same-sex relationship with their faith and they asked him to choose. He told me, with quiet precision, that they had asked him to choose between his conscience and his family, that he chose his conscience and did not regret it, but that he lost them and grieves it.

He was not bitter. He was heartbroken. If the church had been honest with him from the beginning, he said — if they had told him this is what we believe about sexuality, and it is going to cost you something, and we are going to walk with you in that cost without pretending it is easy — he might have stayed. But they did not say that. They said he was welcome, and then they made clear the welcome was conditional, and he could not forgive that kind of dishonesty. That young man is an atheist now. Not because he rejected Jesus, but because he rejected a Jesus he was told welcomed him who, in practice, had conditions. He could not parse the difference between the theology and the cruelty. The church had done too good a job of binding them together.

There was a woman, forty-two, who had spent twenty years in a conversion therapy program and left it when she could no longer pretend it was working. She had been celibate all that time, holding hope that her same-sex attraction would change. It had not, and at some point she had to face a question: would she spend the rest of her life waiting to be different, or would she live the life she had? She came to my office because she was making a decision — she was going to pursue a relationship — and she also loved Jesus, and she was trying to figure out whether those things could coexist. The church she grew up in had said they could not. But she was learning there were other churches, other interpretations, other possibilities.

I told her I could not tell her what to do, but I could tell her the church had failed her: conversion therapy is a sin, the promise that she would change was a lie, her sexuality is not a disease. I also could not tell her that her decision to pursue a same-sex relationship resolved every theological question. What I could tell her was that she deserved to live, deserved to love, and deserved a community that affirmed her without requiring her to pretend she had not spent twenty years in pain. She moved to a different city, joined an affirming church, and six months later started dating someone. The last I heard, she was integrating her faith and her sexuality in ways the conversion therapy world said were impossible. She had to leave the tradition of her childhood to do it. That is a cost, and the church bears responsibility for that cost.

These are not abstractions. These are the people who sit in counseling offices, who lie awake at night trying to hold together faith and identity, who have been told by the church that love and truth are in conflict — the people paying the price for the church's failure to think carefully. I have watched families implode. I have sat with teenagers who were hospitalized after suicide attempts. I have prayed with parents over the loss of a child who could not survive the contradiction between a faith that said it loved them and a faith that rejected them for who they were. I have counseled pastors who had to choose between their denomination and their own children's mental health.

The conversations I have had are not theoretical. They are particular. They are real, full of blood and loss and the desperate search for a way to be both faithful and fully human. That is what is at stake here — not winning a theological argument, not defending a position, not proving you were right, but real lives, real children, real families, real people trying to hold faith and identity together without dying. When we reduce this to theology, we lose sight of that; when we make it about defending Scripture or defending progress, we stop seeing the person across from us, and when we stop seeing the person, we can cause harm while believing we are being faithful. The pastoral room teaches you something different. It teaches you that theology without pastoral care is violence, and that pastoral care without truth is betrayal. Both matter. Both.

How the Debate Became Political

The conversation about LGBTQ+ people and Christianity did not exist in this form thirty years ago; it became urgent only recently, because the conversation became political. The political left embraced LGBTQ+ rights as a core issue and the political right resisted, and suddenly whether you affirmed same-sex marriage became a marker of political identity — you were progressive or conservative based partly on this one question. The church then got conscripted. We had to choose sides politically, aligning with either the left or the right, and we could not think clearly about the theological question because we were forced to answer the political question first.

In the 1990s and 2000s, same-sex marriage became a key ballot issue; states held referenda on whether to ban it, churches mobilized voters, and the issue became the centerpiece of culture war politics. Both sides contributed to the politicization. The left said: support same-sex marriage equality or you are against LGBTQ+ people. The right said: oppose same-sex marriage or you are against biblical morality. Both made it zero-sum. For many evangelical churches, this became the defining test of faithfulness — were you biblically faithful? then you opposed same-sex marriage — until opposing it became the marker of gospel commitment itself.

But here is what happened. Churches that made this the defining issue discovered they had organized themselves around a political cause rather than around the gospel, and when the political tide turned — when same-sex marriage became legal and increasingly affirmed — the church found itself on the wrong side of history, not just theologically but politically. We could have been clear about our theology without being conscripted. We could have said: we teach that sex belongs within male-female marriage, we also love LGBTQ+ people, we welcome them, we do not support legal discrimination, and we think the government should make its own decisions about marriage law while we maintain our own theology within our churches. But we did not say that. We made it political, and in doing so we reduced theology to politics.

The politicization carried real costs. First, it made it impossible for LGBTQ+ people to feel genuinely welcome; if your church is politically aligned with movements opposing LGBTQ+ rights, they do not feel welcome — they feel tolerated, like the issue rather than a person. Second, it made it impossible for the church to think clearly, because we were defending a political position rather than examining the theological question, asking what our tribe says rather than what Scripture teaches. Third, it cost us credibility outside the church, which saw us aligned with political opposition to LGBTQ+ people; we became known for what we opposed, the anti-gay church rather than the loving church. Fourth, it divided us internally as evangelical churches split — some moving toward affirming theology, others digging in on traditional theology in increasingly political ways — until the conversation itself became tribal.

When the Supreme Court legalized same-sex marriage in 2015, something shifted. The political question was settled; same-sex couples would have the legal right to marry, the church could no longer fight the battle politically, and we had to reckon with it theologically. For some churches this was liberating, freeing them to move toward affirming theology; for others it was threatening, because they had invested so much politically in opposition that the legal reality felt like defeat. What should have happened is that the church stepped back and said the political question is settled, so now let us think clearly about the theological question without the pressure of political alignment. Instead, many churches doubled down, becoming more insistent on traditional theology and interpreting it as a form of political resistance — supporting traditional theology as a way of saying we will not be pushed around by secular culture. But that is not theology. That is politics. And the church should not be doing politics. We should be doing theology.

So how should the church respond to political pressure? First, refuse to be conscripted by either side; the left wants us to affirm same-sex marriage and interpret Scripture accordingly, the right wants us to oppose it and organize politically, and both are forms of conscription. Second, maintain theology without political alignment — you can hold traditional theology without being aligned with the right, or affirming theology without being aligned with the left. Third, refuse the binary framing, because you do not have to choose between traditionalist and progressive; you can hold traditional theology while supporting legal equality, or affirming theology while supporting religious liberty, and move through the complexity. Fourth, center pastoral care, not politics, because whether you are traditional or affirming your first job is to care for the people in front of you — meeting them with love, hearing their story, supporting their growth in faith. Fifth, recognize that people of good faith disagree; some Christians read the same texts carefully and reach different conclusions, and that disagreement is not evidence of unfaithfulness but evidence that the text is complex and interpretation requires careful work.

What Repentance Would Actually Require

For many evangelical churches, repentance would mean a sequence of hard admissions. First, acknowledging that we made this political, organizing around the issue in ways the gospel does not require. Second, apologizing to LGBTQ+ people for the harm done by that political mobilization — they felt attacked, they felt the church was against them, and that was our responsibility. Third, stepping back from political alignment, since we can maintain theology without being a political movement and we should. Fourth, focusing on pastoral care, because the real work is not winning political battles but caring for people, listening, and building community where people can wrestle with hard questions. Fifth, admitting that we do not have all the answers, engaging a genuinely complex theological issue with humility rather than certainty.

The church will never be politically neutral; we will always have political implications. But we should not let political movements determine our theology, because that is a capitulation — the domestication of the gospel. We need a church that can say: we believe X theologically, and politically we will defend the rights of people who believe Y. That is integrity. That is the gospel lived out in a pluralistic society. That is not what we have built, but it is not too late to start.

I am asking you to enter a conversation where your tribe does not get to be right without qualification, and where compassion does not require you to abandon conviction. That is harder than picking a side. It is also the only place the gospel can actually be seen. The church has failed LGBTQ+ people; that is not debatable. We have failed them through rejection, through conversion therapy, through exclusion, through selective morality — holding straight people to one standard and gay people to another, allowing pastors to preach about grace while gay kids took their own lives. That is a failure that demands repentance, not defense.

But the church's failure has also made us afraid to speak truth. We have decided that because we got the love part so wrong for so long, we need to get the truth part wrong too — and that is trading one failure for another, which the gospel will not allow. What the gospel demands is harder: hold the text, hold the truth, and love people without condition. Not as a compromise. Not as a way to split the difference. But because that is what Jesus did. This book is an invitation to that kind of faithfulness. It is not a comfortable invitation, and it is not one where everyone gets to be right. But it is true. Let us begin.
