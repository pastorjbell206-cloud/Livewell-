Chapter 1

What the Bible Actually Says About Homosexuality

A complete survey of the six biblical passages on same-sex behavior — and why interpretation, not the text alone, drives the debate.

Your hermeneutical method determines your conclusions more than the text itself does.

The Six Passages That Address Same-Sex Behavior

Every conversation about the Bible and homosexuality must begin in the same place: what does Scripture actually say? Not what we think it says, not what our tradition told us it says, but what the text actually claims. This is harder than it sounds, because we have spent decades hearing these passages through layers of cultural assumption, tribal interpretation, and selective emphasis, treating some texts as clear and others as obscure depending on which conclusion we want, quoting some verses in isolation while ignoring others, doing exegetical gymnastics to avoid facing what the text actually says. That has to stop.

There are six passages in Scripture that explicitly address same-sex sexual behavior. Not six passages that people claim address it if you read them a certain way; not six passages about sexuality broadly that might include homosexuality; six passages where the topic is explicitly in view. Genesis 19 names the sin of Sodom. Leviticus 18:22 states the holiness code prohibition, and Leviticus 20:13 attaches the penalty. Romans 1:26-27 carries Paul's theological argument. First Corinthians 6:9-11 places the matter in a vice list, and 1 Timothy 1:10 repeats that move in the Pastoral Epistles. That is the entire biblical basis for a position on homosexuality. Not six hundred passages. Six.

What is striking is how little Scripture actually addresses this issue directly. We have made it a defining marker of biblical faithfulness when the Bible spends so little time on it; we spend more biblical material on how to treat widows and slaves than we do on homosexuality, and we have more instruction about what to do with your neighbor's ox than we do about same-sex relationships. Yet somehow this has become the issue through which entire theological traditions stand or fall. That tells us something important: the interpretation of these six passages carries a weight not distributed equally throughout Scripture, and our conclusions rest on a narrow textual base. That does not make those conclusions wrong. It means we must interpret these passages with extreme care, because small errors in exegesis produce enormous theological consequences.

Genesis 19 describes the sin of Sodom. Two angels arrive at the city, and the men surround Lot's house demanding to "know" the angels, a request that clearly intends sexual violence. Lot offers his daughters instead; God strikes the men with blindness, and the city is destroyed. For centuries, the traditional reading held that Sodom's sin was homosexuality, that the word "know" carries sexual meaning, and that the demand itself was the sin that brought judgment. That reading has problems we will address in Chapter Two, but the traditional reading is plain enough: Sodom was destroyed because of same-sex sexual sin.

The Levitical prohibitions are written in clear language. Leviticus 18:22 states the law, and Leviticus 20:13 adds the penalty.

You shall not lie with a male as with a woman; it is an abomination.

Leviticus 18:22

If a man lies with a male as with a woman, both of them have committed an abomination; they shall surely be put to death; their blood is upon them.

Leviticus 20:13

The traditional reading of these passages is straightforward: the Torah prohibits same-sex male sexual behavior and attaches serious consequences to that violation. There is no ambiguity in the statement itself, though there is significant ambiguity about how Christians relate to the holiness code.

Romans 1:26-27 is Paul's most extended theological treatment of the issue.

For this reason God gave them up to dishonorable passions. For their women exchanged natural relations for those that are contrary to nature; and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women and were consumed with passion for one another, men committing what is shameful with men and receiving in themselves the due penalty for their error.

Romans 1:26-27

The traditional reading here is that Paul describes same-sex sexual behavior as contrary to nature, as the result of idolatry and God's judgment, and as sinful. This is Paul's theological argument, not merely a cultural observation; he grounds it in creation theology, natural against unnatural, and connects it to idolatry, presenting the behavior he describes as evidence of humanity's rebellion against God.

First Corinthians 6:9-11 sets the matter in a vice list.

Or do you not know that the unrighteous will not inherit the kingdom of God? Do not be deceived: neither the sexually immoral, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor men who practice homosexuality, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor swindlers will inherit the kingdom of God. And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God.

1 Corinthians 6:9-11

The key word is the Greek term arsenokoitai, which appears to mean "men who have sex with men" or something very close. The term is debated extensively, and we will explore that debate in Chapter Five; the traditional reading is that Paul lists same-sex male sexual behavior as a sin alongside adultery, theft, and idolatry, and that this placement marks the behavior as sinful and as something that excludes people from the kingdom of God. First Timothy 1:10 contains a similar vice list, where the same word appears alongside laws against murder and other serious violations, and the traditional reading is consistent: Paul categorizes same-sex sexual behavior as sinful.

Why the Traditional Reading Is Defensible — and Not the Only Reading

This is the complete biblical case, and here is something crucial: the traditional reading of these six passages is not unreasonable. You can read these texts carefully, do solid exegetical work, and arrive at the traditional conclusion. The texts support that reading. They do not make it the only possible reading, but they support it. That needs to be stated clearly, because much of the affirming literature acts as though the traditional position is biblically indefensible. It is not. It is defensible. It requires serious exegetical work to hold, which is precisely what we will do in the chapters ahead, but the idea that you cannot read these texts carefully and arrive at a traditional sexual ethic is simply false.

At the same time, there are legitimate interpretive questions about each of these passages, and some of them are deep. Genesis 19 may not be primarily about consensual same-sex relationships at all; it may be about sexual violence and hospitality. Leviticus 18 and 20 belong to the holiness code, which raises genuine questions about how Christians relate to Old Testament law. Romans 1 describes specific cultural behavior in response to idolatry, and whether Paul's argument applies to modern same-sex relationships in different contexts is not as obvious as it first appears. The meaning of arsenokoitai is disputed; Paul may have coined the term, and its exact meaning is harder to pin down than we often admit.

This is the real conversation. Not: "The Bible clearly condemns homosexuality, so anyone who disagrees is not taking Scripture seriously"; that is too simple. And not: "The Bible says nothing about consensual same-sex relationships, so affirming theology has equal biblical grounding"; that oversimplifies in the opposite direction. The real conversation is this: these passages do address same-sex sexual behavior, the traditional reading of them is defensible, but each passage carries interpretive complexities that affect how we understand the conclusions. Honest people, reading these texts carefully, can disagree about what they finally teach.

That is the ground on which we stand as we move forward. The remaining chapters will examine each passage in depth, explore the strongest arguments for both traditional and affirming readings, and ask what it means to engage in this conversation without reducing people to positions. But first you need to know exactly what Scripture says, and you need to have read it, not through a commentary filter, not through what you were told it says, but the words as they actually claim. If you have not read Genesis 19, Leviticus 18 and 20, Romans 1:26-27, and 1 Corinthians 6:9-11 carefully, stop here and read them. Read them in multiple translations if you can. Let the words sit with you before someone else tells you what they mean. That is the only foundation on which an honest conversation can be built.

The Six Texts in Depth: Yada, Toevah, Para Phusin, Arsenokoitai

Let me walk you deeper into each passage, because the surface-level reading hides genuine complexity. Begin with Genesis 19 and the word yada. It appears about 900 times in the Old Testament, and its basic meaning is "to know"; but in sexual contexts it carries the meaning of sexual relations, and the question is whether Genesis 19 uses it that way. If you wanted to say someone had same-sex relations in ancient Hebrew, would you reach for the word that literally means "to know," or for a more direct term? The fact that yada is used even in sexual contexts for heterosexual intercourse (Genesis 4:1, "Adam knew Eve") suggests the word refers to intimate knowledge, and the same word appears in Genesis 19:5.

The broader context matters. Why would the men of Sodom demand to "know" the angels? The narrative says Lot goes outside, begs them not to commit this "wickedness," and offers his daughters instead, an alternative that is equally sexual and equally immoral, so that the structure of the narrative treats both as equivalent violations. This is the crucial point. If the sin were specifically about same-sex behavior, why would Lot's offer of his daughters, heterosexual behavior, be presented as an acceptable alternative? The narrative structure suggests the sin is not about the sex of the participants but about sexual coercion toward guests, about violated hospitality, about strangers under your roof. The scholarly consensus has shifted. As James Boswell documented, the early church fathers did not primarily interpret Sodom as about homosexuality; they read it as inhospitality and sexual violence. Augustine's reading emphasized pride and excess, and Ezekiel's own interpretation of Sodom emphasizes pride, excess of food, and failure to aid the poor, not sexuality.

Turn next to Leviticus 18:22 and the holiness code. The Hebrew is explicit: "You shall not lie with a male as with a woman; it is an abomination." The word translated "abomination," toevah, appears elsewhere describing shellfish, mixed fabrics, eating animals that chew the cud but do not have divided hooves, a woman bringing a blemished animal for sacrifice, and a man marrying a divorced woman. The word does not mean "most sinful." It means "outside the prescribed category"; it is a classification term, and in the holiness code many behaviors are toevah. The question is why male-male intercourse is prohibited: because of creation order, or because of Israel's boundary-maintenance system?

Robert Gagnon, who holds a traditional view, argues that the prohibition reflects creation theology: God made male and female, and the complementary union is fundamental. Other scholars argue the prohibition is about maintaining Israel's distinct identity and holiness, since the holiness code is specifically about what makes Israel separate from other nations. Leviticus also says: do not boil a young goat in its mother's milk, do not wear garments of two kinds of material, do not plant your field with two kinds of seed, do not plant a vineyard with two kinds of seed. These are all about maintaining boundaries and purity. Is the sexual prohibition part of that system, or is it grounded in creation theology? The text does not tell us explicitly.

Romans 1:26-27 sits against a Stoic natural-law background. Paul uses the phrase "contrary to nature," para phusin, and Paul lived in a world saturated with Stoic philosophy. For the Stoics, "nature" referred to the created order, and to act para phusin was to act against the fundamental structure of reality. But what is that fundamental structure? The traditional reading says it is male-female complementarity, and that Paul is making a universal claim grounded in creation. Consider Paul's argument structure, though. Romans 1 is about idolatry and its consequences; those who turn away from God are given over to dishonorable passions, and the specific sexual behavior described becomes a symptom of spiritual blindness, not the root sin. This is important. Paul is not saying that same-sex desire causes spiritual blindness; he is saying that spiritual blindness leads to various forms of disordered desire, including same-sex desire.

Is Paul making a universal theological claim, or describing what happened in the specific context of the ancient world when people rejected God and were given over to sexual behavior that violated cultural and created norms? The affirming scholars, James Brownson and Justin Vines, argue that Paul's language of "nature" is more complex than a simple appeal to creation order; in Stoic thought "nature" could refer to the individual person's natural inclination, so perhaps Paul is describing people acting against their own nature: those naturally oriented toward opposite-sex partners now acting against that. But this reading requires knowledge of sexual orientation as a fixed category, which Paul almost certainly did not have.

Finally, 1 Corinthians 6:9 and the question of arsenokoitai. The term appears nowhere else in ancient Greek literature before Paul, who likely coined it from Levitical language; it combines arsenos, male, and koite, bed: literally "male-bedders." The context is crucial. Paul lists pornoi (the sexually immoral), arsenokoitai, thieves, the greedy, drunkards, revilers, and swindlers, and then says, "And such were some of you," which suggests that people in the Corinthian congregation had practiced these behaviors and had been changed by Christ.

The companion term malakoi (soft, weak) also appears. In Greek literature it refers to the receptive partner in sexual acts, especially in pederastic relationships; the adult exploiting the young man was not called malakoi but the erastes. So why does Paul list both malakoi and arsenokoitai if he means to condemn all same-sex behavior? One reading holds that Paul is addressing pederasty specifically (the exploitation of young men by adult men, common in the ancient world and commonly condemned even within pagan society for its exploitative nature) and is not addressing consensual relationships between adults. Another reading holds that Paul is comprehensively addressing same-sex sexual behavior in all its forms, and the use of two terms simply shows thoroughness. The honest answer is that we cannot be entirely certain. The term is too rare, too new, too contextually unclear.

Here is what honest exegesis must acknowledge: these six passages do address sexual behavior in ways that appear to condemn same-sex sexual activity, and the traditional reading is defensible. But that reading requires four moves. First, interpreting Genesis 19 as about same-sex behavior specifically rather than sexual coercion and hospitality violation generally, possible, but it requires reading past some exegetical problems. Second, treating the Levitical prohibitions as moral laws that carry forward to Christians while explaining why other Levitical prohibitions do not, possible, but it requires theological work. Third, interpreting Romans 1 as a universal claim about the sinfulness of same-sex behavior rather than the specific consequences of idolatry in Paul's time, possible, but it requires assumptions about Paul's intent. Fourth, deciding what arsenokoitai means with only sparse evidence, possible, but it requires inference and interpretation. None of these moves are dishonest, and all have basis in the text; but none are so self-evident that careful readers cannot come to different conclusions. That is the state of the evidence, not because the Bible is unclear, but because context, translation, and interpretive grids matter in exegesis. They always have.

The Historical-Grammatical Method

The fight over what the Bible says about homosexuality is not primarily about the Bible. It is about how you read the Bible. The dispute is not whether Scripture has authority; the dispute is how to interpret specific texts, and that is where the real work is. Both sides claim the Bible, both claim to be faithful to Scripture. The traditionalist says the passages are clear and same-sex behavior is condemned; the affirming interpreter says the passages must be read in their cultural context and that cultural prohibitions do not necessarily transfer to modern relationships. Who is right? The answer depends on your hermeneutical method: your theory of how to read and interpret the text.

The traditional position relies heavily on what is called the historical-grammatical method, and the idea is straightforward: understand the text in its historical context, examine the grammar of the original languages, and determine what it meant to its original audience. What did these words mean when Paul wrote them? What did the Hebrew mean when Leviticus was written? If you understand the historical and grammatical context, you can determine what the text says. This method has real strength. It is rigorous, it avoids reading modern ideas into the text, and it forces you to grapple with what the author actually said rather than what you wish he had said. Applied here it reasons this way: Paul used the word arsenokoitai, which appears nowhere else in Greek literature before him, so Paul is making a specific claim; the historical context shows him living in a world where pederasty was common and same-sex behavior was understood as contrary to nature; given that context, Paul is condemning same-sex sexual behavior, and the meaning is clear. That is a legitimate reading, and it has force.

The Redemptive-Movement Hermeneutic

But there is another approach: the redemptive-movement hermeneutic. The idea here is that Scripture represents a movement toward redemption (toward greater justice, greater inclusion, greater care for the vulnerable) and that the trajectory of Scripture moves toward liberation. In this view you do not ask what the text meant in its original context; you ask what direction Scripture's movement runs. Is there a trajectory toward more inclusive justice, and if so, how does that trajectory inform the way we apply ancient prohibitions today?

The redemptive-movement interpreter looks at slavery. Scripture regulated slavery but did not abolish it; yet the trajectory of Scripture, the movement of the gospel, pointed toward abolition, and by the time Christians had absorbed the full implications of the gospel, slavery became theologically indefensible. Could the same be true of same-sex relationships? The ancient prohibitions made sense in a world where same-sex behavior was understood as exploitative, as pederasty, as idolatry. But what if the trajectory of Scripture (the movement toward love, toward justice, toward inclusion) points toward a different conclusion when the context changes, when same-sex relationships can be between consenting adults in committed covenant? This method also has strength. It takes the arc of Scripture seriously, and it recognizes that the application of biblical principles changes across contexts; we do not follow all Old Testament laws about slavery, about women, about warfare, because we recognize a trajectory toward greater justice. Could that same trajectory inform how we read the sexual prohibitions?

Why Your Interpretive Method Decides Your Conclusion

Here is what is crucial: your hermeneutical method determines your conclusions more than the text itself does. If you use a historical-grammatical method, you will likely reach traditional conclusions, because the texts appear to condemn same-sex behavior and the historical context suggests that is what they mean. If you use a redemptive-movement method, you will likely reach affirming conclusions, because the trajectory of Scripture moves toward inclusion and the application to modern same-sex relationships might differ from the original prohibition. Neither method is obviously correct. Both have basis in how Christians have read Scripture historically, and both produce careful exegetical work.

This means the hermeneutical conversation is the real conversation. Not: does the Bible say same-sex behavior is sinful? But: what method should we use to determine what the Bible says, and once we have settled on a method, what conclusions does it yield? The traditionalist and the affirming interpreter might be reading the same words and reaching different conclusions not because one is careless but because they are using different hermeneutical grids.

This raises a deeper problem: who decides which hermeneutical method is correct? The historical-grammatical method claims to be objective, to let the text speak for itself without importing modern assumptions, but that is only partially true. Every reader brings interpretive assumptions to the text, and the real question is whether you acknowledge those assumptions or pretend they do not exist. When a historical-grammatical interpreter decides that Levitical sexual laws are moral laws that carry forward to Christians while Levitical dietary laws do not, that interpreter is making a theological choice; it is not obvious from the text but requires reasoning about which laws are ceremonial and which are moral, and that reasoning requires theological authority. Similarly, the redemptive-movement interpreter makes choices about what constitutes the redemptive trajectory (toward inclusion, toward justice, toward liberation) when the text does not label one verse as part of the trajectory and another as not. The interpreter must discern that. Both methods require interpretive authority. Both require someone to decide what the right structure is.

If the hermeneutical method is the real conversation, then the conversation changes. You cannot settle this by proof-texting, and you cannot win by quoting more verses; you have to argue about method. The traditionalist must explain why to use the historical-grammatical method rather than the redemptive-movement method, why to assume that ancient prohibitions apply today without considering whether the trajectory of Scripture points elsewhere, and why to distinguish the sexual laws as moral while rejecting the dietary laws as ceremonial. The affirming interpreter must explain what counts as the redemptive trajectory, how to distinguish trajectory from personal preference, and (if one is willing to move away from the ancient prohibition on same-sex behavior because of trajectory) whether that same trajectory could be used to move away from other things, and where it ends. These are genuine questions, and answering them requires theological sophistication. It requires acknowledging that Scripture does not yield its meaning to casual reading, and admitting that faithful interpreters can read the same texts and reach different conclusions because they are using different hermeneutical grids.

Interpretive Humility and Legitimate Disagreement

What this demands of us is humility: not the performed humility of spiritual language, but actual intellectual humility. The traditionalist should be able to say: the passages support my reading, but the affirming interpreter has a point about trajectory; the text is not as simple as I sometimes make it sound; careful readers can read it differently; my method seems right to me, but I cannot prove it is the only legitimate method. The affirming interpreter should be able to say: the ancient texts appear to condemn same-sex behavior (that is what they say) and the question is whether that ancient prohibition applies today; I believe it does not, based on the trajectory of Scripture and the reality of sexual orientation, but I cannot pretend the text obviously supports my view, because it requires interpretive moves. When both sides make these acknowledgments, the conversation becomes real. We stop pretending the text is clearer than it is, and we start doing the harder work of theology: determining what faithful interpretation looks like in a context the ancient authors never imagined. That is the conversation the church needs to have. Not: is this verse about homosexuality? But: how should we interpret Scripture faithfully when we are asking questions the original authors never considered?

The deepest question is this: if faithful interpreters can read the same text and reach different conclusions, what do we do? One answer is that one group must be wrong, that we must determine who is interpreting correctly and who incorrectly, that the right interpreter determines the truth and the wrong interpreter is in error. Another answer is that different interpretive methods can yield different conclusions, and that this is part of how the church works; the church has multiple traditions, different traditions read Scripture differently, and that is not a problem to solve but how the church functions. This matters because if interpretation is genuinely difficult, if the text does not yield its meaning to casual reading, then disagreement is not evidence of unfaithfulness; it is evidence that the text is complex and interpretation requires care.

That does not mean all interpretations are equally valid. It means that some disagreement is legitimate, and that within that legitimate disagreement the church must figure out how to be one body while holding different theological convictions. That is harder work than simply defending your side. But it is the work the church is called to do.
