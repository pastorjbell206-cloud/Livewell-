Chapter 11

What to Say When Someone You Love Comes Out

A pastoral guide for families — what to say, what to avoid, and how to keep the relationship through disagreement.

At some point, many of you will be in a conversation you were not prepared for. Your child will come to you and say, "I'm gay," or "I think I might be gay," or "I need to tell you something about who I'm attracted to." That moment is decisive; what happens in it will shape that person's faith, their relationship with you, and their sense of whether Christianity is safe for them. Most of us do not know what to say. We panic, we quote verses, we ask questions we should not ask, and we respond in ways that wound. I want to help you do better.

What happens in that moment will shape that person's faith, their relationship with you, and their sense of whether Christianity is safe for them.

What Not to Say in the First Conversation

Let me start with what not to say, because these are the responses that do the most damage. Do not say, "This is a sin and you need to repent." That claim is theologically arguable, but it is not what your child needs to hear in this moment. What your child has just done is trust you with something vulnerable; they have risked your rejection, and the first response should be reassurance, not judgment.

Do not say, "I'm disappointed in you," or "This breaks my heart." That language tells your child there is something wrong with them, when what they need to hear is that there is nothing wrong with them. Their feelings are not a disappointment. They are not a disappointment. And do not say, "Maybe it's a phase," or "Have you tried therapy?" That is rejection dressed up as helpfulness; your child is saying "this is who I am," and you are answering "I do not accept who you are, at least not yet."

Do not say, "I love you, but..." That construction is a classic bait-and-switch — "I love you, but I cannot support your identity," "I love you, but this is sinful" — and the but erases the love. Do not use it. Do not ask, "When did this start?" or "How do you know?" or "Are you sure?" These questions imply that your child does not know their own experience. They do. Trust them.

Do not say, "We will not tell anyone." While you may need time to process, making this a secret is shaming; it tells your child that this is something to hide, something you are embarrassed about. Do not immediately talk about behavior, either. Your child just told you about their identity, and shifting straight to "well, as long as you do not act on it" is not the first conversation — that is a later conversation, if it comes at all. And do not minimize with "I've always kind of thought so" or "It doesn't change anything." Your child is telling you something that has taken tremendous courage. Honor that. Do not act like it is no big deal.

What to Say Instead

So what do you actually say? Here is a structure. First, say thank you: "Thank you for trusting me with this. I know that took courage." That is it. Just that. Your child has given you a gift of honesty; receive it. Second, ask them to help you understand — "Help me understand what you are experiencing" — and then listen to their story, not to correct it or interpret it, but to understand how they experience the world. Let them talk. Do not interrupt. Do not offer solutions.

Third, say something true and calming. This depends on your theology, but it should be something that is both true and kind. If you hold traditional theology, you might say: "I love you. Your identity does not change how I see you. We may need to talk about a lot of things, but right now I want you to know you are loved, and you are safe here." You are not affirming same-sex behavior; you are affirming your child. If you hold affirming theology, you might say: "I am so glad you felt safe enough to tell me. I am proud of you for being honest about who you are. You are loved completely as you are." Either way, the message is the same — you are safe, you are loved, you are not in danger of rejection because of this.

Fourth, tell them you need time. Do not pretend you have this figured out if you do not. Say: "This is a lot for me to process. I want to be thoughtful about how I respond. I may have questions, and I need time to think about them. But I want you to know that I love you and that we are going to figure this out." Fifth, do not make this about you. Do not say, "How could you not tell me?" or "I feel betrayed," or "This is so hard for me." Your child is vulnerable, and they need you to be strong; save your own processing for a therapist or a pastor or a trusted friend, not for your child.

The Ongoing Conversation and the Harder Questions

After the initial conversation, there are things you need to do. First, educate yourself — read books, talk to people who have walked this road, learn what your child is experiencing; do not rely on your child to educate you, because that is too much responsibility. Second, find community. You need people who get it, and whether you hold traditional or affirming theology, there are communities available: organizations like the Evangelical Council for the Homosexuality do good work supporting families who hold traditional theology, and there are PFLAG chapters and similar organizations for those who hold affirming theology. Third, stay connected. Do not withdraw, do not stop including your child in family events, do not treat them differently — they are still your child, and that has not changed. Fourth, take theology seriously but not anxiously; you will eventually need to talk about what you believe and why, but the timing matters, and you do not do that in the first week. You do it when the relationship is secure and your child knows they are loved.

Now let me be honest about the harder questions that arise. If you hold traditional theology and your adult child wants to pursue a same-sex relationship, that is a real conflict. You believe it is sinful; they believe it is good. You cannot both be right, and at some point you will have to decide whether to maintain your theological conviction even if it means some distance in the relationship, or to change your theology. That is genuinely difficult, and it is different from the initial coming out conversation. In the initial conversation, the answer is love. But as the relationship develops, theology matters.

Here is what I would say. Your first responsibility is to your relationship with your child; you cannot sacrifice that on the altar of theology. That said, you also do not have to pretend you believe something you do not. You can hold traditional theology and maintain a loving relationship with your gay child, but it requires four things: your child knowing you respect them as an adult who can make their own decisions; your accepting that there may be some distance between you on this issue; your constant commitment to being present in their life; and your genuine affection and support in other areas of their life. The fear many parents have is, "If I accept this, I am condoning sin." That is confusion. Accepting your child's identity is not the same as affirming every decision they make. You can love your child and disagree with them. You should.

The ultimate question is this: do you love your child more than you love being right? If the answer is yes, you will figure out a way forward. It will not be easy, and you may not agree, but you will maintain the relationship, and your child will know they are loved — and that is what matters most. The gospel says that love covers a multitude of sins. It does not say that love requires you to change your theology, but it does say that love is the primary obligation. When your child comes out, that is not a theological problem. That is a relational moment. Act like you love them more than you love anything else, and that will give you the time and wisdom to figure out the theology. Without love, theology is just noise. With love, even disagreement can be borne.

Practical Scripts for Parents

When your child comes out, the conversation matters enormously, so let me give you some practical language and also name the mistakes parents most commonly make. Consider first what parents actually say and how it lands. When a parent says, "I'm so disappointed in you," the child hears, "There is something deeply wrong with who you are" — that may not be the intent, but that is what lands. When a parent says, "This is a sin and you need to repent," the child hears, "I cannot love you as you are"; the parent may believe they are teaching biblical truth, but the child hears rejection. When a parent says, "I love you, but..." the child hears the but as erasing the love, creating distance, so that even theologically sound words sound like conditions. When a parent says, "We shouldn't tell anyone," the child hears, "This is something shameful," and internalizes the shame. And when a parent asks, "Have you tried therapy?" the child hears, "There is something wrong with you that needs fixing," and concludes, "I am broken."

Here is what the first conversation should sound like instead. "Thank you for trusting me with this. I know that took courage." Pause. Let them talk. "I need to understand what you are experiencing. Help me understand what it is like for you." Listen — not to correct, not to immediately interpret, but to understand. "I love you. This does not change how I see you. You are my child, and I am proud of you for being honest." If that feels like affirming something theologically problematic, that is okay; the first conversation is not about theology, it is about love. "I need time to process this. I may have questions, and I need to think about them. But I want you to know: you are safe here. You are loved. We are going to be okay." That is the system. Everything else is details.

After the initial conversation, there are specific conversations that need to happen, but not immediately. Within a few days, you might say, "I want to keep learning about what you are experiencing. Can you recommend a book, or can we watch something together that would help me understand?" Within a few weeks: "I'd like to talk about how we handle this as a family. How public do you want this? Who else needs to know? What support do you need from me?" Within a month or so: "I want to talk about my beliefs about sexuality. I want to be honest with you about what I believe and why, but I want to do that from a place of love and understanding, not judgment." That last conversation is delicate. Your child does not need to hear about theological questions immediately. They need to know first that they are loved.

The Seven Most Common Mistakes

The first mistake is making it about you — "How could you not tell me?" "I feel betrayed." "This is so hard for me." Your child is vulnerable and needs you to be strong, so process your own feelings with a therapist or pastor, not with your child. The second is asking the wrong questions: "When did this start?" "How do you know?" "Are you sure?" These imply your child does not know their own experience. Trust them. The third is an immediate focus on behavior — "As long as you do not act on it," "You need to find a therapist" — when your child has just told you who they are, not asked what to do about it.

The fourth mistake is isolating your child by making this a secret and not allowing them to tell others, which communicates shame and embarrassment. The fifth is using Scripture as a weapon, quoting verses before you have listened; Scripture is not a conversation-starter when someone is vulnerable — used that way, it is a conversation-closer. The sixth is assuming this is a phase: "I think you're confused," "This is just rebellion." Your child is telling you something true about themselves; treat it that way. The seventh is comparing their identity to other sins — "Well, I struggle with lust too," "We all have temptations." That comparison is demeaning. You are not comparing your experience to your child's identity.

Later, when you have established safety and trust, harder conversations may need to happen. If you hold traditional theology, you will need to talk about it, but the timing and framing matter enormously. What not to say: "The Bible says this is sinful, so you cannot pursue a relationship." What to say instead: "I hold a traditional theology about sexuality. That means I believe sex belongs within male-female marriage. I understand that may feel limiting or painful, and I understand you may not share that belief. I want to understand what you are thinking and feeling. And I want you to know that even if we disagree about this, you are still my child and I still love you." Then listen. Your job is not to convince your child; your job is to maintain the relationship. The theological conversation can happen over time. It does not need to happen immediately.

Here is what you need to know above all: staying present in your child's life is more important than being theologically right. You can always have theological conversations later, but you cannot rebuild a relationship you have broken by rejecting your child. Some parents have said things they cannot take back — that they are ashamed, that the child is no longer welcome — and have thrown their children out or cut off relationship, and some of those relationships have never been restored. Do not do that. Your child's identity is not a threat to you, and your child coming out is not an attack on your beliefs. Your child is asking one thing: can you love me as I am? If the answer is yes, you have preserved the relationship; you can work through the theology, maintaining your convictions while honoring your child. If the answer is no, you have broken something that is very difficult to repair.

Many parents have changed their theology over time by being in relationship with their gay children. Some have not changed theology but have deepened in love. Both outcomes are possible, but both require staying in the relationship. Do not assume you know where this will go. Do not assume your child will leave the faith. Do not assume your child will change. Do not make predictions about the future. Just commit to showing up, to listening, to loving. That is enough. That is everything.

Grieving Without Giving Up When Your Child Leaves

There is a specific pain I have sat with repeatedly. A parent who held traditional theology discovers their child is gay; the parent tries to maintain both the theology and the relationship, but the child eventually concludes, "I cannot be Christian anymore, because I cannot reconcile my identity with this church's teaching." The child leaves the faith — not because they had a crisis of faith about God, but because they had a crisis of faith about the church that claims to represent God. I want to speak directly to that parent.

This pain is distinct from other losses. Your child has not died; they are alive, they might still be in your life, you might still have a relationship. But something has been lost: the faith you hoped to share, the church community you hoped they would be part of, the spiritual future you imagined. And you carry a specific burden — you wonder whether your theology contributed to this. Did your teaching that same-sex behavior was sinful plant the seed? Did your refusal to fully embrace your child's identity make the faith feel like rejection? Did you drive them away from God? Some of you will carry guilt. Not all of it is your fault, but some of it is; you did the best you knew to do, and you also participated in a system that communicated to your child that their identity was not fully welcome. That is a hard thing to carry.

One temptation is to blame the culture — the world is hostile to traditional values, it has turned your child against the faith, the secular culture won. But that is not entirely honest. The culture did not do this alone; the church did its part. We taught that same-sex behavior was sinful, we did not provide language for being both gay and Christian, and we made your child choose. When they chose themselves over the church, that was not the culture winning. That was the church losing someone we should have fought harder to keep. Another temptation is to maintain certainty about your theology and assume your child will eventually come back — she will grow up, she will realize that being gay and Christian is impossible, she will return to the faith. But sometimes that does not happen. Sometimes your child builds a good life outside the faith, and you have to grieve that.

Grief looks like admitting, "I would have preferred my child to remain in the faith. I wanted to share my faith with them. I wanted them in my church. I wanted them to raise their own children in the tradition." That is a real loss, and it deserves to be grieved. But grief also includes this: my child is still my child. Their identity is not a betrayal of me. Their choice to leave the faith is not a rejection of truth — it is an act of protecting themselves; they concluded that they could not be Christian and be themselves, and so they chose themselves. That is a choice I can respect, even if I grieve the outcome.

Here is what you need to know: your child's departure is not proof that traditional theology is wrong, but it is evidence that the church failed to hold traditional theology with love. It is possible to believe that same-sex behavior is sinful and still be the kind of church where a gay person feels welcomed, honored, and part of the family — but we have not built that church. We have built a church where traditional theology is weaponized, used to exclude rather than to welcome. If you hold traditional theology, you owe your child something different than what the church has provided. You owe them acknowledgment that their identity is real — they did not choose this, they do not need to change it, being gay is not a disorder but how they are made. You owe them apology that the church made it seem like being gay and Christian was impossible, which was a failure on our part. And you owe them commitment that if they ever want to explore faith again, you will not make them abandon their identity to do so, but will find a way to honor both their sexuality and their faith.

At some point, you might have this conversation: "I want to tell you something I have been thinking about. When you came out, I tried to maintain both my theology about sexuality and my love for you. I hope I mostly succeeded. But I also want to acknowledge that I was part of a system that made it seem like you had to choose — either abandon your sexuality or abandon your faith. That was wrong. That was the church's failure, and I was part of it. I still hold my theological beliefs about sexuality, but I do not hold them the way I used to. I hold them more humbly. I recognize that they cost you something, and I recognize that the way the church has taught them has been harmful. I want you to know that your identity is not a betrayal of me, and your choice to leave the faith is not a rejection of everything I taught you — it is a choice to protect yourself, and I respect that. I also want you to know that if you ever want to explore faith again, I am here for that. Not to convince you that you were wrong, but to walk with you, to listen, to support you in finding a way to be both yourself and a person of faith, if that is what you want. I love you. I have always loved you. And I always will."

Let yourself grieve. Let yourself feel the loss of the spiritual future you imagined. Let yourself acknowledge that your child's departure from faith is partly your responsibility. But do not let guilt transform into self-punishment, and do not assume that your child's departure means you failed as a parent. You did not; you held theology and love in tension the best way you knew how. Where you fell short — where the church fell short — is that we did not imagine that love and theology could include full welcome. We presented them as in conflict. That was our limitation, not your failure.

Some parents have watched their adult children process their faith experience years later. Sometimes those children have come to their own understanding of faith that integrates their identity and their spirituality; sometimes they have remained outside the faith but with greater understanding of what they are outside of. Do not predict the future. Do not assume your child will come back to faith. Do not assume they will remain outside. Just commit to this: I will remain in relationship with my child, I will honor their identity, I will acknowledge the harm the church has done, and I will be present, with love, whatever their faith looks like. That is enough. That is everything.

What the Church Owes These Families

The church owes these families four things. First, acknowledgment: we made it seem like being gay and Christian was impossible, and that was a choice — we could have done better. Second, apology — not a vague apology for those who felt hurt, but a specific one: we taught things that drove people away from faith, and we bear responsibility for that. Third, change — not just different rhetoric but different practices, different theology taught with different emphasis, different pastoral care. Fourth, support for families in this situation, not to convince the estranged child to come back, but to support the parent, to acknowledge their grief, to help them find a way forward that is faithful and loving.

These families are scattered across churches. Some are quietly grieving. Some are angrily departing. Some are trying to hold on to faith while honoring their child's departure. The church should see these families and say: we are sorry, we did this, we are going to do better, and we are here for you. That would be the gospel lived out.
