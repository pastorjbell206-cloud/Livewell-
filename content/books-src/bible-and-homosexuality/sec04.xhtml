Chapter 3

Leviticus 18 and 20

Understanding abomination, the holiness code, and why the shellfish question deserves a real answer.

Walk into most conversations about biblical sexual ethics, and you will eventually meet the same argument: "The Bible says it, I believe it, that settles it." The speaker cites Leviticus 18:22 or 20:13, quotes the verse, and assumes the conversation is over. That is not how biblical interpretation works, and it is especially not how interpretation works when you are trying to understand what an Old Testament law code means for Christians today.

You shall not lie with a male as with a woman; it is an abomination.

Leviticus 18:22

It is a category marker, not a moral intensity marker.

What Toevah Actually Means

The Hebrew word translated "abomination" is toevah. For years that word has been wielded as proof that same-sex behavior is uniquely objectionable to God, that it stands in a category of moral degradation all its own. But that is not what toevah means. The word appears throughout the Old Testament describing things that are detestable, ritually unclean, or contrary to the norm. Eating shellfish is toevah. Wearing clothes made of two different fabrics is toevah. A woman offering a blemished animal for sacrifice is toevah. Marrying a divorced woman is toevah, in some interpretations. A dishonest scale is toevah.

The word does not mean "uniquely sinful," and it does not mean "the worst possible sin." It means "not in accordance with the norm" or "outside the prescribed category." It is a category marker, not a moral intensity marker; calling something toevah places it in a specific classification, but it does not tell you why it is in that classification or how serious a violation it represents. This matters, because the church has used the word to suggest that same-sex behavior is categorically worse than other sexual violations, and that is not what the Hebrew supports. That is a cultural reading we have laid onto the text.

The Holiness Code and Israel's Distinct Identity

There is a larger question underneath the vocabulary: why does Leviticus prohibit same-sex male behavior (note that female same-sex behavior is not addressed in the same way), and what do we do with Old Testament law codes as Christians? Leviticus is part of the Torah, the law given to Israel at Sinai, and it contains ceremonial laws, civil laws, and what are often categorized as moral laws. The question Christians have wrestled with for two thousand years is which of these laws still apply.

No serious Christian argues that all of Leviticus applies to us today. We do not follow the dietary laws. We do not wear tzitzit on the corners of our garments. We do not keep Sabbath according to the Old Testament law, observe the feast days, or make sin offerings at a temple. The deeper issue is that Leviticus 18 and 20 belong to the holiness code, a section about separation, about being set apart, about not mixing things that should not be mixed. The laws maintain boundaries: between clean and unclean, between Israel and the nations, between the holy and the profane.

With that context, the same-sex prohibition takes on a different character. It is not presented as a universal moral law that all nations should follow; it is presented as part of Israel's particular calling to be a distinct people. That does not make it arbitrary. It means it is rooted in something specific to Israel's covenant identity.

Moral, Ceremonial, and Civil Law — Who Decides?

The standard Christian interpretation is that the ceremonial and civil laws of the Torah were specific to Israel under the old covenant, while the moral law, the law that reflects God's character and ethical standards, continues to apply. That is the system most Christian traditions use, and here is where it gets complicated: how do we distinguish ceremonial law from moral law? Why do some sexual prohibitions carry forward while dietary prohibitions do not? What is the logic? This is not a small question. It is the foundational exegetical move that determines whether Leviticus 18 and 20 still govern Christian sexual ethics.

Consider the contents. Leviticus 18 prohibits same-sex male behavior; it also prohibits menstrual intercourse, incest, bestiality, child sacrifice, and prostitution. Most of these prohibitions, we believe, carry forward, grounded in creation order, in the structure of human sexuality, or in the dignity of persons. But Leviticus also forbids planting two kinds of seed in a field, wearing garments woven of two kinds of material, and eating animals that chew the cud but do not have divided hooves. Christians do not follow those laws; we have determined they were specific to Israel's ceremonial system. The affirming argument presses the obvious tension: all of these are in the same law code, so if we drop the dietary laws as ceremonial, why do we keep the sexual laws? Why do we pick and choose?

The three-category approach has real problems, but understanding its structure helps us see why the traditional argument has force. The three categories are moral law, which reflects God's character and continues for Christians; ceremonial law, specific to Israel's worship system and no longer in force; and civil law, Israel's judicial system, no longer in force. The trouble is that the text does not organize itself this way. The categories are interpretive tools we bring to the text. Leviticus does not label which laws are which. How do we know shellfish laws are ceremonial and sexual prohibitions are moral? We infer it from theology and reasoning.

The comparative evidence complicates things further. The Code of Hammurabi, the Hittite Laws, and the Assyrian Laws all regulate sexuality extensively, prohibiting certain behaviors, prescribing penalties. Some prohibitions appear across multiple ancient codes, such as those against incest, against rape, against bestiality; some appear only in specific cultures; same-sex restrictions appear in some codes but not all. One insight follows from this: ancient societies used sexual regulations to mark group identity. Who may marry, what behavior is allowed, how reproduction is regulated: these were ways of distinguishing one people from another. The holiness code explicitly says Israel should be holy, set apart, distinct, and sexual regulations were part of that distinctness. That does not make the prohibition arbitrary; it means understanding its purpose requires understanding Israel's broader covenant identity.

The Shellfish Argument and Why It Has Force

When these conversations come up, someone inevitably says: "You eat shellfish, don't you? The Bible says shellfish are an abomination. Why do you keep that law and not the law about homosexuality?" The argument has real force. It points out an inconsistency: if we reject the shellfish law, why keep the sexual law, when both are in Leviticus and both are labeled toevah?

It is compelling for several reasons. First, it highlights that we all do pick and choose from Old Testament law; we do not follow all of it, and we have decided which parts apply to Christians. Second, it questions the very logic of distinguishing ceremonial from moral law: what makes you so sure that sexual law is moral while dietary law is ceremonial, when the text does not label them this way? Third, it presses the parallel directly: if we can reject the dietary laws, why not the sexual laws? Fourth, and most uncomfortably, it implies that our selection is driven by culture, not by careful exegesis. We keep the sexual laws because our culture cares about sexual ethics; we reject the dietary laws because our culture does not care about them. That is a troubling admission.

The Limits of the Shellfish Argument

But the argument also has limits. First, the Bible itself distinguishes between types of law: the New Testament distinguishes ceremonial from moral, Peter's vision in Acts suggests the dietary laws no longer apply, and Paul explicitly discusses eating meat offered to idols and concludes the prohibition does not bind Christians. Second, the sexual prohibitions are grounded differently than the dietary ones: the sexual prohibitions appear in contexts discussing God's design for humanity, while the dietary prohibitions appear in contexts discussing Israel's separation and holiness, so even within the same code they may function differently. Third, the prohibited sexual behaviors (incest, bestiality, same-sex relations) involve fundamental questions about the structure of sexuality and family, while the prohibited foods raise no comparable question about human nature or design. Fourth, the New Testament addresses sexual ethics extensively while saying almost nothing about dietary laws; Paul, who addressed both, explicitly releases Christians from food laws yet maintains sexual ethics, and that distinction matters.

Rather than pretending the shellfish argument does not work, the better approach acknowledges its force while explaining why the distinction is justified. Both dietary and sexual laws are in Leviticus; both are labeled violations of God's order; the real question is how we determine which Old Testament laws apply to Christians. The Bible itself suggests that ceremonial laws specific to Israel's worship do not apply, while moral laws reflecting God's character do, so the question becomes which category these laws fall into. For dietary laws the New Testament is explicit: Peter's vision, Paul's letters, and the early church's decision to allow Gentiles to eat non-kosher food all point the same direction. For sexual laws the evidence is less direct; the New Testament does not explicitly address all of them, and we have to infer whether they are moral or ceremonial from other factors.

Creation Theology as the Traditional Grounding

The traditional response is that the sexual prohibitions are grounded in the created order, reflecting God's design of human sexuality from Genesis 1 and 2, while the dietary and fabric laws have no such grounding and are specific to Israel's worship system. Is that logic ironclad? Not entirely; you could argue that all the laws reflect the created order, or that all of them were specific to Israel. But the traditional logic has substance. When Paul addresses sexual sin in 1 Corinthians, he explicitly grounds it in creation theology rather than arbitrary tradition.

Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ? Therefore I will not bring any of them under the power of any sexual sin. Flee from sexual immorality. All other sins a person commits are outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body.

1 Corinthians 6

Paul treats sexual ethics as continuous between the testaments, and his reason is creation theology. Several factors press in the same direction. One: the sexual prohibitions are not unique to Israel: bans on incest, bestiality, and sexual violence appear across many cultures and reflect widely shared human values. Two: they are grounded in creation theology, with Genesis describing the creation of male and female and connecting sexual design to God's original work. Three: the New Testament explicitly addresses sexual ethics and treats them as continuous with Old Testament teaching. Four: the consequences of violating these laws are described in terms of spiritual and relational harm, not merely ritual impurity. These factors do not prove that the sexual laws are moral laws that carry forward, but they provide a reasonable basis for distinguishing them from the dietary laws.

What Intellectual Honesty Requires

Here is where honest exegesis gets difficult. You can read Leviticus 18 and 20 in the traditional way, as a moral prohibition on same-sex behavior that carries forward into Christian ethics; you have to do theological work to explain why these laws carry forward while others do not, but you can make the argument. You can also read Leviticus as a law code specific to Israel's holiness system, where the same-sex prohibition was part of maintaining Israel's distinct identity, a reading in which the law retains historical and theological importance but is not necessarily binding on Christians the way the prohibition on murder is binding. Both readings require serious theological work. Both must contend with the fact that Leviticus is not cleanly sorted into permanent and temporary laws. Both must explain how Christians relate to the Old Testament law. The affirming position often argues that Christ breaks down the boundaries Leviticus erected, clean and unclean, Jew and Gentile; the traditional position argues that Christ redeems the moral content while releasing us from the ceremonial observance. Neither is obvious from the text alone.

Intellectual honesty requires several admissions. First, we do not have an airtight principle for determining which Old Testament laws apply; we use theological reasoning, and that reasoning can be questioned. Second, people of good faith disagree about how to categorize these laws: some affirming scholars argue that the sexual laws, like the dietary laws, were specific to Israel's holiness system and do not apply to Christians, and that is a reasonable reading. Third, the fact that we have kept the sexual laws while rejecting the dietary ones could be explained by culture rather than exegesis: we are uncomfortable changing sexual ethics because they affect us deeply, and comfortable changing dietary laws because they do not. That is a troubling possibility. Fourth, if we want to maintain that the sexual laws continue to apply, we should be able to explain clearly why they apply while the dietary laws do not, and if we cannot, we should acknowledge the limits of our certainty.

What we can say with certainty is this: Leviticus does prohibit same-sex male behavior, and the toevah label places it in a category of things outside the norm. How we interpret that prohibition, how we understand its applicability to Christians, how we weigh it against the rest of the biblical material: those are the real exegetical questions, and they cannot be settled by simply quoting the verse. The traditional reading is exegetically defensible. It is not the only exegetically defensible reading, and the church has too often presented it as though it were. The shellfish argument is no knockdown blow against traditional sexual ethics, but it is a serious challenge, and responding to it honestly means admitting that our hermeneutic is not as simple as some would like. The conversation must continue, not because Leviticus is unclear, but because Leviticus belongs to a larger biblical system that Christians have had to interpret carefully for two thousand years.
