Chapter 6

The Affirming Theology Case

The strongest Side A arguments for same-sex marriage in the church, presented fairly and at full strength.

The affirming case for same-sex marriage in the church has become increasingly sophisticated, and pretending otherwise is the first failure of honest engagement. This is not a sloppy theological position. It is careful biblical work, done by scholars who love Scripture and who, after years inside the text, have concluded that it does not condemn same-sex relationships. If you mean to engage this conversation honestly, you need the strongest version of the argument, not the weakest one you can find.

If we are going to disagree, we should disagree with positions we actually understand, not with caricatures.

The Trajectory Argument

The first sophisticated argument is the trajectory argument, and it begins with a pattern that is genuinely there in the text. Scripture itself, the claim goes, shows a movement toward inclusion. The Bible opens amid tribalism and genocide and gradually becomes more expansive; slavery is endorsed in Leviticus and condemned as evil by the nineteenth century; women were subordinate in the Old Testament and restricted in ministry in the New, yet the gospel's own logic keeps pushing toward their equality.

The affirming position holds that this trajectory continues, that Scripture's movement is always toward the inclusion of those previously excluded, and that committed same-sex relationships represent the next step along that same line. The Bible never explicitly addressed covenantal same-sex partnerships, so the question becomes what the trajectory of Scripture would suggest about them. This is a significant argument, grounded in real biblical patterns; the principle of inclusion does run through the canon. The traditional response concedes that trajectory matters but insists it cannot override what Scripture explicitly commands, and it presses on the assumption that Scripture is moving in a direction beyond what it actually states, an assumption that remains contested.

The Orientation Argument

The second argument is the orientation argument, and it has tremendous force. The claim is that Paul did not know about sexual orientation as we understand it; he assumed all people were heterosexual and that same-sex behavior was a choice, a perversion of one's natural bent. But what if some people are simply oriented toward the same sex, not as a corruption of a heterosexual nature, but as how they are made? If Paul did not have that category available to him, how can his writings be applied to people whose orientation he never had in view?

Paul almost certainly did not carry a concept of sexual orientation as a fixed aspect of identity; he lived in a world where sex was understood as behavior, not as the self. The traditional response answers that Paul's argument is grounded in creation theology rather than in assumptions about individual experience, so that whether or not someone is naturally drawn to the same sex, the created design for sexuality remains male and female, and that design is not suspended by a particular orientation. Still, the affirming position has a point worth conceding plainly: if Paul lacked our category, his argument may not reach the question of people with same-sex orientation seeking committed relationships. He may simply never have had that scenario in front of him.

The Fruit, Justice, and Hermeneutical Arguments

Third is the fruit argument. The claim is that same-sex relationships produce spiritual fruit: that you can watch the Holy Spirit at work in the lives of gay Christians, see the love, faithfulness, sacrifice, and commitment in covenanted partnerships, and trace real moral and spiritual growth there. If the fruit is present, the question runs, can the tree be bad? This is a powerful argument, especially for people who know and love LGBTQ+ Christians; it is hard to condemn what you are watching produce genuine fruit. The traditional response is that fruit alone cannot establish doctrine, since plenty of false teachings yield apparent good, and the real question is not only whether good results but whether the behavior itself is consistent with Scripture; you can bear good fruit from a bad root by living one part of your life faithfully while disobeying in another.

Fourth is the justice argument. To deny same-sex relationships, the claim goes, is to deny LGBTQ+ people what is available to everyone else: the ability to form a legal, recognized marriage with the person they love. It forces them to choose between faith and identity, between belonging to the church and being true to themselves, and how could a just God demand that choice, or call it consistent with the gospel? The argument carries weight because it names a real injustice; the church has done real harm by forcing that choice. The traditional response is genuinely complex. Some hold that justice and truth are not always aligned in the short term and that fidelity to Scripture may require accepting short-term injustice; others argue that a sexual ethic of male-female marriage is not unjust because it applies equally to everyone regardless of orientation; still others simply sit in the tension and wrestle with how to hold truth and justice at once.

Fifth is the hermeneutical argument, which appeals to a higher authority inside Scripture itself. We read Scripture through the lens of Jesus, the claim runs, and Jesus never explicitly condemns same-sex relationships; he affirms grace, inclusion, and a subversive love, and he rebukes religious leaders for legalistic application of the law. If Jesus is the fulfillment and center of all Scripture and does not condemn these relationships, does that not shift the weight? The traditional response is that silence is not endorsement: Jesus also never explicitly addresses whether we should read Scripture as authoritative, yet that hardly licenses treating it as optional, and the principle of interpretation cannot simply be that whatever Jesus did not condemn we may affirm.

The Historical-Context Argument

The historical-context argument completes the set. Every biblical prohibition on same-sex behavior, it claims, emerges from ancient Near Eastern settings where such behavior was understood as exploitation, pederasty, or idolatry; none of them addresses the modern phenomenon of committed, consensual, covenantal same-sex relationships. To apply these ancient prohibitions to modern marriage, then, is to take a text aimed at a specific historical practice and universalize it beyond what its authors intended. This argument has force because it attends to context; you cannot read any ancient text without understanding its world. The open question is whether grasping the original context means those texts no longer apply across contexts, or whether it means they must be applied with more care, not less.

Engaging Brownson and Vines at Their Strongest

To meet the affirming position at full strength, you have to read the major works rather than summaries of them: Brownson's Bible, Gender, Sexuality; Vines' God and the Gay Christian; Kwok's Postcolonial Imagination and Feminist Theology; Johnson's The Message and the Body. These are not sloppy arguments. They are books by scholars who have spent years with the text, who read the languages and know the history, and who have simply concluded differently than the traditional position.

David Brownson argues that the traditional interpretation rests on particular assumptions about what counts as natural and how we come to know it. The traditional reading assumes that complementarity (male-female difference) is essential to the design of sexuality, that this complementarity is grounded in creation and cannot be suspended, and that same-sex relations violate that design. Brownson says these assumptions require theological work to justify; they are not self-evident from the page. His alternative prioritizes covenantal love and fidelity over any particular form love must take: Scripture orders sexuality toward love, commitment, and covenant, and if same-sex relationships can embody that covenant, they fit within Scripture's sexual ethic. This is not a rejection of sexual ethics but a reframing of what they are about. His strength is his care with the text; his weakness, in the traditional view, is that he elevates one theme, covenantal love, over others, complementarity and creation order. Scripture contains both, and the dispute is which is more fundamental: Brownson answering covenant, the tradition answering creation.

Justin Lee Vines argues that Paul had no concept of sexual orientation as a fixed identity and so could not have been addressing modern same-sex relationships at all; the people Paul calls "contrary to nature" were, on this reading, acting against their own assumed heterosexual nature. Vines further stresses that every biblical prohibition occurs in a context of cultural exploitation (pederasty, prostitution, idolatry) and none addresses committed covenantal partnerships, so the ancient prohibitions cannot simply be extended to them. His strength is his attention to historical context and to orientation as a real category; his weakness, in the traditional view, is that he may be reading a modern category back into Paul, since claims grounded in creation might apply regardless of how Paul understood individual orientation.

John Boswell's argument was more demanding still. He held that some same-sex relationships in Christian history were actually blessed by the church, reading the medieval adelphopoiesis or brother-making ceremonies as same-sex union rites and concluding the church was more affirming than we have admitted. The claim has been widely disputed; most scholars now read adelphopoiesis as a ceremony of friendship or spiritual kinship rather than marriage. But Boswell raised a question worth keeping: whether the church's apparently unanimous condemnation was genuinely unanimous, or whether the historical record has been selectively preserved.

Affirming scholars do not reject creation theology so much as reinterpret it. What is created, they argue, is not complementarity but difference: God made humanity in diversity (different temperaments, gifts, and orientations) and the image of God is reflected in that diversity. "Male and female he created them" need not mean, on this reading, that every sexual union must join one male and one female, but that humanity exists in gender diversity, and what matters is that sexual relationships express genuine difference between persons who grow in love. Some hold that same-sex couples express difference in other ways (personality, gifts, strengths) even where not in gender; others read the creation account as describing the diversity of humanity rather than prescribing a single form for sexuality.

Brownson's Excess-Passion and Honor-Shame Readings

Brownson's work in Bible, Gender, Sexuality deserves serious engagement precisely because it is good scholarship, not dismissive of the traditional position, not pretending the texts are easy, but arguing for a different interpretive grid. One of his key contributions is what he calls the excess-passion reading of Romans 1, and understanding it is crucial to the conversation. In Romans 1, Brownson argues, Paul describes not all same-sex behavior but specifically excessive passion: sexual behavior driven by lust, by loss of control, by idolatry. The contrast is with behavior ordered toward covenant, mutual love, and faithfulness. Same-sex behavior driven by idolatrous passion violates the created order; same-sex behavior ordered toward covenant love, on this reading, might not be what Paul is condemning.

This is not arbitrary. Brownson grounds it in Paul's own language: people who "exchanged natural relations" for those "contrary to nature," with the emphasis falling on the exchange, the movement from what is natural to what is against it. For Brownson this signals a contrast between ordered sexuality and excessive sexuality driven by passion. Applied to the present, a same-sex relationship grounded in covenant, mutual love, and faithfulness might count as ordered, distinct from behavior driven by excess or idolatry. The strength of the reading is that it takes Paul's language seriously (Paul is not merely condemning a category but behavior marked by loss of control and idolatry) and it makes sense of his contrast with righteousness, where what is opposed is dishonorable passion rather than committed love. It also acknowledges what the traditional position sometimes minimizes: that exploitative behavior such as pederasty and prostitution differs from covenantal relationship, and Paul may have been condemning the former without addressing the latter.

The reading has limits, and they are real. Paul's language about nature being exchanged could point to the fundamental structure of sexuality, not merely to loss of control, in which case even ordered same-sex relations still involve the exchange Paul names, and the excess-passion reading minimizes this. The reading also assumes Paul understood covenantal same-sex relationship as a category, when he almost certainly did not; he was thinking about sexual behavior in pagan contexts, so the reading imports a modern category into his text. And it depends on reading into Paul a contrast he may not have intended: Paul condemns the behavior, the open question is whether that condemnation reaches covenantal relationships, and the excess-passion reading assumes covenant changes the moral status where Paul may have drawn no such line.

Brownson also engages the honor-shame dynamics of the ancient Mediterranean world, where honor and shame were central social facts. To penetrate someone was honorable, an assertion of status; to be penetrated was shameful, a loss of it, and this was about social position as much as the sexual act itself. In that system same-sex behavior was shameful because it involved a man losing his status, an act of dominance for one partner and degradation for the other. A covenantal relationship between equals, where both partners keep their honor, would be a different thing, free of the status-shame of ancient pederasty. The reading has strength because it explains why Paul emphasizes shame and presents the behavior as dishonorable, which fits an honor-shame context. But again it assumes Paul was contemplating covenantal relationships between equals, a very modern assumption.

Brownson is strongest, then, on three points: the absence of any biblical example of a covenantal same-sex relationship, since Scripture addresses sexual behavior in specific cultural contexts rather than partnership as we mean it; the centrality of covenant in biblical sexual ethics, so that a relationship embodying commitment and faithfulness aligns with biblical values; and the role of context in interpretation, since applying ancient prohibitions to modern situations requires discernment about what is fundamental and what is contextual. His genuine gaps run alongside these strengths: the reading assumes a distinction between excessive passion and covenantal love that his text does not explicitly support; it assumes the fundamental issue for Paul is covenant and passion rather than complementarity or created design, when Paul's language about nature in Romans 1 may reach something more fundamental than passion; and it does not adequately reckon with the fact that Paul lived without the category of sexual orientation, so that even a Paul concerned with covenant was not contemplating people with a fixed orientation seeking covenantal life together.

What the Affirming Case Must Still Acknowledge

Even a reader who rejects Brownson's conclusions should let his work make traditional theology more careful. Traditional theology often presents itself as self-evident, but Brownson shows that careful interpretation takes real work; you cannot read the text and have the meaning simply fall out. The traditional position should grant what he demonstrates: that the biblical texts are best understood as addressing specific cultural contexts (pederasty, idolatry, honor-shame dynamics) rather than covenantal relationships as we mean them today. That acknowledgment does not refute the traditional position, but it complicates it, requiring theological argument about whether ancient prohibitions apply to modern contexts, which is harder than proof-texting.

Affirming theology, for its part, has its own honesty to maintain. The biblical texts do appear to condemn same-sex sexual behavior, and the traditional reading has strong textual support that cannot be waved away by an appeal to context. The real question is whether that condemnation reaches modern covenantal relationships; Brownson argues it does not, but the argument depends on interpretive moves that are defensible without being obvious. Honest engagement means admitting the texts condemn something, and the affirming reader owes an explanation of why those condemnations do not apply to modern same-sex relationships, an explanation that itself requires theological argument.

The affirming case is strongest where it presses five things: the absence of any biblical reference to committed same-sex relationships, since Paul was not addressing what we mean today; the reality of sexual orientation as a fixed aspect of identity for many people, neither chosen nor disordered but the way some are made; the trajectory of Scripture toward inclusion, given that the church has changed its mind about slavery and about women; the fruit visible in affirming communities, where gay Christians are being changed by grace and growing spiritually; and the harm caused by traditional interpretations, harm that is real and must be named. The traditional position cannot dismiss these. They require serious engagement.

The affirming case is weakest where it pretends the biblical texts do not say what they plainly say, since they do address sexual behavior and appear to condemn same-sex behavior; where it denies that traditional interpretation has a strong textual base, since the passages do support the traditional reading and the affirming reading requires moves that are defensible but not obvious; where it ignores that other Christian traditions have held consistently to traditional teaching out of informed conviction rather than mere cultural preference; and where it assumes modern categories like sexual orientation can simply replace ancient ones without affecting interpretation, a move that itself demands argument.

Read Brownson carefully and you see the truth about where this stands. The conversation is not between people who read carefully and people who do not. It is between people using different hermeneutical grids and reaching different conclusions, each able to claim fidelity to Scripture, each able to point to textual evidence, because the evidence is not self-interpreting and requires frameworks, assumptions, theological work. The work of this chapter has been to present the strongest version of the affirming case so that everyone in the conversation knows what is actually being argued. The conversation moves forward not by proving the other side wrong but by recognizing where each side has strength and where each side has limits. That recognition does not settle the question. It shows how careful people can read the same text and still disagree.
