Chapter 10

Same-Sex Marriage and the Law

Religious liberty, civil rights, and the common good after Obergefell — coexistence without coercion.

These are not actually in conflict unless we demand that civil law enforce religious conviction.

The Civil and Religious Questions Are Different

There is a question distinct from "What does Scripture teach?" and that is "What should civil law require?" These are different conversations, and the church has often confused them. When the two collapse into one, every legal ruling starts to feel like a theological verdict, and every theological conviction starts to demand the force of law.

Part of the problem is that we have not maintained a clear distinction between civil rights and religious conviction. Civil law can say that same-sex couples have the right to marry and to receive all the legal benefits of marriage, and that the government cannot discriminate; that is a civil rights question. Religious conviction can simultaneously say that marriage is between a man and a woman, that a church will not perform same-sex ceremonies, that a denomination will not ordain gay clergy, and that a ministry can require adherence to traditional sexual ethics; that is a religious conviction question.

These are not actually in conflict unless we demand that civil law enforce religious conviction. The real question is whether we can have civil equality without religious coercion. The answer should be yes. But we have made it harder by framing the whole thing as a zero-sum conflict, where one side's gain can only be the other side's loss.

What Obergefell Did and Did Not Require

The Obergefell decision in 2015 made same-sex marriage legal throughout the United States. This was a legal decision about civil rights, and it is worth being precise about its reach. It did not require any church to perform same-sex ceremonies; it did not require any Christian to affirm same-sex marriage theologically; it simply said that the state cannot deny marriage licenses to same-sex couples.

Since then, the church has wrestled with what this means. For some Christians, it is a straightforward issue: we disagree with the decision, but the law is the law, and we can coexist in a plural society where people disagree on fundamental things. For others, it represents a threat to religious freedom and Christian conviction. Both responses are sincere, and the distance between them is much of what this chapter is about.

The Religious Liberty Concern and Its Limits

The religious liberty concern is real and worth taking seriously. If the government can require Christians to perform actions that violate their conscience—even when the action is "civil," like a marriage ceremony, rather than "religious," like a sacrament—then conscience protections are at risk. There have been cases of wedding vendors—florists, bakers, photographers—who refused to provide services for same-sex weddings on religious grounds; some have been sued, and some have lost. The question is whether a Christian business owner should be forced to participate in an event they believe violates Scripture.

This is genuinely difficult. On one hand, there are LGBTQ+ people who have been denied services and felt rejected; on the other, there are Christian business owners who believe they are being forced to violate their conscience. The traditional response from advocates of religious liberty is that the government should not force people to violate their conscience, even if that inconveniences others, even if it means some LGBTQ+ people have to search harder for a vendor—because conscience protection is fundamental. The affirming response is that religious liberty does not mean you get to discriminate in the marketplace; if you operate a public business, you serve the public, and you cannot use religion as an excuse to exclude people. Both positions carry legitimate concerns, and the honest conversation acknowledges that those concerns are in tension and that resolution is hard.

It helps to say plainly what reasonable religious liberty protections look like. First, churches and religious organizations cannot be forced to perform ceremonies that violate their theology; a Christian church should not be made to perform same-sex ceremonies, and this is not a hardship exception but a basic protection for religious institutions. Second, religious organizations should not be forced to hire people who do not align with their theology; a Christian school with a traditional theology should not be forced to hire a gay teacher who publicly affirms same-sex marriage, which is basic freedom of association. Third, individuals with sincere religious beliefs should have some conscience protections in their personal lives—if a Christian photographer genuinely believes she cannot participate in a same-sex wedding, that is worth weighing.

But there are limits, and they matter as much as the protections. You cannot use religion to exclude entire categories of people from public accommodations; you cannot refuse to serve gay people entirely, even if you may decline specific services that directly conflict with your theology, because no one should be the target of blanket exclusion. Religious exemptions from civil rights laws should be limited to religious organizations and sincere religious practitioners; a business that is primarily secular should not get broad religious exemptions just because the owner is religious. And public officials cannot use their office to promote religious views; if you are a civil servant performing marriages, you serve all people regardless of your personal beliefs. These are reasonable limits that balance religious freedom with civil equality.

The Masterpiece Cakeshop Problem

In 2018, the Supreme Court decided Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission. A cake shop owner refused to make a cake for a same-sex wedding, citing religious beliefs; the couple sued, arguing this was discrimination; the Colorado Civil Rights Commission sided with the couple; and the baker appealed to the Supreme Court. The Court ruled largely in the baker's favor, though on narrow grounds. The decision did not settle the underlying tension between religious liberty and civil rights. It just kicked the question down the road.

Both sides in these cases have legitimate concerns. The baker has a conscience, and forcing someone to participate in an event that violates their religious convictions raises real concerns about religious freedom; compelling speech or action that contradicts your deepest beliefs is a serious violation. The couple has a dignity interest; they are citizens seeking a commercial service, and denying them that service because of who they are is humiliating—it sends the message that you are not welcome here, that your relationship is not worthy of being celebrated. The traditional approach says conscience protection should win, that a sincere religious belief should not be overridden even if it inconveniences others, because the couple can more easily find another baker than someone can be made to violate their conscience. The civil rights approach says conscience cannot be an exception to civil rights law, that if you operate a public business you serve the public and do not get to decide which customers are worthy based on their identity.

Religious liberty does not mean you can do whatever you want based on your beliefs; there are limits. You cannot refuse service to all same-sex people, which is blanket discrimination based on identity. But you might decline to provide a service that constitutes participation in an event you believe violates your theology. A florist declining to provide flowers for a same-sex wedding ceremony, because she believes the ceremony itself is sinful, is different from a florist refusing to sell flowers to a gay person. Is the distinction meaningful? Arguably it is: the first declines participation in an event, while the second discriminates based on identity. But it is also a distinction that can be easily abused, because what counts as "participation" is genuinely unclear—does selling a product to someone constitute participation in their life choices?

Underneath the cakes and flowers, the real issue is whether traditional Christian beliefs about sexuality will be tolerated in public. As same-sex marriage becomes legal and affirmed, there is a question of whether there will still be space for people who hold traditional beliefs and want to organize their lives around them. That is a legitimate question, and the answer should be yes: people should be free to hold traditional theology, to teach it, and to order their families and communities around it. But that freedom has limits—you cannot deny people public services based on your beliefs, and you cannot refuse service to all gay people. You can hold your theology; you cannot impose it on others through commercial discrimination. The culture war frame has made this much harder, with Christians feeling persecuted if they cannot refuse service and LGBTQ+ people feeling rejected when denied it, when in fact there is middle ground: religious conviction can be protected without allowing blanket discrimination, and civil rights can be protected without forcing people to violate sincere beliefs, if both sides step back from zero-sum thinking.

How the UK, Canada, and Australia Draw the Line

How should a democracy handle disagreement about sexual ethics? Different jurisdictions have answered differently, and watching how they answer reveals something about how pluralism works—and does not work—in practice. In the United Kingdom, same-sex marriage is legal throughout; civil partnerships became legal in 2005 and same-sex marriage in 2014, and anti-discrimination law protects sexual orientation. But the UK also has exemptions: religious organizations can maintain their theology, and the Church of England, which has not endorsed same-sex marriage, is permitted to continue teaching traditional theology. The arrangement is equal protection under law in the marketplace, while religious organizations retain the right to teach and organize according to their theology.

Canada is similar. Same-sex marriage is legal and anti-discrimination law is strong, but religious organizations can maintain traditional teaching and practice. Canada's approach is instructive: religious organizations can enforce their theology in hiring and doctrine, but you cannot refuse service to same-sex couples based on religious conviction. The line is drawn between religion protecting itself and religion discriminating in the marketplace—civil equality in public law, religious freedom for religious organizations.

Australia legalized same-sex marriage in 2017 through a popular vote that was not close; 62 percent supported legalization. Afterward, the government had to address religious exemptions, and the approach has been to allow some while maintaining anti-discrimination protections broadly: religious organizations can refuse service in contexts directly related to their religious practice, but a religious business cannot blanket-refuse service based on sexual orientation. Australia has also debated a broader religious freedom bill that would expand exemptions, and that conversation is ongoing, but the default has been that civil equality comes first, with narrow religious exemptions.

The United States legalized same-sex marriage through the Supreme Court in 2015, and the tension since has centered on how broad religious exemptions should be. Masterpiece Cakeshop raised this directly when a cake maker refused to create a cake for a same-sex wedding, citing religious belief; the Court did not fully settle the matter, and the underlying question remains. Different states have answered differently—some have passed Religious Freedom Restoration Acts that provide broad exemptions, while others have interpreted civil rights law more strictly.

These approaches reveal different theories about how pluralism should work. The broad-exemption approach says religious conviction is fundamental and people should not be forced to violate their conscience even if it inconveniences others. The narrow-exemption approach says civil equality is fundamental, and that once a right is recognized—access to public accommodations—you cannot carve out blanket exemptions based on conscience, because conscience has limits. The compromising approach of the UK, Canada, and Australia tries to protect both: civil equality in law, religious freedom for religious organizations, but limits on individual conscience exemptions when you are operating in the marketplace. Most democracies have concluded that civil equality is more important than individual conscience objections in public accommodations, while still protecting religious organizations to maintain their theology.

A Reasonable Framework for Pluralism

The tension keeps focusing on individual conscience exemptions—a florist who will not provide flowers for a same-sex wedding, a baker who will not make a cake, a photographer who will not photograph a same-sex ceremony. The broad-exemption view says these business owners are practicing their religion and their conscience should be protected. The narrow-exemption view says that once you are in the marketplace you serve the public and do not get to deny service based on your beliefs about others' identities. The compromise says you might have limited protection for refusing specific services directly connected to religious conviction, but you cannot refuse service to all LGBTQ+ people.

The real question is what happens, in a pluralistic society, when my freedom of religion conflicts with your freedom from discrimination. When same-sex marriage is only legal for some and illegal for others, the conflict does not arise: the church teaches traditional theology and the law permits it. But once same-sex marriage is legal and anti-discrimination law protects sexual orientation, the conflict emerges, because a religious traditionalist who refuses service based on belief that same-sex marriage is sinful runs into anti-discrimination law. There are no perfect solutions; someone's interests get limited. The broad-exemption view limits LGBTQ+ people's ability to access services, and the narrow-exemption view limits religious people's ability to practice their beliefs in the marketplace.

A reasonable framework would do four things. First, it would protect religious organizations completely: churches, religious schools, and religious nonprofits should not be forced to violate their theology, and they can require adherence to belief and teach their doctrine—that is religious freedom working. Second, it would distinguish between religious organizations and religiously motivated individuals, so that a purely religious organization enjoys full freedom while a business, even one motivated by faith, operates under different rules. Third, it would limit individual conscience exemptions in public accommodations: if you operate a business in the public marketplace, you serve the public and do not get to refuse based on identity, though you might have protection for declining to participate in a specific service, like performing a wedding ceremony, that directly conflicts with sincere religious belief. Fourth, it would create actual space for both sides, which means sometimes someone has to find another vendor and sometimes religious people have to operate under civil rights law—not persecution, but the cost of living in a diverse democracy.

Coexistence Without Coercion

The deepest question underneath all of this is whether Christians who hold traditional theology can coexist in a society where same-sex marriage is legal and affirmed. The answer is yes, but it requires something from both sides. It requires Christians to accept that in a pluralistic society the government will make decisions we disagree with; that is not persecution but pluralism, and you do not get a religious veto over every decision the government makes. It requires LGBTQ+ advocates to accept that some Christians genuinely hold the view that traditional theology is right and same-sex relationships are sinful; we may ask those Christians to be kind and generous, but we should not ask them to pretend they believe something they do not. And it requires both sides to stop treating this as a war, because the culture war framing has done enormous damage, turning a theological disagreement into a tribal conflict and making compassion difficult.

So what should the church do? Maintain our theology honestly: if you believe traditional sexual ethics are biblical, say so clearly, do not soften or hide it, but hold it without cruelty. Respect civil rights: even if you believe same-sex marriage is sinful, respect that others disagree and that the government has made decisions accordingly, working for legal change through democratic means while recognizing that the government's decision is not persecution. Protect conscience: push back against efforts to force Christians to violate their conscience, but recognize that conscience protection has limits, and that there is a difference between protecting your own freedom and imposing your beliefs on others. Love LGBTQ+ people: regardless of your theology, demonstrate genuine love—not condescension, not judgment, but real love. And build community across differences, because one of the failures of the church is that we have segregated ourselves by theology, with churches for traditional folks and churches for affirming folks, when we might instead build communities where people with genuinely different views on this issue work together, pray together, and learn to love each other.

That would be harder. It would require more wisdom and more vulnerability. But it would also be a more honest witness to the gospel. The legal question is real and important, and the law can protect rights—but it cannot create the respect and dignity that genuine pluralism requires; that has to come from people choosing to treat each other well despite disagreement. The deepest questions were never about cakes or flowers or court rulings. How do we love each other across genuine theological disagreement? How do we maintain conviction without cruelty? How do we preserve our integrity while respecting those who disagree? Those are the questions the church should be asking.
