Chapter 2

Sodom and Gomorrah

What the sin of Sodom really was according to Scripture — sexual violence, pride, and the failure of weak proof-texting.

We have cited an attempted gang rape as the biblical foundation for opposing marriage equality.

What Genesis 19 Actually Describes

For centuries, Sodom has been used as biblical proof that God abhors homosexuality. The story is cited as the foundation of Christian sexual ethics; sermons have been preached on it, denominations have built positions on it, and people have been condemned using it. The entire weight of Sodom hangs over the biblical case against same-sex relationships. The problem is that careful biblical exegesis suggests Sodom's sin was not what we have been told it was.

Let me be clear about what I am not saying. I am not saying the Bible endorses same-sex relationships; that is not my argument. I am saying that Genesis 19 is not a passage about consensual same-sex love, and that using it as the foundation for that argument does damage both to the text and to people who deserve better than sloppy exegesis.

The story itself is straightforward. Two angels come to Sodom, and Lot welcomes them into his home; before they sleep, the men of Sodom surround the house demanding to "know" the visitors. Lot goes out and begs them not to do this, and he even offers his daughters instead. God strikes the men with blindness, and the next day the city is destroyed. Lot and his family escape, and Sodom and Gomorrah burn.

The Meaning of Yada — Did the Men of Sodom Demand Sex?

The word translated "know" is the Hebrew yada, a word that appears throughout the Old Testament with multiple meanings: to know, to understand, to be acquainted with, to have sexual relations with. The key question in Genesis 19 is what yada means in this context. Does it mean the men want sexual relations with the angels, or does it mean something else?

The traditional reading takes yada as a sexual term: the men of Sodom want to have sex with the angels, and this homosexual demand is presented as the sin that brings destruction. This reading has support in the text, since the word can mean sexual relations elsewhere. But there is more happening in the passage than the traditional reading often notices.

Why Lot's Offer Reframes the Story

Consider what Lot does. He goes outside and begs the men not to commit this "wickedness," and then he offers his daughters instead. If the men of Sodom were simply seeking to rape someone — which is what the demand is — Lot's offer makes a terrible kind of sense; he is trying to redirect their violent intention toward his daughters, a horrifying suggestion, but one that would be understood in that context as an alternative to the violence they are threatening. The fact that Lot offers his daughters suggests he understands the demand as sexual violence, not as a desire for consensual same-sex relations.

What happened in Genesis 19 looks much more like an attempted gang rape than like a statement about consensual same-sex relationships. The men are responding to strangers in their city; they demand sexual access, and it is violent, coercive, an expression of power and domination. When Lot offers his daughters as an alternative, the narrative treats that as an equivalent — because both are violent sexual acts. None of this is about love. None of this is about two people in a committed relationship. None of this is about what we mean today when we discuss same-sex marriage or same-sex partnership.

How Ezekiel and Jude Interpret Sodom's Sin

More importantly, when Scripture itself interprets what happened in Sodom, it does not describe the sin as homosexuality. In Ezekiel, the prophet names the guilt directly.

Behold, this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy. They were haughty and did an abomination before me; therefore I removed them, as you have seen.

Ezekiel 16:49-50

That is Scripture's own interpretation of what Sodom's sin was: pride, excess, failure to help the poor — not homosexuality. The word "abomination" appears in this passage, but it refers to the entire complex of idolatry and injustice, not specifically to sexual behavior. There is another reference in Jude that does use sexual language.

Just as Sodom and Gomorrah and the surrounding cities, which likewise indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural desire, serve as an example by undergoing a punishment of eternal fire.

Jude 7

Here Jude does reach for sexual language, but the phrase "unnatural desire" is broader than homosexuality; it refers to the angels — to pursuing sexual relations with beings who are not human. That is the "unnatural" element. If the sin were simply consensual same-sex relations between humans, Jude would likely be more specific; instead, he describes a demand for sexual relations with non-human beings, something clearly outside the bounds of human sexuality entirely.

This is the exegetical problem. When we look at what Scripture itself says about Sodom's sin, the primary focus is not on homosexuality but on pride, oppression, and idolatry. The sexual element is present, but it appears to be part of a broader pattern of disobedience and violence, not the core sin for which the city is destroyed.

Now I need to be careful, because the traditional interpretation uses Sodom to make a larger point. The argument runs like this: if Genesis 19 describes sexual violence that is presented as sinful, and that violence is between same-sex partners, then same-sex sexual behavior itself is sinful — the reasoning being that if even violent same-sex behavior is wrong, then consensual same-sex behavior must also be wrong. That logic has force. It rests on the assumption that same-sex sexual behavior is inherently sinful, and Genesis 19 is offered as evidence; but the logic works only if you already accept the premise. A different reading suggests this: Genesis 19 condemns sexual violence, the coercive demand for sex, the attempt to violate guests who are under your roof. It does not necessarily make any statement about consensual same-sex relationships, because the behavior described is not consensual. The sin is the violence, not the fact that the desired partner is of the same sex.

Judges 19 and Ancient Hospitality Codes

The narrative structure of Genesis 19 becomes clearer when you read it alongside Judges 19, a passage almost identical in structure. In Judges 19, a Levite is traveling with his concubine, and they stay in Gibeah; at night the men of the city surround the house, demanding to have sex with the Levite. The Levite gives them his concubine instead, they gang rape her all night, and she dies. This is not presented as judgment from God. This is presented as the depth of human depravity, and the story ends with the tribes of Israel rising up against Benjamin in response to the outrage. Judges 19 is about the horror of sexual violence, not about the sexual orientation of the perpetrators.

The parallel is significant. Genesis 19 and Judges 19 use nearly identical language — the same demand, the same structure, the same offering of an alternative — but Judges 19 is explicitly and unambiguously about sexual violence and the violation of hospitality codes; it is not even claimed by anyone to be about homosexuality. If Judges 19 uses identical language and is clearly about sexual violence, then Genesis 19 is likely about the same thing. The sin is not the sex of the participants. The sin is the violation of the guest-right, the demand for sexual access to strangers, the attempt to shame and dominate the visitors.

In ancient Near Eastern hospitality codes, the guest-right was sacred. When you invited someone into your home, you took responsibility for their safety, and violating that was a fundamental breach of social order. Lot's response — going out to plead with the men, offering his daughters — makes sense in this context; he is trying to protect his guests, and his offer of his daughters, horrifying to modern ears, would have been understood as redirecting the violent intent away from the guests he had taken responsibility for. The sin is hospitality violation. The sin is sexual violence. The sin is the attempt to shame and dominate strangers. It is not specifically about the gender of the desired parties.

Jude's reference is instructive on the same point. The term "unnatural desire" — sarkos heteras, literally "of different flesh" — is significant; some argue it refers to the desire for beings of a different kind, the non-human angels, which would make the sin specifically about the violation of natural boundaries between human and non-human rather than about same-sex relations. The church has built an enormous structure on Sodom, but the foundation is shakier than we have admitted.

What Ancient Near Eastern Law Codes Reveal About Sexual Regulation

When you read Leviticus 18:22 in isolation, it seems like a simple prohibition — you shall not lie with a male as with a woman — but when you read it in the context of other ancient Near Eastern law codes, something becomes clearer. Sexual behavior was heavily regulated across all ancient societies. The questions worth asking are why, and what the regulation tells us about what mattered to ancient peoples.

The Code of Hammurabi, the most famous ancient law code, contains extensive sexual regulations: it prescribes penalties for adultery, regulates who can marry whom, and addresses rape and sexual violence. But it also addresses behavior based on social status, so that what is rape for a free person is not rape for a slave, and what is adultery for a woman is not adultery for a man. The law code is concerned with sexual behavior because sexuality is connected to property, to family, to social order. Who can reproduce with whom? Whose offspring matter? How is the family line established? These are not abstract moral questions in ancient societies; they are structural questions about how society is organized.

In the Hittite Laws there are regulations about sexual behavior with animals, about incest, about intercourse with captives, and such regulations appear in many ancient law codes — the Code of Hammurabi, the Assyrian laws, Egyptian texts all contain them. But not all regulations appear in all codes. Some prohibitions are universal: incest is prohibited virtually everywhere, bestiality is prohibited virtually everywhere, rape is treated as a crime virtually everywhere. Other prohibitions vary. Some codes restrict women's sexuality more than men's, some regulate economic transactions around marriage, and some address situations that other codes ignore.

Same-sex behavior appears in some law codes but not all. The Assyrian Laws contain a prohibition against male same-sex behavior, and the penalty is severe — it involves mutilation — but the prohibition appears only in Assyrian law; the Code of Hammurabi does not mention same-sex behavior, and neither do the Hittite Laws. What that tells us is that same-sex behavior was not uniformly prohibited across ancient Near Eastern societies. The prohibition was culture-specific.

All societies regulate sexual behavior, but what they regulate and how varies enormously. Modern American society regulates sexuality around consent; ancient societies regulated sexuality around property, status, and reproduction. In a society without modern contraception, where reproduction carries huge social consequences, sexuality is inevitably bound to social order — who marries whom determines inheritance, determines status, determines whether your children are legitimate. You cannot separate sexual regulation from social regulation in ancient societies. This is what the holiness code is doing. Leviticus 18 begins, "You shall not do as they do in Egypt, where you lived, and you shall not do as they do in the land of Canaan, to which I am bringing you." The sexual laws are part of Israel's identity; they are what makes Israel distinct from other nations. This does not mean the laws are arbitrary. It means they are connected to Israel's self-understanding, and that self-understanding is different from our own.

Why does Leviticus prohibit male same-sex intercourse? The text does not say; you have to infer. One inference is that it violates the complementarity of male and female that is foundational to creation — God made male and female, and sexual union should reflect that created difference. Another is that it represents a boundary violation, one of many laws designed to keep Israel separate from other nations, part of the system that makes Israel holy and set apart. A third is that it violates reproductive purpose, since sex is for producing offspring and same-sex behavior is non-procreative. Roy Gagnon, who defends traditional theology, argues that the prohibition is grounded in creation theology — in the fundamental design of male and female — and that has force, since Genesis 1-2 does emphasize the creation of male and female as something foundational. Other scholars argue that the prohibition is part of a boundary-maintenance system: Israel is called to be holy, to be distinct, and the sexual laws maintain that distinctness. Both readings have basis, and both are defensible, but they yield different implications. If the prohibition is grounded in creation theology, it applies universally and all same-sex behavior violates the created design; if it is part of a holiness system specific to Israel, then the question becomes whether Christians maintain that holiness system, or whether the New Covenant changes the boundaries.

What we can say based on comparative law is that sexual prohibitions are culture-specific — what one society prohibits, another permits; what is unthinkable in one context is normal in another. This does not mean all sexual ethics are relative; it means sexual regulation is connected to cultural context, and the same act can carry different meanings and social implications in different settings. In ancient Assyria, same-sex behavior was understood as penetration by a superior onto an inferior, connected to status and dominance, and the Assyrian prohibition reflected that understanding. In ancient Rome, it was understood differently, where it was common for older men to have relationships with younger men, framed as educational and mentoring. In ancient Israel, the context was different again, and the understanding of sexuality was different. The question for modern interpretation is whether understanding the ancient context changes the meaning of the prohibition, or whether the prohibition transcends its context because it is grounded in something universal, such as creation design.

Here is what matters most: the ancient world did not have the category of sexual orientation as a fixed identity. They did not think of people as gay or straight; they thought of behavior. All people were assumed to be capable of same-sex behavior or opposite-sex behavior, and what mattered was the choice — you could choose to act on attraction to the same sex, and some people did and some did not. The modern category of sexual orientation, the understanding that some people have a fixed, non-chosen orientation toward the same sex, did not exist. This means that when Paul or the Levitical author condemned same-sex behavior, they were not condemning what we might call sexual orientation; they were condemning behavior that people were understood as choosing. Does that change the interpretation? The traditionalist says no — the created design stands regardless of how people experience orientation. The affirming interpreter says yes — without the category of orientation, the ancient author was not addressing the modern question.

The honest assessment of comparative law is this: sexual regulation varies across cultures, and what appears in one law code does not appear in others, which suggests that sexual prohibitions are culturally specific. But some prohibitions do appear universally — incest, rape, bestiality — which suggests that some sexual regulations reflect something deeper than cultural preference. The question is where the prohibition on same-sex behavior falls. Is it like the universal prohibitions, grounded in something fundamental about human sexuality? Or is it like the variable prohibitions, culturally specific and connected to particular social needs? The traditionalist argues it is grounded in something fundamental: the created design of male and female, the procreative purpose of sexuality, the complementarity that is foundational. The affirming interpreter argues that it is culturally specific, grounded in ancient understandings of sexuality, of honor and shame, of status and dominance, reflecting the ancient context rather than universal truth. Both are reading the same comparative data and reaching different conclusions; both are using the ancient Near Eastern context, but interpreting it differently. That is the state of the evidence. The ancient world sheds light on what the prohibitions meant in their context, but whether those prohibitions apply today requires theological judgment — and faithful interpreters can make different judgments.

Why Sodom Is the Weakest Proof Text

The strongest case for the traditional sexual ethic does not rest primarily on Sodom. It rests on Romans 1, where Paul makes an argument grounded in creation theology; it rests on what Scripture teaches about the design of sexuality within the covenant of marriage; it rests on the consistent biblical pattern that sex outside of that covenant structure is sinful. Using Sodom as your proof text for opposition to same-sex relationships is weak exegesis — weaker than the traditional position deserves. And it has real pastoral consequences, because it means people have been condemned based on a misreading of Scripture, and it means the church's case has rested on faulty foundations.

That does not mean the church is wrong. It means the church has used bad arguments to defend its position, and there is a difference; if we care about truth, we need to acknowledge that difference. What Sodom teaches is that sexual violence is sinful, that sexual coercion is sinful, that violating others for your own gratification is sinful. Those conclusions are important and they matter, but they do not settle the question of whether consensual same-sex relationships are sinful. That question requires looking at different passages and making different arguments.

The sobering reality is that we have used a passage about sexual violence to condemn people in loving relationships. We have cited an attempted gang rape as the biblical foundation for opposing marriage equality, and that is bad exegesis that has caused real harm. If we are serious about Scripture, we have to be serious about what Scripture actually says. Sodom was destroyed for pride, for oppression, for idolatry, for sexual violence. It was not destroyed because two people of the same sex loved each other. Scripture does not say that. We have said that. Scripture has not. The question remains, and it is a different question: What does Scripture say about consensual same-sex relationships? That is the question Romans 1 is actually addressing, and that is where we need to focus our exegetical work.
