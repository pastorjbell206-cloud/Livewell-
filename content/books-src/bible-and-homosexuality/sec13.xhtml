Chapter 12

Building a Church That Tells the Truth and Loves Like Jesus

A practical model for a church that holds conviction and offers genuine welcome — beyond the culture war.

We do not need to resolve the theology to start practicing love.

What the Third-Way Church Looks Like

Somewhere in the past thirty years, the church decided this issue was worth going to war over. We decided that sexual ethics was a defining marker of who was in and who was out; we made it a litmus test, a border we would defend at any price. That was a mistake. And it has cost us.

The cost is not only to LGBTQ+ people, though the cost there is grievous. The cost is to the church itself. We have divided ourselves into camps and made this a matter of tribal identity, until we lost the ability to speak about sexual ethics without sounding either cruel or compromised. We have made it impossible to hold a traditional position without people assuming you hate gay people, and impossible to be compassionate without people assuming you have abandoned Scripture. What if there were a different way?

Some churches are beginning to explore what might be called the "third way" — churches that hold a traditional sexual ethic, that sex within male-female marriage is God's design, while simultaneously being the safest, most welcoming place in the world for LGBTQ+ people. This is not a compromise position. It is not "we all agree to disagree." It is a position that takes both things seriously at full weight: Scripture's teaching, and the dignity and worth of LGBTQ+ people.

Saying What You Believe — and Backing It With Action

What would that look like in practice? First, it means saying clearly what you believe. "Our church believes that sex is designed by God for the covenant of male-female marriage. We believe that all people are made in God's image and are worthy of respect and love, regardless of sexual orientation. We welcome LGBTQ+ people into our community without reservation." That statement has weight. It is not saying "Come and we will fix you," and it is not saying "We pretend this issue does not exist." It is saying: you belong here, and we mean what we say about our theological position.

Second, it means backing that statement up with action. A church that says it welcomes LGBTQ+ people needs to hire LGBTQ+ staff in positions of leadership and visibility, to use inclusive language in worship and teaching, to affirm LGBTQ+ people publicly and not just privately, to provide real pastoral support for people navigating different theological positions, and to build genuine friendships rather than programs. The danger is that we talk about welcome while leaving LGBTQ+ people to figure out the details themselves; a genuinely welcoming church is active in its welcome. It creates space. It builds relationships.

Third, it means dealing with the difficult conversations directly. If you hold traditional theology, do not pretend you do not, and do not soften the message so much that it becomes incoherent — but say it with kindness. What that might sound like: "We teach that sexual intimacy is designed for marriage between a man and a woman. That is a real teaching in our church. If you are LGBTQ+, that may be difficult to hear. But I want you to know that this teaching does not change how we see you. You are welcome. Your identity is not a problem. Your presence here honors us." That is hard. It is vulnerability. But it is honest.

A church that holds traditional theology yet welcomes LGBTQ+ people also needs to build systems of support that make that life sustainable. That begins with real pastoral care for people navigating celibacy. If you believe that some LGBTQ+ Christians should remain single and celibate, you need to create a community where that is possible — mentoring relationships with other single Christians, community that does not center on marriage and nuclear families, meaningful roles and leadership opportunities that do not depend on being married, genuine friendship and companionship, and even financial support when celibacy makes earning a living harder. This is more difficult than preaching that people should be celibate; it is actually walking with people through it.

It also requires spaces for people with different theological views to learn from each other. You do not need everyone to agree, but you need somewhere that people with traditional theology and people with affirming theology can study Scripture together, pray together, and actually listen to each other. It requires transparency about the costs: do not hide the fact that following Jesus in a world that affirms same-sex behavior will be costly for some, but be clear that Jesus never promised following him would be easy. The Sermon on the Mount is full of costly discipleship. And it requires community, because LGBTQ+ people need to know other LGBTQ+ Christians — to see that it is possible to be gay and Christian regardless of the path they choose. A church might facilitate gatherings where LGBTQ+ Christians can be together without the constant need to explain themselves or defend their identity.

Membership, Small Groups, and Youth Ministry

How do you actually implement a church that holds traditional theology while being radically welcoming to LGBTQ+ people? It is not complicated in principle. It is challenging in practice, because it requires changing systems rather than only changing rhetoric.

Start with membership and new member classes. When someone joins your church — particularly an LGBTQ+ person — what do you teach them about church theology? Many new member classes teach sexual ethics in a way that feels judgmental: the church's sexual ethic is this, and if you do not agree, you are making a choice to sin. A better approach would be honest about complexity. "Our church teaches that sex is designed for male-female marriage. We know that not everyone shares this belief. Some of our members hold affirming theology. Some of our members experience same-sex attraction and are navigating different paths. We want to be honest about what we believe while also being genuinely welcoming to people with different beliefs and experiences." That does not pretend uniformity; it acknowledges reality, and it makes space for different people to belong. In the membership class you should state explicitly that LGBTQ+ people are welcome in leadership and in all ministries, that their identity does not disqualify them from any role, that you want their gifts and their presence.

Small groups need the same rethinking. Many churches organize groups around life stage — couples, singles, parents — but that system marginalizes LGBTQ+ people. A gay couple does not fit the "couples" group if your theology is traditional; a celibate gay Christian does not fit the "singles" group, which is often focused on dating or loneliness. Organize instead around spiritual formation, Scripture study, and shared life experience: a group studying Romans, a group working on generosity, a group exploring doubt, where people of all orientations and identities take part in the same conversation. Consider also having explicit groups for LGBTQ+ Christians — not as a ghetto, but as a space where they can be together without constantly explaining themselves. That matters especially if you hold traditional theology, because LGBTQ+ Christians in a traditional church need space with others who understand the specific challenges.

Youth ministry is often focused on purity culture — waiting until marriage, avoiding lust, remaining chaste — and that structure is particularly difficult for LGBTQ+ youth. If your theology is traditional, the message to a gay teenager becomes: you will probably never marry, you should never have sex, you should never have relationships, remain single and celibate. That is too much to lay on a teenager. A better approach reduces the emphasis on sexual purity altogether and instead emphasizes integrity, honesty, faithfulness, and respect — the character you want your youth to develop, treating people with dignity, the values that matter across all relationships. If you do discuss sexuality and same-sex attraction, do it with LGBTQ+ youth in mind: acknowledge that some of them may experience same-sex attraction, that it is real, that it is not shameful, that you will talk about sexuality honestly while recognizing people handle this differently. Do not make same-sex relationships the focus of critique. Many youth groups spend disproportionate time arguing against same-sex marriage while saying little about sexual ethics in heterosexual relationships, and that imbalance sends a clear message: we are really concerned about gay people. Include LGBTQ+ speakers and mentors, and let youth see LGBTQ+ Christians modeling faithfulness — whether in traditional or affirming theology, let them see that you can be gay and Christian.

Preaching and Pastoral Care Without Cruelty

Preaching about sexuality is necessary. Sexual ethics matter. But how you preach about it makes an enormous difference. Do not use LGBTQ+ people as the negative example, and do not point to same-sex relationships as the prime example of sexual sin; heterosexual sin is equally serious — lust, adultery, divorce, pornography, sexual coercion — so preach against all of it. When you discuss sexuality, name LGBTQ+ people affirmatively: "We have LGBTQ+ members who are living faithfully. They deserve your respect and your love." Use inclusive language — "Whether you are married, single, divorced, in a same-sex relationship, or navigating your sexuality" — and when you preach theology that would be difficult for LGBTQ+ people, acknowledge the difficulty: "I know this teaching is hard for some of you. I know it may feel like we are asking something of you that we are not asking of others. I want to talk about how we can support you in this."

Your pastoral staff needs training on how to counsel LGBTQ+ people — not conversion therapy training, but training on how to care for people navigating complex identity and theology questions. Your pastors should be able to sit with the questions that actually come: "I am gay and I want to follow Jesus. What does that look like?" "My parents rejected me when I came out. How do I process that?" "I am in a same-sex relationship. I am trying to figure out my theology. I do not know what to do." "I am celibate and I am lonely. How do I find community?" These are not questions with quick answers; they require pastoral skill and empathy. Train your staff to listen, to ask good questions, and to provide support rather than judgment.

And if your church teaches that some LGBTQ+ Christians should be celibate, invest money in supporting that. Do not just teach it; fund it. Provide scholarships for LGBTQ+ students, fund retreats for celibate Christians, support counseling and therapy for people navigating complex identities, support organizations that serve LGBTQ+ people. Money is how you demonstrate that you are serious. Words are cheap. Money shows commitment.

Implementing all of this is not complicated, but it does require five things. It requires clarity from leadership: the pastor has to be clear that LGBTQ+ people are welcomed — not merely tolerated — and welcomed as a gift, not as a burden. It requires training, so that staff know how to respond when someone comes out, volunteers know the language to use, and small group leaders know how to be inclusive. It requires money, because changing systems, hiring trained staff, and supporting ministries all cost money. It requires humility, because you will make mistakes, you will say the wrong thing, you will exclude people without intending to — and when that happens, you apologize and do better. And it requires persistence, because this is not a one-time change but a continuous commitment to becoming more welcoming, more honest, more faithful. Most churches have not done this work. We have changed our rhetoric while keeping our systems intact. But real change requires changing systems.

The Bridgetown Church Example

Some churches are attempting to handle this in genuinely thoughtful ways, and it is worth looking at concrete examples of what it could look like. Bridgetown Church in Portland, Oregon, under the leadership of Keith Giles, has worked to hold both theological conviction and genuine welcome. The church holds what appears to be a traditional sexual ethic, teaching that sex is designed for marriage between a man and a woman, but it also states clearly that LGBTQ+ people are welcome in all aspects of church life. The theology is clear. The welcome is genuine. LGBTQ+ people know what the church believes — they are not confused about the theology — but they also know that the church loves them, and the theology does not translate into rejection.

The church has hired LGBTQ+ staff, which is significant. It means that when LGBTQ+ people walk into the church, they see people like them in positions of leadership and authority; the church is not just tolerating them but welcoming them into roles of responsibility. That requires courage from leadership, because it means facing criticism from traditionalist churches that believe LGBTQ+ people should not be hired — but Bridgetown has done it. The church also emphasizes community in ways that benefit everyone, including LGBTQ+ people. It is not just a Sunday gathering; it is a community that meets throughout the week, that eats together, prays together, and knows each other. For LGBTQ+ people, especially those navigating celibacy or different theological positions, that kind of community is essential.

Bridgetown explicitly acknowledges that this is hard. The church has hosted forums where people with different theological positions dialogue, where LGBTQ+ people ask questions, where the church listens. It does not pretend everything is resolved; it acknowledges tension, acknowledges pain, and holds room for people to work through complexity. The model requires several things: leadership with courage, because it is easier to preach a traditional position and not hire LGBTQ+ people than to maintain theology while genuinely welcoming them into leadership; honesty about the tension, refusing to pretend traditional theology is obviously correct or that everyone should accept it, and creating space for disagreement; willingness to learn, listening to LGBTQ+ people and letting their presence shape how the church teaches and serves; and commitment to pastoral care, where the first job is not defending theology but caring for people — meeting them where they are, listening, offering support.

Other churches have tried different approaches. Some Anglican churches hold traditional theology but have created explicit space for LGBTQ+ people in certain roles; some mainline Protestant churches have moved toward affirming theology; some independent churches have created explicitly LGBTQ+-affirming communities. The models vary, but the best ones share certain characteristics: clarity about theology, so that whatever the church believes, it says clearly; genuine hospitality rather than mere tolerance; community rather than programs, real relationship; listening, so that the church is not only speaking but hearing; and willingness to change, wrestling with hard questions rather than rigidly defending a position.

Moving From Tolerance to Real Integration

The hardest part of all of this is integration. It is not enough to have an LGBTQ+ small group or an LGBTQ+-affirming service; LGBTQ+ people need to be integrated into the full life of the church. They need to be visible in leadership, part of the regular community, fully part of the body. Many churches have LGBTQ+ people who are not integrated — they are tolerated, and they feel the lack of full welcome. Genuine integration requires effort: it requires examining systems that exclude, making space, acting with intention. But it is possible, and churches that have done it report that it reshapes the community. The presence of LGBTQ+ people challenges the community to love more deeply, to think more carefully, to embody the gospel.

The culture war frame has destroyed the church's credibility on this issue. Both sides have weaponized Scripture. Both sides have made this a tribal marker. Both sides have lost sight of the fact that we are talking about real people's lives. Moving beyond the culture war means refusing binary thinking, because you do not have to choose between loving LGBTQ+ people and believing in traditional sexual ethics — you can hold both, and many faithful Christians do. It means refusing political alignment, because the culture war has made it so that holding traditional theology gets you assumed conservative and holding affirming theology gets you assumed progressive, when in fact a church could hold traditional theology and be politically progressive, or hold affirming theology and be politically conservative. It means refusing tribal identity: LGBTQ+ Christians are not your team and LGBTQ+ advocates are not your opponents; they are neighbors, they are people, and they deserve to be treated as people rather than as representatives of a cause. And it means refusing the assumption that this is the defining issue, because it is not — the defining issues are the ones Jesus emphasized: love God, love your neighbor, seek justice, love mercy, live humbly. On these things, Christians of all views agree. The sexual ethics questions are important, but they are not defining.

An honest church on this issue would take Scripture seriously, refusing to pretend the Bible says what it does not say and engaging the hard texts carefully. It would take LGBTQ+ people seriously, listening to their stories, honoring their experiences, refusing to pretend their pain is not real. It would hold conviction without cruelty, saying what it believes and why, but saying it with gentleness and respect. It would build community across difference rather than segregating itself into tribal pockets, creating space where people with different theological views could work together. It would repent of its failure, acknowledging the harm it has caused and working toward healing. It would stop fighting culture wars, stop using this issue as a political weapon, stop reducing LGBTQ+ people to a policy position. And it would lead with love — not slogans, not theology, but genuine, costly, self-sacrificing love that seeks nothing in return.

Leading With Love Without Abandoning Conviction

I have sat with LGBTQ+ people who stayed in the church when every rational argument told them to leave, and what I have learned from them has cost me my own certainty in ways I am still processing. There is the woman I counseled whose teenage daughter came out to her in 2019. The mother held traditional sexual ethics, and she also held her daughter with unreserved love, and that collision broke her for two years. She did not abandon her convictions. She abandoned her confidence that those convictions had ever adequately prepared her to be present to someone she loved. The theology that seemed clear in the abstract became complicated by the specific human in front of her who was also made in God's image, also praying, also asking the questions that should be asked when a church tells you who you are supposed to be.

There is the Side B man — a term I dislike because it reduces him to a categorical box, but the term exists because he exists — who chose celibacy to honor his conviction that Scripture constrains sexual expression to heterosexual marriage. He made that choice not because the church guilted him into it but because his faith in Christ was more fundamental than his desires, and he did it while living in a world that told him denying his sexuality was psychological harm, while churches told him his celibacy was either a kind of spiritual superiority or a kind of spiritual sickness — never simply the costly expression of genuine conviction. What he needed was not celebration of his choice; that would have been patronizing. He needed what almost no church provided: visibility in community. He needed to not be invisible. He needed other people who understood that choosing to stay celibate while gay in twenty-first-century America is not a minor thing. It is a deep askesis. The church's response to that askesis was either to pretend it did not exist or to treat him as though his sacrifice somehow proved his sanctity, and both responses avoided the real task: showing up.

There is the teenager who kept coming to youth group when the environment was not safe. I do not mean physically unsafe. I mean spiritually unsafe — the casual jokes, the assumptions, the way "homosexuality" was discussed as a sin that belonged in a different category than the others. She kept coming because she loved Jesus more than she feared the confusion, and that testimony indicts the church more than her presence honors it. And there is the gay man who returned to the church after thirty years away, who did not return because his old church changed its theology but because someone — a pastor, a friend — finally modeled what he had never seen: traditional conviction paired with genuine affection. The theology did not change. The tone did. And the tone finally matched what the theology claimed — that he was worth dying for.

What I have learned from these people is that the church has no right to ask LGBTQ+ people to stay without first asking what it would owe them for staying. We offer conviction but not community. We offer theology but not accompaniment. We ask them to trust Scripture while the people who handle Scripture have often been careless with their hearts. That is what the church owes these people: not affirmation of behavior it believes is wrong, not condemnation dressed as care, not invisibility, not celebration — just the presence that says, we believe what we believe about Scripture and sexuality, and we also believe you are fully human and fully welcome here. We will disagree. We will do it anyway.

The church has harmed LGBTQ+ people in ways that require more than doctrinal correction. It requires lament. It requires acknowledgment that even if our theology is right, our pastoral response has often been catastrophically wrong. It requires the humility to learn from people who stayed when the church did not deserve them. What the testimony of LGBTQ+ Christians reveals is that the church's real crisis is not doctrinal but relational: we have people who love Jesus and love Scripture and arrive at different conclusions, and we do not know what to do with that except banish them or affirm them into a false peace — and neither response honors their faith. The Side B Christian who chooses celibacy, the teenager who stays in youth group, the woman who holds both conviction and love for her daughter — these are not arguments waiting to be won. They are people waiting to be known. The church that knew them would be less comfortable, more complicated, more reflective; it would have to hold reality as it actually exists instead of as its categories predict.

This is an invitation to the church to be the church — not to win a culture war, not to be politically relevant, but to actually embody the gospel. That gospel says God loves you, all of you, every part of you; your sexuality, your identity, your desires — God is not shocked by any of it, and God has room for you. The gospel also says there are patterns for living that lead to flourishing, that sexual desire, like all desire, can either be ordered toward love and covenant or become disordered — but the gospel never says the solution to disordered desire is shame. The gospel says: you are welcome. Come as you are. Bring your whole self — your doubt, your questions, your sexuality, your identity, all of it — and we will figure this out together.

If the church became that kind of place, a place where people could bring their whole selves, where truth was spoken with love, where LGBTQ+ people knew they were genuinely welcomed, the conversation would change. We do not need to resolve the theology to start practicing love. We can hold different positions on what Scripture teaches while all agreeing that LGBTQ+ people are image-bearers, that they deserve dignity, that they are welcome. That is where we should start — not with perfect theology, but with love. And if the church were actually known for love, for safety, for welcome, then when we speak about what the Bible teaches, people would listen. They would know we are not trying to hurt them. They would know we genuinely care. And they might even come to trust what we are saying about Scripture. That is the church beyond the culture war. It is not perfect. It is not without tension. But it is faithful. And it is possible. I have been reshaped by these people — not because they convinced me to change my theology, but because they showed me that theology alone is an incomplete response to the theological crisis before us.
