Chapter 9

Can You Be Gay and Christian?

Identity, orientation, and following Jesus — two faithful paths and the difference between attraction and behavior.

This is the question that matters most in pastoral terms. Not what the Bible says in the abstract; what it means concretely for a person sitting in a church pew and wondering whether they can be both gay and Christian. The answer, unambiguously, is yes. But the path looks different depending on where you land on the theological questions we have been exploring.

The question 'Can I be gay and Christian?' has an answer: Yes. But how you live out that reality is a question only you can answer.

The Affirming Pathway and the Traditional Celibate Pathway

For those who hold affirming theology, the answer is straightforward. You are gay; your orientation is part of who God made you, and God made you this way. The gospel affirms your identity, and in Christ you can find a love that accepts you fully, including your sexuality. You can pursue relationships with same-sex partners, and you can build a life that is integrated and whole. In affirming communities, gay Christians are encouraged to accept their orientation as a gift, told their sexuality is not sinful, and welcomed into full participation in church life; they can marry, they can lead, they can be fully integrated into the community.

This approach offers something powerful: wholeness. You do not have to compartmentalize, you do not have to suppress part of yourself, and you can integrate your identity, your faith, and your sexuality. The question for affirming communities is whether they are being honest about all the biblical texts, whether they acknowledge that this approach requires a particular interpretation of Scripture, or whether they are pretending Scripture is clearer than it is. The most credible affirming communities are honest about the interpretive choices they are making.

For those who hold traditional theology, the path is different. The position is that you are gay, that this is part of your identity, but that same-sex sexual behavior is sinful, so pursuing a same-sex relationship is not an option, and the call is to celibacy. Here it becomes important to distinguish between what the theology says and what the church has actually done.

The theology of Side B says: you are a beloved child of God; your orientation is not your fault and you did not choose it; you are not broken or less-than because of it. Your call is to remain single and celibate, which is a real calling honored throughout Scripture. Your sexuality is not something you need to act on, your identity is primarily in Christ rather than in sexual orientation, and the church has a responsibility to support you in that calling. That is the theology. It is internally coherent, and it is something a person could actually live out. It requires sacrifice, but sacrifice is part of following Jesus. The Sermon on the Mount is full of sacrifice: taking up your cross, losing your life to find it. Not everyone is called to the same sacrifice, but everyone is called to some sacrifice.

The problem is that the church has not actually supported that theology. The church has preached that gay people should remain single and celibate, yet it has not created real community for single people; it has emphasized marriage and family to such an extent that single people, even straight single people, are marginalized. Add to that the loneliness of being gay and closeted, or out but having your sexuality treated as a problem, and celibacy becomes a very hard road to walk alone. Side B Christians need real community, mentors, deep friendships, and roles in the church that do not depend on their ability to find a spouse; they need the church to treat their celibacy as a genuine calling, not as a failure to find someone to marry. Has the church provided that? Mostly, no. We have told people to be celibate while leaving them isolated.

What It Means to Be "Gay" — Orientation vs Identity

There is a deeper question here that deserves attention: what does it mean to be "gay"? When we say someone is gay, are we describing their behavior, their attraction, their identity, their community, or all of the above? For affirming theology, being gay is an identity category. It is who you are; it encompasses attraction, community, culture, and often political alignment, and the integration of that identity into your Christian identity is the goal.

For traditional theology, being gay is more complicated. You can acknowledge someone's same-sex attraction, their orientation, without endorsing the identity category of "gay." The orientation is real and the attraction is real, but the identity, the way "gayness" gets constructed socially and politically, is treated as optional. This is where the conversation gets difficult, because we are using the same word to mean different things. When someone says, "I'm gay," do they mean they experience same-sex attraction, that they have embraced a cultural and political identity, or that they are looking to have same-sex relationships? Perhaps all three.

The traditional position can affirm the reality of same-sex attraction while questioning whether embracing the identity of "gay," with all the cultural and political baggage that now comes with it, is necessary or wise for a Christian. This is not homophobic; it is a genuine conversation about identity construction. If you are a Christian who experiences same-sex attraction, there are multiple ways to construct an identity; you could organize it primarily around your Christian faith and secondarily around your sexual orientation, you could pursue friendships, service, and purpose without any emphasis on your sexuality, or you could build a rich, meaningful life without ever adopting the identity category of "gay." Is that healthy? For some people, yes; for others, no. It depends on the person and their needs.

The danger in the traditional conversation is suggesting there is only one healthy way to handle this. Some people genuinely need to acknowledge and integrate their same-sex attraction into their identity in order to be healthy, and telling them to suppress it, to act as though it is not real, to hide it entirely, is not pastoral care. The danger in the affirming conversation is suggesting that embracing the cultural identity of "gay" is essential for health. For some people that is true; for others, there are other paths to wholeness.

Attraction Is Not Behavior

One crucial distinction gets lost in the debates: the distinction between orientation and behavior. A person can have same-sex attraction and not act on it; a person can have opposite-sex attraction and not act on it. Orientation is about attraction. Behavior is about action. The traditional position often conflates the two, treating same-sex attraction itself as sinful, but attraction is not a choice, and behavior is.

A more careful traditional position would say that same-sex attraction is not sinful and the desire itself is not a moral problem; what matters is how you respond to the desire. Sexual behavior outside of male-female marriage is, in this view, sinful, but the attraction itself is not. This distinction allows for honesty: a celibate gay Christian can acknowledge genuine same-sex attraction while maintaining that they are choosing not to act on it. They are not denying their orientation. They are making a behavioral choice. The affirming position says that sexual orientation is part of who you are and that acting on it in committed, loving relationships is not sinful, the orientation and the behavior are both aspects of your genuine self. Both positions can be coherent if they maintain the orientation-behavior distinction clearly.

The traditional position gets into trouble precisely when it implies that same-sex attraction is sinful in itself. If the attraction is the sin, you have an impossible situation: the person cannot help being attracted, yet they are sinful for it, and that leads to self-hatred and despair. The better traditional position says the desire itself is not a choice for which you are morally responsible, but how you respond is a matter of moral choice. That position allows for honest acknowledgment of experience; you did not choose your orientation, your desires are real, but your response is yours to make.

The "Born This Way" Argument and Its Limits

When the "born this way" argument emerged in the 1990s, it seemed to settle something. If sexual orientation is biological, it is not chosen; if it is not chosen, it is not sinful; therefore, condemning people for being gay is unjust. The logic seemed airtight. But the science is more complicated than the logic suggests, and the moral logic itself has problems.

The argument has rhetorical power because it suggests immutability. If you are born with your sexual orientation, you cannot change it, so it is not sinful to be gay, and condemning people for it is unjust. Yet that argument has problems. First, it is based on incomplete science: if orientation turned out to be not primarily biological, would that make homosexuality more sinful? Most people, including those in the LGBTQ+ community, would say no, being chosen does not make something sinful. Second, it concedes a problematic frame. It accepts the idea that if you were born with something it is acceptable, which leads to strange conclusions; if someone was born with a predisposition toward violence, we would not call violence acceptable. We distinguish between inclination and action. Third, the argument can be turned against LGBTQ+ people: conversion therapy advocates have pointed to research suggesting some plasticity of orientation as evidence that change is possible, so if the case depends on immutability, it breaks down the moment any change appears.

The better argument is not "born this way." It is that sexual orientation is a fundamental aspect of human identity that is not chosen and not sinful, and whether it is determined by biology, environment, or something else is irrelevant. The honest position is that sexual orientation is not chosen by most people; it is discovered. How it emerges is complex and not fully understood. But whether it is chosen or not chosen does not determine whether it is moral or immoral.

What the Science Actually Shows

Is sexual orientation determined by biology, by environment, by some combination? The research is genuinely complicated. There is no "gay gene," no single biological marker that determines sexual orientation. There is evidence that biological factors such as prenatal hormones and brain structure play a role, and evidence that environmental factors such as family dynamics and early experiences play a role. Most likely, sexual orientation emerges from a complex interaction of biological and environmental factors that we do not fully understand.

Twin studies show that if one identical twin is gay, the other is more likely to be gay than in the general population, but if orientation were purely genetic, the concordance rate would be 100 percent, and it is not. Some identical twins have different orientations, which indicates that environment also plays a role. Brain imaging studies show some structural differences between gay and straight brains, but these are not deterministic: some gay people have typical straight brain structures, and some straight people have atypical ones. Prenatal hormone exposure appears to influence orientation in some cases, but the effect is probabilistic, not absolute. Developmental factors (family dynamics, early experiences, trauma) sometimes correlate with orientation, but causation is not clear; we cannot say whether family dynamics caused the orientation or whether the orientation shaped how a person experienced family dynamics. Correlation is not causation.

The honest scientific assessment is that we do not know what causes sexual orientation. We know it is multifactorial. We know it is not purely biological and not purely environmental. We know that for most people it is experienced as discovered rather than chosen, and that it is remarkably stable across the lifespan. But we do not know the precise mechanisms that produce it.

Why Origins Matter Less Than Belonging

The deepest insight is that sexual orientation's origins matter less than we have made them matter. If someone experiences same-sex attraction (whether biological, environmental, or some combination) the pastoral question is the same: how will we care for this person? The origin question, nature or nurture, does not change the answer. It does not tell us whether it is sinful to be gay, whether to welcome LGBTQ+ people into the church, or what sexual ethics should apply. What it tells us is that the person did not wake up one day and decide to be attracted to the same sex. The attraction was not chosen.

That is important information for pastoral care. It means you do not shame someone for their attraction, you do not suggest they should "just choose" differently, and you do not imply that their orientation is evidence of moral failure. But beyond that basic insight, the origins question is largely irrelevant. The real question is simpler: how will we treat people who experience same-sex attraction? Will we welcome them? Will we listen to them? Will we honor their dignity, provide community, and accept them as they are? Those questions do not depend on whether orientation is biological or environmental. They depend on whether we are committed to loving people as they actually are.

The honest conversation acknowledges three things. First, same-sex attraction is real; some people experience it, it is not a choice, and it is not a sin in itself; it is how some people are oriented. Second, how you respond to that attraction, how you understand it, and how you construct your identity around it are choices, and those choices have real consequences that deserve thought, prayer, and community wisdom. Third, the church has failed to provide genuine pastoral care for people in this territory: we have either told them to suppress it entirely, which is damaging, or told them there is only one healthy way to live, which is also limiting.

A Healthy Pastoral Response

A person comes to you and says, "I am attracted to the same sex. What do I do?" A healthy pastoral response says: that is real; that is not a choice you made; you did not get here because of sin in your life; your orientation is how you are. Now, given that you are oriented this way, what does faithfulness look like? That depends on your theology and your community's theology, but the starting point is acceptance, not condemnation. A harmful response says the opposite: that this is a sign of spiritual disorder, that you are broken, that prayer and discipline should fix it. The first response allows for genuine pastoral care; the second makes it impossible. If the starting point is that something at the person's core is wrong, you cannot help them; you can only shame them.

Research suggests that both approaches to identity can be healthy, depending on the person and context. Some people thrive while living openly as gay; others thrive while acknowledging orientation privately and organizing identity around faith and other commitments. The danger in either direction is when it is imposed as the only healthy way. "You must come out and embrace your gay identity to be healthy" and "You must suppress your sexuality and organize identity around faith alone" both become problematic as universal prescriptions rather than options. The honest pastoral position holds that there are multiple ways to understand and integrate your sexuality, and what matters is integrity, not denying the truth about yourself, and finding a way to live that is honest and whole. That requires more pastoral sophistication than most churches can offer. It requires not having an answer ready before you listen to the person, and genuine openness to how different people find wholeness.

A healthy approach would recognize a few things plainly. You can be LGBTQ+ and Christian; that is not in question. Jesus loves you, you are welcome, and you are a full member of the body of Christ. How you handle your sexuality is between you, God, and your community of faith: a decision that deserves careful thought, prayer, and pastoral guidance. If you are gay and you hold traditional theology, you have a real calling to faithfulness that is hard, and you deserve real support; the church should honor your sacrifice and provide community that makes celibacy sustainable, not merely rhetorical. If you are gay and you hold affirming theology, you have a different path that is also legitimate, but it requires honest engagement with Scripture rather than a pretense that the text says something it does not. The church's job is to provide pastoral care for both paths, not to force people into one or the other, but to recognize that faithful Christians walk different roads.

So the question "Can I be gay and Christian?" has an answer: yes. But the next questions (how will you live out that reality, what community will support you, what role will your sexuality play in your identity, how will you remain faithful to Scripture and to yourself) are questions only you can answer, with prayer, with community, and with humility about the fact that this is genuinely difficult terrain. The church's job is to walk with you as you answer them. Not to answer them for you. And not to shame you for asking them in the first place.
