Sooner or later every honest believer runs into the same wall. The New Testament's promises sound total: new life, freedom from sin, a peace that passes understanding, death itself defeated. Then the alarm goes off and you're still anxious, still fighting the habit you confessed last week, still driving home from a graveside. Most of us decide that something has to give, either the promises or ourselves. One week we suspect the Bible oversold the product. The next we suspect we haven't believed hard enough to receive it.

The skeptic has noticed the swing, and his charge deserves its full strength. Christians, he says, have built a machine for never being wrong. Whatever the gospel seems to deliver gets filed under "already," whatever it fails to deliver gets filed under "not yet," and the scheme is unfalsifiable, a theology designed after the fact to explain away disappointment. If that were true, a thinking person would be right to walk away. So the question matters. Was "already but not yet" a patch applied to a leaking theory, or was it built into the gospel from the first week?

This essay argues that it was built in, and that we can say with some precision where it came from. The Jews of Jesus' day divided history into two ages, this present age and the age to come, with the resurrection of the dead standing at the border between them. Then one man was raised in the middle of history, and the border split. The age to come broke into the present without ending it. The first Christians didn't invent the overlap to cope with disappointment. They discovered it on Easter morning and spent the rest of the New Testament learning to live in it, which is what we still have to do, in grief and illness, in marriage and the raising of children, on an ordinary Tuesday.

## Two Ages and the Border Between Them

By the first century a great many Jews thought about history in two great blocks. There was "this age," the present order of exile, foreign empires, sickness and death. And there was "the age to come," the order God would bring when he finally acted to set the world right. The seam between them was the day of the Lord, and the sign that the seam had been crossed would be the resurrection of the dead. The clearest early statement comes near the end of Daniel, written for a people under persecution: "And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2, ESV). In 2 Maccabees 7, seven brothers martyred under Antiochus IV go to their deaths confessing that the King of the universe will raise them up again. Resurrection was a hope for the end, and a hope for everyone together.

The pattern hardens in the writings that follow. The apocalypse known as 4 Ezra, written around AD 100 by a Jew grieving the Roman destruction of the temple, has its seer protest that the righteous suffer while the wicked prosper, and the angel answers, in chapter 7, that the Most High made not one age but two. The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, opens the tenth chapter of tractate Sanhedrin with the teaching that all Israel has a share in the world to come. Jewish expectation varied, and these texts disagree on details. But the outline was common property: this age is the time of waiting and suffering, the age to come is the time of resurrection, judgment and restored creation, and between them stands one decisive act of God.

Jesus spoke inside this framework. He promised that anyone who left home and family for his sake would receive "a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:30, ESV). When Lazarus died, Martha answered Jesus in the standard grammar of Jewish hope: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day" (John 11:24, ESV). She located resurrection where everyone did, at the end.

That is the background that makes Easter so strange. N. T. Wright, in *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003), surveys the ancient evidence at length and argues that no strand of Jewish expectation looked for the Messiah to die and then be raised alone, in the middle of history, while the rest of the dead stayed in their graves. When the first disciples said God had raised Jesus, they were using an end-of-history word for something that had happened on a particular Sunday in Jerusalem, with Pilate still governing and Caesar still on his throne. The age to come had shown up early, in one body, and the present age had not stopped.

The prophets attached one more sign to the border: the Spirit poured out on all flesh (Joel 2:28). At Pentecost Peter quoted Joel and sharpened the opening words: "in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (Acts 2:17, ESV). The last days had begun, in a city where the sick still lay in the porticoes and beggars still sat at the temple gate. Both signs of the border had arrived. The border itself had not.

## What Easter Did to the Timeline

Paul was trained as a Pharisee, and the Pharisees were the party of the resurrection. He carried the two-age map long before he met the risen Christ on the Damascus road, and that meeting redrew the map rather than erasing it. Christ "gave himself for our sins to deliver us from the present evil age" (Galatians 1:4, ESV). The Scriptures were written "for our instruction, on whom the end of the ages has come" (1 Corinthians 10:11, ESV). The present evil age is still running, and the end of the ages has already arrived. Paul says both because he had seen the thing that made both true.

His word for it came from the harvest. "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV). Under the law of Moses, the first sheaf was brought to the priest and waved before the Lord before the rest of the field was cut (Leviticus 23:10-11). Firstfruits aren't a different crop. They are the harvest begun, a pledge in hand that the rest is coming and will be of the same kind. Paul gives the order: "But each in his own order: Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:23, ESV). The general resurrection Martha expected has started. It started with one man, and the church lives between the first sheaf and the full harvest. Paul's second image comes from the marketplace. God "has given us the Spirit as a guarantee" (2 Corinthians 5:5, ESV), and the Greek word, *arrabōn*, means a down payment: not a promise about the future but part of the future, paid early.

The man who saw this most clearly in the modern era was Geerhardus Vos, who taught biblical theology at Princeton Seminary for nearly four decades. In *The Pauline Eschatology* (1930) he argued that eschatology isn't the last chapter of Paul's theology, tacked on after the part about salvation. It is the frame of the whole. Vos drew two simple diagrams. The first shows the scheme Paul inherited: a line for this age, a single point of divine intervention, then a line for the age to come. The second shows the scheme Paul came to hold. The age to come begins at the resurrection of Christ and runs on in the heavenly realm, above and alongside this age, which continues until Christ's return ends it. Between Easter and the second coming the two lines run in parallel, and the believer, joined to the risen Christ by the Spirit, belongs to the upper line while still walking the lower one. Herman Ridderbos worked the same reading out at book length in *Paul: An Outline of His Theology* (1966), arguing that the governing theme of Paul's preaching is the arrival of the fullness of time in Christ's death and resurrection.

This bears directly on the skeptic's charge. If "not yet" were an excuse invented when Jesus failed to return on schedule, we'd expect the earliest Christian writings to speak only of immediate, total fulfillment and the later ones to invent the gap. The evidence runs the other way. On most scholars' dating, Paul's letters are the earliest Christian documents we have, written within roughly two or three decades of the crucifixion, and the overlap is already the load-bearing wall of his thought, because it comes from the shape of Easter itself: one resurrection ahead of the many. The delay was real, and it pressed on the church. By the time 2 Peter was written, scoffers were asking, "Where is the promise of his coming?" (2 Peter 3:4, ESV). But the delay pressed on a structure that was already there. It didn't create it.

## How the Scholars Argued Their Way to the Middle

Vos's diagrams went largely unread outside conservative Presbyterian circles for a generation, while the wider academy fought the same question out from both ends. The full story belongs to what Jesus meant by the kingdom, told in [What Is the Kingdom of God? What Jesus Meant by It](/writing/what-is-the-kingdom-of-god-and-why-it-changes-everything). What matters here is the shape of the fight: the overlap collapsed first to one side, then to the other.

Albert Schweitzer, in *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906), collapsed it toward the future. His consistent eschatology read Jesus as a prophet of the imminent end who expected this age to close within his generation and was simply wrong. On that reading there is no "already," only a "not yet" that never came. C. H. Dodd, in *The Parables of the Kingdom* (1935), collapsed it the other way. His realized eschatology held that in Jesus' ministry the kingdom had come, and he tended to treat the future-sounding sayings as the early church's later reframing, so that the final consummation receded into a timeless order beyond history. On that reading there's no real "not yet." Each man could hold his own texts only by explaining away the other's.

The way out came from scholars who refused to drop either set of texts. Werner Georg Kümmel, in *Promise and Fulfilment* (1945), argued from the Gospels that Jesus proclaimed the kingdom as future and as already present in his own person and works. Oscar Cullmann, in *Christ and Time* (German original 1946), published the year after the Second World War ended, supplied the image that has carried the synthesis ever since. He compared the cross and resurrection to the decisive battle of a war, after which the outcome is no longer in doubt though the fighting goes on until the day of final victory. Preachers have shortened his picture to D-Day and V-Day. Between them soldiers are still really fighting and really dying, but the war has been decided. James Barr's *Biblical Words for Time* (1962) later dismantled the word studies Cullmann had used to argue for a distinctively biblical concept of time, and Barr was largely right about the lexicography. The structure survived, because it never rested on the dictionary. It rests on the story: a resurrection that has happened, a resurrection that hasn't yet, and a people standing between them.

In America the case was made most influentially by George Eldon Ladd, a Baptist at Fuller Seminary writing with one eye on the dispensationalism of his own evangelical world, which pushed the kingdom almost wholly into the future. *The Presence of the Future* (1974) and *A Theology of the New Testament* (1974) argued that the kingdom is God's dynamic reign, come into history in Jesus to defeat the powers and coming again to finish what it began. Ladd didn't coin the language of "already" and "not yet," but he did more than anyone to make it the ordinary vocabulary of evangelical pulpits.

It helps to name the order of the question. That Christ has risen and will come again to judge the living and the dead is the confession of the creeds, the floor every orthodox church stands on. How to read the millennium of Revelation 20 is second-order, and faithful Christians differ. The already-and-not-yet reading has become common ground across those differences, held by amillennial Reformed scholars like Ridderbos and premillennial Baptists like Ladd. It hasn't answered every hard text; the saying about some standing there who would not taste death before seeing the kingdom (Mark 9:1) still resists every tidy solution. But its critics have to explain the same evidence it explains, and neither collapsed reading has managed to.

## Three Texts From Paul, Read Where He Wrote Them

Lifted out of their letters, Paul's best-known sentences can be made to sound either triumphant or merely wistful. Put back in place, each says both at once.

"Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come" (2 Corinthians 5:17, ESV). The verse is usually preached as a promise of personal change, and it includes that. But the Greek is more abrupt, closer to "if anyone is in Christ, new creation," and many commentators hear in it the arrival of a new world, not only a renovated individual. Then look at who is writing. Second Corinthians is Paul's defense of a ministry that looked like failure to his critics. He answers them by building on his weakness: "But we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Corinthians 4:7, ESV). A few lines later: "Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day" (2 Corinthians 4:16, ESV). The man announcing that the new creation has come is a man whose body is visibly wearing out, and he sees no contradiction.

Romans 8 is the fullest account of the overlap in the New Testament, and it turns on one word used three times. "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us" (Romans 8:18, ESV). Paul doesn't shrink the sufferings. He weighs them and finds another side to the scale. Then the groaning begins. "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now" (Romans 8:22, ESV). Then believers: "And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23, ESV). Then the Spirit himself, who "intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26, ESV).

A few verses earlier Paul told the Romans they had already "received the Spirit of adoption as sons" (Romans 8:15, ESV). Now they wait for adoption. The legal act is done, and the bodies of God's children are still subject to decay. Notice, too, that the ones who groan are precisely the ones who have the firstfruits. The Spirit doesn't end the groaning. He gives it a voice, because the believer now knows what the world is meant to be and can feel the distance. "For in this hope we were saved. Now hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what he sees?" (Romans 8:24, ESV). Saved in the past tense, hoping in the present, seeing in the future: the overlap is written into Paul's verbs.

The third text went to a city that knew what it meant to live in one place and belong to another. Philippi was a Roman colony (Acts 16:12), resettled with army veterans after the battle fought there in 42 BC. Its colonists were Roman citizens, governed by Roman law and living by Roman custom in the middle of Macedonia. A colony didn't exist so its people could evacuate to the capital. It existed to carry the capital's life into the provinces. So when Paul wrote, "But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 3:20, ESV), his readers would have heard themselves described as an outpost, not refugees waiting for a ship. "Savior" and "Lord" were titles the empire gave to Caesar. Paul gave them to someone else.

The next verse is about bodies: "who will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body, by the power that enables him even to subject all things to himself" (Philippians 3:21, ESV). What that finished world will be like belongs to [What Is Heaven Actually Like?](/writing/what-is-heaven-actually-like). What belongs here is the surrounding letter. A few verses earlier Paul confessed, "Not that I have already obtained this or am already perfect, but I press on to make it my own, because Christ Jesus has made me his own" (Philippians 3:12, ESV). And he had told the Philippians about their own messenger, Epaphroditus: "Indeed he was ill, near to death. But God had mercy on him, and not only on him but on me also, lest I should have sorrow upon sorrow" (Philippians 2:27, ESV). The letter that promises glorious bodies records a friend nearly dying. The apostle who had seen the risen Christ still pressed on, still feared sorrow upon sorrow, and wasn't embarrassed to say so.

## Two Ways to Collapse the Overlap

The church's characteristic errors are attempts to escape the overlap, and the New Testament already records both.

The first collapses everything into the already. Paul met it in Corinth and answered with open irony: "Already you have all you want! Already you have become rich! Without us you have become kings! And would that you did reign, so that we might share the rule with you!" (1 Corinthians 4:8, ESV). Anthony Thiselton argued in a 1978 article, "Realized Eschatology at Corinth," that much of that church's trouble, from its spiritual elitism to its contempt for the body to its doubts about a future resurrection, came from believers who thought the age to come had fully arrived for them. Later, Hymenaeus and Philetus were "saying that the resurrection has already happened. They are upsetting the faith of some" (2 Timothy 2:18, ESV).

The modern form says that because Christ has won, the believer with enough faith should have health, wealth and unbroken victory now, so when the cancer returns the fault must lie with the sufferer. Many sick people have been handed that verdict on top of their diagnosis. Kate Bowler, a historian at Duke Divinity School whose *Blessed* (2013) is a leading history of the American prosperity gospel, was diagnosed with stage IV cancer in her mid-thirties, and in *Everything Happens for a Reason: And Other Lies I've Loved* (2018) she describes finding how much of that logic she had absorbed herself, the quiet sense that a well-lived life is owed a good outcome. Her honesty should keep us from pointing only at televangelists. Triumphalism has quieter forms: the congregation where no one admits a struggling marriage because the Spirit-filled are supposed to be past that, and the political hope, on the right and on the left, that the right laws and the right election could make a nation look like the age to come. Each mistakes a real good for the finished kingdom, and each ends by treating the people who don't fit the victory as the problem.

The second error collapses everything into the not yet. Its New Testament voice is the scoffers of 2 Peter, for whom "all things are continuing as they were from the beginning of creation" (2 Peter 3:4, ESV). Inside the church it wears pious clothes. The world is a waiting room, salvation is the soul's departure from it, and there's no point expecting a marriage to heal, a habit to break or a neighborhood to be better for a church's presence. It makes Christians passive about their sin and about the world's suffering. It can pass for humility, but often it's a way to stop hoping so we can't be disappointed again.

Augustine, writing *The City of God* after the Goths sacked Rome in 410, refused both errors. He described the city of God and the earthly city as intermingled in this age, not to be separated until the final judgment. He wrote to answer pagans who blamed the disaster on the Christians, and to steady Christians who had come to read a Christian empire as God's favor made visible and now watched it shake. The church couldn't claim to be the finished kingdom, and it couldn't pretend the heavenly city was absent from the world.

Most of us live in both collapses, sometimes in the same week, and those of us who preach are no exception. When things go well we talk like triumphalists. When things go badly we drift toward the waiting room, where nothing is expected and nothing can let us down. The two moods feel like opposites. Underneath they're the same refusal to live where God has placed us, between a resurrection that has happened and one that hasn't.

## The Overlap on an Ordinary Tuesday

Ephesians holds the tension in two sentences a few pages apart. God "raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus" (Ephesians 2:6, ESV). Then: "Put on the whole armor of God, that you may be able to stand against the schemes of the devil" (Ephesians 6:11, ESV). Seated and armed, the same people in the same letter. The doctrine sounds abstract until it walks into the rooms where people live.

Grief is where most of us meet the overlap without a theory. Paul wrote, "But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13, ESV). He didn't tell them not to grieve. He told them to grieve differently. At the tomb of Lazarus, minutes from raising him, "Jesus wept" (John 11:35, ESV). The one who is the age to come in person still grieved what the present age had done to his friend. A funeral that hurries past the coffin to the victory hasn't understood that, and neither has one that buries the hope with the body.

Illness works the same way. Christians who pray for the sick are asking for a taste of the age to come, and sometimes God gives it. But Paul asked three times for his thorn to be removed and heard instead, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, ESV). Near the end of his life he mentioned in passing, "I left Trophimus, who was ill, at Miletus" (2 Timothy 4:20, ESV). In the overlap, prayer and a physician's care aren't rivals, and neither recovery nor decline is a verdict on anyone's faith. Nothing here is medical advice; the sick need a doctor as well as a church.

Marriage belongs to this age and points past it. Jesus said that "the sons of this age marry and are given in marriage," but those raised to "that age" do not (Luke 20:34-35, ESV), and Paul says the union of husband and wife "refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32, ESV). A Christian marriage is a sign of the age to come lived by two people still in this one. That's why it disappoints the triumphalist, who expected to marry someone finished, and defeats the despairing, who decides that what hasn't changed in ten years never will. Both spouses are new creations, and both carry old wounds into the kitchen every morning. The overlap asks for a patience that neither pretends the other is complete nor writes the other off. It never asks anyone to endure abuse. Patience with a sinner is not permission to be harmed, and no one is required to stay in danger. If you aren't safe at home, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, or in a crisis call or text 988.

Raising children may be the purest schooling in the overlap there is. The triumphalist reads "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6, ESV) as a contract that binds God if the method is followed, though Proverbs is wisdom about how life usually goes, and parents who treat it as a guarantee end up either proud or crushed. The despairing parent stops forming children at all because the culture seems too strong. Paul gives the better word: "And I am sure of this, that he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6, ESV). The work is begun, the completion belongs to God, and the finish line is the day of Christ, not a child's eighteenth birthday.

## What We Do Not Yet See

The letter to the Hebrews states the whole problem honestly before it answers. God has put everything under the feet of his Son, the writer says, and then admits what every reader can see: "At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (Hebrews 2:8, ESV). He doesn't argue with the evidence. He points somewhere else. "But we see him who for a little while was made lower than the angels, namely Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone" (Hebrews 2:9, ESV).

That's where the overlap finally rests. Not on a theory about time, but on a crucified man who has been raised and is still waiting for his enemies to be made his footstool. We don't see everything. We see him. And the one thing already finished is a body that went into the ground and came out again.

So the not yet is still here tomorrow morning: the diagnosis, the marriage that hasn't healed, the child who hasn't come home, the grave. None of it is proof the promises failed. It's the ground between the first sheaf and the harvest, and Christ has already walked all the way across it. We still have to walk it, grieving honestly and hoping without pretending, until the day the ages close.
