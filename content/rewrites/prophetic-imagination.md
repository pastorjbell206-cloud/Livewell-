Almost everyone in the American church wants to be prophetic now. On the right the word names the preacher who defies the culture over sexuality and the unborn; on the left it names the activist who defies the church over race, money and power. Both uses tend to mean the same thing: I am saying something hard, to people I am not, at no cost I can see. Every pulpit, mine included, holds out that small temptation: to be called brave for telling a room what it already believes about someone who isn't in it.

Walter Brueggemann, an Old Testament scholar in the United Church of Christ, wrote a short book in 1978 that should have made that use of the word impossible. *The Prophetic Imagination*, reissued in a second edition in 2001, argues that the prophet's work is neither chiefly to predict the future nor chiefly to denounce the present. It is to nurture and evoke a consciousness and perception alternative to the one the dominant culture has trained into its members, through two acts that sound soft and are not. The prophet criticizes by grieving what the powerful refuse to feel, and energizes by hoping for what the powerful cannot imagine. The book shaped a generation of preachers, most of them in the mainline churches, and it has also served as a license for people who read the title and skipped the argument.

One distinction runs through all of it. In Brueggemann's usage, imagination is not invention. It is perception, the capacity to see what is really there and what the reigning order has trained people not to see. A prophet isn't a person with an unusually creative mind. He is a person who has stopped being numb.

### The empire Moses left and the people he formed

Brueggemann begins with Moses, because in his reading the exodus is what makes prophecy possible at all. Pharaoh's Egypt is his picture of a settled world, and he named two things in it that Moses had to dismantle. One was a religion of static triumphalism, in which the gods were fixtures of the regime, never surprised, never moved, never on the side of the brickmakers. The other was a politics of oppression and exploitation, in which order was kept by the taskmaster. Beneath both lay an economy in which the few were well supplied and the many made the bricks. Each part protected the others. A god who can't be moved by the cry of a slave is exactly the god a slave economy requires.

Against that world, Brueggemann argues, Moses did something more radical than lead a revolt. He dismantled the empire's religion by announcing a God who is free, and he formed a community whose economics and politics answered to that God. Brueggemann's names for the alternative are the religion of God's freedom and a politics of justice and compassion. The hinge of the story is a sound: "the people of Israel groaned because of their slavery and cried out for help. Their cry for rescue from slavery came up to God" (Exodus 2:23, ESV). Pain spoken aloud is already a break with the empire, whose stability depends on pain staying private and therefore unreal. And God answers in words no Egyptian god could use: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings" (Exodus 3:7, ESV).

The community that came out of Egypt was meant to be Egypt's opposite. Its economics began with manna, which could not be hoarded: "whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack" (Exodus 16:18, ESV). The canon never forgot that economics of enough; Paul quotes the verse to the Corinthians when he asks them to share with the poor believers in Jerusalem (2 Corinthians 8:15). Its politics were bound to covenant rather than to a throne, and when Deuteronomy made room for a king, it hedged him with prohibitions that read like Pharaoh in reverse. The king "must not acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses," nor gather many wives or "excessive silver and gold" (Deuteronomy 17:16-17, ESV). Its religion began in Miriam's song on the far shore: "Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea" (Exodus 15:21, ESV). The horses were the empire's power, and the doxology says they are gone.

The story doesn't flatter the people it freed. In the wilderness they looked back on slavery with longing: "Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full" (Exodus 16:3, ESV). That may be the most honest sentence in the Pentateuch about how the royal consciousness holds people. It does not hold them only by force. It holds them by the memory of full stomachs, and a free people will always be tempted to trade its freedom back for the meat pots.

### How Solomon rebuilt Egypt in Jerusalem

The center of the book is Brueggemann's claim that Israel, within a few generations of Moses, rebuilt Egypt with its own hands, and that Solomon was the builder. He calls the result the royal consciousness: a way of seeing the world from the palace, in which the present arrangement is assumed to be permanent, prosperity is taken as proof of God's favor, and whatever the arrangement costs is kept out of sight. He reads Solomon's tenth-century reign as a counterrevolution against Moses and names its achievement in three parts: an economics of affluence, a politics of oppression, and a controlled, static religion in which God and his temple have become fixtures of the royal establishment, available to the king on the king's terms.

The narrator of Kings supplies the evidence with a dry eye. The economics of affluence appear in the palace's daily supply list: "Solomon's provision for one day was thirty cors of fine flour and sixty cors of meal, ten fat oxen, and twenty pasture-fed cattle, a hundred sheep, besides deer, gazelles, roebucks, and fattened fowl" (1 Kings 4:22-23, ESV). The politics of oppression appear in the labor draft: "King Solomon drafted forced labor out of all Israel, and the draft numbered 30,000 men" (1 Kings 5:13, ESV). The book records without comment that Solomon married Pharaoh's daughter (1 Kings 3:1), imported his horses from Egypt (1 Kings 10:28), and gathered wives and gold far past anything Deuteronomy allowed. A reader who knows the law of the king hears each line as a charge. The people who came out of Egypt had a Pharaoh of their own.

The religion changed with the economy. The tabernacle had moved with the people; the temple stood beside the palace, and Solomon dedicated it with a sentence that, read with Brueggemann's categories in mind, sounds like the royal consciousness in miniature: "I have indeed built you an exalted house, a place for you to dwell in forever" (1 Kings 8:13, ESV). The God who had gone with slaves into the desert now had an address, and the address belonged to the king. God became available on the regime's terms, guaranteeing its permanence and never disturbing its accounts. It is Egypt's static triumphalism returned under Israel's own roof: a God whose victory is identical with the present order, and who therefore has nothing left to say to it.

Brueggemann's most penetrating word for what this does to people is numbness. A society organized around its own permanence can't afford to feel its own costs, so it trains its members not to. Pain is managed, grief is hurried, and death is kept out of the conversation. He meant this of Solomon's Jerusalem and plainly also of the affluent West in the late 1970s. The numbness isn't cruelty. Most people inside a royal consciousness are decent; they simply can't feel what their comfort costs someone else, because the arrangement has been designed so that they won't have to.

At Solomon's death the northern tribes came to his son Rehoboam with one request: "Your father made our yoke heavy. Now therefore lighten the hard service of your father and his heavy yoke on us, and we will serve you" (1 Kings 12:4, ESV). It is the groan of Exodus 2 again, and Rehoboam answered it the way Pharaoh had, with a heavier yoke (1 Kings 12:11). The kingdom split around 930 BC and was never whole again.

### Criticism begins in grief

If the royal consciousness runs on numbness, then the first prophetic act, Brueggemann argues, is not an argument. It is grief spoken in public, pain brought to speech that the arrangement has made unspeakable. His model is Jeremiah, and the choice is exact.

Jeremiah was called in the thirteenth year of King Josiah (Jeremiah 1:2), about 627 BC, and he did not want the work: "Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth" (Jeremiah 1:6, ESV). For some forty years he told Jerusalem that its establishment would fall, while priests and prophets who spoke in God's name told the city the opposite. He quoted their reassurance back to them: "They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14, ESV). In the temple sermon, which Jeremiah 26 places early in the reign of Jehoiakim, he named static triumphalism in its own vocabulary: "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (Jeremiah 7:4, ESV). For saying that God was not bound to the building, the priests and prophets demanded his death (Jeremiah 26:11).

What makes Jeremiah the prophet of grief, and not merely of doom, is that he weeps over the people he indicts: "Oh that my head were waters, and my eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people!" (Jeremiah 9:1, ESV). He doesn't pronounce sentence from outside the walls; he stays inside, is lowered into the mud of a cistern for his trouble (Jeremiah 38:6), and is still a prisoner in the court of the guard when the city falls in 587 BC (Jeremiah 38:28). Brueggemann's point is that the grief is itself the criticism. The royal consciousness can absorb opposition; it has offices for that. What it can't absorb is someone who feels what it has forbidden people to feel and says so where everyone can hear.

Here Brueggemann leaned on Abraham Joshua Heschel, whose *The Prophets* (1962) remains one of the deepest modern studies of the prophetic mind. Heschel's central idea is divine pathos. The God of the prophets, he argued, is not the unmoved mover of Greek philosophy but a God affected by what people do to one another, wounded by injustice and grieved by faithlessness, and the prophet is someone drawn into sympathy with that pathos. Jeremiah's tears are the grief of God carried in a human voice. Heschel also pressed a point the American church badly needs. Where wrong is done by some and tolerated by the rest, he argued, guilt may belong to a few, but responsibility belongs to everyone.

Classical Christian theology guards something true with its doctrine of divine impassibility, that God is not at the mercy of moods, and one needn't settle that long argument to receive Heschel's point. Whatever else God is, he is not indifferent.

That yields the distinction on which the rest of the argument turns. Grief and outrage can use the same words, but they are opposite acts. Outrage stands outside the people it condemns and enjoys the distance; it needs an enemy and is quietly disappointed if the enemy repents. Grief stands inside, shares the loss, and wants nothing so much as the repentance that would end it. The prophets were often angry, and God's anger is real in their books, but it is anger on behalf of the wounded, spoken from inside the wound. Anger without grief is only the royal consciousness with a microphone.

### Hope that comes after grief

Grief is the first prophetic task, never the last. The second is what Brueggemann calls energizing: a hope concrete enough, and unlike the present enough, that people can begin to live toward it. His model is Isaiah 40 through 55. Brueggemann, with most critical scholars, assigns these chapters to an anonymous prophet of the exile; many conservative scholars hold that the whole book comes from Isaiah of Jerusalem, speaking ahead of events. On either view the chapters address a people who had lost their city in 587 and who had concluded that their God was defeated or had forgotten them. The text names Cyrus of Persia (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1), whose decree of 538 BC would send them home.

The prophet's first word is tenderness: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (Isaiah 40:1, ESV). He quotes their despair back to them, the only honest way to answer it: "Why do you say, O Jacob, and speak, O Israel, 'My way is hidden from the LORD, and my right is disregarded by my God'?" (Isaiah 40:27, ESV). And he announces a future the exiles had stopped letting themselves picture: "Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it? I will make a way in the wilderness and rivers in the desert" (Isaiah 43:18-19, ESV). The new thing is another exodus. The God who heard the groan in Egypt hears it in Babylon.

Where criticism answers the royal consciousness's numbness, energizing answers its despair, the settled conviction that nothing new can happen. Brueggemann's word for the new thing breaking in is amazement, and he insisted that this hope is not a mood but a way of speaking that breaks the empire's monopoly on the future. A people who can "sing to the LORD a new song" (Isaiah 42:10, ESV) in Babylon are no longer entirely Babylonian. A second distinction belongs here. Optimism is confidence that the present arrangement will keep improving on its own terms; it is the temper of the palace, and it needs no grief because it never admits a loss. Hope is trust in a God who acts from beyond the arrangement, and it is open only to people who have admitted that the arrangement has failed. In the order of Israel's experience, Isaiah 40 comes after Lamentations, and its comfort is spoken to people who have wept.

Brueggemann's closing chapters argue that Jesus brings both tasks to their fullness. The criticism begins in his mother's song: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate; he has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich he has sent away empty" (Luke 1:52-53, ESV). Brueggemann's most arresting claim is that Jesus's compassion was itself a form of criticism. When Jesus "saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34, ESV), he felt what the crowd's rulers had stopped feeling. Coming down toward Jerusalem for the last time, he did what Jeremiah had done: "And when he drew near and saw the city, he wept over it" (Luke 19:41, ESV). Then he entered the temple and turned Jeremiah's temple sermon against it: "It is written, 'My house shall be a house of prayer,' but you have made it a den of robbers" (Luke 19:46, ESV; compare Jeremiah 7:11).

For Brueggemann the cross is the deepest criticism of all: the royal consciousness, Roman and religious together, executed the one man who would not be numb, and God entered the death it inflicted. The resurrection is the deepest energizing, the new thing no empire can administer. The church can stand with him there gladly. It is also where his argument most needs pressing.

### Where Brueggemann needs pressing

A fair reading owes Brueggemann's critics a hearing. The first objection is that his Solomon is a sharper villain than the canon's. The same book of Kings that records the labor draft says that God gave Solomon wisdom as a gift (1 Kings 3:12), that the glory of the LORD filled the temple so the priests could not stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10-11), and that Solomon's dedication prayer contains one of Scripture's great confessions of God's freedom: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27, ESV). Isaiah received his call inside that temple (Isaiah 6:1); Jesus called its successor his Father's house (John 2:16). The canon does not set Moses against the temple as liberation against captivity. The fairer reading is that the institution was God's gift and was captured by the royal consciousness, and that the prophets attacked the capture, not the gift. Brueggemann's scheme illuminates, but it runs smoother than the text.

The second objection concerns history, and it comes from two sides. Brueggemann's picture of early Israel as an egalitarian alternative to the city-states of Canaan sat close to the sociological reconstructions of George Mendenhall, whose 1962 article "The Hebrew Conquest of Palestine" described Israel's origins as a withdrawal of Canaanite villagers from the city-state system, a kind of peasant revolt, and Norman Gottwald, whose *The Tribes of Yahweh* (1979) developed the thesis at length. Later scholarship has criticized those reconstructions heavily (Mendenhall himself rejected Gottwald's Marxist framing of his idea), and critics ask how much of Brueggemann's Moses is a social theory of the 1970s read back into Exodus. Meanwhile Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman argued in *The Bible Unearthed* (2001), on the basis of a lowered chronology for the monumental buildings once credited to Solomon, that tenth-century Jerusalem was a modest highland town and that Solomon's glittering kingdom is largely the creation of later writers. Others, among them Amihai Mazar and Yosef Garfinkel, have contested that chronology and that scale from their own excavations and readings of the evidence.

Even on the minimal view, the portrait of Solomon in Kings exists, and it exists as Israel's indictment of itself; the people who told the story put their most celebrated king in the dock and read him against Deuteronomy. The critique of the royal consciousness is not something Brueggemann imported into Scripture. It is Scripture's own.

The objection from the other side goes deeper. In his *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997), Brueggemann organized Israel's faith around testimony and counter-testimony and deliberately declined to rule on what lay behind the testimony, treating the text's speech about God as the place where theology happens. Critics asked whether that bracket could hold, and the question reaches back to the earlier book. If the exodus is chiefly a story that energizes, and the resurrection chiefly a symbol that interrupts, then the prophetic alternative is only as strong as the imagination of whoever tells it. A community sustained by a moving story is finally sustained by itself.

Here the opening distinction has to be held firmly. The prophetic imagination is perception, not invention. Jeremiah did not imagine Babylon; he saw it coming when the court could not. And the hope of the New Testament rests on a claim about what happened on a particular morning outside Jerusalem. Paul put it without a hedge: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, ESV). The church confesses the bodily resurrection as an event, not a metaphor for renewal, and that confession is what gives prophetic hope its teeth. The royal consciousness can live comfortably with a beautiful story about liberation; it sells such stories every day. What it can't live with is a God who actually raises the dead and brings down the mighty. Brueggemann's book is strongest where it trusts that God, and weakest where it seems content with the imagining.

The third objection is political, and it deserves its strongest form. For many conservative Christians, "prophetic" has become the word the left uses to baptize its platform: the prophets are invoked for policies the educated progressive class already held, and the result is a partisan God, static triumphalism for the other side. That critique has real force. The book was taken up most eagerly in the mainline Protestant world, and some of what was called prophetic there was the royal consciousness of a different palace. The remedy is to read the prophets whole. Amos condemned the comfortable women of Samaria who crushed the needy (Amos 4:1) and the courts that took bribes and turned the needy aside in the gate (Amos 5:12). Isaiah opposed Jerusalem's trust in Egypt's horses (Isaiah 31:1) as fiercely as he opposed its worship without justice (Isaiah 1:13-17). Any use of that instrument that only ever cuts one way has stopped being prophetic.

### The royal consciousness in the American church

Brueggemann wrote about Solomon, but he was writing to Americans. The honest way to use his categories is on every part of the church, with the same instrument and the same grief, beginning with ourselves.

Start with static triumphalism, the part we are least able to see. Robert Bellah argued in "Civil Religion in America" (1967) that the nation carries a religious dimension of its own, a faith in its calling under God that runs alongside the churches without being identical to them. Bellah saw the Civil War, and Lincoln above all, deepen that faith with themes of sacrifice and rebirth. At its best it held the nation under judgment; Lincoln's second inaugural allowed that the war itself might be God's judgment on North and South alike. At its worst it produces what Jeremiah heard at the temple gate. On the Christian right the phrase has often been "this is a Christian nation," doing the same work as "the temple of the LORD": a guarantee that God's favor rests on the arrangement, so that whatever threatens the arrangement threatens God. Where that confidence has fused the cross with the flag and made God the guarantor of national greatness, it has crossed from patriotism into Christian nationalism, and the prophets' word for that was idolatry. The conservative case deserves its strongest form first. Many who love this country believe, with reason, that its liberties grew from Christian soil, and they fear a culture grown hostile to the faith that shaped it. That fear isn't foolish. But Jeremiah's hearers had good reasons to love Jerusalem too, and their love was what made them deaf.

The left has its own temple, harder for its worshipers to see because it flies no flag. In much of progressive Christianity the presiding God guarantees history's direction and turns out to agree with the reasonable people in the room. Confidence that one stands on the right side of history is static triumphalism in a newer suit. It needs no grief for its own sins, because its sins always belong to somebody else, and it can call itself prophetic without once saying anything its own congregation would find costly. Where that faith has subtracted the gospel's offense (judgment, resurrection, Christ's claim on the body and the bed and the bank account) until nothing costs anything, it has become the chaplaincy of a different class. The progressive case deserves its strongest form too: the American church has often stood on the wrong side of the poor and the stranger, and Christians who see that are right to refuse silence. But a prophet who only ever indicts the other tribe has become a court prophet, and it hardly matters which court.

Beneath both temples runs a common religion. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, in *Soul Searching* (2005), found that the working faith of many American teenagers, across traditions, was what they called moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be good and nice, whose chief aim for them is that they be happy and feel good about themselves, and who otherwise stays out of the way until needed. The authors' own image was a God who works like a combination "Divine Butler and Cosmic Therapist." The teenagers learned it from us. That God is static triumphalism for private life, and he sits as comfortably in a conservative megachurch as in a progressive sanctuary.

Beneath all of it runs the economics of affluence and its companion numbness. American Christians, right and left, are among the wealthiest believers in the church's history, and our worship shows it. Lament, which many Old Testament scholars count as the most common form of psalm in the Psalter, has nearly vanished from many of our liturgies. The phone in every pocket supplies a steady feed of outrage, and outrage, as Jeremiah's tears show, is not grief but numbness that has learned to shout. We are angry about nearly everything and sorrowful about almost nothing. That is the royal consciousness, fully furnished.

I can't write this from outside it. I have served as Lead Pastor of First Baptist Church of Fenton, Michigan, for twelve years, and every pastor knows the pull to send people home lighter than they came, to hurry past grief because grief is hard on a Sunday morning, and to say the hard thing about people who aren't in the room. Pastors are the priests at Jeremiah's temple gate far more often than we are Jeremiah. Few of us built the palace, to borrow Heschel's distinction, but all of us are responsible for living comfortably inside it.

What the prophetic imagination asks of a church like ours is less a program than a recovery. It asks for grief first: lament prayed aloud in worship, confession that names our own side's sins before the other side's, and a refusal to let a death, a shuttered plant, a deported neighbor or an aborted child become a statistic. It asks for concreteness, because the royal consciousness is at ease with generalities about justice and life and can't bear specifics about the people in our own towns whose pain pays for our ease. And it asks us to hope after grief and not instead of it, trusting a God who acts rather than a history bound to turn out our way.

The last image belongs to Jeremiah. In the tenth year of Zedekiah (Jeremiah 32:1), with the Babylonian army around Jerusalem and the prophet confined in the court of the guard, God told him to buy a field. It was his cousin's land at Anathoth, in the path of the invading army and about to be worthless. Jeremiah paid seventeen shekels of silver, signed the deed before witnesses, and had it sealed in a clay jar, because "thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: Houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (Jeremiah 32:15, ESV). The man who had wept for the city more than anyone bought property in its ruin. His grief had not been despair, and his hope was not optimism. He had seen the end of the royal consciousness clearly enough to mourn it, and the faithfulness of God clearly enough to invest in what would come after.

That field is where the church is asked to stand. The cross is God's grief over a world that crucifies whoever will not be numb, and the empty tomb is God's refusal to let the palace have the last word. Between them lies the work of a people who can weep and still buy the field. Most of us, right and left, have learned to feel strongly about the other side's sins and very little about our own. Until that changes, we are not the prophets. We are Solomon's court, with better music, waiting for a groan we have trained ourselves not to hear.
