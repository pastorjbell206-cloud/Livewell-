Say the phrase aloud and notice what it does. *The right side of history.* It doesn't argue; it announces. It is one of the few sentences in public life that ends a conversation rather than starting one, and that is much of why it has become so common. It shows up in presidential speeches and corporate statements, in sermons and on the signs people carry. It sounds less like an opinion than a report on the future, filed from somewhere ahead of us. And it feels, when you're the one saying it, wonderful.

I have to admit that first, because I've felt it. I have stood in a room certain that time itself was on my side, that to stand at the leading edge of the present was to stand close to the heart of God. It's a very good feeling. Good feelings are among the cheapest disguises that pride can wear.

The phrase deserves a serious hearing, because it began as a Christian confession and it still carries real moral weight. But somewhere along the way it changed its meaning, and the change is theological. In its original form it said that God is at work in history, bending it toward justice. In its current form it says that history bends itself, and that our task is to discern its direction and get there early. The first is a doctrine of providence. The second is a faith in progress. They sound alike, and they are different religions, with different gods and different judges. The American church, on its left and on its right, has been trading the first for the second for a long time without ever quite deciding to.

## Why the phrase carries such weight

Start by being fair to it, because the strong version is the one worth answering.

The sentence behind the phrase comes from a Unitarian minister in Boston named Theodore Parker. In a sermon published in 1853 in his *Ten Sermons of Religion*, Parker, an abolitionist who sheltered fugitives from slavery, confessed that he could not see very far along the arc of the moral universe; his eye reached only a little way. But from what he could see, he said, he was sure it bends toward justice. A century later Martin Luther King Jr. took that conviction and made it a refrain. Standing before the Alabama state capitol on March 25, 1965, at the end of the march from Selma, he told the crowd that the day of freedom was not far off, "because the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice."

In their mouths it was not a slogan. It was a confession of faith in a God who acts. King was a Baptist preacher, and for him the arc bent because Someone was bending it. It was also a word spoken from the underside of history, by people for whom the present was unbearable and the future was the only place God's justice could yet be seen.

The progressive Christian who uses the phrase today usually means something close to this, and the case deserves to be stated in full. The church has been on the wrong side of the great moral questions of the last two centuries more often than it likes to remember. American Christians defended slavery from the Bible. Methodists and Baptists split over it in the 1840s, long before the nation did; Baptists in the South organized their own convention in 1845 in part to defend the right of slaveholders to serve as missionaries. White churches in the twentieth century produced defenders of segregation, and many more that simply counseled patience while it went on. When a Christian says "the right side of history," she often means: we have been wrong before, badly, and we should have the humility to learn from it. We should ask where the vulnerable are being crushed now, and be on their side before it is safe.

There's a deeper point in her favor, and the historian Tom Holland has made it better than most. In *Dominion* (2019), Holland argues that the moral instincts modern Westerners take for granted, the conviction that the weak have dignity, that victims matter, that the powerful should be judged by how they treat the least, are not the universal inheritance of humanity. They are the long aftershock of Christianity, of a God who was crucified as a slave. When the secular progressive speaks of the arc bending toward justice, she is speaking Christian, whether she knows it or not. The phrase is borrowed from our vocabulary. It carries our capital.

All of that is true, and this essay keeps every word of it. The question isn't whether the church has been wrong, or whether justice matters. The question is what happens to the phrase when the God who bends the arc drops out of the sentence.

## An arc that bends itself

The phrase didn't stay in the abolitionist pulpit. It passed through the nineteenth century and came out on the other side with its subject removed.

The decisive figure is G. W. F. Hegel, whose lectures on the philosophy of history, delivered in Berlin in the 1820s and published after his death in 1837, taught the educated West to read world history as a single rational process: the progress of the consciousness of freedom, Spirit working itself out through the rise and fall of peoples. Hegel thought of himself as a Christian philosopher, and his scheme was full of borrowed theology. But it moved the engine of history from a personal God who judges and redeems to a process that unfolds by its own necessity. After Hegel, and after the many who absorbed his confidence without ever reading him, it became natural to say that history itself was going somewhere, and that the direction could be read.

In 1949 the German philosopher Karl Löwith, who had fled the Nazis and watched one of those confident readings of history end in catastrophe, published *Meaning in History*. His argument was that the modern philosophies of progress, liberal and Marxist alike, are secularized versions of biblical eschatology. They took the Hebrew and Christian conviction that history is moving toward a goal set by God, removed God, and kept the goal. The result, Löwith thought, was a faith that lived off Christian capital while denying the Christian account, and that could not explain why history should have a direction at all once no one was directing it.

Eighteen years before Löwith, a young Cambridge historian named Herbert Butterfield, a Methodist lay preacher, had named the same habit from inside his own discipline. *The Whig Interpretation of History* (1931) described the way historians tend to write the past as a story that leads, step by step, to the enlightened present, praising those who anticipated us and condemning those who stood in our way. Butterfield's warning was that this makes the present the judge of all previous ages, and it flatters the historian into thinking his own moment is the summit. The phrase "the right side of history" is the Whig interpretation turned toward the future. It assumes that tomorrow will look back on today the way we look back on yesterday, and that we already know how tomorrow will vote.

So here is the change. Parker and King said the arc bends because God bends it, and they submitted their own causes to that God's judgment. The modern phrase says the arc bends by itself, and the task is to guess its direction and lean. In the first version, history is under judgment. In the second, history is the judge. That substitution is the whole problem. A God who bends the arc can also rebuke the people who claim to be riding it. A history that bends itself cannot rebuke anyone except the people it leaves behind.

## The people who were sure

If history has a right side, how does anyone know which side it is before history is finished? The phrase claims to read a verdict that hasn't been handed down, and the record of people who were sure they had read it correctly is not encouraging.

A century ago the Americans most confident that they stood on the right side of history were the educated, the scientific and the forward-looking, and a great many of them were eugenicists. On May 2, 1927, the Supreme Court decided *Buck v. Bell* by eight votes to one, upholding Virginia's law for the forced sterilization of a young woman named Carrie Buck. Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., one of the most admired legal minds the country ever produced, wrote the sentence that should haunt anyone who reaches for our phrase: "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." Tens of thousands of Americans were sterilized under such laws in the decades that followed.

The churches were not bystanders. Christine Rosen's *Preaching Eugenics* (2004) documents how many liberal Protestant ministers, along with some Catholic and Jewish leaders, embraced eugenics as the application of their faith to the best science of the day, and how the American Eugenics Society courted them, down to sponsoring contests in the late 1920s for the best sermon on the subject. The progressive pulpit, eager to be on the side of the future, preached the future's cruelty. And who stood on the wrong side of history in 1927? Largely the people the progressives considered backward: Catholic leaders, whose opposition Pius XI made explicit when his encyclical *Casti Connubii* condemned state sterilization in 1930; G. K. Chesterton, whose *Eugenics and Other Evils* (1922) was dismissed as the grumbling of a reactionary; and ordinary Christians who simply believed that a poor woman in Virginia bore the image of God. The future vindicated them. It took a world war and the revelation of where eugenic logic finally led.

This is not an argument that the progressive side is always wrong. It is an argument that being early is not the same as being right, and that confidence about the direction of history has been attached to monstrous causes as easily as to noble ones.

And my own side has no standing to enjoy that paragraph, because the conservative Christian has claimed history just as confidently, only facing the other way. In 1845 the editor John O'Sullivan coined the phrase "manifest destiny" for the conviction that Providence had assigned the continent to the American people, and a great many preachers baptized the claim and the conquest that followed it. Sixteen years later Christians North and South went to war, each side sure it could read God's purposes in events and sure the purposes favored it. The Christian who today insists that some vanished America was God's settled order has made the same mistake as the progressive who is sure of the future: he has fused the kingdom to a moment in time. His moment just sits behind him instead of ahead. Nostalgia and progressivism are the same idolatry facing in opposite directions.

The most theologically serious sentence any American president has spoken was spoken against both. On March 4, 1865, with the war nearly won, Abraham Lincoln stood on the Capitol steps and declined to claim God's side. Of North and South he said, "Both read the same Bible and pray to the same God, and each invokes His aid against the other." And then, "The Almighty has His own purposes." Lincoln had every reason to announce that history had vindicated the Union. He refused, and instead placed both sides, his own included, under a judgment neither could fully read. That is what providence sounds like when it is believed rather than claimed.

## What Scripture calls the future

The Bible's word for the future is not progress. It's judgment, and then mercy, and the two are never pried apart.

The prophet Amos preached in the northern kingdom of Israel in the middle of the eighth century BC, under Jeroboam II, in a season of prosperity and military success. Israel had every reason to believe history was on its side, and it looked forward to the day of the Lord as the day of its own vindication. Amos turned the expectation inside out: "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18, ESV). He told them God hated their feasts and would not accept their offerings, and that what God wanted was this: "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24, ESV). By every available measure Amos was on the wrong side of history. Within about forty years Assyria had swept the northern kingdom away, and his words were kept because they had turned out to be true. He was on the right side of God, and the prophetic tradition is a long argument that the distance between those two can be very great.

Notice that the justice Amos demands is a command he received rather than a trend he observed. It rolls down, like water from higher ground. It doesn't rise from below as the accumulated wisdom of the age. That's the grammar of biblical justice everywhere: it comes from the character of God and is owed because of who he is, not because the future will approve.

Daniel's vision puts the same truth in an image. Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great statue of gold, silver, bronze and iron, a succession of empires, each confident in its own permanence, and then "a stone was cut out by no human hand, and it struck the image on its feet of iron and clay, and broke them in pieces" (Daniel 2:34, ESV). The kingdom of God doesn't arrive as the next stage of the statue's development. It isn't the best of history's empires or its final one. It comes from outside the process altogether, and it breaks the whole thing.

Augustine learned this the hard way, with an empire. When Rome was sacked by the Goths in 410, pagans blamed the Christians for abandoning the old gods, and many Christians were shaken, because they had quietly assumed that the Christian empire was the kingdom of God coming into history. Augustine spent the next thirteen years writing *The City of God*, the longest refusal in Christian literature to let any earthly order bear the weight of final hope. There are two cities, he said, formed by two loves, and they are tangled together in every nation and every heart until the end. Rome was never the kingdom. The Christian who mourned Rome as though the kingdom had fallen with it had confused his two cities. We make the same mistake when we swap the city for a date on the calendar.

The historian Christopher Lasch, who was not writing as a believer, drew the distinction this essay has been reaching for. In *The True and Only Heaven* (1991), his long reckoning with the modern faith in progress, Lasch distinguished optimism from hope. Optimism, as he described it, is the confidence that things will get better, and it depends on the evidence; it collapses when the trend turns. Hope is something older and sturdier: a trust in the goodness of life and the reality of justice that doesn't require believing that history is improving, and that can survive disappointment because it never rested on the forecast. Lasch thought the modern West had traded hope for optimism and was poorer for it. Christians have a name for what he was describing. We call it providence, and we call the posture that trusts it faith.

## The one event that no trend predicted

At the center of the Christian claim is an event that is the exact opposite of the right side of history.

On the Friday of that week in Jerusalem, history had rendered its verdict. The teacher from Galilee had been condemned by the religious authorities, executed by the Roman governor, and buried in a borrowed tomb under guard. His followers had scattered. Every serious person in the city would have told you the matter was settled, and that the movement around him would fade like the others had. The right side of history, that weekend, was a sealed stone and a squad of soldiers.

Then the matter was not settled. The resurrection is not the arc bending upward. It is God reversing history's verdict from outside history, vindicating the one whom every trend had written off. The future didn't produce the resurrection; the resurrection produced the future, and it keeps arriving through the people the trend lines have already dismissed. That's why the Christian hope doesn't depend on whether our cause is rising or falling this decade. It rests on a verdict that has already been announced from the far side of death.

The book of Hebrews keeps a different ledger from the one we're all anxious to be written into. Its eleventh chapter lists the faithful, and then says of them what we tend to skip: "These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13, ESV). Later in the chapter it names people who were mocked and flogged, stoned, sawn in two, who wandered in deserts and caves, "of whom the world was not worthy" (Hebrews 11:38, ESV). By every measure available in their lifetimes, they lost. They were on the wrong side of history. God called them faithful, and that's the only verdict that finally holds. The same Scriptures end not with a trend line but with a proclamation: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (Revelation 11:15, ESV). That is a statement about who wins, not about which of us will be proved early.

## The test, and the judge

The trade from providence to progress never announces itself. It doesn't feel like apostasy; it feels like relevance. Nothing about the conviction has to change for it to happen. What changes is the court it answers to. A Christian can hold exactly the same view of the poor or the stranger that the prophets held and still have moved it, without noticing, from under the judgment of God to under the judgment of the future, so that its real authority is no longer *thus says the LORD* but *this is where things are going*. From the inside the two feel the same. The difference shows only on the day the future seems to turn, and the conviction that was resting on the forecast begins to wobble.

I've caught this in myself. I have found myself weighing a sermon not by whether it was true but by whether it would age well, whether ten years from now I'd be embarrassed to have preached it. That can be a real question. But look at the judge I had seated on the bench: the future, the applause of a generation not yet born. I had quietly swapped the God before whom I will actually stand for a jury of imagined grandchildren, and I did it while feeling more righteous, not less. That is how this particular sin arrives: dressed as conscience.

The conservative reader has his own version, and it's no better. He weighs his convictions by whether they'd have been approved by some remembered generation, by whether his grandparents' church would have recognized them. He has seated a jury of the dead where the progressive seated a jury of the unborn. Both have installed a human tribunal where only God belongs. Both have made history the judge.

So here is the test, and it's a hard one. Take the conviction you're proudest of, the one that makes you feel, when you hold it, like one of the good ones. Now imagine that history turns against it. Imagine your grandchildren are taught that you were a fool, that the future you bet your name on lost, that the room you were glad to stand in emptied and the door was locked behind you. Would you still hold it? If what keeps the conviction alive is your confidence that tomorrow will agree with you, then it was never faith. It was forecasting.

There's a question God asks near the very beginning of the Bible, when the first man and woman have hidden among the trees: "But the LORD God called to the man and said to him, 'Where are you?'" (Genesis 3:9, ESV). He doesn't ask where history is going. He asks where we are standing, and before whom. That's still the only question that is actually ours to answer. None of us knows which way history is going, and all of us have pretended to, and the pretending has let us avoid the question that was put to us.

History will end. It won't end by arriving at the place our side predicted. It will end before the one who walked out of his own grave on a morning no one saw coming, and who will judge the living and the dead, the progressive and the reactionary, the early and the late, with the same justice and the same mercy. There is a right side worth being on. It was never history's. It's the side of the one who will outlast it, and the only way to stand there is the way the faithful of Hebrews 11 stood, and the way Lincoln tried to: not sure of our own vindication, but sure of his.
