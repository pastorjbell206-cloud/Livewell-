The question doesn't usually arrive in a lecture hall. It arrives on the drive home.

You've just done the one thing you were sure you'd never do. You prayed and meant it, something in you that had been locked for years came open, and now you're sitting in the car in the dark trying to account for what happened. There seem to be only two stories available. Either you finally reached out and took hold of God, the best decision you've ever made, or he reached down and took hold of you, and the reaching that felt like yours was already his. For most of my life I was an atheist, so I know the weight of that door swinging open, and I have never once been able to say which side pushed it. Everything in me wants a clean answer. Scripture keeps handing me both.

For others the question comes later and darker, at three in the morning. If God chooses, did he choose me? What if I'm one of the ones he passed over? A doctrine meant to be a comfort has become a trap door.

The formal names for all this are predestination and free will, and Christians have argued about them for sixteen centuries. This essay won't settle the argument, and it's worth saying why before anyone suspects a dodge. It's a second-order question. It divides Reformed churches from Arminian and Wesleyan ones, and Baptists from other Baptists, but it divides people who confess the same Christ, the same cross, the same empty tomb and the same grace none of us earned. So the honest work is to trace how the church came to the question, to give each position in a form its own defenders would sign, to draw the distinction that clears the most fog, and to name what each side has still not answered.

## Older than the Reformation

Popular memory dates the quarrel to John Calvin, which starts the story eleven centuries late. It begins in Rome around the year 400, with a British monk named Pelagius who was appalled by the moral slackness of the Christians around him. God does not command the impossible, he argued, so if God commands holiness, human nature must still have the power to obey. Grace, for Pelagius, was help offered to a will that could take it or leave it. Augustine of Hippo had written a prayer into his *Confessions* (c. 397–400), usually rendered "Give what you command, and command what you will," and he later recalled that Pelagius objected sharply when he heard it quoted in Rome. The prayer assumed what Pelagius denied: that the power to obey is itself a gift.

The controversy ran from about 411 to 418, when the Council of Carthage condemned Pelagius's teaching, and Augustine spent his last years pressing further than many admirers wanted to follow. In *On the Predestination of the Saints* (c. 429) he argued that God did not choose the elect because he foresaw their faith, since faith itself is God's gift, and he credited one verse with changing his own mind years before: "What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you boast as if you did not receive it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7). If even my believing was received, then nothing in my salvation is mine to boast about, including the yes.

Not everyone who rejected Pelagius accepted all of Augustine. Monks in southern Gaul, John Cassian among them, taught that the will could sometimes make the first small movement toward God. The Second Council of Orange answered them in 529. It ruled that even the beginning of faith, the first desire to believe, is a work of grace and not of unaided nature. But Orange stopped short of Augustine's hardest conclusions. It said nothing about grace being irresistible, and it condemned with open abhorrence the idea that God predestines anyone to evil. The Western church settled that grace goes first. It declined to settle how grace and the human will meet.

In the ninth century the monk Gottschalk of Orbais taught a double predestination, to life and to death, and the church did not so much debate him as break him: condemned at Mainz in 848 and Quierzy in 849, he was flogged and confined until his death. The church has tried settling this question with a stick. The stick settled nothing.

Thomas Aquinas, in his *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274), held with Augustine that predestination doesn't rest on foreseen merit. But he added something the later quarrels needed: God's causing and our choosing don't compete on one level, like two people dividing the pull on a rope. God is the first cause who gives creatures their own real causality, so when he moves a human will he moves it as a will, and the act is wholly ours and wholly his at different depths. Both sides have borrowed the idea since.

## From Wittenberg to Dort, and into the Baptist family

In 1524 Erasmus published *On Free Will*, a learned and cautious book: Scripture seemed to him to speak on both sides, and it was wiser to grant the will some small part in salvation than to broadcast doctrines that might drive ordinary people to despair. Luther replied in 1525 with *The Bondage of the Will*, thanking Erasmus for going straight to the vital issue, and rebuking Erasmus's caution in a line that still stings anyone who leaves this question open: "The Holy Spirit is no skeptic." The stakes were pastoral. If his salvation rested even partly on his own will, he could never be sure; because it rests on God, it is certain.

Calvin gave the Reformed position its classic form in the final *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1559), and it matters where he put it. Election doesn't appear in Book I as a first principle from which everything is deduced. It comes in Book III, after faith, repentance and prayer, as an answer to a pastoral puzzle: why does the same gospel land in one heart and bounce off another? He warned against a labyrinth of speculation and admitted that the decree by which God passes over some is *horribile*, a word closer to "dreadful" than to our "horrible." He didn't pretend it was easy.

Jacobus Arminius had studied at Geneva under Theodore Beza and taught theology at Leiden from 1603 until his death in 1609. The next year his followers issued the Remonstrance of 1610. Its five articles held that God decreed before the world to save in Christ those who, through the grace of the Holy Spirit, believe and persevere; that Christ died for all; that no one can do anything truly good or believe without the regenerating grace of God; that this grace can be resisted; and that whether believers can finally fall away needed further study. The third article matters most for fairness. Arminius was no Pelagian. He stood on the floor Orange laid.

The Synod of Dort (1618–19) rejected the Remonstrance point by point; its Canons, later compressed into the modern mnemonic TULIP, became the Reformed standard. The aftermath is a grief the Reformed family should carry honestly. Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the statesman who had protected the Remonstrants, was beheaded in May 1619, Hugo Grotius was sentenced to life imprisonment, and Remonstrant ministers were deposed and banished.

Rome had its own version of the quarrel and handled it differently. In *Concordia* (1588) the Jesuit Luis de Molina proposed that God has a "middle knowledge" of what every free creature would freely do in any possible circumstance, so that by choosing which circumstances to bring about, God ordains everything without determining the choice. The Dominicans called this Pelagian and were called Calvinists in return. In 1607 Pope Paul V ended the dispute by allowing both schools and forbidding either to call the other heretical. A church can decide not to decide, and sometimes that's the wisdom.

The revivals carried the quarrel forward. In 1739 John Wesley preached and printed his sermon "Free Grace," arguing that unconditional predestination makes preaching pointless and God unjust, and George Whitefield answered him in a letter dated December 1740 and printed early the next year. The two friends broke, were later reconciled, and when Whitefield died in 1770, Wesley preached his funeral sermon at Whitefield's own request. The disagreement was real, and so was the brotherhood.

Baptists carry both streams in their blood. The General Baptists, whose first congregation on English soil Thomas Helwys gathered around 1612, held that Christ died for all. The Particular Baptists, who issued the First London Confession in 1644, were Calvinist, and by the eighteenth century some of their churches had drifted into a hyper-Calvinism that hesitated to offer the gospel to the unconverted. Andrew Fuller broke that reticence in *The Gospel Worthy of All Acceptation* (1785), using Jonathan Edwards's distinction between natural and moral inability to argue that every sinner is obligated to believe and every preacher obligated to invite. Out of that argument came William Carey and the Baptist Missionary Society in 1792, and with them what historians often call the modern missionary movement. Calvinists launched it. In America, Calvinist Philadelphia Baptists and Benjamin Randall's Free Will Baptists grew side by side, and the Southern Baptist Convention has held both kinds since 1845. Its present confession, the Baptist Faith and Message (2000), calls election "the gracious purpose of God" and says it is "consistent with the free agency of man," without explaining how. It's a treaty as much as a doctrine, and a wise one.

## The Reformed case at full strength

The caricature says Calvinists believe in a cold God flipping coins over souls. No serious Reformed Christian believes that.

The case begins where Paul begins, before the foundation of the world, in the mind of God: "even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him. In love he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will" (Ephesians 1:4–5). The choosing precedes anything you did to recommend yourself, and its reason is the purpose of God's will, not your foreseen goodness. The Reformed instinct guards that with everything it has, because the moment salvation depends on something in you, even the decision to say yes, you've found one corner of your rescue you can take credit for, and Paul spent his life burning down every corner like that.

Then the Reformed reader turns to Romans and refuses to flinch. "For those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, in order that he might be the firstborn among many brothers. And those whom he predestined he also called, and those whom he called he also justified, and those whom he justified he also glorified" (Romans 8:29–30). No link in that chain drops a person between the calling and the glory. In chapter 9, speaking of Jacob and Esau, chosen before they were born, Paul concludes: "So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy" (Romans 9:16). Then comes the objection Paul expects: "You will say to me then, 'Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?'" (Romans 9:19). Nobody raises that objection against election based on foreseen faith. It arises only if Paul means something close to what Augustine and Calvin said, and his reply is a rebuke, not a softening: "But who are you, O man, to answer back to God?" (Romans 9:20).

John 6 carries the same weight from the lips of Jesus. "All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (John 6:37). "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day" (John 6:44). The ones the Father draws are the ones raised; the drawing isn't a general tug most people successfully resist. And the pattern holds at the center of history. Peter tells the crowd at Pentecost that "this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). The cross was planned, and the men who drove the nails were guilty. If God can ordain the worst act in history without excusing those who did it, divine ordination and human responsibility aren't rivals.

That's why the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), having said that God did "freely and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass," adds in the same sentence that "neither is God the author of sin, nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures, nor is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established." Jonathan Edwards gave the philosophical account in *Freedom of the Will* (1754). A person is free, Edwards argued, when he does what he wants. The sinner's inability to come to God isn't a locked door keeping out someone who longs to enter. It's the absence of the longing. Grace doesn't drag the unwilling; it makes the unwilling willing.

Beneath the machinery is comfort. If God chose you before the foundation of the world, your worst Tuesday can't unchoose you. Your salvation never rested on the part of you that fails. To the people who hold it, this is the warmest doctrine they know, and the reason they can sleep.

## The Arminian case at full strength

Give the other side the same refusal to caricature. The Arminian doesn't think he saved himself; he denies it as hotly as any Calvinist.

The Arminian begins elsewhere in the same Bible, with what God says he wants. "This is good, and it is pleasing in the sight of God our Savior, who desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:3–4). Explaining why the Lord's return seems slow in coming, Peter says he "is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). Jesus looks over Jerusalem and says, "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (Matthew 23:37). The Arminian won't let those texts be shrunk to mean "all kinds of people." If God genuinely desires the salvation of all, a doctrine in which he decided before the world began to leave most of them to perish has made that desire unreal.

The Arminian answers with a doctrine worth learning by name: prevenient grace, the grace that goes before, which Wesley called "preventing grace" in the old sense of coming first. No sinner chooses God by his own unaided strength. God comes first, always, and wakes the dead heart enough that it can respond. But having opened the lock, he doesn't force the door. The response is enabled by grace and still genuinely the person's own, which means it could have gone the other way.

The Arminian also reads Romans 9 inside the whole argument of chapters 9 through 11. The question Paul is answering is announced early: "But it is not as though the word of God has failed" (Romans 9:6). Israel has largely rejected its Messiah, and Paul must show that God has kept his promises anyway. Jacob and Esau stand for two peoples as well as two men; "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated" (Romans 9:13) quotes Malachi 1:2–3, where the prophet speaks of Israel and Edom. On this reading Romans 9 concerns God's freedom to choose whom he will use in the history of salvation, not a roster of who goes to heaven. And the argument doesn't stop at 9:20. It runs through God's lament, "All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people" (Romans 10:21), to its summit: "For God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all" (Romans 11:32). A reader who stops at chapter 9, the Arminian says, has stopped in the middle of a sentence. Paul opened that same chapter with "great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" (Romans 9:2) for his unbelieving kinsmen, which is hard to square with a matter already sealed.

John 6, read whole, also says, "For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life" (John 6:40), and Jesus later promises, "And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (John 12:32). The drawing, on this reading, reaches everyone; the coming is the response.

Beneath the exegesis sits an intuition about love that Wesley pressed hard. A love that cannot be refused, the Arminian says, is something wearing love's face. If the unchosen can't finally say yes, the pleading of the prophets becomes a performance with the ending fixed. When Jesus grieved over Jerusalem, the "you were not willing" was real. The Remonstrants weren't trying to steal God's glory. They were trying to keep him from being the author of the sin he condemns.

## What the creeds bind, and what they leave open

One distinction clears most of the fog. Both sides affirm that God is sovereign and that human beings are responsible. The deepest disagreement is about what freedom is. A compatibilist holds that a choice is free when it flows from the person's own desires without coercion, even if God determined those desires. A libertarian holds that a choice is free only if, with everything else the same, the person could have chosen otherwise. Edwards and Westminster are compatibilist; Wesley and the Arminian tradition are libertarian. When a Calvinist says, "You chose freely, and God ordained your choice," the Arminian hears a contradiction. When an Arminian says, "God enabled you, and you could have refused," the Calvinist hears that the decisive difference between the saved and the lost lies in the human. Each is being consistent. They're using the word "free" for two different things.

Now name the order of the question. The creeds bind a great deal: one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit; Christ fully God and fully man, crucified, bodily risen and coming again; salvation as God's own act of grace. Orange adds, for the Western church, that the first stirring of faith is itself grace. Augustine and Aquinas, Calvin and Arminius, Molina and Wesley all stand on that floor. What no ecumenical creed defines is the mechanism by which sovereign grace and the human will meet. That's what makes this second-order. It divides faithful churches, but it doesn't divide the faith.

Scripture sets both edges in a single breath. "Therefore, my beloved, as you have always obeyed, so now, not only as in my presence but much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:12–13). The command lands on you, and in the same sentence even your willing is God's work. Paul doesn't tell you which clause bears the load. Wesley preached a whole sermon on those verses, "On Working Out Our Own Salvation" (1785), and kept both halves, as Reformed preachers have. The traditions at war over this question are quoting the same verse.

Two modern positions deserve a fair hearing. Karl Barth opened the doctrine of election in his *Church Dogmatics* II/2 (1942) with the claim that "the election of grace is the sum of the Gospel." He objected that the older Reformed view left a hidden decree behind the back of Jesus. For Barth, Jesus Christ is both the electing God and the elected man, who bears the rejection that belonged to us; we are chosen in him, as Ephesians says. Critics ask whether that leads to universal salvation, which Barth neither affirmed nor denied. But he recovered something both older traditions knew and sometimes forgot: election is to be looked for in Christ, not behind him.

Open theism, set out by Clark Pinnock and his colleagues in *The Openness of God* (1994), holds that the future free choices of creatures don't yet exist to be known, so God knows everything knowable but not what free creatures will do. Its defenders say this honors the texts in which God relents and grieves. It should also be marked plainly as outside the historic mainstream. Augustine and Pelagius agreed that God foreknows all things, and so did Aquinas, Calvin, Arminius, Molina and Wesley. Open theism doesn't settle the old argument. It leaves the ground on which both sides stood.

## The objections still standing

Each side has put its hardest question to the other.

To the Arminian, the Reformed Christian says: you've kept your freedom, but look what it cost. If grace enabled everyone equally, the enabled still divide into those who said yes and those who said no, and the line runs through something in the human. However small you make it, that one thing came from you. Are you sure that, at the last inch, it isn't a work? The Arminian replies that receiving a gift isn't earning it, and a beggar who opens his hand has nothing to boast about. It's a good reply. Whether it fully answers the question of 1 Corinthians 4:7, what do you have that you did not receive, is exactly what Augustine thought decided the matter.

To the Calvinist, the Arminian says: you've protected God's sovereignty, but look what you risked. If God chose, before I drew a breath, to pass over the man down the street, and then commanded me to tell him that God desires his salvation, is that desire real? And if every choice, including the first sin in the garden, was ordained before the world began, whose hand finally moved when the fruit came down? The Reformed answer reaches for Acts 2:23 and for Joseph's words to his brothers, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20). That answer has real force. But how the desire of 1 Timothy 2:4 is sincere toward the one God passes over, many Reformed theologians admit they can't finally say, and they call it mystery. Calling it mystery may be honest. It doesn't make the question go away.

One more objection is aimed at this essay. Luther's "The Holy Spirit is no skeptic" is the strongest charge against leaving the question open: Scripture isn't vague, he would say, and you've mistaken indecision for humility. The essay hasn't fully answered him. It can say that declining to choose between two faithful readings isn't doubting that Scripture speaks. The Spirit is no skeptic about the grace that goes first, the cross that saves or the invitation that goes out to everyone. Whether he has told us how grace and the will meet is what faithful readers have disputed since Orange, and both the council in 529 and the pope in 1607 judged some questions better left open than closed by force. Luther wouldn't be satisfied. He may be right not to be.

You can close this page a Calvinist and remain a brother or sister to the Arminian in the next pew. What the question is built to teach is the thing most of us are worst at, and I'm among them: to hold a conviction with everything you have in front of someone who holds the other one, and to refuse to make him a fool for standing where the church has let faithful people stand for four centuries. That's harder than winning the argument. It may be the point of an argument God has left open.

## Am I chosen?

Come back to the person awake at three in the morning. Am I chosen? What if I'm not?

When the two traditions turn from each other toward that person, they say the same thing. Calvin, supposedly the cold one, told his readers not to hunt for their election in the secret counsels of God but to look at Christ, whom he called the mirror in which we are to contemplate our election. The Canons of Dort contain an article written for exactly this fear: those who don't yet feel a lively faith or settled assurance, but still use the means God gives and long to please him, are told not to be alarmed by the doctrine of reprobation or to count themselves among the reprobate. People who don't care about God don't lie awake wondering whether he wants them. And Wesley would have told the same person what he told the crowds in the fields: the invitation is for you, tonight, whoever you are.

Traditions that disagree about nearly everything in the machinery send the frightened soul to the same place. Neither sends you into the hidden decree to search for your name. Both send you to the cross, where the choosing God isn't hidden at all. "All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (John 6:37). You don't have to settle which half of that verse bears the weight to act on the second half. If you aren't sure you've come, you may come now, and the promise doesn't check your doctrine of election at the door.

John Bunyan, in *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners* (1666), described long torment over whether he was among the elect, and he lived to write *The Pilgrim's Progress*. If the dread has become a loop you can't break however often you hear the promise, tell a pastor, and don't be ashamed to see a Christian counselor. The fear is a burden to be carried with help. It isn't a verdict on your soul.

The question from the car never quite resolves. Some nights I read Ephesians and I'm sure he took me. Some nights I read the last invitation in the Bible, "let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who desires take the water of life without price" (Revelation 22:17), and I'm sure he waited for me to come. Paul ended three chapters of the hardest argument in Scripture not with a solution but in worship: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!" (Romans 11:33). A God I had invented would have picked a side and let me sleep. This one chose before the world was made and still stands with his hands held out all day long, and he has never told me how both are true. He has only shown me where to look. At the cross the rulers scoffed, "let him save himself, if he is the Christ of God, his Chosen One!" (Luke 23:35). The Chosen One was rejected there so that no one who comes to him would be. That doesn't answer the question in the car. It's what you carry out of it, and it's enough to kneel on.
