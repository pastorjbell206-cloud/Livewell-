A coworker is off wine until Easter. A friend is deleting the apps from his phone for six weeks, and someone at church admits it's chocolate again this year. A thoughtful skeptic usually sees one of two things in it: a wellness program in vestments, or something darker, a bargain in which people make themselves a little miserable so that a watching deity will think better of them. The second is exactly the kind of religion many people walked away from, some of them for good reasons.

Some of that suspicion is earned. A good deal of what passes for Lent in American churches is a diet with a liturgical label, quietly abandoned by the second week. And the charge that fasting becomes a way of paying God isn't an outsider's caricature. The Reformers pressed it from inside the church, the prophets pressed it before them, and Jesus pressed it first.

But that's only part of the story. Two fasts can look identical and point in opposite directions. One is fasting as payment: a work offered to God in order to move him, a sacrifice of comfort that earns favor or proves how serious we are. The other is fasting as hunger: the body's way of wanting a God who has already given himself, and of confessing that we have wanted lesser things more. The first treats God as a creditor who must be satisfied. The second treats him as a bridegroom who has gone away for a while and is coming back. Nearly everything in the Christian practice of fasting, from Moses on the mountain to the ash on a forehead at the start of Lent, turns on which of those two fasts a person is keeping.

**Christians observe Lent and fast because Jesus assumed his followers would, and because the church learned early that a people who never go hungry on purpose slowly forget what they were made to be hungry for.** Lent is the church's forty-day form of that practice, set before Easter: a season of chosen emptiness in which the comforts we use to muffle our restlessness are set aside long enough for a deeper hunger to be heard.

I came to faith from atheism, and for a long while I had never once gone hungry on purpose. What I learned when I finally tried was humbling, and at first it had little to do with God: how much of an ordinary day ran on small, constant appeasements of appetite, and how rarely I had let even one go unanswered long enough to ask what it was covering. How the church keeps time across the whole year has [its own essay](/writing/why-the-church-has-a-calendar). This one stays with the fast itself.

## Where the Forty Days Come From: Moses, Elijah and Jesus

In Scripture forty marks a span of testing and formation: the rain of the flood, Israel's years in the wilderness, and three long fasts the church has read together for most of its history. Each is more merciful than the popular picture of Lent as spiritual endurance.

The first belongs to Moses, and its setting matters more than its length. Exodus 32 tells how Israel, soon after hearing God's voice at Sinai, cast a golden calf and worshiped it. Moses broke the tablets. Then God called Moses back up the mountain to cut new stones, and passed before him proclaiming his own name as merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love (Exodus 34:6). Only then comes the fast: "So he was there with the LORD forty days and forty nights. He neither ate bread nor drank water. And he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the Ten Commandments" (Exodus 34:28). The first forty-day fast in the Bible is not a man earning God's favor. It's a man held in God's presence while a broken covenant is written again, too occupied with God to eat.

Elijah's forty days sit even more oddly with the idea of fasting as achievement. After Mount Carmel he ran from Jezebel, sat under a broom tree, and prayed to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life" (1 Kings 19:4). God's answer to a despairing prophet was a cake baked on hot stones, water, sleep, and more food. The text never calls what follows a fast, though the church has long read it as one, and it is carried by that food: "And he arose and ate and drank, and went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God" (1 Kings 19:8). At Horeb, the same mountain as Sinai, God was not in the wind or the earthquake or the fire but in "the sound of a low whisper" (1 Kings 19:12). Grace came first, and the forty days were walked on borrowed strength.

Then Jesus. Matthew places the wilderness immediately after the baptism, where the voice from heaven said, "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17). Matthew goes straight on: "Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil" (Matthew 4:1). The next verse follows: "And after fasting forty days and forty nights, he was hungry" (Matthew 4:2). The order carries the theology. The Son is named beloved first and only then led into hunger, and the tempter goes straight at the name, daring him as Son of God to turn stones into bread (Matthew 4:3). Jesus answers from Deuteronomy 8, where Moses explained Israel's forty years of manna: "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). Where Israel grumbled in the wilderness and Adam reached for the offered food, the true Israel and last Adam goes hungry and waits for his Father.

Read together, the three fasts buy nothing. Moses fasts inside a mercy already proclaimed, Elijah on bread already given, Jesus as a Son already named. The forty days follow grace rather than purchase it, and they lead toward a meeting with God rather than a reward from him. When the church settled on forty days before Easter, it understood itself to be walking behind Jesus into his wilderness, not climbing toward God on its own stamina. The season borrows its length from him. So does its meaning.

## What Scripture Says Fasting Is For

Scripture doesn't treat fasting as self-evidently good. Its sharpest words about it are warnings to people who were fasting correctly by every outward measure. Isaiah 58 opens with the people complaining that God hasn't noticed their fasting, and with God's reply that on their fast days they pursue their own interests and oppress their workers (Isaiah 58:3). They had kept the fast and expected payment. God names the fast he actually wants: "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke?" (Isaiah 58:6). And the next verse brings it all the way home: "Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house; when you see the naked, to cover him, and not to hide yourself from your own flesh?" (Isaiah 58:7). The bread you don't eat is supposed to end up on someone else's table. Zechariah heard the same word from another angle. After the return from Babylon, a delegation from Bethel asked whether to keep the fasts the people had kept for decades, and God answered with a question: "Say to all the people of the land and the priests, When you fasted and mourned in the fifth month and in the seventh, for these seventy years, was it for me that you fasted?" (Zechariah 7:5). After seventy years of observance, the only question that mattered was whom it was for, and it hangs over every Lent.

Joel supplies the positive form. Facing a locust plague, the prophet calls the whole people to a public fast: "‘Yet even now,’ declares the LORD, ‘return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning’" (Joel 2:12). Joel won't let the fast become theater: "and rend your hearts and not your garments.’ Return to the LORD your God, for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love; and he relents over disaster" (Joel 2:13). Those words are Exodus 34 again. Joel's people fast toward the character God has already revealed, not to coax him into kindness, which is why Ash Wednesday has read Joel 2 for centuries.

Jesus inherits both the practice and the warning. "And when you fast, do not look gloomy like the hypocrites, for they disfigure their faces that their fasting may be seen by others. Truly, I say to you, they have received their reward" (Matthew 6:16). He says "when," not "if," setting fasting beside giving and praying as ordinary parts of life before the Father. "But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face" (Matthew 6:17), he goes on, and gives the reason: "that your fasting may not be seen by others but by your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you" (Matthew 6:18). The reward he means is the Father's own attention, which the hypocrite traded for an audience.

The most important verse on fasting in the New Testament may be the one quoted least. Asked why his disciples didn't fast, Jesus answered with a wedding: "Can the wedding guests mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them? The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast" (Matthew 9:15). Christian fasting, on Jesus's own account, belongs to the time between his going and his return. A fast that is about our discipline or our spiritual résumé has missed the bridegroom entirely. A fast that is about his absence is simply what longing does in a body.

The church in Acts kept that kind of fast, and kept it together. In Antioch, among the church's prophets and teachers, "While they were worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit said, ‘Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them’" (Acts 13:2). The response was more of the same: "Then after fasting and praying they laid their hands on them and sent them off" (Acts 13:3). The fast was shared, joined to worship and prayer. Nobody in Antioch was fasting to earn a mission. They were fasting to hear one.

## How the Early Church Turned a Short Fast into Lent

Lent wasn't handed down from the apostles as a finished season. It grew over roughly three centuries out of older practices.

The earliest layer is weekly. The Didache, a church manual from the late first or early second century, tells Christians not to fast with "the hypocrites" on Monday and Thursday, the customary days of pious Jewish fasting and probably the days behind the Pharisee's boast, "I fast twice a week; I give tithes of all that I get" (Luke 18:12). Christians should fast on Wednesday and Friday instead (Didache 8). The irony is hard to miss: the earliest Christian rule about fasting defines itself partly against someone else's fast, the very spirit Jesus warned against in that Pharisee. The danger was present from the start.

The second layer is baptism. The same manual asks the one baptizing, the one being baptized and any others who can to fast beforehand, and the candidate to fast for a day or two (Didache 7). Around 155, Justin Martyr described the practice in Rome for a pagan emperor: those who come to believe are taught to pray and to ask God, with fasting, for the forgiveness of their past sins, and the church prays and fasts with them (*First Apology* 61). That last clause is the seed of Lent: a community going hungry alongside the people it was about to receive.

The third layer is the fast before Pascha, the Christian Passover. Late in the second century Irenaeus of Lyon wrote to Victor of Rome during a quarrel over the date of Easter, in a letter Eusebius preserved (*Ecclesiastical History* 5.24). Churches disagreed, Irenaeus observed, about the fast as well as the day: some thought they should fast one day, others two, others more, and some reckoned their one day as forty hours, day and night together. As late as about 190, then, the fast before Easter was short and not uniform. Around 200 Tertullian named Pascha the most solemn day for baptism (*On Baptism* 19), and as Easter became the great night of initiation, the catechumens' final preparation gathered into the weeks before it, with the whole church fasting beside them and public penitents brought through the same weeks toward reconciliation.

Where exactly the number forty came from is still argued. Thomas Talley argued in *The Origins of the Liturgical Year* (1986) that one root lies in an Egyptian forty-day fast that followed the feast of Christ's baptism, imitating his wilderness fast after the Jordan. Paul Bradshaw and Maxwell Johnson, in *The Origins of Feasts, Fasts and Seasons in Early Christianity* (2011), conclude that Lent has several roots, braided together in the fourth century. The first conciliar notice is not in dispute. The fifth canon of the Council of Nicaea in 325 told each province to hold two synods a year to review sentences of excommunication, one of them before the *tessarakoste*, "the Forty," so that quarrels might be put away and the offering made to God with a clean heart. The canon doesn't create or regulate the fast. It assumes the bishops already know what "the Forty" is. By the 330s Athanasius of Alexandria was announcing a forty-day fast in his annual Easter letters, and in 340, writing from exile in Rome, he pressed the Egyptian churches to keep the whole forty days, so that Egypt would not be laughed at as the one place that feasted while the rest of the world fasted.

East and West counted the forty differently. In the West, where Sundays stayed feasts of the resurrection and weren't fasted, reaching forty fasting days meant beginning on a Wednesday. Its ashes came from public penance, where penitents were marked with ash and clothed in sackcloth at the start of Lent and restored at its end. Over time the ashes spread to everyone; a synod at Benevento in 1091 under Pope Urban II is usually cited as the point at which the Western church commended them to all the faithful. The words spoken over the ashes come from God's sentence in Eden: "for you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19). The whole church took its place in the penitents' line, which was the right instinct.

Leo the Great, preaching his Lenten sermons in fifth-century Rome, tied the fast to almsgiving, to forgiving injuries and to reconciling enemies, so that what was withheld from the stomach went to the poor. That is Isaiah 58 in a Roman pulpit. But over the following centuries the season thickened with regulation, and fasting was folded into the penitential system as satisfaction for sin. The fast that began as a church standing with its new members had become, for many by the late Middle Ages, a law with a price list. That was the problem the Reformers inherited.

## Why the Reformers Objected to Lent

The most famous Lenten meal in Protestant history was a plate of sausage. In March 1522, in the Zurich house of the printer Christoph Froschauer, a group of men ate smoked sausage during the Lenten fast. Huldrych Zwingli, the people's priest at the Grossmünster, was present and didn't eat, but he defended the men from the pulpit and in April published the sermon as *On the Choice and Freedom of Foods*. His argument wasn't that fasting is wrong. It was that no human authority may bind a Christian conscience where Scripture leaves it free: let whoever wishes fast, but no church may make eating a sin that God never called one. Paul had said as much: "For the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit" (Romans 14:17). The sausage opened the Swiss Reformation.

John Calvin gave the Reformed objection its most careful form in the final edition of the *Institutes* (1559), in the chapter on church discipline (IV.12). What's striking is how much fasting Calvin kept. He named three honest purposes for it: to tame the flesh, to prepare the heart for prayer and meditation, and to bear witness to our humility before God when we confess our guilt. He commended public fasts in times of calamity or weighty decision. His objection was aimed at three errors: the outward fast without the rent heart, which was Joel's warning; the fast treated as a meritorious work or a form of divine worship; and the fast exacted too strictly as a chief duty and praised so extravagantly that people imagined they had done something admirable by fasting. Later centuries, he added, had gone further and bound consciences with laws against eating flesh. On Lent he was blunt. He called its observance superstitious, a fast set up under the pretext of imitating Christ, whose forty days, he argued, were not an example to copy but a sign, like the miracle of Moses' fast when the law was given, that his gospel came from heaven.

The Westminster Assembly's *Directory for the Public Worship of God* (1645) declared that festival days had no warrant in Scripture, but kept a section on public, solemn fasting, a whole day kept when great judgments had fallen or threatened, or when some special blessing was to be sought. The Puritans fasted often but refused a fixed season, a habit that reached as far as Abraham Lincoln's proclamation, in March 1863, of a national day of humiliation, fasting and prayer. Lutherans and Anglicans, by contrast, kept much of the old calendar.

Both sides deserve their strongest form. The Reformed case runs like this. Scripture commands fasting but never commands Lent, and whenever the church binds consciences with rules God didn't give, it drifts into the religion Paul described: "These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (Colossians 2:23). History, on this view, shows what a fixed season produces: legalism for the scrupulous, loopholes for the comfortable, and a quiet belief in both that God is impressed.

The case for keeping Lent is just as serious. The church in Acts fasted together, and a common season rescues fasting from the private zeal that turns it into a personal project, while keeping the old logic of Justin's Rome, the whole church hungry beside the people it will welcome at Easter. Catholic and Orthodox Christians add that they don't teach fasting as the purchase of justification; the Council of Trent's decree on justification (1547) itself says that nothing preceding justification, faith or works, merits its grace. Penitential fasting, in their account, is a response to grace and a sharing in Christ's suffering. Whether the late medieval church lived up to that is another question, and the Reformers had reason to doubt it.

This is not a first-order question. The creeds say nothing about fixed fasts, and faithful churches have answered it differently without dividing the faith. This essay concludes that Jesus's "when you fast" makes fasting ordinary Christian practice, not an extra for the unusually devout, and that Lent is an old and wise way to keep that fast together, held as a freedom and never as a law. A Baptist or Presbyterian church that keeps no Lent but teaches its people to fast is not less faithful than one that marks every Ash Wednesday. The strongest objection, which this conclusion doesn't fully answer, is Calvin's argument from history: fixed seasons have repeatedly hardened into law and merit, and no structure can guarantee they won't again. The only honest reply is vigilance, Zechariah's question put to every fast every year.

## Wesley, Foster and the Recovery of Fasting

John Wesley refused to let fasting go. At Oxford, in the circle his mockers called the Holy Club, he kept the old Wednesday and Friday fasts of the Didache, going without food until three in the afternoon. In *The General Rules of the United Societies* (1743), among the ordinances every Methodist was expected to attend upon, beside public worship, the Lord's Supper, prayer and searching the Scriptures, he listed "fasting or abstinence." In the seventh of his discourses on the Sermon on the Mount (1748), he explained the second word as eating less, a partial fast for those who can't fast entirely because of sickness or bodily weakness. The same sermon held exactly the Reformers' line: fasting is a way God has ordained for waiting on his mercy, never an end in itself, and we must beware of imagining we merit anything by it. To those who said a Christian should fast from sin rather than from food, he answered that God requires both: abstain from sin always, and from food often, for the ends Scripture and experience show it serves. He held the Reformation's doctrine of grace and saw no conflict with a weekly fast.

The practice faded anyway, because a prosperous, busy people stopped thinking they needed it. Richard Foster's *Celebration of Discipline* (1978) had to make the case for fasting to serious Christians who had never been taught it. His central claim is that fasting, more than any other discipline, brings to the surface the things that control us. We cover anger, anxiety and restlessness with food and other comforts, and when the comforts are withdrawn, what was underneath comes up. That is the first mercy, since nobody can repent of what he refuses to see. Dallas Willard's *The Spirit of the Disciplines* (1988) supplied the logic: we can't do by direct effort in the moment of temptation what we can learn to do by training beforehand. A person who has never let a small appetite go unanswered has nothing in reserve when a large one arrives.

Scot McKnight's *Fasting* (2009) added the correction the recovery needed. McKnight argued that biblical fasting is chiefly a response, the whole person's reaction to a grievous sacred moment such as sin exposed, death or calamity. People in Scripture fast because something has happened, not to make something happen, which sets him against fasting as a technique for getting results from God, the merit-fast in modern clothes. The Orthodox theologian Alexander Schmemann, in *Great Lent* (1969), described the season from within a tradition that still fasts hard as a return to the God we've forgotten, marked by what he called a bright sadness, grief over sin lit from the start by the coming Pascha. The Catholic Church, in Paul VI's *Paenitemini* (1966), reduced its binding fast days to Ash Wednesday and Good Friday and commended works of charity as penance. Across the traditions the recovery has moved the same way, from fasting as a law that counts toward fasting as a response of love.

Fasting from food is not for everyone, and the church has always known it. This essay is not medical advice, and nothing in it replaces a doctor who knows you. Anyone who has struggled with an eating disorder, or any pattern of restricting or punishing the body with food, should not undertake a food fast at all. The vocabulary of self-denial can feed a disorder that already speaks in moral tones, and no discipline is worth a relapse. Someone in that place can keep Lent by giving up something that isn't food, the phone or the noise, with a counselor and a pastor who know the history. Women who are pregnant or nursing, people with diabetes, especially those on insulin or other medicines that lower blood sugar, anyone on medication that must be taken with food, people with heart or kidney conditions, the frail and elderly, children and teenagers, and anyone underweight should talk with a physician first, and many should not fast from food at all. Catholic law itself binds only adults from eighteen to fifty-nine, and its pastoral guidance excuses the sick. And remember Elijah, fed and rested before he went anywhere. Hunger taken up for God never requires hurting the body he made and redeemed.

## Fasting to Earn, or Hungry for the Bridegroom

The merit-fast is attractive, and I'm not above it. It offers what the heart badly wants, a way to be good at religion. Whether the ledger is kept by a medieval confessor or by an American evangelical quietly proud of a sugarless Lent, the logic is the same. The fast becomes a thing I offer, God becomes the one I'm trying to impress, and the season meant to empty me of myself leaves me fuller of myself than before. The wellness version is the same fast aimed at a different god, a sharper, lighter, more productive me, called spiritual because it happens in February.

The hungry fast works the other way around. It begins from the name already spoken at the Jordan, the mercy already proclaimed on Sinai, the bread already baked under the broom tree. It doesn't try to get anything from God. It admits that we've been trying to be satisfied by everything except God, and it takes the ordinary comforts away for a season so that the admission can be felt rather than merely said. Then it turns outward, because the bread not eaten belongs to the hungry. And it waits, as Jesus said his friends would in the days after the bridegroom was taken from them.

That is why Lent ends where it does. The forty days walk straight into Holy Week, to a Friday when the bridegroom really was taken away, and then to a Sunday when the tomb was found empty and the feast began again. Easter isn't a reward for a good Lent. It's the thing the hunger was for. Even then the church doesn't stop fasting for good, because the wedding supper is still ahead. The prophet saw where it ends: "Thus says the LORD of hosts: The fast of the fourth month and the fast of the fifth and the fast of the seventh and the fast of the tenth shall be to the house of Judah seasons of joy and gladness and cheerful feasts. Therefore love truth and peace" (Zechariah 8:19). Every Christian fast is provisional, kept by people who know the fasting will be turned into a feast and who aren't there yet.

So the ash still goes on the forehead, and the words are still true: dust, and to dust. Most years I would rather be full than hungry, and comfortable enough not to notice what I'm missing. The fast doesn't let me keep that arrangement. It asks, in the plainest language the body knows, whether I actually want the one I say I love, or only the life he's made comfortable. That question doesn't get settled in forty days. It follows you out of the sanctuary on Ash Wednesday into a kitchen on an ordinary Friday, and it waits there, past the moment the stomach complains and the phone lights up, for an answer only you can give. The bridegroom is still away. Whatever we're hungry for, this season will tell us.
