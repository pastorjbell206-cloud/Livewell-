Most people who have never opened a Bible know what a good Samaritan is. The phrase names hospitals, relief agencies and the laws that protect a bystander who stops to help from being sued for trying. When a stranger pulls over in the rain to change someone's tire, the local news calls him a good Samaritan, and everyone understands: a decent person helped when he didn't have to.

Some of that is true. A civilization that names its emergency rooms after this story has been shaped by it. But it is the easy part. Jesus did not tell this parable to encourage kindness in general. He told it to a man who wanted to know where his obligations ended, and he made the hero a member of the one group his hearers least wanted to owe anything to. Luke never calls the Samaritan good. That adjective came later, from readers who could no longer feel how strange it would have sounded.

The parable turns on a distinction we still need. The lawyer asked, in effect, who qualifies as my neighbor. Jesus answered by asking who proved to be one. The first question is about the edges of a category, and it can be settled at a desk. The second is about what kind of person I will be when someone is bleeding in front of me, and it can only be answered on a road.

## Who Is My Neighbor? The Question Behind the Parable

Luke sets the story on a road with a history. A chapter earlier, "he set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51, ESV), and at once a Samaritan village refuses to receive him. James and John answer the way their culture trained them to: "Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?" (Luke 9:54, ESV). Jesus rebukes them. A few paragraphs later he tells a story in which a Samaritan is the only one who keeps the law. Luke means for us to remember the fire.

"And behold, a lawyer stood up to put him to the test, saying, 'Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?'" (Luke 10:25, ESV). He is an expert in the Torah, and Jesus sends him back to his own field: what does the law say, and how do you read it? The lawyer joins the command to love God with all one's heart, soul, strength and mind (Deuteronomy 6:5) to the command in Leviticus 19:18 to love "your neighbor as yourself." Jesus agrees: "You have answered correctly; do this, and you will live" (Luke 10:28, ESV). The exchange could have ended there. "But he, desiring to justify himself, said to Jesus, 'And who is my neighbor?'" (Luke 10:29, ESV).

That question deserves a fair hearing before we call it bad faith, because serious people were arguing about it. Leviticus 19:18 sits inside a sentence about "the sons of your own people," so a careful reader could take "neighbor" to mean a fellow Israelite. Sixteen verses later the chapter widens the circle: "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 19:34, ESV). So where is the line? The community behind the Dead Sea Scrolls bound its members, in its Community Rule, to love the sons of light and hate the sons of darkness, and Jesus himself cites a popular gloss of the same kind: "You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy'" (Matthew 5:43, ESV).

Christians still ask it, and not always wrongly, because finite people cannot love everyone in the same way. Augustine argued in *On Christian Doctrine* (397) that all people are to be loved equally, but that since we cannot do good to all, we owe particular care to those whom time, place and circumstance have bound more closely to us. Paul is blunter: "But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever" (1 Timothy 5:8, ESV). There is an order to love. A father who feeds the city while his sons go hungry has not become more Christian.

But Luke has told us what this question was for. The lawyer had heard the command land, and he wanted to know how far out it reached so he could be sure he stood inside it. A question about the order of love became a question about the edge of love. Ask the first and you are trying to love well with limited strength. Ask the second and you are looking for the place where you are allowed to stop. A neighbor with edges always produces people outside them, and that edge is exactly what Jesus declined to draw.

## The Jericho Road and the Men Who Passed By

Jesus doesn't give a definition. He tells a story on a road everyone in his audience knew. "A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and he fell among robbers, who stripped him and beat him and departed, leaving him half dead" (Luke 10:30, ESV). The road ran roughly seventeen miles from the hills of Jerusalem down to Jericho, below sea level near the Jordan, dropping more than three thousand feet through broken wilderness. Pilgrims, merchants and priests traveled it constantly, and so did robbers.

Notice what the robbers took. In a world where you placed a stranger by his clothing and his accent, a stripped and unconscious body could not be placed at all. Kenneth Bailey makes much of this in *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* (2008): the man in the ditch has no tribe, religion or rank that anyone passing can read. Jesus has built a victim who gives the lawyer's question nothing to work with.

"Now by chance a priest was going down that road, and when he saw him he passed by on the other side" (Luke 10:31, ESV). A Levite does the same. Preaching has usually explained them through ritual purity, and that explanation deserves its strongest form. The law forbade priests to make themselves unclean for the dead except for their nearest kin (Leviticus 21:1–3), and it said plainly, "Whoever touches the dead body of any person shall be unclean seven days" (Numbers 19:11, ESV). A man left half dead might be fully dead. On this reading a conscientious priest was caught between two commands and chose the one that protected his service to God.

The story doesn't support the excuse well. The priest is going down from Jerusalem, away from the temple, and the Levite wasn't bound by the priestly corpse rule. The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200 and so later than the parable, holds that even a high priest, who may not defile himself for his own kin, does defile himself for an abandoned corpse with no one else to bury it (Mishnah Nazir 7:1), and the conviction that saving a life overrides nearly every other command runs deep in Jewish tradition. Amy-Jill Levine, a Jewish New Testament scholar, presses this in *Short Stories by Jesus* (2014). She argues that no first-century Jewish listener would have assumed purity required leaving a dying man in the road, and that Christian interpreters reached for the purity explanation because it let them blame the priest's religion instead of the priest. Her charge lands. For generations Christian preachers made this story a lesson about Jewish legalism, using a parable about refusing to draw boundaries to draw one. Jesus gives the priest and the Levite no reason at all. We are the ones who keep supplying motives, and the motives we supply tell on us.

In 1973 John Darley and Daniel Batson published "From Jerusalem to Jericho" in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. They asked forty students at Princeton Theological Seminary to prepare a short talk, some on seminary careers and some on the Good Samaritan, and sent each across campus to give it, some told they had time to spare, some that they should go now, some that they were already late. On the way each passed a man slumped in a doorway, coughing and groaning. Of those with time to spare, 63 percent offered help; of those in a moderate hurry, 45 percent; of those who were late, 10 percent. Overall, 40 percent offered some help. Preparing to speak on the parable made no significant difference, and the authors noted that more than one student hurrying to talk about the Good Samaritan stepped over the man to get there.

It was a small study and shouldn't carry more than it can bear, but the authors' interpretation is worth keeping: the hurried students were less callous than conflicted, bound by a promise to be somewhere that was louder than the stranger in the doorway. That is the priest and the Levite exactly. The religion that walks past the dying rarely hates them. It has somewhere to be, and the somewhere is usually respectable.

## Why Jesus Made the Hero a Samaritan

Jesus's first hearers would have expected a third traveler. Israel was commonly reckoned in three ranks: priests, Levites and Israelites. Levine points out that once a storyteller says "a priest" and "a Levite," the audience is waiting for "an Israelite," the layman who shames the professionals. Jesus tells a different story. "But a Samaritan," Luke writes, and the room would have gone cold.

The hostility was old, and each side told it so that it was the faithful party. When Assyria conquered the northern kingdom and its capital, Samaria, in 722 BC, it deported much of the population and settled foreigners in their place (2 Kings 17:24), and the Judean account treats the worship that followed as a compromise. The Samaritans saw themselves as the heirs of northern Israel who had kept the Torah and the true place of worship, Mount Gerizim, the mountain of blessing in Deuteronomy 27. It was a family quarrel about which branch had kept faith, and family quarrels are the bitterest kind. After the exile, Sanballat, governor of Samaria, opposed the rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls (Nehemiah 4:1–2). The Samaritans' own temple on Gerizim stood until the Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus destroyed it late in the second century BC, as Josephus records in his *Jewish Antiquities*. The traditional date is 128 BC; excavations on the mountain point to about 110 BC. Josephus also tells how, early in the first century AD, some Samaritans scattered human bones in the Jerusalem temple's porches at Passover. John explains for his readers, "(For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans.)" (John 4:9, ESV), and when Jesus's opponents wanted to insult him, they reached for the worst word they had: "Are we not right in saying that you are a Samaritan and have a demon?" (John 8:48, ESV).

So the Samaritan is not a foreigner who happens to be kind. He is the heretic and the rival. Levine suggests we will only hear the parable if we put in his place the person we would least want to owe our life to. Then the question is not only whether I would help my enemy, but whether I could bear to be helped by him, and admit he might show the mercy my own people withheld.

Then Jesus slows down, as though he wants the lawyer to watch every movement. The Samaritan came to where the man was, "and when he saw him, he had compassion" (Luke 10:33, ESV). The verb is *splanchnizomai*, a word rooted in the gut, compassion felt before it is reasoned through. Luke uses it only three times: of Jesus meeting the widow of Nain on the way to bury her only son (Luke 7:13), of the father who "saw him and felt compassion" in the parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15:20, ESV), and of this Samaritan. In Luke's vocabulary the enemy on the Jericho road feels what God feels.

Every step after that costs him. He binds the wounds with oil and wine, the ordinary medicine of the day and, as Bailey observes, the same oil and wine offered at the temple the priest and Levite had just left. He sets the man on his own animal, which means he walks. He brings him to an inn and cares for him through the night. Then he pays two denarii, about two days' wages for a laborer (Matthew 20:2), and leaves an open account: "Take care of him, and whatever more you spend, I will repay you when I come back" (Luke 10:35, ESV). Bailey argues that a penniless man in an inn could be held for his debts, and that a Samaritan arriving with a wounded Jew risked suspicion, even revenge. He made himself responsible for someone he did not choose, with no end date on the obligation.

## Which of These Proved to Be a Neighbor?

Then Jesus hands the question back, and he hands it back changed. "Which of these three, do you think, proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers?" (Luke 10:36, ESV).

The lawyer stood at the center and asked which people around him qualified for his love. Jesus asks which of the three proved to be a neighbor, using the ordinary Greek verb for becoming. Now the man in the ditch is at the center, and "neighbor" is no longer a status other people hold in relation to me. It is something I become, or fail to become, when I meet someone in need. The lawyer wanted a list. Jesus gave him a mirror.

Søren Kierkegaard saw this in *Works of Love* (1847). Christ, he observed, does not teach the lawyer how to recognize his neighbor but how to be one, as the Samaritan was, by mercy. This is what separates Christian love from preferential love: preference picks its objects, the friend, the beloved, the people like us, and so it always has a boundary.

So the distinction this essay stands on is simple to state and hard to live. "Who qualifies as my neighbor?" is a question about other people. It can be argued at length in the abstract and settled so that I am already in compliance. "Will I be a neighbor?" is a question about me, and it can't be answered in the abstract at all. It is answered on a road, in the presence of a particular body, usually at an inconvenient hour. The first question can be asked cheaply. The second can only be answered at a cost.

The distinction does not abolish the order of love that Augustine and Paul defended. A mother still owes her children what she does not owe a stranger. But the order of love is a question for someone who has already decided to stop; it helps a willing person spend limited strength wisely, and it becomes a lie the moment it explains why I didn't cross the road. The Samaritan had no kinship or citizenship binding him to the man. What he had was proximity: the need in front of him became his obligation because it was in front of him.

The lawyer's answer is famous for what it leaves out. "He said, 'The one who showed him mercy.' And Jesus said to him, 'You go, and do likewise'" (Luke 10:37, ESV). Many readers, Bailey among them, hear a man who can't bring himself to say "Samaritan," though he may simply have named the quality that mattered. Either way, he has confessed that the enemy kept the law he had just recited.

And the command circles back. "Do this, and you will live" (Luke 10:28, ESV) has become "You go, and do likewise" (Luke 10:37, ESV). The lawyer came looking for a law he could satisfy and was handed one he could not. No honest hearer thinks, *I have loved like that, without limit, at my own cost, toward the people I was raised to despise.* Taken simply as law, the parable justifies nobody. It leaves the listener lying in the road of his own failure, and that is where the church's oldest reading of the story begins.

## How the Church Has Read the Good Samaritan

For most of Christian history the parable was read first as a picture of salvation. Origen, in the third century, passed along an interpretation he credited to an elder before him, in which the wounded man is Adam and the Samaritan is Christ. Augustine gave the reading its most famous form around 400, in his *Questions on the Gospels* (2.19). The man is Adam, humanity itself, going down from Jerusalem, the heavenly city of peace, toward Jericho, whose name Augustine linked with the moon and so with mortality. The robbers are the devil and his angels, who strip humanity of immortality and leave it half alive, still able to know God, and half dead under sin. The priest and Levite are the ministry of the old covenant, which could not heal. The Samaritan, a name Augustine took to mean "guardian," is the Lord; the animal is the flesh in which he came to us; the inn is the church, where travelers returning home are restored; the innkeeper is the apostle Paul.

It is easy to smile at this. Nothing in Luke suggests Jericho means the moon. But feel what the reading did for those who heard it. It put every listener in the ditch, rescued at another's cost before being asked to rescue anyone. It kept the parable from becoming a whip.

The objections grew. John Calvin, commenting on the passage in his *Harmony of the Evangelists* (1555), dismissed the allegory, especially its use of "half dead" to argue that free will survived the fall, and said that reverence for Scripture forbids us to disguise its natural meaning. The decisive break came with the German scholar Adolf Jülicher, whose *Die Gleichnisreden Jesu* appeared in two volumes, the first in 1886 and the second in 1899. Jesus's parables, he argued, were not allegories; each made a single point, and hidden meanings in the details were the church's invention. He classed this one as an "example story" that simply models the conduct it commends. C. H. Dodd, opening *The Parables of the Kingdom* (1935), held up Augustine's reading as a specimen of the method scholarship had to leave behind.

The gain was real. Once the Samaritan is simply Christ, his ethnicity stops mattering, and the offense that makes the story work disappears. The historical reading gave the enemy back his face.

The loss was also real. Reduced to an example story, the parable became a law and nothing more: be kind, go and do likewise. That is the Jesus of much liberal Protestantism, a teacher of ethics whose death is incidental, and just as surely the Jesus of much conservative moralism, a lawgiver whose rules we are expected to keep. Either way the reader is left where the lawyer was left, facing a command no one has fulfilled, with no word about how a person who walks past becomes a person who stops.

Recent scholarship has made a partial return. Craig Blomberg argued in *Interpreting the Parables* (1990) that Jülicher's single-point rule was too rigid, and that Jesus's parables often carry a point for each main character, so some allegorical elements belong to the stories themselves. Bailey found in the Samaritan's costly, unexpected rescue a demonstration of the love the gospel describes. Joseph Ratzinger, writing as Benedict XVI in *Jesus of Nazareth* (2007), argued that the Fathers' reading, though not the parable's first meaning, catches something true: humanity itself lies wounded beside the road, and God in Christ has come near.

The honest way to hold this is to distinguish allegory from recognition. Allegory assigns meanings to details from outside the story. Recognition notices that the story's own shape rhymes with the Gospel it sits in: Luke uses one word of compassion for Jesus, the prodigal's father and this Samaritan, and in John's Gospel Jesus's enemies called him a Samaritan. None of that makes the inn the church. It does suggest Luke expected his readers to see more in the one who crossed the road than good manners.

Levine resists christological readings of the parable, and she is right to insist that the first sense, a Jewish teacher speaking to Jewish hearers, must not be erased. But on one point she and Augustine, from opposite directions, agree. The listener's first place in the story is in the ditch.

## King on the Jericho Road, and the Church Between Right and Left

On the night of 3 April 1968, in Memphis to support the city's striking sanitation workers, Martin Luther King Jr. preached at Mason Temple the sermon now known as "I've Been to the Mountaintop." He recalled driving the Jericho road with his wife and seeing how its winding course invited ambush, and he allowed that the priest and the Levite may have been afraid. Then he named the question he thought had stopped them: if I stop to help this man, what will happen to me? The Samaritan, King said, reversed it and asked what would happen to the man if he did not stop. King turned the question on everyone in the room weighing what solidarity with the strikers would cost. The next evening he was shot and killed at the Lorraine Motel.

The lawyer asked who qualifies. The priest, in King's reading, asked what it would cost. The Samaritan asked what the wounded man needed. Each question moves the center of gravity further from the self.

King also saw that the parable reaches past the ditch to the road. Exactly a year before his death, on 4 April 1967, in "Beyond Vietnam" at Riverside Church in New York, he said that playing the Good Samaritan on life's roadside can only be a first act, and that eventually the whole Jericho road must be changed so that people are not beaten and robbed on it again and again. Yet in the Mountaintop sermon he teased the priest and the Levite with the possibility that they hurried past on their way down to Jericho to organize a Jericho Road Improvement Association, preferring to attack the problem at its root rather than get tangled up with one bleeding man. King held both halves together: the road must be changed, and talk about changing the road is no excuse for stepping over the body on it. That double judgment is the instrument the church needs, because Christians on the right and the left have each found a way to answer the lawyer's question in their own favor.

On the right, the temptation is to shrink the circle to the fellow citizen, the fellow church member, the deserving poor, the person whose paperwork is in order. The order of love becomes a reason never to meet the stranger, and the parable is kept strictly private, as though who keeps sending men into that ditch were no concern of ours.

On the left, the temptation is to enlarge the circle until no one in particular stands in it. Humanity is loved at the scale of policy, a vote or a post is treated as the crossing of the road, and the Samaritan becomes a slogan that costs the one wielding it nothing. And the left draws its own boundary just as firmly: the political enemy is the one person who may never be imagined as the rescuer.

Levine's exercise cuts both with the same edge. For some readers the Samaritan is the undocumented laborer who pulls them from a wrecked car on a county road. For others he is the man with the flag on his truck who stops when everyone else drives by. Either substitution offends, and it is meant to.

I am inside this indictment. I have walked past on the other side, and it rarely looked dramatic; it looked like a full calendar. Churches do the same at scale, serving the poor at a safe institutional distance and reporting service hours without anyone having been changed by the people served. The priest and the Levite had a system that let them pass by without thinking of themselves as bad men. So do we.

What the parable asks of the church is as plain as what the Samaritan did. He came near, and a church cannot love its neighbors from a distance it never intends to close. He paid, spending oil, wine, animal, night and money on someone who could return none of it, and love that costs nothing has not yet become neighbor love. And he crossed a line his whole people had drawn, so the test of a congregation is whether it will stop for the ones its own tribe has taught it to despise. On an ordinary Tuesday that looks like margin in the schedule for an interruption, a congregation that knows the names on its own block, and a willingness to be seen helping someone our side would rather we ignored.

## The One Who Crossed the Road

None of this is done by resolve alone. If the story ended with "go and do likewise," it would be the heaviest law in the Gospels, and Luke doesn't let it end there. The man who told it was walking the other direction on that road, up toward Jerusalem, with his face set. At the end of that road he would be stripped, his clothes divided by lot. He would be beaten, and he would die outside the city while the respectable passed by. The one who told the story of the man in the ditch became the man in the ditch.

And he became the Samaritan too, the despised outsider who crossed over to people with no claim on him. Paul says it without a parable: "For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life" (Romans 5:10, ESV). We were not neighbors when he stopped. We were the enemy, stripped and half dead and unable to explain ourselves, and he came to where we were and paid what we could not.

So "Will I be a neighbor?" is not finally a question about willpower. People lifted out of the ditch by the one they despised have lost the right to ask who qualifies, and they slowly gain, against their own instincts, the ability to stop. The inn is where the wounded are restored. It is not where they stay.

The road is still there. It runs past the hospital room you have been avoiding, the family on your street whose trouble you have learned not to notice, the neighbor whose politics tighten your jaw. Somewhere on it this week someone is lying where the robbers left him, and you have somewhere to be. The lawyer's question will always give you a reason to keep walking. The other question gives you none. It only asks you to look at the one in front of you and remember who stopped for you.
