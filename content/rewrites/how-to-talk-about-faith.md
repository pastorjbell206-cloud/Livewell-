Most Christians know the moment. A coworker mentions over lunch that her mother is dying, or a friend on the sideline at your kid's practice asks what you did this weekend, and for half a second a door stands open to say something about God. Then the other half of the second arrives, full of reasons to let it close. You don't want to be that person. You've met that person, and you remember the look on the other face when a conversation turned into a presentation. So you say something kind and general, the moment passes, and on the drive home you honestly can't tell whether you were being wise or just afraid.

Both of those fears are reasonable. The fear of being weird is the fear of treating a person as a project, and people can feel that from across a room. The fear of saying nothing is the fear that a faith which never speaks has quietly stopped believing its own news. Most advice picks one fear and waves off the other. Neither survives contact with the New Testament, or with the people we're actually talking to.

I came to faith from atheism, so the unbeliever isn't a category I've only read about. And the awkwardness Christians feel in these moments isn't only nerves. Some of it has been earned, by methods and scandals and alliances that the person across the table knows about even when we'd rather she didn't.

Talking about faith isn't what makes Christians weird. What makes it weird is a borrowed form: the shape of a sales encounter, with its opening, its presentation and its close, laid over a relationship that was supposed to be a friendship. The New Testament offers a different form, older than the booklets and better suited to the neighbors we actually have. In it, Christ is honored first in the speaker's own heart, the other person is heard before she is answered, and the answer comes, when it comes, to a question the Christian's life has raised.

## Who the nones in your life actually are

The word *nones* comes from survey research: people who answer "none" when asked their religion. In its January 2024 report, *Religious "Nones" in America: Who They Are and What They Believe*, the Pew Research Center put them at 28 percent of American adults. How that happened is its own question, taken up at length in [Why Are People Leaving the Church?](/writing/why-people-are-leaving-the-church). The question here is narrower and more personal. Who is actually sitting across from you?

The first answer is that most of them aren't atheists. In that same report, 17 percent of the nones called themselves atheists and 20 percent agnostics. The other 63 percent said their religion was "nothing in particular," and most nones told Pew they believe in God or some other higher power. The political scientist Ryan Burge spends much of *The Nones* (2021) on that largest group, and what he finds there is mostly indifference rather than anger. The nothing-in-particulars haven't reasoned their way out of faith so much as drifted loose from it, and from much else that once tied Americans to their neighbors. For the most part they aren't rebels. They're unattached.

This matters because Christians usually rehearse for the wrong person, the confident atheist with an objection about evolution or suffering. He exists, and he deserves serious answers. But the woman in the next cubicle is more likely to be someone who hasn't thought much about God since middle school, who figures there's probably something out there, and who prays when her son is in the hospital. You can't win an argument she isn't having. Approaches built for the lapsed churchgoer, which press on guilt he was taught to feel and doctrines he was taught to know, find nothing in her to press on. Some nones, especially younger ones, were never given the Christian story in the first place, so there's nothing for them to have rejected.

What most of them have been given is a great deal of information about Christians. Alongside a vague and often sincere spirituality, many nones keep something like a ledger: the abuse scandals and the cover-ups, the preacher with the private jet, the church that turned away a cousin, the rally with a cross on the stage. Some entries are unfair. Many aren't. The concealment of sexual abuse in the Catholic Church was not a rumor invented by the church's enemies, and neither was the 2022 Guidepost Solutions report on how leaders of the Southern Baptist Convention had handled abuse claims. No pastor, this one included, can read that report as a story about strangers.

The ledger has a political page too, and it cuts in more than one direction. For many nones the word *Christian* now names a voting bloc on the right, a faith conscripted into a flag. For others, the churches that speak loudest from the left sound like one more advocacy group with hymns, a faith dissolved into a cause. Either way the word has been annexed by a politics, and the person across the table needs some reason to believe we mean something older than the last election.

So when a none hears a Christian start talking about God, she often isn't hearing about Jesus of Nazareth at all. She's hearing the ledger. That isn't fair to every believer, and it's still the room we're speaking into. The first honest move in talking about faith is to admit that the ledger exists and that we wrote a good many of its entries.

## Why the old methods feel like a sales call

Most American Christians who were taught anything about sharing their faith learned methods from the middle of the twentieth century, and those methods deserve a fair hearing. In 1965 Bill Bright, who had founded Campus Crusade for Christ in 1951, published a booklet, *Have You Heard of the Four Spiritual Laws?*, that could walk a stranger through the gospel in a few minutes. Its first law was that God loves you and offers a wonderful plan for your life. In 1962 D. James Kennedy, pastor of Coral Ridge Presbyterian Church in Fort Lauderdale, began training church members in what became *Evangelism Explosion*, a presentation opened by two diagnostic questions: whether a person was sure of going to heaven, and what he would say if God asked why he should be let in.

Neither man was a cynic. Both believed, as the church always has, that the stakes of the gospel are eternal, and both were troubled by Christians who believed it and never said a word. Their programs gave frightened laypeople words, putting sin and grace, the cross and the call to trust Christ, into a form a college sophomore could carry into a conversation. Real people came to real faith that way. The strongest defense of the old methods is also the simplest: if you actually believe a person's eternal life is at stake, silence isn't a form of respect.

The sharpest version of that defense has come from an unbeliever. The magician Penn Jillette, an outspoken atheist, once said in a short video that he had no respect for Christians who believe in heaven and hell and keep it to themselves. If you were sure a truck was about to hit someone, he reasoned, you'd tackle him. That challenge is fair.

What went wrong was mostly the form. These programs taught a sequence any salesman of the era would have recognized: an approach, a qualifying question, a presentation, objections handled, a close, and success counted in decisions recorded. The habit is older. Charles Finney argued in his *Lectures on Revivals of Religion* (1835) that a revival is not a miracle but the result of rightly using the appropriate means. He meant to wake a drowsy church, and he did, but he also taught American Protestants to think of conversion as the product of a technique. The form also assumed a country in which the hearer already knew the vocabulary, already assumed God, and already felt guilt pointed roughly the right way. Ask the nothing-in-particular woman in the next cubicle whether she's sure she'd go to heaven, and the question doesn't arrive as good news. It arrives as a pitch, for a product she never asked about, from an institution whose ledger she has already read.

A distinction carries much of the weight here, because without it the reaction against the old methods slides into a reaction against speaking at all. Persuasion and manipulation are different acts. Luke tells us that Paul reasoned in the marketplace in Athens every day and that in Corinth he "tried to persuade Jews and Greeks" (Acts 18:4, ESV). Persuasion addresses a person's reason and conscience in the open. It tells her everything you know, the weak points included, and it leaves her free to say no and still be your friend. Manipulation works around a person's reason. It manufactures urgency, conceals its agenda, uses the relationship as a lever, and needs a yes. The first honors the image of God in the other person. The second treats that image as an obstacle.

The softer alternative, usually called friendship evangelism, sometimes made things worse by hiding the technique inside the friendship. Few things wound like discovering that the neighbor who brought soup had a conversion target date. The test is simple and uncomfortable: if this person never believes, will I still be here? If the honest answer is no, the friendship was a method, and people can feel a method from a long way off. Most of us who were trained in these habits know what it is to listen to someone's grief with half our attention on the turn we hoped to make. That half of our attention wasn't love, and the person we were talking to usually knew it before we did.

## What Peter told a church with no advantage

The verse most often quoted in evangelism training is 1 Peter 3:15, and it's usually quoted from the middle. Peter was writing to "elect exiles" scattered across Asia Minor (1 Peter 1:1, ESV), small households with no standing, no protection and a reputation as troublemakers. He has just told them not to fear their accusers or be shaken by them, borrowing Isaiah's counsel to Judah in a season of threat (Isaiah 8:12–13). Then he writes:

> "but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you;" (1 Peter 3:15, ESV)

Before Peter mentions the neighbor, he addresses the speaker's own heart. He takes Isaiah's instruction to regard the Lord of hosts as holy and quietly applies it to Christ. The order is practical wisdom as well as theology. A Christian whose heart isn't settled on Christ will reach for pressure to make up the difference, because he needs the conversation to go well for his own sake. A Christian whose heart is settled can let the other person think it over, or say no.

Then comes the phrase we read past: *to anyone who asks you*. The word translated "defense" is *apologia*, the ordinary word for a reply in a courtroom, and a reply presupposes a question. Peter takes for granted that the exiles' lives, their steadiness under accusation, their honesty in trade, their households run on something other than the empire's appetites, will be odd enough to prompt questions. The witness he describes isn't an opening move. It's an answer. And what needs answering for is "the hope that is in you," something the questioner has already glimpsed in a person she knows, rather than a system of ideas delivered cold.

The next verse is the half the training usually drops:

> "yet do it with gentleness and respect, having a good conscience, so that, when you are slandered, those who revile your good behavior in Christ may be put to shame." (1 Peter 3:16, ESV)

The manner belongs to the command. "Gentleness" is the quality Jesus claimed for himself when he said "I am gentle and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29, ESV). "Respect" translates *phobos*, the ordinary word for fear, here meaning reverence, whether toward God or toward the one asking. Either way, contempt can't live alongside it, and neither can any procedure that handles a person as an objective. Peter then points to the conscience and conduct that make slander look foolish. The exiles' whole case rested on how they lived between the questions, which is exactly where the nones' ledger says the American church has been weakest.

Paul, writing to Colossae from prison, asks the church to pray that God would open a door for the word (Colossians 4:3); he asks God to open it rather than telling them to force it. Then he turns to their conduct:

> "Walk in wisdom toward outsiders, making the best use of the time." (Colossians 4:5, ESV)

> "Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how you ought to answer each person." (Colossians 4:6, ESV)

*Outsiders* is Paul's plain word for those outside the church, not a list of prospects. "Making the best use of the time" carries the image of buying up the moment, which the King James rendered "redeeming the time." It means being awake to the moment that actually comes, the dying mother or the question about your weekend, rather than forcing one that hasn't. "Always gracious" covers all our speech, including how we talk about the boss. And like Peter, Paul assumes the other person speaks first: the Christian's part is to *answer*. The last two words close the door on every script: *each person*. A method answers everyone the same way. Wisdom answers the person in front of you.

## Paul at Athens: listening before speaking

If Peter gives us the posture, Luke gives us a conversation. Acts 17 shows an apostle talking about faith with people who had no Scriptures and no shared vocabulary, the nearest biblical parallel to a lunch table in a secular office. It opens with Paul alone in the city:

> "Now while Paul was waiting for them at Athens, his spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols." (Acts 17:16, ESV)

Luke doesn't pretend Paul was serene; the verb behind "provoked" is the one English borrowed as *paroxysm*. What's striking is what Paul did with the disturbance. He spent his days in the marketplace talking with whoever happened to be there (Acts 17:17), long enough that the philosophers took an interest and brought him to the Areopagus to explain himself. His first words there show he had been paying attention:

> "So Paul, standing in the midst of the Areopagus, said: “Men of Athens, I perceive that in every way you are very religious.”" (Acts 17:22, ESV)

The Greek word behind "very religious" could lean toward "superstitious," and Paul, provoked as he was, chose its courteous edge. Then he tells them how he knows:

> "For as I passed along and observed the objects of your worship, I found also an altar with this inscription: ‘To the unknown god.’ What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you." (Acts 17:23, ESV)

Paul walked their city and read their inscriptions before he asked them to consider his message. Later in the speech he quotes Aratus, a poet from Paul's own province of Cilicia, that "we are indeed his offspring" (Acts 17:28, ESV). He starts from what they already half-knew, in words they can follow. Much of the church's vocabulary, *saved*, *washed in the blood*, *convicted*, *born again*, carries real weight among believers and sounds like the dialect of a closed group everywhere else. Putting it into ordinary words keeps the substance and assumes the other person is worth being understood by.

But Paul didn't stop at common ground. His speech moves from the unknown god to the Creator, then to a call to repent, and finally to a man whom God has appointed to judge the world and has raised from the dead (Acts 17:30–31). He said the offensive thing, plainly and last, after he had earned a hearing. Luke records the result without embarrassment:

> "Now when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked. But others said, “We will hear you again about this.”" (Acts 17:32, ESV)

Some mocked, some wanted another conversation, and a few believed, among them Dionysius and a woman named Damaris (Acts 17:34). Luke doesn't call that failure. The scene also holds a distinction the church badly needs. The offense of the gospel is one thing, and the offense of the Christian is another. Paul's hearers stumbled over the resurrection, which is to say over the claim itself, and not over contempt or a hidden agenda, because he gave them neither. If our friends are going to stumble, let it be over the empty tomb and the lordship of Jesus rather than our rudeness. The question the claim most often raises gets a careful answer in [Is Jesus Really the Only Way?](/writing/is-jesus-really-the-only-way)

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, drawing in *Life Together* (1939) on the shared life of the underground seminary at Finkenwalde that the Gestapo had closed in 1937, argued that the first service Christians owe one another is to listen, and that someone who stops listening to his brother will soon stop listening to God. He meant life inside the church, but the principle runs outward. Listening isn't waiting for an opening. It's attention that takes the other person's experience as real before trying to correct it. When a friend says she doesn't believe in God, it's often worth asking, with real curiosity, which God she means. Often the god a none has rejected is one Christians should reject too: the cosmic bookkeeper who rewards the well-behaved, the tribal deity who takes one country's side. When a friend says she left the church, ask what happened, and listen without rushing to defend the institution. If she describes cruelty or hypocrisy, she deserves to hear a Christian say that it was wrong and shouldn't have happened, with no "but" attached. If she describes abuse, she deserves more than sympathy: believe her, and help her reach people who can act, including the police where a crime was committed. For sexual abuse, the National Sexual Assault Hotline run by RAINN (1-800-656-4673) is a place to start.

## The table before the argument

For its first three centuries the church grew inside an empire that by turns ignored, mocked and persecuted it. The sociologist Rodney Stark, in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), estimated growth of roughly 40 percent a decade. The Mennonite historian Alan Kreider asked how it happened, and his answer in *The Patient Ferment of the Early Church* (2016) unsettles a church that thinks in programs. The early Christians, he observes, wrote no treatises on evangelism and ran no outreach campaigns. Their worship was often closed to the unbaptized, and instruction could last years; the *Apostolic Tradition*, traditionally attributed to Hippolytus of Rome, envisions three years. What they did write about was patience. Tertullian wrote a treatise on it around the year 200, and Cyprian of Carthage wrote another in the 250s. Kreider reads patience as the virtue that shaped their whole witness: trust that God, not their own hurry, governs the outcome.

His image for the result is the one Jesus used, yeast worked through dough (Matthew 13:33). The church spread less by announcement than by ferment, through households, trades and friendships, through Christians who nursed their sick during epidemics and refused to abandon their newborns in a world where exposing an unwanted infant was legal and common. Outsiders met the faith first as a way of life among people they knew, and only later as teaching they were invited to learn. The words mattered, and the catechesis was demanding, but the words came to people the life had already unsettled.

Lesslie Newbigin saw the modern version of this. He gave most of his working life, from 1936 to 1974, to India, first as a missionary and then as a bishop, and he came home to a Britain he judged harder missionary ground than India had been. In *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) he asked how anyone in a skeptical culture could come to believe that the final word about human life belongs to a crucified man, and his answer was the congregation. A hermeneutic is what makes a text readable. Newbigin's argument, in substance, is that the only thing able to make the gospel readable in such a culture is a community of people who believe it and live by it.

That has a consequence for ordinary Christians we rarely draw. You were never meant to be the only Christian your friend knows. One kind person's faith can be explained as temperament. A whole community of very different people who forgive each other, share money and sit with the dying is harder to explain away. For most nones the natural first invitation is dinner with you and a few friends from church. The harder consequence cuts the other way. If the congregation is the hermeneutic, then a congregation that covers for abusers, fleeces the poor or baptizes a party platform is teaching the neighborhood how to read the gospel too. I have seen this done well and done poorly, and the difference was rarely the quality of a program. It was whether the church was, in fact, what it said it was.

Rosaria Butterfield knows this from the other side. When she came to faith she was a tenured English professor at Syracuse University, living as a lesbian and openly hostile to evangelical Christianity. In *The Secret Thoughts of an Unlikely Convert* (2012) she tells how a local Presbyterian pastor, Ken Smith, and his wife, Floy, answered a critical article she had written with a kind letter and then an invitation to dinner, and how their table became a place she kept returning to for a long time before she believed. In *The Gospel Comes with a House Key* (2018) she argued for what her subtitle calls "radically ordinary hospitality," homes and tables opened to neighbors as a daily habit.

The strongest objection deserves a hearing. Not everyone can host every night. The single mother working two jobs or the man caring for a parent with dementia may read Butterfield and feel one more standard they can't meet. The objection is right about the scale and wrong about the principle. Hospitality scales down to the coffee you bring a coworker, the lawn chair on the driveway, the carpool, the text that says you remembered her mother's surgery was today. What it can't do is become a technique. A table can be turned into a funnel as easily as a booklet can, and the difference is whether the meal is the point. If the dinner is bait, the guest will eventually find the hook. If it's a gift, it stays a gift whether or not she ever asks why you set the table.

## When someone finally asks

None of this is a case for silence, and the fear of being weird can become a very comfortable place to hide. A life can raise the question, but it can't supply the answer. Christianity stands on a claim about what happened outside Jerusalem on a particular morning, and a claim has to be spoken to be heard. The Christian who lets years of friendship pass without ever saying why he hopes may call it respect, but Penn Jillette's challenge suggests a less flattering name: either he doesn't quite believe it, or he doesn't much care. The friend deserves better than either.

So when the question comes, answer it. It may arrive sideways: how are you so calm about this? Answer with your own reason for hope, in plain words, and keep it short enough that she can reply. Say what you believe about Jesus, that he died and was raised and is Lord, and don't hide the costly parts under softer language, because a friend who later finds what you left out will feel handled, and she'll be right. If you have doubts, say so; honesty is more credible than a performance of certainty. If she wants the evidence for the resurrection, send her something longer, like [Did the Resurrection Happen?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen). Then stop and ask what she thinks. Offer to read a Gospel with her. Then stay her friend on the same terms whether she takes you up on it or not.

That last part is the real test, and Mark shows why. In Mark 10 a wealthy man kneels before Jesus and asks what he must do to inherit eternal life, and says he has kept the commandments since his youth. Mark records a detail the other Gospels leave out:

> "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, “You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me.”" (Mark 10:21, ESV)

> "Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions." (Mark 10:22, ESV)

Jesus loved him, told him the hardest true thing, and let him walk away. He didn't lower the price to secure a yes or follow him down the road with a better argument. The one person with every right to compel a decision declined to, and the reason runs to the center of the faith. The God who went to the cross rather than overpower his enemies can't be faithfully commended by pressure, and grace that is forced on someone stops being grace in the act of forcing it.

That leaves most of us, and it leaves me, with more to repent of than to teach. We have been salesmen and we have been cowards, sometimes in the same week, and neither posture came from believing both that the gospel is true and that the person in front of us is loved by God. The nones in our lives are the coworker whose mother is dying, the brother-in-law who stopped going to Mass after the scandals, the neighbor who has never once been inside a church. They are owed the truth, and they are owed the freedom to refuse it. They are rarely offered both at once, by someone who will still be at the table next week.

For a long time the surrounding culture raised the question of God for us, and we grew used to answering questions nobody in the room had asked. That arrangement is gone. The question that remains isn't what we'll say when the door opens. It's whether our hope, our gentleness, our tables and our congregations give anyone a reason to wonder. Tomorrow the coworker will be back at her desk, her mother will still be dying, and she may never ask you anything at all. Be there anyway.
