## Why anyone would burn for it

Most people meet the Reformation as a date and a hammer. A German monk named Martin Luther objected to the sale of indulgences, fixed a list of complaints to a church door in 1517, and Western Christianity broke in two. Protestants usually tell that story as a rescue and Catholics as a wound. The skeptic hears in both a preview of the religious wars that followed, and takes it as fair evidence that faith doesn't make people better, only more certain. Each telling holds something true. None of them explains why ordinary men and women let themselves be burned over it. Nobody walks into a fire to protest a building campaign.

They walk into it over a question, and the one at the center of the Reformation is older than 1517 and hasn't gone away. On what ground does a guilty person stand before a holy God, and who has the authority to say? The money explains where the argument started, the printing presses how fast it spread, and the princes why Luther lived to finish it. Only the question explains why a professor's Latin theses set a continent on fire.

The honest account has to hold together two things each side would rather keep apart. The Reformation was a schism, and schism is a sin to be regretted, because on the night he was betrayed Jesus prayed that those who believed in him would be one. It was also the recovery of something the late medieval church had buried under its own machinery, and a recovery is a gift to be received. A Protestant who celebrates the recovery without grieving the schism hasn't counted the cost. Anyone who grieves the break without receiving what it found hasn't reckoned with Paul. I'm a Protestant pastor, a debtor to this history and an heir of its divisions, and I don't get to tell it from outside.

## A church that knew it needed reform

The Reformation didn't begin with Luther. For two centuries before 1517 the Western church's own councils had called for reform "in head and members," and its own history gave them reason. In 1309 Pope Clement V settled at Avignon, and for most of seventy years the popes ruled the church in the shadow of the French crown. The return to Rome in 1377 was followed within a year by the Great Schism, in which two men, and after the Council of Pisa in 1409 three, claimed to be the vicar of Christ. The Council of Constance ended the schism in 1417, and its decree *Haec Sancta* (1415) claimed an authority from Christ that even a pope must obey. But in 1460 Pius II forbade any appeal from a pope to a future council. The one mechanism that might have reformed the church from within had been tried and shut down. By 1517 the Western church had no agreed way to correct itself.

The same council that healed the schism burned a reformer. John Wycliffe, the Oxford theologian who died in 1384, had set Scripture above every church authority and inspired the first complete English Bible, made by his followers in the 1380s, and in 1409 Archbishop Arundel's Constitutions forbade unlicensed translation. In Prague, Jan Hus read Wycliffe, preached in Czech, and in 1412 denounced the indulgences sold to fund the Pisan pope John XXIII's war against Naples. He came to Constance under a safe-conduct from King Sigismund, was condemned anyway, and was burned on 6 July 1415. A century before Luther, a preacher who had objected to the sale of indulgences and appealed to Scripture over the church's rulers had already gone to the stake.

The deepest preparation was theological. Heiko Oberman showed in *The Harvest of Medieval Theology* (1963) how the late medieval *via moderna*, above all in Gabriel Biel, taught that God had bound himself by covenant to give grace to those who "do what lies within them." It was meant as mercy, since God asks only one's honest best. For a scrupulous conscience it became a trap, because no one can know whether his best was enough. Luther was trained in that theology at Erfurt, entered the Augustinian friary there in 1505, did everything the religious life prescribed, and found that the harder he tried the less certain he became. The Catholic historian Joseph Lortz argued in *Die Reformation in Deutschland* (1939–40) that Luther overthrew within himself a Catholicism that was no longer fully Catholic, and his book opened the Catholic reappraisal that came to read Luther as a man of real religious seriousness rather than a mere rebel. Add the humanist cry of *ad fontes*, back to the sources, which put Erasmus's Greek New Testament in Luther's hands in 1516, and the kindling was laid. The indulgence was only the match.

## Tetzel, the theses and the stand at Worms

Be fair to what an indulgence was, because the caricature lets everyone off too easily. Official teaching never sold the forgiveness of sins. Guilt was forgiven in penance, through contrition, confession and absolution. What remained was a temporal penalty, paid in this life or in purgatory, and an indulgence remitted it by drawing on a treasury of merit won by Christ and the saints, a doctrine Clement VI set out in 1343. In 1476 Sixtus IV extended indulgences to the souls in purgatory. On paper the system assumed repentance rather than replacing it.

The practice was another matter. Albrecht of Brandenburg, already holding two sees, paid Rome for leave to add the archbishopric of Mainz in 1514 with a loan from the Fugger bank. Leo X authorized an indulgence for rebuilding St. Peter's, and in Albrecht's lands half the proceeds went to Rome and half to Albrecht to pay down the Fugger loan. Its best-known preacher was the Dominican Johann Tetzel. Barred from Luther's Saxony, Tetzel preached just over the border, and Wittenbergers walked there to buy. A jingle long attributed to him promised that the soul springs from purgatory as the coin rings in the chest, and Luther's twenty-seventh thesis dismissed exactly that preaching as human invention. Whatever the theologians meant, a peasant heard that grace had a price. Luther was a pastor as well as a professor, and the pastor objected first.

On 31 October 1517, the eve of All Saints, Luther wrote to Albrecht and enclosed ninety-five theses in Latin for an academic disputation. The letter survives and the date is certain. The first thesis set the terms: when Jesus said "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 4:17, ESV), he meant the whole life of believers to be repentance, a turning and not a transaction. The hammer is another matter. The earliest account of the nailing comes from Philip Melanchthon, written after Luther's death in 1546, and Melanchthon reached Wittenberg only in 1518. In 1961 the Catholic historian Erwin Iserloh argued that the posting never happened, a case he published as *Luthers Thesenanschlag: Tatsache oder Legende?* (1962). Others answered that the church door served as the university's notice board. Then in 2006 the historian Martin Treu identified a handwritten note by Luther's secretary Georg Rörer recording that on the eve of All Saints in 1517 Luther's theses on indulgences were posted on the doors of Wittenberg's churches. Rörer, who came to Wittenberg only in the 1520s, wasn't an eyewitness either, but his note shows the posting was told as fact in Luther's own circle. It records a posting, not a hammer. The posting now looks likely; the famous scene remains a tradition. The letter is a fact, and so is what followed: printers put the theses into German, and within weeks they were read across the empire.

The argument then outran its author. At Heidelberg in April 1518 Luther set a "theology of the cross" against a "theology of glory," the one finding God where he chose to be found, in weakness and suffering, the other looking for him in power and success. At Leipzig in 1519 Johann Eck pressed him until he said that some of Hus's condemned teachings were Christian and that councils could err. In June 1520 the bull *Exsurge Domine* gave him sixty days to recant, and he spent them writing. *To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation* called on lay rulers to reform the church. *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* cut the seven sacraments down to those he found instituted by Christ. *The Freedom of a Christian* set out its paradox, that the Christian is a free lord of all, subject to none, and a dutiful servant of all, subject to all. He burned the bull in December and was excommunicated in January 1521.

That April he stood before the Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms and was told to recant. He answered that unless he were convinced by Scripture or plain reason, his conscience was captive to the Word of God, and he could not and would not recant, since acting against conscience is neither safe nor right. The line everyone remembers, "Here I stand, I can do no other," isn't in the transcripts taken in the hall. It first appears in early printed versions of the speech, and whether Luther said it is doubted. Roland Bainton, who took it for the title of his 1950 biography, thought it might still be genuine. It may be, and honesty requires saying it may not. Declared an outlaw, Luther was hidden at the Wartburg, where he translated the New Testament into German.

## What the Reformers believed they had recovered

In 1545 Luther recalled the turn. He had hated the phrase "the righteousness of God," he wrote, because he'd been taught to hear it as the justice by which God punishes sinners. Then he saw how Paul joins it to faith: "For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith'" (Romans 1:17, ESV). The righteousness revealed in the gospel is the righteousness God gives, the gift by which he justifies the one who trusts him, and Luther said it was as if the gates of paradise had opened. Historians still argue over when it happened, sometime between 1513 and 1519.

Paul's quotation has a setting. Habakkuk wrote as Babylon rose to swallow Judah, and his line sets the proud against the faithful: "Behold, his soul is puffed up; it is not upright within him, but the righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4, ESV). Paul builds on it an argument that first brings Jew and Gentile alike under judgment and then, in Romans 3:21–28, announces that the righteousness of God "has been manifested apart from the law" (Romans 3:21, ESV). The verdict is universal, "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23, ESV), and so is the remedy: "and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24, ESV). God put Christ forward at the cross, Paul goes on, "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26, ESV). His conclusion follows: "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28, ESV). He had said it more sharply to the Galatians, recounting how he confronted Peter at Antioch for drawing back from the table he had shared with Gentile believers, as though faith in Christ weren't enough to make them one family: "yet we know that a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ, so we also have believed in Christ Jesus, in order to be justified by faith in Christ and not by works of the law, because by works of the law no one will be justified" (Galatians 2:16, ESV).

For the Reformers, justification is God's verdict, and its ground is not a righteousness grown inside the believer but Christ's own, an alien righteousness, Luther said, because it comes from outside us and is credited to the one who trusts him. Faith doesn't earn it. Faith receives it, as an empty hand receives a gift. The believer is at once righteous and a sinner, righteous in Christ and still being made new. None of this made good works optional. A good tree bears fruit because it is good, Luther argued in *The Freedom of a Christian*, not in order to become good. Works moved from the root of salvation to its fruit.

How new was it? B. B. Warfield judged, in essays gathered as *Calvin and Augustine* (1956), that the Reformation was inwardly the triumph of Augustine's doctrine of grace over Augustine's doctrine of the church. On grace, the Reformers were recovering Paul and Augustine. But Alister McGrath shows in *Iustitia Dei* (2005) that the sharp distinction between justification as declaration and sanctification as renewal was a sixteenth-century development, above all in Melanchthon, since Augustine had understood justification as God making the sinner righteous. The Reformers can argue that Paul requires the distinction. They can't claim every church father drew it.

The Catholic case deserves its strongest form, and it begins in Scripture: "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (James 2:24, ESV). Trent's *Decree on Justification* (1547) didn't teach that anyone earns salvation. Its first canon condemned anyone who says a person can be justified by his own works apart from divine grace, and it taught that nothing before justification, whether faith or works, merits the grace of justification. The parting came over what justification is. For Trent it includes the inner renewal of the person, grace poured into the heart so that the believer is truly made just. For the Reformers that renewal flows from God's verdict and never grounds it. Both confessed salvation by grace. They disagreed over whether the righteousness by which God declares us just is Christ's credited to us or grace's work within us. That isn't a quarrel over words, and it was never the cartoon of grace against works.

Around justification the Reformers gathered other recoveries, now summed up as the solas: Scripture, grace, faith and Christ alone, and glory to God alone. They used each phrase, though the tidy list of five was assembled in the twentieth century. *Sola scriptura* didn't mean reading the Bible without the church. The magisterial Reformers received the ancient creeds and argued from the fathers; they meant that Scripture is the final authority by which every other is judged. The priesthood of believers grew from the same root. Luther argued in *To the Christian Nobility* that every baptized Christian is consecrated a priest, drawing on Peter's words to the whole church: "But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9, ESV). Peter is echoing Sinai, where God called all Israel "a kingdom of priests" (Exodus 19:6, ESV). For Luther this meant access to God through Christ without another mediator, and the dignity of every honest calling, the farmer's and the mother's as much as the monk's. It didn't make every Christian his own pope; Luther kept an ordained ministry.

And it meant Scripture in the common tongue. Luther's German New Testament appeared in September 1522 and the whole Bible in 1534. William Tyndale's English New Testament, printed at Worms in 1526, reached England by smuggling, and in 1536 Tyndale was strangled and burned near Brussels. John Foxe reported his hope that a ploughboy would one day know more Scripture than the clergy who opposed him. English Catholic exiles published their own New Testament at Rheims in 1582, so the vernacular Bible won on both sides, but it was the Reformers who forced the question.

## Many Reformations, and Rome's own at Trent

There was never one Reformation. Ulrich Zwingli began preaching through Matthew in Zurich on 1 January 1519 and never agreed with Luther about the Lord's Supper. John Calvin published the first edition of his *Institutes* in 1536 and from 1541 made Geneva a school for Reformed churches from Scotland to Hungary. In January 1525 some of Zwingli's own followers began baptizing one another as confessing believers, and the Anabaptists were hunted by Catholics and Protestants alike. England broke with Rome in 1534 over a king's marriage more than a doctrine of grace, and became Reformed in much of its doctrine while keeping its bishops. How those streams became today's denominations is told in [Why Are There So Many Christian Denominations?](/writing/why-there-are-so-many-christian-denominations). The recovery began dividing almost as soon as it began.

For a moment the break might have narrowed: at Regensburg in 1541, Cardinal Gasparo Contarini, Melanchthon and Martin Bucer agreed on an article on justification, which Rome and Luther both rejected. The Council of Trent then sat, on and off, from 1545 to 1563, and it was both reform and response. As response it drew hard lines: Scripture and apostolic tradition received together, seven sacraments affirmed, the Protestant formulas on justification condemned one by one. As reform it did much of what Hus and Luther had asked. It required bishops to live in their dioceses, ordered seminaries for the training of priests, and in July 1562 abolished the office of the *quaestores*, the alms-collectors who had hawked indulgences through the towns. Its closing decree of December 1563 kept the doctrine of indulgences while ordering all base gain attached to them abolished, and in 1567 Pius V cancelled every grant of indulgences tied to fees or other financial transactions. The Jesuits and Teresa of Ávila gave that reform a spiritual depth that outlived its polemics. Rome reformed itself and hardened itself in the same council.

## What it cost

A Protestant telling this story owes it a debit column, and the entries begin with Luther himself.

In 1525 the peasants of southern and central Germany rose against their lords. Their Twelve Articles, drawn up at Memmingen that March, asked for the right to choose their own pastors and relief from serfdom, and argued from Scripture and the Christian freedom Luther had preached. His *Admonition to Peace* rebuked the princes first, blaming their greed for the rising. But as the violence spread he wrote *Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants*, urging the princes to strike the rebels down as one kills a mad dog. At Frankenhausen in May 1525 the princes' army destroyed the peasant force, and before the rising was crushed tens of thousands were dead. Luther feared that anarchy would discredit the gospel, and the fear wasn't foolish. But the man who had told Christians they were free lords of all told the rulers to slaughter the poor who took him at his word.

The second entry is worse. In 1523, in *That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew*, Luther had rebuked Christian cruelty to Jews. In *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543) he urged rulers to burn synagogues and schools, destroy Jewish homes, seize prayer books and the Talmud, silence the rabbis, take their wealth and drive them out. It wasn't a stray line. It was a program, and it found its takers. In November 1938, shortly after Kristallnacht, the Lutheran bishop Martin Sasse of Thuringia published extracts from Luther's anti-Jewish writings and welcomed the burning synagogues as their fulfillment. In 1994 the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America formally repudiated those writings. A man who believed he stood before God on borrowed righteousness had no ground for contempt toward anyone, least of all the people from whom that righteousness came in the flesh. The doctrine he recovered condemns what he wrote.

The third entry is the wars: the Schmalkaldic War of 1546 and 1547, the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 that let each German prince choose Lutheran or Catholic for his territory, France's Wars of Religion from 1562 with the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of Huguenots in 1572, and the Thirty Years' War of 1618 to 1648. Off the battlefield, Mary Tudor burned nearly three hundred Protestants, Cranmer among them. Elizabeth's government executed Catholic priests. Calvin's Geneva burned Michael Servetus in 1553, and Protestant magistrates drowned and beheaded Anabaptists. No tradition leaves the sixteenth century with clean hands, and none of its heirs has reason to think its own ancestors would have been gentler with power.

The skeptic reads this as proof that religion breeds violence, and he deserves an honest answer. William Cavanaugh argues in *The Myth of Religious Violence* (2009) that the story in which religion caused the wars and the secular state ended them is partly a founding myth of the modern state. These were also wars of state-building, and they didn't always follow confessional lines; from 1635 Catholic France fought on the Protestant side against the Catholic Habsburgs. Cavanaugh is right that religion makes too tidy a culprit. He doesn't make the blood disappear. Christians killed Christians and called it faithfulness, and the church has to confess that before it corrects anyone's history.

The weightiest charge comes from a Catholic historian. Brad Gregory argues in *The Unintended Reformation* (2012) that the Reformers agreed Scripture should be the final authority but couldn't agree on what it said, and had no shared authority left to settle their disputes. After a century of war, rulers made religion private to keep the peace. A shared account of the good life dissolved into individual preference, and the market and the state moved into the space it left. No Reformer wanted any of this, and Gregory argues that all of it followed from what they did. He traces roots back into late medieval philosophy and begins with the medieval church's failure to live by its own ideals. A Protestant should feel its force, because the fragmentation is there to be counted.

The critics have answers. Gregory needs a unified Christendom to fall from, and Avignon, the Great Schism and the burning of Hus suggest the unity was already cracked. Charles Taylor had already offered a different genealogy in *A Secular Age* (2007), tracing secularity to a far longer drive he calls "Reform," running from medieval reform movements through the Catholic and Protestant reformations alike, the effort to make everyone a fully committed Christian. Europe's most militant secularism broke out in 1789 in Catholic France. And Alister McGrath, in *Christianity's Dangerous Idea* (2007), concedes the core charge, that Protestantism's dangerous idea is every believer's right to interpret the Bible, while arguing that the same idea is the source of its power to renew itself.

This essay lands with the Reformers on the gospel, and it grants Gregory more than Protestants usually do. The Reformation recovered the gospel with an instrument, the individual conscience bound to Scripture, that cuts in every direction, and we have been cutting one another with it for five hundred years. The strongest objection this essay can't fully answer is his. Protestants still have no agreed, visible way to settle what Scripture says when faithful readers disagree, and Christ prayed for a unity the world could see.

## Schism to regret, recovery to receive

What has changed should be described exactly. On 31 October 1999, in Augsburg, the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed the *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification*. It states a consensus in basic truths of the doctrine: together, it says, Lutherans and Catholics confess that by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work and not because of any merit of ours, we are accepted by God and receive the Holy Spirit, who renews our hearts and calls us to good works. It judges that the remaining differences of language and emphasis don't undo that consensus, and that the sixteenth-century condemnations, so far as they concern justification, don't apply to either partner's teaching as the declaration presents it. It doesn't retract Trent or the Lutheran confessions. It leaves authority, the papacy, ministry and the sacraments untouched, and the Catholic Catechism still teaches indulgences. Other traditions have since joined it: the World Methodist Council affirmed it in 2006, and the World Communion of Reformed Churches associated itself with it in 2017. It had critics on both sides, and many confessional Lutherans judged that it blurred the difference over imputation. Mark Noll and Carolyn Nystrom asked *Is the Reformation Over?* (2005) and gave the honest answer. On justification, the churches stand far closer than their ancestors did. On the church and its authority, the Reformation isn't over.

The five-hundredth anniversary was marked as no earlier one had been. In 2013 the Lutheran-Roman Catholic Commission on Unity published *From Conflict to Communion*, urging that 2017 be commemorated together, with joy for the gospel and repentance for the division, and on 31 October 2016 Pope Francis joined the Lutheran World Federation at a common service in Lund, Sweden, to open the anniversary year. Tetzel and Luther would both have found it unthinkable.

Here the distinction the whole history requires has to be drawn plainly. The Reformation is a schism to regret and a recovery to receive, and the two judgments answer different questions. The regret belongs to the broken body. Christ prayed "that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me" (John 17:21, ESV). By that prayer the visible division of his people is a sin against the world's chance to believe, and it's no less a sin because each side can explain why the other caused it. The reception belongs to the gospel. Grace as a gift, Christ's righteousness counted to the ungodly, Scripture in the language of the plough and the kitchen: none of it is a Protestant possession, and Rome confessed the heart of it in 1999. The two mistakes mirror each other. One buys unity by quietly surrendering what was recovered, as though the terror of the friar at Erfurt had been a misunderstanding. The other keeps the recovery and shrugs at the schism, as though five centuries of division were simply the cost of being right. Neither is faithful. The Catholic reader is asked to receive what Rome has said it can receive. The Protestant reader, and I'm one, is asked to grieve what the recovery cost and to stop celebrating 31 October as a victory over people Christ calls brothers.

And the question underneath hasn't gone anywhere. Taylor describes a secular age in which belief in God has become one option among many, while the hunger for a life that counts hasn't gone away. It has only lost its judge. So we stand every day before other courts, the market and the feed and the mirror, and they're harsher than any medieval tribunal, because they never pronounce us finished. The anxious friar doing what lay within him and never knowing whether it was enough looks a great deal like us. I preach justification by faith, and I still catch myself measuring my worth by the size of the room and the warmth of the reviews, which means I've heard Luther's discovery without always believing it all the way down. My sons will learn what I actually run on, not what I say about grace.

On 16 February 1546, two days before he died at Eisleben, Luther wrote a few lines on a scrap of paper that end with the words "We are beggars. This is true." That is where the Reformation's recovery finally lands, not in a stand before an emperor but in open hands. It is also where its schism has to be judged, because beggars at the same door have no standing to despise one another. The righteousness the Reformers found in Paul was never theirs to own. It was Christ's, given at the cross to the ungodly, Catholic and Protestant and unbeliever alike, and it condemns every pride since built on it, theirs and ours. Five hundred years on, the gift is still offered and the wound is still open. Whoever receives the first has to carry the second.
