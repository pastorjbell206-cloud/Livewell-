## The Question Beneath Screen Time

Most of us know the moment. You pick up the phone to check one thing, the weather or a message from a friend, and twenty minutes later you surface somewhere you never meant to go, carrying an opinion about a stranger's argument and a low hum of unease you can't quite name. Nothing terrible happened. You didn't see anything you'd need to confess. And still you set the phone down a little more scattered and a little less able to do whatever it was you picked it up to avoid. Repeat that many times a day, for years, and you have something that deserves a more serious name than a habit.

The usual Christian conversation about phones stays at the level of content and quantity. Is what you're watching clean? How many hours is too many? Those aren't foolish questions; there's real filth online, and time is a stewardship we'll answer for. But both assume the phone is a neutral instrument we pick up for our own purposes, so the only thing they can ask is whether we're using it well. The older question is what the tool is doing to the person who uses it. The church has always known that practices form people. The monastic hours, the Christian year and the Lord's Table all rest on the conviction that repetition shapes the soul beneath the level of decision. The phone is a repetition too, for many of us the first thing touched in the morning and the last thing seen at night, handled more often than any Bible or any other human being. If practices form us, this one is forming us, and the only open question is into what.

The distinction that governs this essay is between two ways of regarding attention. In the economy that built our devices, attention is a resource: scarce, measurable and for sale. In the Christian tradition, attention is a faculty: the capacity of a person to turn toward something and receive it, whether a page of Scripture, a grieving friend or the living God. A resource is harvested. A faculty is trained, well or badly. The algorithm doesn't have to corrupt what we look at in order to harm us. It only has to treat as a crop the very capacity by which we pray and love.

This essay is written for adults. What phones do to children has its own treatment in [When Should My Child Get a Smartphone?](/writing/parenting-raising-kids-in-a-screen-age), which I won't repeat here. But grown men and women tend to hear the screen debate as a conversation about teenagers, and that is its own blindness. The phone got to us first, with our full consent and usually our enthusiasm. I write as a pastor, and no pastor stands outside this indictment. Anyone who preaches now speaks to a room of people carrying a device designed by some of the most capable engineers alive to be more interesting than any sermon. That isn't a complaint about them. It's a description of the water all of us swim in, the pulpit included.

## A Long Argument About What Media Do

In 1964 a Canadian professor of English named Marshall McLuhan published *Understanding Media*, the book that turned "the medium is the message" into a slogan. The slogan has outlived its argument, which was this: a medium matters less for what it carries than for what it rearranges. The railway, he pointed out, didn't bring movement into the world; it sped up and enlarged what people already did, and in doing so it produced new kinds of cities, new kinds of work and new kinds of leisure. The most prophetic chapter of the book, though, is the one McLuhan built around Narcissus. We read that myth as a parable of vanity, but McLuhan noticed that Narcissus didn't know the face in the water was his own. He took an extension of himself for someone else, the fascination numbed him, and he became, in McLuhan's word, the servomechanism of his own image. It's hard to imagine a better description of an algorithmic feed. It studies what we linger over and shows it back to us, refined, until what feels like a window onto the world is largely a mirror of our own appetites, and we lean over it without quite knowing whose face we're seeing.

Neil Postman, who learned from McLuhan and went further, drew the consequences for belief. In *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985) he argued that American public life had once been formed by print, by readers who could follow a long argument (crowds listened through the three-hour Lincoln-Douglas debates of 1858), and that television was re-forming it into entertainment. His chapter on televised religion is the one the church most needed and least wanted. His complaint wasn't that television preachers were vulgar. It was structural: a medium saturated with commercials turns whatever it carries into one more show, and the deeper danger, as he framed it, was not that religion would become the content of television but that television would become the content of religion. In *Technopoly* (1992) he added that technological change is ecological rather than additive. A new medium doesn't add one thing to a culture and leave the rest in place. It changes the whole environment.

Protestants have particular reason to listen, because our tradition was itself a media event. Luther's Ninety-Five Theses of 1517 spread across the German lands within weeks on the movable-type printing that Gutenberg had brought into use at Mainz in the middle of the fifteenth century, and the Reformation made a faith of readers: the vernacular Bible, the catechism, the sermon as a sustained argument. The same tradition carried by print has been the quickest to assume the screen is just a faster pipe for the same message. The medium that made us a people of the book is not the medium in our hands now.

The journalist Nicholas Carr made the cost personal. His 2008 essay in *The Atlantic*, "Is Google Making Us Stupid?", grew into *The Shallows* (2010). He had found he could no longer sink into a long book; his concentration began to drift after two or three pages, and he described himself as someone who had once dived deep in a sea of words and now skimmed across the surface. Drawing on neuroplasticity, the brain's capacity to reorganize itself around what it repeatedly does, Carr argued that constant links, alerts and switching train us in scanning while the capacity for sustained, contemplative reading weakens for lack of use.

Carr's critics deserve a fair hearing. In June 2010 Steven Pinker pushed back against this whole line of argument in *The New York Times*, in an op-ed titled "Mind Over Mass Media." He argued that every new medium has provoked the same alarm, that experience doesn't rewire the brain's basic capacities as wholesale as Carr suggested, and that the remedy for distraction is ordinary self-control: switch off email and social media when there's thinking to be done. Pinker is partly right, and a Christian should want him to be; the fear of new media has an embarrassing history. But notice how much his remedy concedes. If the answer is self-control, then attention is a moral matter and not only a neurological one, which is what the church has always said. And the environment in which that self-control must be exercised was built by people who are paid, handsomely, to defeat it.

## The Harvest of Attention

In 1971 the economist Herbert Simon, who would win the Nobel Prize in economics seven years later, observed that information consumes the attention of those who receive it, so that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." Without intending to, he described the business plan of the next half century.

The plan is older than Simon. Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants* (2016) follows it back to the penny press of the 1830s, and his history reduces it to a single transaction repeated for two centuries: give people something they want for free or nearly free, gather their attention, and resell it to someone who wants to reach them. The newspaper, the poster, the radio and the television each refined the model. The smartphone changed its reach and precision. The penny paper got a quarter hour of your morning. The phone goes with you to the dinner table and the dark bedroom, and it learns from every second you give it what will keep you giving the next one.

That learning was designed. In 1998 the Stanford researcher B. J. Fogg founded the Persuasive Technology Lab and named its field captology, the study of computers as instruments for changing what people think and do, the subject of his book *Persuasive Technology* (2003). His model of behavior is almost austere: an action happens when motivation, ability and a prompt arrive together. Make a behavior easy and prompt it at the right moment, and people will do it with very little deciding. Fogg is no villain here, and much of his work has aimed at helping people build healthier habits. But his methods passed into an industry whose revenue depends on engagement, and engagement is the polite word for attention held as long as possible.

Tristan Harris came out of that Stanford world and was working at Google in 2013 when he circulated an internal presentation arguing that the company bore moral responsibility for its users' attention. He became Google's design ethicist, left in 2016, and in 2018 co-founded the Center for Humane Technology to make the case in public. His best-known comparison is the slot machine. Behavioral psychologists since B. F. Skinner have known that a reward delivered on an unpredictable schedule produces more persistent behavior than one delivered reliably, and Harris pointed out that pulling down to refresh a feed or checking whether anyone has responded works the same way. Sometimes there's something there. Usually there isn't. The not knowing is the hook, and the hand learns it long before the mind consents.

Honesty requires naming what the phone gives, because it gives a great deal. A grandmother watches her grandson's ballgame from a thousand miles away. In the spring of 2020, when church doors closed, many homebound saints worshiped with their congregations for the first time in years. A text reaches a friend in the one hour it could matter. A Christian critique of technology that can't say so will be dismissed, rightly, as nostalgia with a verse attached. Nor did most of the builders set out to hollow anyone. They built what we wanted, and we did want it, and we still do.

So the argument isn't that the phone is evil or that faithful Christians should move to a cabin. It's narrower and harder. The device in our hands has been optimized, carefully and brilliantly, for a purpose other than our good, and a tool optimized for someone else's purpose will form us toward that purpose unless something stronger forms us first. A tool that studies you in order to keep you has stopped being only a tool.

## What the Evidence Can and Cannot Say

The most widely read book on this subject is Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024). It concerns adolescents, and its argument about children is weighed in the parenting essay linked above. But one chapter speaks straight to adults. Haidt, a social psychologist at New York University who doesn't write as a believer, calls it "Spiritual Elevation and Degradation." He argues that practices religious communities have kept for millennia (shared sacred time, embodied worship, stillness and silence, self-forgetfulness, slowness to anger and readiness to forgive, awe before nature) tend to lift human beings up, and that a life lived through a phone tends to pull the other way, toward the fragmented, the self-absorbed and the quick to judge. He writes with children in view, but the logic doesn't stop at sixteen. By the road of social psychology he arrives near the place the desert fathers reached by the road of prayer.

Haidt's larger causal claims have drawn serious criticism, and it should be stated at its strongest. The developmental psychologist Candice Odgers, reviewing the book in *Nature* in March 2024, argued that its central causal claim isn't supported by the science as it stands: the large effects Haidt describes are not what most studies have found, which is a mix of small associations, mixed ones and none at all; the arrow may point the other way, since people who are already struggling may spend more time online; and a single villain can distract from causes with stronger evidence. These aren't the objections of a technology booster. They're the objections of a careful scientist who doesn't want frightened people chasing the wrong cure.

The evidence about adults is thinner still, which makes one well-designed experiment worth knowing. In the *American Economic Review* in 2020, the economists Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer and Matthew Gentzkow reported paying a large group of American Facebook users to deactivate their accounts for four weeks before the 2018 midterm elections. Compared with a control group, those who stepped away spent more time on offline activities, including time with friends and family (and, less flatteringly, watching television alone), reported modest gains in well-being, and grew somewhat less polarized in their political views, though they also knew a little less about the news. In the weeks afterward they used Facebook markedly less than the control group did. The effects were real and modest, neither apocalypse nor acquittal.

A related finding matters for anyone who takes in the news through a feed. In 2018 three MIT researchers, Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, published a study in *Science* tracing some 126,000 cascades of news stories and rumors on Twitter between 2006 and 2017, each checked against independent fact-checking organizations. False stories reached more people, and reached them faster, than true ones. Automated accounts spread true and false news at about the same rate, so the difference came from people.

How much the phone has damaged our mental health is disputed and will stay disputed for years. What the platforms are built to do is not. Those are different questions, and the church has been answering the second for a long time. The social sciences can measure well-being with increasing care, but they can't say what attention is for. They see the symptom clearly. The anthropology lies beyond their instruments.

One word of care. If what you meet when the phone goes quiet is more than restlessness, a heaviness that won't lift or thoughts of harming yourself, that isn't a failure of discipline, and it isn't something an essay can treat. Talk to a doctor or licensed counselor, and if you're in danger, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Nothing here is medical advice.

## Attention Is the Substance of Prayer

No modern writer took attention more seriously than Simone Weil. In 1942, the year she left France, she wrote a short essay for students, "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God," later gathered into *Waiting for God* (1951). Its startling thesis is that prayer is made of attention, so that the student who works honestly at a problem is being prepared for prayer whether or not the answer ever comes. What matters most here, though, is her account of what attention is. It isn't the clenched brow we usually picture; she observed that students told to pay attention knit their brows and hold their breath and end up attending to nothing. She called it a negative effort: thought suspended, the mind emptied of its own agenda and held open, waiting to receive what is actually there. That is precisely the capacity a feed is designed to preempt, because a mind that waits is a mind that might put the phone down. If Weil is right, the attention economy is more than an economic arrangement. It's a rival claim on the faculty of prayer. Most of us won't lose the ability to pray by deciding against God. We'll lose it by selling the faculty in small pieces, many times a day, to buyers glad to have it.

The Gospels saw this long before Weil. When Jesus came to the home of Martha and Mary, "Martha was distracted with much serving" (Luke 10:40, ESV), and Luke's verb, *perispaō*, is built from words meaning to drag around. A word's parts don't fix its meaning, and in ordinary use it meant to be overburdened or preoccupied, but the picture suits her. Her work was hospitality to the Lord himself, and Jesus doesn't scold it. He names the division: "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her" (Luke 10:41–42, ESV). Mary, seated at his feet and listening, is the picture of attention, and Jesus calls it the portion no one can take away. The feed's whole design is to take it, a few seconds at a time.

James K. A. Smith gave the church a vocabulary for how that taking works. In *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) he argued that we are lovers before we are thinkers, and that what forms our loves is less the ideas we hold than the rituals we practice. He called those rituals liturgies, and his best-known example is the shopping mall, with its own processions, icons and promises of redemption. The phone is a liturgy in the fullest sense. It has a morning office, kept before our feet touch the floor. It has a posture, the bowed head every earlier generation would have recognized as the posture of prayer. It has a creed it never says aloud: something is happening elsewhere, you may be missing it, and the next thing may be the thing you need. Liturgies form us beneath argument, which is why a reader can agree with every word here and reach for the phone before the paragraph ends.

Paul knew that formation runs through the body. After eleven chapters on the mercy of God, he turns: "I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship" (Romans 12:1, ESV). He begins with bodies, with where they go and what hands and eyes are given to, and then comes to minds: "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect" (Romans 12:2, ESV). The word behind "world" is *aiōn*, age, and the verb for "conformed" is related to *schēma*, outward form or pattern. Etymology can be overpressed, but Paul's contrast is plain enough: one way of being shaped comes from the age, the other from God. The age has a pattern, and it fits that pattern onto anyone who doesn't resist. Both verbs are usually read as passives, because in both cases something is done to us. The renewal is finally God's work, but it comes to people who have laid their bodies on the altar. Here the governing distinction comes into focus. The attention the feed harvests is the same attention Paul asks us to offer. It will be spent either way. The question is whether it is seized or given.

The setting of Philippians matters too. Paul writes from custody (Philippians 1:13) to a church where two women who had labored beside him have fallen into a quarrel he must address by name (Philippians 4:2–3). To that anxious, divided congregation he writes: "Finally, brothers, whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things" (Philippians 4:8, ESV). Read beside the MIT study, the order is pointed. Truth comes first, and the feed ranks what we see by what holds us. Then comes the line we skip: "practice these things, and the God of peace will be with you" (Philippians 4:9, ESV). What we dwell on becomes what we do, and what we do trains what we can dwell on.

Jesus named one more danger before there were screens: "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them, for then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 6:1, ESV). Social media makes every Christian a small public figure with an audience and a count. Prayer, conviction and anger can all be performed, and the platform pays in exactly the currency Jesus said would be the whole reward. How outrage in particular is rewarded, on the right and the left alike, is taken up in [Righteous Anger or Christian Outrage?](/writing/outrage-is-not-a-spiritual-gift). A faith lived constantly in view slowly forgets how to live before the Father who sees in secret.

## Be Still, and Know

Psalm 46 is usually read as a gentle invitation to quiet time, and it is fiercer than that. Read straight through, it sounds like a news feed. The earth gives way, the mountains are moved into the heart of the sea, the waters roar and foam (Psalm 46:2–3). "The nations rage, the kingdoms totter; he utters his voice, the earth melts" (Psalm 46:6, ESV). This is a psalm for people who have heard the news, and it doesn't ask them to stop knowing it. It asks where they will stand while they know it. It opens with a location: "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble" (Psalm 46:1, ESV). Near the end it redirects the eyes: "Come, behold the works of the LORD, how he has brought desolations on the earth" (Psalm 46:8, ESV). Only then does the famous line arrive:

> "Be still, and know that I am God. I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth!" (Psalm 46:10, ESV)

Interpreters differ over whether the command is addressed to the raging nations or to God's frightened people, and the essay on silence and solitude linked below follows the Hebrew verb, which leans toward letting go more than calming down. For our purposes what matters is the order of the psalm. The stillness comes after the news has been heard in full, and it's less a mood than the surrender of control. That speaks to a distinctly adult temptation. We tell ourselves we keep refreshing because responsible people stay informed, and there's truth in it; a Christian who loves his neighbors will want to know what is happening to them. But the endless scroll through catastrophe is often not information at all. It's a way of feeling in control of a world we can't control. Psalm 46 hears all the same news and says: look somewhere else first. Know who holds the kingdoms before you read about them tottering.

The church keeps two old practices built for this, and each has its own essay here, so I'll only name them. The Sabbath is a day each week when nothing is harvested from us and nothing produced by us, a day that tells the body what the mind forgets, that the world was never held up by our vigilance; see [What Is the Sabbath and Should Christians Keep It Today?](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it). Silence is the discipline the desert fathers learned when the restless, novelty-hungry distraction they called *acedia* followed them into the cell; see [What the Desert Fathers Teach About Silence and Solitude](/writing/silence-and-solitude). What they share matters most here. Both are ways of receiving rather than being harvested, and both hand attention back to the one who made it.

It would be easy to turn this into a program of apps to delete and hours to track. Some rules help, kept best by a household or a few friends together: the phone that sleeps outside the bedroom, a psalm before the feed, a table with no devices on it. They're sensible, and none of them is holy. The distinction that matters is between regulation and formation. A rule can restrain a habit, but it can't make anyone want God, and a Christian who trades the feed for a screen-time tracker has found a more respectable way to stay anxious. Formation gives attention something better to do, over years, by repetition, in the company of other people.

That is where the church comes in, and where it has often failed. The New Testament was itself written in a medium, and its writers knew the medium's limits: "Though I have much to write to you, I would rather not use paper and ink. Instead I hope to come to you and talk face to face, so that our joy may be complete" (2 John 12, ESV). The elder trusted the letter enough to send it and distrusted it enough to say it wasn't the thing itself. Our faith rests on a God who did not settle for sending messages: "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14, ESV). A church that gathers in bodies, eats at one table and sings in one room is practicing a counter-liturgy whether it knows it or not. Streaming a service to someone who can't come is a mercy. Treating the stream as the service teaches a different faith.

## Could You Not Watch One Hour?

On the night he was betrayed, Jesus took three of his closest friends into Gethsemane and asked them for one thing: "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me" (Matthew 26:38, ESV). He didn't ask them to fight or to understand. He asked for their attention, their presence awake beside him in the worst hour of his life. He came back and found them asleep, and his question has outlived every technology since: "So, could you not watch with me one hour?" (Matthew 26:40, ESV). Then, with more mercy than accusation: "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak" (Matthew 26:41, ESV).

The scene guards us against two errors. The first is thinking the phone invented our distraction. Peter, James and John held no device, and they couldn't keep watch for an hour. What the attention economy has done is study that ancient weakness with enormous care and build an industry on it. The second error is thinking the scene ends with the sleepers. Jesus went from that garden to the cross carrying the failure of the friends who couldn't stay awake for him. The attention they couldn't give him, he gave them, all the way to the end. Whatever hours we've given away, and all of us have given away more than we'd care to count, were known in that garden and carried out of it.

On the far side of the cross is a detail worth staying with. Mary Magdalene stood weeping outside the empty tomb, looking at the risen Lord without recognizing him, until he spoke: "Jesus said to her, 'Mary.'" (John 20:16, ESV). The feed knows us as a pattern of behavior and counts us in its reports. The risen Christ knew her by name, and the name turned her around. That is the voice the Christian life trains us to hear, not a notification that wants something from us but a voice that has already given everything for us.

None of this resolves neatly. Tomorrow morning something will reach for your attention before you're fully awake, and most mornings, if we're honest, it will get it. The one who asked for an hour in Gethsemane is still asking, and he asks people who have failed to give it before. He isn't asking for a better screen-time report. He's asking for the faculty the whole machine was built to harvest, and he asks for it as a gift, because it was his gift before it was ever anyone's crop. What we do with that in the next idle moment, and the next reach for the glowing glass, no one else can decide for us, and no one else can keep watch in our place.
