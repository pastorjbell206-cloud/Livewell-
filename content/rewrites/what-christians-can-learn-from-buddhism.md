On the morning of 10 December 1968, Thomas Merton read a paper to a gathering of Catholic abbots and monks at a conference center outside Bangkok. He had spent about eight weeks in Asia talking with Tibetan lamas, Theravada monks and the young Dalai Lama, though his subject that morning was, of all things, Marxism and the monastic life. After lunch he went to his room to rest, and later that afternoon he was found dead, apparently electrocuted by a faulty standing fan. He was fifty-three. It was twenty-seven years to the day since he had walked through the gate of the Abbey of Gethsemani in Kentucky to become a Trappist.

The story gets told in two ways, and both are wrong. In one, the most famous monk in America drifted east at the end, halfway to becoming something else. In the other, his trip licenses every kind of borrowing, as though the religions were finally one. The record supports neither. Merton went to Asia after twenty-seven years of the Psalter, the Mass and silence, as a monk who expected to come home. What made the conversations possible was not that he held his faith loosely. It was that he held it deeply enough to recognize disciplines he already knew, and to name where the two parted.

The church in the West has mostly had two reflexes about Buddhism. One locks the door and files the whole tradition under paganism without reading a line of it. The other puts it on the wellness shelf and never asks what the practices were for. I came to faith from atheism, and I understand why so many thoughtful unbelievers who would never set foot in a church will sit on a meditation cushion. Buddhism doesn't ask them to believe in God, it looks at suffering without blinking, and its practitioners often seem calmer and kinder than the Christians they know. We earned some of that comparison, and it's worth saying so before anything else is said.

**Buddhism is not a vague Eastern mood that Christians may either fear or sample. It is a disciplined, many-branched tradition with its own account of what is wrong with us and what would set us free, and it deserves to be described as its own teachers would describe it. Described that way, it can teach Christians things our own tradition once knew and has largely forgotten: honesty about suffering, a map of craving, trained attention and compassion practiced as a discipline. Described that way, it also differs from the gospel at the root, and those differences are not misunderstandings to be smoothed away. They are the places where each faith says what it most believes about God, the self, grace and pain.**

## What the Buddha taught, in terms his followers would recognize

Siddhartha Gautama was born into the ruling family of the Shakya people in the Himalayan foothills, in what is now southern Nepal, probably in the fifth century before Christ; scholars disagree about his dates by as much as a century. The tradition says he was raised in deliberate comfort until he saw four things his father had kept from him: an old man, a sick man, a corpse and a wandering ascetic at peace. He left home, spent years in a self-denial that nearly killed him, and abandoned it for what he called the middle way. Seated beneath a fig tree at Bodh Gaya, he attained what his followers call awakening, and from then on he was the Buddha, the one who woke up.

His first teaching, given to five former companions in the deer park at Sarnath near Varanasi, is preserved in the Pali Canon as the discourse on setting in motion the wheel of the dharma. Its content is the Four Noble Truths, often compared to a physician's diagnosis, cause, prognosis and prescription. The first truth is *dukkha*. The word is usually translated "suffering," but that is too narrow. Buddhist teachers distinguish plain pain, the suffering of change (every pleasure ends), and a deeper unsatisfactoriness woven into conditioned existence itself. The second truth names the origin of *dukkha* as *tanha*, literally thirst: craving for sensual pleasure, craving to become something, and craving for non-existence. The third truth is that the thirst can cease, and its cessation is nirvana. The fourth is the path to that cessation, the Noble Eightfold Path: right view and right intention, which belong to wisdom; right speech, right action and right livelihood, which belong to moral conduct; and right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration, which belong to the training of the mind.

Beneath the Four Truths lie what the tradition calls the three marks of existence. The first is *anicca*, impermanence: everything that arises passes away. The second is *dukkha* again, because what passes away cannot finally satisfy a creature who wants to hold on. The third is *anatta*, non-self, and it is the teaching Westerners most often get wrong. In his second discourse the Buddha takes the five bundles that make up a person (the body, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness) and asks of each whether it is permanent, whether what is impermanent can be satisfying, and whether it is fitting to look at it and say, this is mine, this I am, this is my self. The answer each time is no. *Anatta* does not deny that there are persons in the ordinary sense, who remember, choose and bear the results of their deeds. It denies that behind the stream there is a fixed, unchanging essence, a soul in the sense the Upanishads meant by *atman*. Walpola Rahula, the Sri Lankan monk whose *What the Buddha Taught* (1959) remains a standard short account, argued that this denial of a permanent self is what sets the Buddha's teaching apart from nearly every other religious philosophy. Even inside Buddhism it has never been a simple doctrine.

Nirvana means a blowing out, the way a flame goes out, and the early texts describe it as the extinguishing of the three fires of greed, hatred and delusion. It is not, in the Buddha's own account, annihilation. Pressed on whether an awakened one exists after death, he asked in return which direction a fire goes when it goes out: north, south, east or west? The question does not apply, and the Buddha held that the same is true of the awakened one. Some passages call it the unborn and the unconditioned, speaking of it mostly by what it is not. Rupert Gethin's *The Foundations of Buddhism* (1998) is careful here: the traditions resist turning nirvana into a place a self goes on to enjoy, because the self that would enjoy it is what has been seen through.

The Buddha's refusal to answer certain questions is part of the teaching, and he explained it with a parable. In the discourse numbered 63 in the Majjhima Nikaya, a monk named Malunkyaputta threatens to give up the monastic life unless the Buddha tells him whether the world is eternal, whether it is finite, whether the soul and the body are the same, and whether an awakened one exists after death. The Buddha answers with a picture. A man is struck by a poisoned arrow, and his friends bring a surgeon, but the man won't let the arrow be pulled until he knows the archer's name and clan, his height and complexion, his village, his bow and the feathers on the shaft. The man would die, the Buddha says, before any of it was answered. He left those things undeclared because they don't lead to peace; what he declared was suffering, its origin, its cessation and the way.

A Christian should read that parable slowly, because it is not the Western atheist's denial of God. It is a refusal to let speculation delay the cure. Most Buddhist traditions do teach no creator, and the early texts treat the god Brahma, who imagines he made the world, with gentle comedy. But the Buddha's silence was a physician's silence before it was a philosopher's. He thought the arrow mattered more than the archer.

## One teaching, many schools

Nothing flattens Buddhism faster than speaking of it as one thing. A Christian who wouldn't let Buddhists describe Christianity by averaging an Orthodox liturgy, a Pentecostal revival and a Quaker meeting owes them the same care.

Theravada, the "teaching of the elders," is the tradition of Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos and Cambodia. Its scriptures are the Pali Canon, first written down in Sri Lanka in the first century before Christ, and its ideal is the *arahant*, the one who has walked the path to its end and been freed from craving. Mahayana, the "great vehicle," arose in India around the turn of the era and became the Buddhism of China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam. Its ideal is the bodhisattva, who vows to put off final release until every sentient being is freed, and so it places *karuna*, compassion, beside wisdom as one of the two wings of awakening. Its great philosopher, Nagarjuna, around the second century, pressed non-self outward into *sunyata*, emptiness: not only persons but all things lack independent existence, because everything arises in dependence on everything else. Emptiness in that sense is not nothingness. It is the claim that nothing exists on its own.

Vajrayana, the "diamond vehicle," reached Tibet in the seventh and eighth centuries and shaped the Buddhism of Tibet, Bhutan and Mongolia. It keeps the Mahayana philosophy and adds tantric practice: visualization, mantra, ritual and a close bond with the teacher. Tibetans regard the Dalai Lama as an emanation of Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion. Zen (Chan in China, where it began) is a Mahayana school that distrusts words and concepts and sends its students to sit. The Japanese master Dogen (1200 to 1253) taught that sitting, *zazen*, is not a technique for reaching awakening later but its expression now. Pure Land Buddhism, the most widely practiced form in East Asia, turns from one's own power to the vow of the Buddha Amitabha, Amida in Japan, to bring all who call on his name to birth in his pure land. Honen and Shinran pressed that trust so far that it is often compared with the Reformation's teaching on grace, a comparison (and Karl Barth's answer to it) treated in [Why Christianity and Not Another Religion?](/writing/why-christianity). A Christian who says every Buddhist believes in salvation by self-effort has not met the largest Buddhist community in Japan.

Christians and Buddhists have been meeting for a long time. Monks of the Church of the East, led by one the Chinese records call Alopen, reached the Tang capital of Chang'an in 635, and the stone they raised there in 781 describes the faith partly in Buddhist and Daoist vocabulary. In the medieval West the Buddha's life, retold through Arabic and Georgian into Greek and Latin as the legend of Barlaam and Josaphat, made the prince who left his palace a Christian saint; the name Josaphat descends from the word bodhisattva. In 1716 the Jesuit Ippolito Desideri reached Lhasa, studied in a Gelug monastery, and wrote treatises in Tibetan against rebirth and emptiness; Donald Lopez and Thupten Jinpa show in *Dispelling the Darkness* (2017) that he disagreed only after learning to argue inside his opponents' logic. And in 1965 the Second Vatican Council, in *Nostra Aetate*, said of Buddhism that in its various forms it recognizes the radical insufficiency of this changing world and teaches a way to perfect liberation or supreme illumination, sought by one's own efforts or with help from beyond. The council's careful "or" is the whole range of Buddhist schools in a single word.

## What Merton went to Asia to find

Merton's interest in Buddhism did not begin on the plane. From the late 1950s he corresponded with the Japanese scholar D. T. Suzuki, who had done more than anyone to bring Zen to Western readers. In *Zen and the Birds of Appetite* (1968) he argued that Zen is less a doctrine to be compared with Christian doctrine than a discipline of seeing, and that Christians who wanted it as one more experience to collect had already missed it. In May 1966 the Vietnamese Zen monk Thich Nhat Hanh, then touring the United States to plead for an end to the war in his country, visited Gethsemani. Merton wrote an essay afterward called "Nhat Hanh Is My Brother," recognizing a fellow monk whose silence had sent him into the world's suffering rather than away from it.

The Asian trip began in October 1968. In notes for a paper prepared for a conference in Calcutta he wrote, in substance, that a Christian could by now remain wholly faithful to his own monastic commitment and still learn in depth from a Buddhist discipline and experience, and *The Asian Journal of Thomas Merton* (1973), published after his death, shows him doing it. In early November he met the Dalai Lama three times at Dharamsala, and they talked about meditation and the monastic life with the frankness of two professionals. Early in December he visited the great carved Buddhas at Polonnaruwa in Sri Lanka, and his journal records that standing before them he was suddenly jerked out of his habitual, half-tied way of seeing things into an inner clarity, beauty and spiritual truth arriving together. A few days later he went on to Bangkok. His body was flown back to the United States on a military plane that was also carrying American dead from Vietnam.

Thich Nhat Hanh outlived Merton by more than half a century, most of it in exile, and his *Living Buddha, Living Christ* (1995) is probably the most widely read Buddhist book about Jesus. It is generous. He writes of keeping images of both the Buddha and Jesus on his altar as his spiritual ancestors, and he suggests that what Christians call the Holy Spirit and what Buddhists call mindfulness are close kin, each the energy that makes a person truly present and able to heal. A Christian should receive that generosity with gratitude, and also notice what it does. It honors Jesus as a teacher of awakened presence and sets aside what Christians say matters most about him: that he is the eternal Son, that he died for sinners, that he rose bodily from the tomb. That isn't a failure of charity. It is the move Christians have made in reverse whenever we praised the Buddha as a noble moralist who never quite reached the truth. Each of us, being kind, translates the other into our own language. Merton's gift was that he let the Buddhists he met remain Buddhists, and remained a Christian himself, and found the conversation deeper for it.

## What a Christian can honestly learn

Begin with the First Noble Truth, where the American church most needs the rebuke. The Buddha wouldn't let his hearers skip the diagnosis. In one discourse he says the untrained person, struck by pain, adds grief and panic to it and so is struck by two arrows, while the trained person feels the first and declines the second. A story in the later commentaries tells of a mother, Kisa Gotami, who carried her dead child from house to house begging for medicine. The Buddha asked her for a mustard seed from a home where no one had ever died. She found no such home, and came back able to bury her son.

Christians have the same honesty in our own canon, and we have let it go quiet. The Preacher of Ecclesiastes calls everything under the sun *hebel*, vapor, breath: "Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 1:2). The psalmist is just as plain: "As for man, his days are like grass; he flourishes like a flower of the field; for the wind passes over it, and it is gone, and its place knows it no more" (Psalm 103:15–16). That is *anicca* in Hebrew dress, spoken by the people of God, not their critics. Yet much of American Christianity has offered a faith that promises to keep the arrows away: a prosperity gospel that treats pain as a failure of faith, a therapeutic gospel that treats it as a mood to be managed, a triumphant worship that has no room for lament. We have too often hurried from the wound to the victory. The Buddhist teacher who says, sit with this, it's real, is often more faithful to Ecclesiastes than we are.

The second gift is a map of craving. The Buddha's analysis of *tanha*, the thirst that grasps at what can't be held, is among the most searching accounts of compulsive desire in the ancient world. Christians have a close relative in Evagrius Ponticus, who traced eight tempting thoughts from their first stirring to their hold on the soul (see [Why People Fled to the Desert](/writing/why-people-fled-to-the-desert)). Jesus was not less blunt: "Take care, and be on your guard against all covetousness, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15). A church that markets itself to consumers and treats God as the supplier of what we already wanted has baptized the very thirst the Buddha diagnosed. In fairness to both, neither condemns desire as such. Buddhist teachers commend the wholesome desire to be free, and Augustine, in the *Confessions* (c. 400), said that our hearts are restless until they rest in God. The problem in both is not wanting. It is wanting the wrong things in the wrong order, with a grip that crushes what it holds.

The third gift is trained attention. Right mindfulness, *sati*, is steady, clear awareness of the body, feelings and mind, so that a person sees what is happening in him before he is carried off by it. The New Testament has the verb from which the desert monks took their word for it, *nepsis*, sobriety or watchfulness: "Be sober-minded; be watchful. Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour" (1 Peter 5:8). The contemplatives built a discipline of attention on that command, traced in [What the Desert Fathers Teach About Silence and Solitude](/writing/silence-and-solitude) and [Who Were the Christian Mystics?](/writing/the-christian-mystics). A Christian who learns from a Buddhist how to sit still and let a thought pass without obeying it has learned something the church once taught and mostly stopped teaching. But a distinction matters here. Buddhist attention aims at seeing clearly. Christian attention, in the end, is attention to someone. The monk watches his thoughts so that he can pray, and prayer is not awareness but address, speech to the God who first spoke.

The fourth gift is compassion practiced as a discipline. *Karuna*, the wish that others be free from suffering, is one of four "immeasurable" states Buddhist monks deliberately cultivate, alongside loving-kindness, sympathetic joy and equanimity, and the practice extends good will outward in widening circles until it reaches the enemy. The Sri Lankan Jesuit Aloysius Pieris, whose scholarly life has been spent among Sri Lankan Buddhists, argued in *Love Meets Wisdom* (1988) that Christianity speaks chiefly the idiom of redeeming love and Buddhism the idiom of liberating wisdom, and that neither idiom is complete without the other. Christians are commanded to love our enemies, and Jesus "had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matthew 9:36). But we rarely train for it. We treat compassion as a feeling that comes or doesn't; the Buddhist monk treats it as a capacity to be exercised daily, and it's hard to watch without some shame.

## Mindfulness without the Buddha

Most Americans who meditate never met the Buddha's version of mindfulness. They met a clinical one. In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn, a molecular biologist who had trained with Zen teachers, opened a stress reduction clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School and built a course, later called Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, for patients with chronic pain and illness. He presented it without Buddhist vocabulary so hospitals would accept it, and *Full Catastrophe Living* (1990) carried it to a wide public. The course has helped many people, and nothing here is medical advice; anyone using it under a clinician's care should keep talking with that clinician. But the word traveled farther than the clinic. It now sells apps and corporate retreats, often as a way to keep stressed workers productive in the very conditions that stress them.

Buddhist critics have been sharper about this than Christians. *Sati* is related to the Sanskrit word for memory: it means keeping the dharma in mind, and it sits inside a path whose other strands include ethics and right livelihood. The management scholar Ronald Purser, himself a Zen practitioner, argued in *McMindfulness* (2019) that stripping mindfulness of its moral frame turns it into a private coping skill that leaves greed and injustice untouched, and even makes them easier to bear. Christians should hear that Buddhist complaint, because we did the same to our own practices when we turned prayer into a tool for a calmer week.

In 1989 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, under Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, sent the Catholic bishops a letter on Christian meditation, known by its opening Latin words as *Orationis Formas*. It did not forbid learning from Eastern methods. It said, in substance, that genuine elements in them may be taken up, provided the Christian understanding of prayer is never obscured, and its warnings were specific: that bodily techniques can produce a sense of calm and warmth which is easily mistaken for the consolation of the Spirit; that an emptiness sought for its own sake can crowd out the personal God; and that Christian prayer is finally not a method for producing a state but a gift, a dialogue of love between creature and Creator. A Protestant need not accept the Vatican's authority to see the wisdom of that. "Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10). But the verse has an object, and the stillness exists for the knowing.

## Where the two faiths part

The differences deserve the same care, stated at a strength a Buddhist would accept. The first concerns God. Most Buddhist traditions teach no creator, and the Buddha set the question aside as the archer's name. Paul Williams, a British scholar of Mahayana philosophy who practiced Tibetan Buddhism for some twenty years, explains in *The Unexpected Way* (2002) that he became a Catholic largely over this point: dependent arising explains how each thing comes from other things, but not why there is a chain of things at all rather than nothing. The Buddhist has a serious reply: to demand a first cause is to reach once more for something to hold, one more question about the arrow's feathers. Christians can't accept it, because for us the question of the archer is finally whether anyone loves us, and Scripture answers with a name.

The second concerns the self. It's too simple to say that Buddhism denies the self and Christianity affirms it. The New Testament has its own death of the self: "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake and the gospel's will save it" (Mark 8:35). The Sri Lankan Methodist Lynn de Silva argued in *The Problem of the Self in Buddhism and Christianity* (1975) that biblical anthropology knows no immortal soul-substance either; the person exists in relation, held in being by the Spirit of God. The difference lies elsewhere. In Buddhism the self, seen through, is released. In the gospel the self, crucified, is given back, and given by someone: "Fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine" (Isaiah 43:1). When the Kyoto philosopher Masao Abe read Paul's hymn on Christ's self-emptying alongside *sunyata* in *The Emptying God* (1990), his Christian respondents found real kinship and a real gap. The Son "emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men" (Philippians 2:7). That emptying is the act of a person who remains himself, poured out for others. It is not the discovery that there was no one there.

The third concerns grace. The Buddha's last words, as the tradition preserves them, were that all conditioned things pass away and his disciples should strive on with diligence, and the *Dhammapada* says that each must make the effort himself, since the Buddhas only point the way. Pure Land Buddhism stands against that current, and Christians owe it the honesty described in [Why Christianity and Not Another Religion?](/writing/why-christianity). But even there the difference remains: grace in the gospel is not a vow but a Person who took flesh and died.

The fourth difference, the deepest, concerns what becomes of pain. Buddhism offers the extinguishing of suffering by the extinguishing of craving. The gospel offers something stranger: suffering taken up by God himself, carried to a cross, and redeemed rather than erased. "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now" (Romans 8:22). A fire that goes out goes nowhere; labor pain is going somewhere. A thoughtful Buddhist will say that the Christian hope of endless personal life looks like the last and largest craving. The Christian answer is that this life is not clung to but received, and that the one who promises it kept his wounds. After the resurrection Jesus said to Thomas, "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe" (John 20:27). The risen body does not forget the nails.

Merton came home on a military plane, among young men killed in a war his Vietnamese brother had begged the world to end. The Buddhist looks at that plane and says, this is *dukkha*, and the way out is to stop clinging. The Christian looks at the same plane and says, this is what God entered, and he has promised that "death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore" (Revelation 21:4). Both answers are serious, and they cannot both be true.

What a Christian carries out of this conversation is finally a question about ourselves rather than a verdict on our Buddhist neighbors. The Buddhist monk has often looked at suffering more honestly than our churches, and practiced compassion more deliberately than we practice the love we were commanded. He did it without the cross. We have the cross, and we have often used it to skip the diagnosis. If the gospel is true, then it is truer than the Four Noble Truths about suffering, not quieter; it goes further into the wound, not around it. Whether anyone could tell that by watching us is the part of the conversation that is still ours to answer.
