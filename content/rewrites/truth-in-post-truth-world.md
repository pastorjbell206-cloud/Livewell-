Most people who say they no longer know what to believe aren't describing a philosophy. They're describing a kind of tiredness. A claim arrives in the family group chat, forwarded by someone they love, and it's either an outrage or a fabrication, and there's no time to find out which. Nearly every institution that once settled such questions (the newspaper, the university, the public health office, the pulpit) has at some point been caught shading the truth to protect itself, so each new claim arrives already discounted. The result isn't that people believe nothing. It's that they believe fiercely and trust almost no one.

In November 2016 Oxford Dictionaries named "post-truth" its word of the year. The editors described it as an adjective for circumstances in which objective facts do less to shape public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief, noting that its use had surged around that year's Brexit vote and American presidential election. The word caught on because it named something people already felt, and a decade of feeds tuned to whatever keeps us scrolling has made the condition ordinary.

There's an easy Christian response to all of this: the world has abandoned truth, and the church still holds it. Some of that is true. The church does confess that truth is real, that it can be known, and that it finally has a name. But that is only part of the story. The church isn't standing outside the post-truth age, pointing in. Our congregations forward the same fabrications as everyone else, and our institutions have been caught concealing abuse, misusing money, and protecting their reputations before they protected the people in their care. People who distrust us are often reading the evidence correctly. (If abuse in a church has touched you, where a crime was done it belongs with the police, and for sexual abuse RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline answers at 1-800-656-4673.)

This essay argues that the deepest trouble in a post-truth age isn't that people have stopped believing truth exists. It's that we have stopped caring whether what we say is true when something else is at stake: our side, our comfort, our standing. That was Pilate's trouble too, and he's the right place to begin, because the most famous question ever asked about truth was not a philosopher's question at all.

## What Pilate knew

John's account of the trial (John 18:28 to 19:16) moves between two spaces. Outside the governor's headquarters stand the religious leaders, who won't enter for fear of defilement before the Passover. Inside stands Jesus. Pilate walks between them, and John lets us watch a powerful man try to get out of a problem he didn't want.

Pilate asks whether Jesus is a king. Jesus answers that his kingdom is not of this world, and then he says why he came: he was born, he says, "to bear witness to the truth." Then he adds a sentence that quietly turns the interrogation around.

> "Everyone who is of the truth listens to my voice." (John 18:37)

The prisoner has just told the judge that the real question in the room is not what Pilate will decide about him, but whether Pilate belongs to the truth. Pilate's reply is the question every generation since has repeated:

> "What is truth?" (John 18:38)

It's usually read as a weary skeptic's shrug. Francis Bacon, opening his essay "Of Truth" in 1625, called him "jesting Pilate" and noted that he didn't wait for an answer. Bacon may have been right about the jest. But read the next sentence. Having asked the question, Pilate goes straight back outside and tells the crowd, "I find no guilt in him" (John 18:38). Whatever Pilate thought about truth as a philosophical problem, he knew the truth of this case. He knew it well enough to say it out loud, and John records him saying it three times (John 18:38; 19:4; 19:6).

What moves him in the end isn't a lack of information. It's a sentence shouted from the courtyard: "If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend" (John 19:12). Pilate had reached a true conclusion and then set it aside when it threatened his position. His failure was not doubt about whether truth could be known. He knew it, and found that it didn't matter enough.

John adds one more exchange that serves as his verdict on the whole scene. Pilate asks Jesus where he comes from, "But Jesus gave him no answer" (John 19:9). Stung, Pilate reminds him who holds the power: "Do you not know that I have authority to release you and authority to crucify you?" (John 19:10). Jesus answers, "You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above" (John 19:11). Pilate believes power decides, in the end, what will count as true. The man in front of him knows that power is itself answerable to a truth it didn't make and can't unmake.

## The truth who is a person

The other great sentence about truth in John's Gospel was spoken hours earlier, at supper, to frightened men. Jesus had told his disciples he was going away, and that they knew the way. Thomas said what the others were thinking: "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" (John 14:5). Jesus answered him, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6).

The verse is usually quoted as a boundary marker in arguments about other religions, and it carries an exclusive claim no honest reader can soften. But it was first spoken as an answer to confusion, on the night before an execution. Thomas asked for directions, and Jesus gave him a person. Truth, on this account, is not only something Jesus teaches. It is something he is. John prepared for that in the prologue, where the Word becomes flesh, "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).

The Greek word John uses is *aletheia*. It is sometimes explained from its parts as "unhiddenness," a derivation Martin Heidegger made famous. But words mean what their users mean by them, not what their roots once suggested, and in first-century Greek *aletheia* meant roughly what we mean by truth: the way things really are. Behind John's Greek, though, stands the Hebrew Bible, and there the word most often rendered "truth," *'emet*, leans toward reliability. It comes from a root associated with firmness and support, the root that also gives us "amen." When the LORD proclaims his name to Moses, he is "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6), and the word translated "faithfulness" there is *'emet*. Many interpreters hear that ancient pairing of steadfast love and faithfulness behind John's "grace and truth."

That observation needs a caution. James Barr's *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961) is a standing warning against building theology on word roots, and among the habits he criticized was the tidy contrast between a "Hebrew" truth of relational faithfulness and a "Greek" truth of abstract correctness. Hebrew could say that a report was true in the plain sense that it matched the facts; the law required a charge of idolatry to be investigated diligently and found "true and certain" before anyone acted on it (Deuteronomy 17:4). Greek could speak of a faithful friend. So the difference isn't between two languages or two mentalities. It lies within the biblical idea of truth itself.

Truth in Scripture has two faces that belong together: accuracy and fidelity. A true statement matches what is so. A true person keeps faith with what he has said and with what he knows. Pilate had the first and failed the second; his judgment was accurate, and he wouldn't keep faith with it. The answer Jesus gave Thomas unites the two. He doesn't only describe reality correctly. He is the one in whom what God says and what God does never come apart. And the claim concerns public events: it stands or falls on whether this person really said and did what the Gospels report, and whether he really rose. The modern argument about truth has mostly been an argument about accuracy alone, as though fidelity had nothing to do with it. The post-truth age has shown how wrong that was.

## How the West learned to suspect truth

The post-truth age didn't begin in 2016, and it can't be blamed on social media alone. For most of Christian history, the church's teachers assumed that truth is discovered, not manufactured. Augustine, writing his *Confessions* around the year 400, asks in the twelfth book where two people see it when both recognize that something is true. Neither sees it in the other, he answers; both see it in the unchangeable truth above their minds. Truth belongs to no one in particular, and whoever tries to make it private has already lost it.

The modern project kept that conviction and moved its foundation. René Descartes, in his *Discourse on Method* (1637) and *Meditations* (1641), resolved to doubt whatever could be doubted and rebuild knowledge on a method any honest mind could run for itself, without resting on the authority of tradition. The hope was noble, and it carried a hidden weakness: anyone who noticed that the method had been run by interested parties could call the whole result into question.

Friedrich Nietzsche noticed. In an essay written in 1873 and left unpublished in his lifetime, "On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense," he argued that what we call truths are metaphors and conventions whose origins we've forgotten, worn smooth by long use until we mistake them for reality. In *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) he pressed further, insisting that there is only perspectival seeing and perspectival knowing, and that moral codes presented as eternal truth often turn out, when their history is traced, to be the disguised will of particular people. His notebooks, published after his death, contain the line most often quoted as his summary, that there are no facts, only interpretations.

A Christian owes Nietzsche something before criticizing him. He was right that truth-claims are made by people with interests, and that those interests shape what they're able to see. He was right, above all, that Christians have often used the language of truth to defend their own position. The philosopher Merold Westphal argued in *Suspicion and Faith* (1993) that believers should read Marx, Nietzsche and Freud, the thinkers Paul Ricoeur had called the masters of suspicion, as a Lenten discipline, because their exposure of self-serving religion largely repeats what the prophets and Jesus had already said. Jeremiah put it bluntly:

> "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9)

Paul describes human beings not as neutral inquirers after truth but as people "who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth" (Romans 1:18). Scripture is more suspicious of the human knower than the Enlightenment ever was.

A century after Nietzsche, Jean-François Lyotard gave suspicion its most influential modern form. In *The Postmodern Condition* (1979), a report on knowledge commissioned by Quebec's council of universities, he defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives." By that he meant the grand stories that explain and justify everything else, such as the march of reason toward emancipation or of history toward a classless society. His incredulity was the conclusion of a generation that had watched camps and gulags built by people who were certain history was on their side.

Stated at full strength, the postmodern critique says that every claim to universal truth has been made by someone, from somewhere, usually in that someone's interest; that the stories claiming to explain everything have justified terrible things; and that the humble response is to distrust anyone who says he has the whole truth. A thoughtful Christian should be able to sign much of that. The church has told its own story as the triumph of Christendom, and in the name of that story it blessed crusades, conquests and slave ships. Nietzsche and Lyotard got the symptom right.

What they couldn't do was stop. Suspicion that is total eventually eats itself, because the claim that every truth-claim is a disguised bid for power is itself a truth-claim, offered for our agreement by an author with interests of his own. Worse, if nothing is so apart from who is saying it, the last word belongs to whoever is loudest or strongest. The critics meant to protect the weak from the strong, and a world without truth protects no one. The prophets suspected the powerful in the name of a truth that judged them. The modern masters of suspicion kept the suspicion and lost the truth that made it just.

J. Richard Middleton and Brian Walsh argued in *Truth Is Stranger Than It Used to Be* (1995) that the biblical story is a metanarrative of an unusual kind, one that carries within itself a judgment on its own misuse, because it is told from the side of the suffering and centers on a God who hears the cry of the oppressed. The gospel is a grand story, but it's the one grand story that puts an executed man at its center and summons his executioners, and everyone who has ever used the story as a weapon, to repent.

## The liar, the indifferent, and the cynic

The philosophers explain how the educated lost confidence in truth, but the everyday post-truth age is less a matter of theory than of habit. The American philosopher Harry Frankfurt helps more. His short essay on the speaker who is indifferent to whether what he says is true first appeared in 1986 and became an unlikely bestseller when Princeton published it as a small book in 2005, and it draws a distinction everyone recognizes once it's named. The liar, he argued, still cares about the truth. He has to know what's true in order to hide it. Another kind of speaker isn't tethered to reality at all. He says whatever serves his purpose, and whether it happens to be true or false simply doesn't interest him. Frankfurt argued that such a speaker is a greater enemy of truth than the liar, because the liar at least concedes that truth matters enough to conceal. In a companion book, *On Truth* (2006), he went on to argue that no society can do without caring about truth, and that we come to know ourselves only by running up against facts we didn't make and can't wish away.

That distinction explains Pilate better than skepticism does, and it explains much of what circulates online. The person who forwards it hasn't checked it and doesn't intend to. It feels true, or it's the sort of thing that ought to be true about people like that, or it will make the right people angry. Its truth is simply beside the point.

Hannah Arendt fled Nazi Germany and spent her life trying to understand what had happened to it. In *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951) she observed that the ideal subject of totalitarian rule was not the convinced believer in the party's ideology but the person for whom the difference between fact and fiction, and between true and false, no longer existed. She described audiences lied to so often that they would believe anything and nothing, and who met an exposed lie by admiring the liar's cleverness.

In "Truth and Politics," published in *The New Yorker* in 1967, she carried the argument into free societies. Factual truth, she argued, is more fragile than the truths of mathematics or philosophy, because facts depend on witnesses and records, and once they're lost no reasoning can recover them. The traditional political lie concealed a secret. The modern one tries to rewrite what everyone already knows. The result of lying on that scale, she said, is less that people believe the lie than that they lose their bearings in the real world altogether. The endpoint, she warned, is a peculiar cynicism, a refusal to believe that anything is true. A people in that condition isn't freer. It's easier to rule.

Put Frankfurt and Arendt together and the post-truth age comes into focus: fewer liars than people who have stopped caring, and a smaller number who have learned to profit from that. The problem can't be solved by more information. You can't fact-check someone out of indifference. Indifference to truth is a moral condition before it is an intellectual one, and Scripture treats it that way.

Jesus's harshest words about falsehood come in an argument with people who were sure they were Abraham's children. The devil, he tells them, "was a murderer from the beginning, and does not stand in the truth, because there is no truth in him"; and "When he lies, he speaks out of his own character, for he is a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44). The pairing of murder and lying isn't accidental. In John's Gospel the lie is what makes the killing possible: the charge against Jesus is false, the judge knows it, and the crucifixion goes ahead. Paul traces the same pattern when he describes people who "exchanged the truth about God for a lie" (Romans 1:25). The exchange is always for something. We trade truth for whatever the lie protects: a god we can manage, a side we can trust, a picture of ourselves we can live with.

## The ninth commandment and our own feeds

It would be comfortable to stop there, with the world diagnosed. But the church has been some of the most fertile ground misinformation has found.

The command is short: "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16). Its first setting was the law court, where one false witness could cost an innocent man his life (Deuteronomy 19:15 to 21). But Israel's law pressed the command out of the courtroom and into ordinary speech, with a sentence that reads as though it were written for the group chat: "You shall not spread a false report. You shall not join hands with a wicked man to be a malicious witness" (Exodus 23:1). A false report doesn't have to be invented by us to become ours; it becomes ours when we pass it on.

Martin Luther's *Small Catechism* (1529), which counts this as the eighth commandment, taught that it requires us not only to avoid lying about our neighbor but to defend him, speak well of him, and put the best construction on what he does. The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), in its answer to Question 112, adds that believers must not join in condemning anyone rashly or without a hearing, and should love the truth and speak it candidly. Read those lines, and then scroll through a week of what Christians share about their political opponents.

On the right, the damage of the last decade has been hard to miss. QAnon, a conspiracy theory that began on an anonymous message board in October 2017, found a home in some congregations, passed along through prayer chains and small-group texts by people who would never knowingly bear false witness. After the 2020 election, many churches heard, and some pulpits repeated, claims that the election had been stolen, claims that were tested and rejected in court after court, including by judges the losing candidate had appointed. For many believers, partisan media became a more constant catechism than the church. When the cross is conscripted into a flag, the flag's propaganda begins to feel like a matter of faith, and doubting it begins to feel like betrayal. That is not a political failure first. It's idolatry, and idols always require us to lie on their behalf.

The left has its own version, harder to see because it usually travels under the banner of compassion. Both sides share the most ordinary form of the sin. A clip of a few seconds goes viral as proof of some stranger's cruelty or bigotry, and within hours thousands of people, Christians among them, have condemned someone they know nothing about. When the longer footage or the fuller story arrives, the correction travels a fraction as far as the accusation did. Conservatives do this with clips that confirm what they already suspect about the left, and progressives do it with clips that confirm what they suspect about the right. Each rush is a failure of the ninth commandment by people who wanted the story to be true. The progressive temptation is quieter than the conspiracy theory: to judge a claim by whether it serves the cause rather than whether it is so, to call an inconvenient fact harmful instead of asking whether it's accurate, to tell a useful story about a group and leave out whatever complicates it. Plato called it the noble lie in the *Republic*, a false story told for the good of the city. It isn't more noble when the cause is ours.

The two failures aren't always equal in scale or consequence, and pretending they are would be its own small dishonesty. But they're judged by the same commandment, and neither side may reserve it for its enemies. Pastors aren't exempt either. I have preached with more certainty than I had, because certainty calms a nervous room, and I have softened things I believed because saying them plainly would have cost more than I wanted to pay. Both were failures of truthfulness, and both came from the same fear.

Scripture offers a discipline for the feed that is older than the feed. "The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him" (Proverbs 18:17). Love "does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6), which means love takes no pleasure in the damning story about the person it dislikes, and is relieved rather than disappointed when the story turns out to be false. And Paul grounds truthful speech not in a principle of accuracy but in a fact about the body:

> "Therefore, having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor, for we are members one of another." (Ephesians 4:25)

A lie in the church is a wound the body inflicts on itself, a tearing at the thing Christ died to make.

## Living within the truth

How should a Christian speak in an age like this? The church tends to reach for one of two answers, and both fail. The first is volume: an answer for every question, certainty on every controversy, conviction confused with combativeness. It looks like courage and is often anxiety, and it confirms the suspicion that our truth-claims are bids for control. The second is retreat: qualify everything, soften whatever offends, let the gospel dissolve into a spirituality nobody could object to. It looks like humility and is often fear of the cost.

Paul's phrase "speaking the truth in love" is usually quoted as advice about tone, but it closes an argument about maturity. Christ gives shepherds and teachers to the church, Paul says, "so that we may no longer be children, tossed to and fro by the waves and carried about by every wind of doctrine, by human cunning, by craftiness in deceitful schemes" (Ephesians 4:14). Then comes the alternative: "Rather, speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ" (Ephesians 4:15). The contrast is between children, blown about and exploited by clever schemers, and adults anchored enough in Christ to be hard to fool and slow to condemn.

In the tenth book of the *Confessions* Augustine observes that everyone wants happiness, and that happiness is joy in the truth; yet people love truth when it shines on them and hate it when it rebukes them, wanting to see without being seen. That is the post-truth age, described sixteen centuries early. We aren't against truth. We're against truth that exposes us. His answer was a higher view of truth: where he found truth, he says, he found his God. Because truth isn't made by us, we can speak it with confidence that it will stand when we've fallen. Because it isn't ours, we can't wield it as private property, and we should expect it to judge us first. Lesslie Newbigin called this posture proper confidence, in a book by that title (1995), a theme taken up more fully in [Can You Have Faith and Doubt at the Same Time?](/writing/can-you-have-faith-and-doubt). Paul holds the same pair together: "Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Partial knowledge is still knowledge.

In 1978 Václav Havel, then a dissident playwright in communist Czechoslovakia, wrote "The Power of the Powerless." At its center is a greengrocer who puts a party slogan urging the workers of the world to unite in his shop window, among the onions and carrots. He doesn't believe it; he puts it up because not doing so would cause trouble. Havel's point was that the whole system of lies rested on millions of small acts like that, and could be undone by ordinary people who decided to live within the truth. That's where the church's answer to Pilate begins, in the places we actually govern: checking before we forward, and not forwarding what we can't check, least of all when it flatters our side; correcting ourselves in public; telling the truth about our own institutions, and reporting abuse to civil authorities instead of handling it quietly; saying "I don't know" when we don't, and saying plainly what we do know when it costs us.

Pontius Pilate would be a footnote in Roman history if not for the church. Instead his name is spoken every Sunday in the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed, which confess that Christ suffered and was crucified under him. The church put him there for a reason Arendt would have understood: the faith rests on factual truth, the most fragile kind, and the creed pins it to a name that fixes it in datable history. Christ suffered under a particular official who knew he was innocent and handed him over anyway. And the truth Pilate couldn't be bothered with didn't stay buried. Matthew reports that the first answer to the empty tomb was a paid story, soldiers given money to say the disciples had stolen the body (Matthew 28:11 to 15). The story spread. The risen Christ outlasted it.

The mercy of the gospel is that the one who stood before Pilate died for Pilate's kind too, for everyone who has known what was true and let it go because something else mattered more. That mercy doesn't excuse the indifference; it's the only thing strong enough to cure it, and it comes by way of repentance. Most of us are closer to the governor than we'd like to think. We've known, and shrugged, and washed our hands (Matthew 27:24). The question that finally matters is the one Jesus put to him without saying a word, and puts to us each time a story arrives that we want to believe, or the truth about our own church would cost us something: now that you know, what will you do? We recite Pilate's name every week. It would be a terrible thing to recite it and not recognize the face.
