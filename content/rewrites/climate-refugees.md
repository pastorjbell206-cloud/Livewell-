The book of Ruth opens with a failed harvest. "In the days when the judges ruled there was a famine in the land, and a man of Bethlehem in Judah went to sojourn in the country of Moab, he and his wife and his two sons" (Ruth 1:1, ESV). The town's name means house of bread, and the bread ran out. Elimelech took his family east to Moab, a people Israel had old reasons to distrust, and within about ten years his widow Naomi had buried him and both of her sons. The story the church loves for its loyalty begins as a story about weather, hunger and a family that left because staying meant starving.

We have a newer phrase for people like Elimelech, and it reaches most of us already sorted into teams. To one reader "climate refugee" sounds like a summons to conscience. To another it sounds like a slogan built to move a policy agenda, with a grieving family placed in front for effect. Both hear something real. The displacement is real, and so are the inflated numbers sometimes attached to it. The people are real, and so is the temptation to use them.

Underneath the quarrel, two questions have been fused that Scripture keeps apart. The first is who caused this, a question of restitution: who owes what to whom because of what they did. The second is who is my neighbor, a question of mercy: what I owe the person in front of me because God made him and put him there. The first is hard, and honest people will argue about it for decades. The second was settled on the road to Jericho, by a teacher who wouldn't let a lawyer answer it in advance. Much of the right has argued the first question in order to escape the second. Much of the left has shouted the answer to the first so loudly that the second becomes a policy position instead of a person. I have burned the fuel, boarded the flights and enjoyed every comfort of the world that emitted the most without once asking, until embarrassingly recently, who was standing downwind. The indictment in this essay has no exemption for its author.

*The neighbor has never had to wait for the verdict on cause.*

## Why the law has no category called climate refugee

Modern refugee law began as an improvisation. In 1921 the League of Nations appointed the Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen its High Commissioner for Russian Refugees, and in 1922 he secured for Russians left stateless by revolution and civil war an identity certificate that came to be called the Nansen passport. The 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, written by states looking back at the Second World War, defines a refugee as a person outside his own country who cannot or will not go back because of a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. The 1967 Protocol removed its original limits of time and place. It did not change the grounds.

Read the definition slowly and the trouble with the phrase "climate refugee" is plain. The Convention protects people from persecution, and persecution needs a persecutor with a reason. A drought has no motive. A rising sea sorts its victims by elevation, not by creed or party. That is why UNHCR, the UN refugee agency, avoids the term and speaks instead of people displaced in the context of disasters and climate change. The agency has also said, in legal guidance it published in 2020, that the Convention can still apply in some of these cases: when a government withholds relief from a people it already disfavors, for instance, a disaster becomes the instrument of an old persecution. But the global treaty has no category for the family whose well turned salt.

Most people uprooted by weather never cross a border anyway. They move from a flooded village to a city's edge in the same country, which makes them internally displaced persons. No treaty governs them, only the UN's nonbinding Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement (1998), which name disasters among the causes. The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre in Geneva keeps the most careful count, and in its 2023 global report it recorded, for 2022, 32.6 million internal displacements triggered by disasters that year, more than were triggered by conflict. The number needs care. It counts movements, not people, so a family evacuated twice is counted twice; many were evacuations ahead of storms, soon followed by return. Nor does it say how many of those floods a warming climate made worse. It does say that disasters already move more people inside their own countries in a year than war does.

Jane McAdam, a scholar of international refugee law, argues in *Climate Change, Forced Migration, and International Law* (2012) that a new treaty for climate refugees would be the wrong tool. The causes are too entangled to define a protected class cleanly, most movement is internal, and many of the people most exposed, especially in the Pacific, have said plainly that they do not want to be refugees at all. Kiribati's former president Anote Tong spoke instead of migration with dignity: moving by choice and over time, as workers and neighbors, not as a rescued population. In 2023 Australia and Tuvalu signed the Falepili Union, which opens a special pathway for Tuvaluans to live and work in Australia. That is the law as it stands: bilateral deals, soft principles, and a Convention never built for this.

Ioane Teitiota, from the atoll of Tarawa in Kiribati, sought refugee status in New Zealand, arguing that rising seas, contested land and fouled water were making his home unlivable for his family. New Zealand's courts turned him down, the Supreme Court last of all in 2015, and he was deported. He took his case to the UN Human Rights Committee, claiming under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights that sending him home violated his right to life. In views adopted in late 2019 and published in January 2020, the Committee found against him. It judged that he had not shown a real, personal and reasonably foreseeable risk to his life when he was removed, and it gave weight to the ten to fifteen years it thought Kiribati still had to take protective measures with international help, including relocation if it came to that. But the Committee also said, in words widely read as a first for a UN treaty body, that the effects of climate change in a person's home country could, in principle, expose him to a violation of the right to life serious enough to forbid sending him back. Two members dissented, one arguing that the majority had set a threshold that would be met only once conditions were already lethal.

The law's answer is honest and incomplete. In treaty there is no such thing as a climate refugee, and the reasons are not merely bureaucratic, since stretching a persecution treaty over slow environmental change could overload a system that already struggles to shelter the persecuted. But the absence of a legal category is not the absence of a person. And the law's silence here is not Scripture's silence.

## What the science can bear, and what it can't

The physics is older than the politics. In 1859 John Tyndall showed in his laboratory that water vapor and carbon dioxide absorb heat radiation; in 1896 Svante Arrhenius calculated that doubling atmospheric carbon dioxide would warm the planet by several degrees; in 1958 Charles David Keeling began the Mauna Loa measurements whose rising line has not stopped. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change was founded in 1988 to assess the evidence, and its 2022 report on impacts concluded, with what it called high confidence, that weather and climate extremes are increasingly driving displacement in Africa, Asia and North America, with small island states in the Caribbean and South Pacific hit hard out of all proportion to their size. Creation is ordered and can be studied, and those who study it mostly agree on the direction of travel, which a Christian can take seriously without treating any panel as an oracle.

Humility belongs at the next step, where physics meets the decision to move. People rarely leave home for one reason. A farmer in the Sahel whose rains have turned erratic is also facing falling prices, a neglectful government, a cousin in the city with work, and perhaps armed men on the road. Anyone who claims to isolate the climate share of that decision claims more precision than the field has. Moving also costs money, networks and somewhere to go, which is why the very poorest often cannot leave at all. The British government's Foresight report, *Migration and Global Environmental Change* (2011), made that point central: environmental stress may produce trapped populations as much as moving ones, people who are too poor to escape precisely because the land has failed them.

The largest projections should be read with that in mind. The World Bank's first *Groundswell* report (2018) modeled three regions (sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and Latin America) and found that under its most pessimistic scenario, with high emissions and unequal development, more than 143 million people could be moving within their own countries by 2050 because of slow-onset impacts such as water scarcity, failing crops and rising seas. The second report (2021) extended the model to six regions and put the pessimistic figure at up to 216 million by 2050. The same report found that rapid cuts in emissions combined with inclusive development could reduce that number by as much as 80 percent. These are scenarios, not forecasts, and the reports present them that way; they say as much about the choices still open as about the future.

Skeptics of these numbers have a record to point to. In the 1990s the Oxford-based environmental scientist Norman Myers estimated that environmental refugees could number some 200 million by the middle of this century, and the figure was repeated for years in speeches and reports long after its rough method was questioned. The migration scholar Hein de Haas argues in *How Migration Really Works* (2023) that predictions of climate-driven mass migration toward wealthy countries are largely unfounded: most environmentally related movement is short-distance and internal, and the image of a tide of refugees breaking on the borders of Europe and North America owes more to alarm than to evidence.

Put together, the picture is sober. The direction is not seriously in dispute: a warming world will make some places harder to live in, and it will fall hardest on people who have the least room to absorb a failed season. The magnitude is genuinely uncertain, and whoever preaches a projection as prophecy has earned the skepticism he meets. And the shape of the problem is less a crowd at our border than a family in Bangladesh or Burkina Faso that moves to the nearest city or cannot move at all. That changes the church's question from who we will let in to who we will stand beside where they already are.

## The last time it was us

Americans don't have to imagine people pushed off the land by a changed climate and a changed economy at once. Our grandparents lived it. In the 1910s and 1920s, with wheat prices high and tractors newly cheap, farmers on the southern plains plowed up millions of acres of grassland that had held the soil in place for thousands of years. When drought came in the early 1930s, the exposed topsoil lifted and blew. On 14 April 1935, remembered as Black Sunday, a wall of dust rolled across Oklahoma and Texas and turned the afternoon dark. The historian Donald Worster argued in *Dust Bowl* (1979) that this was no mere act of nature but the predictable result of a culture that treated land as capital to be mined for the quickest return; the drought exposed what the plow had done.

Not everyone who left came from the dust. James N. Gregory showed in *American Exodus* (1989) that many of the migrants who went west to California in the 1930s came from the cotton country of eastern Oklahoma, Arkansas and Texas, pushed out by collapsing prices, by tractors that made tenant families unnecessary, and by federal crop-reduction payments that went to landowners who then had less reason to keep tenants on. Drought, soil, markets, machines and policy were tangled together, and no researcher could have separated the weather's share of one family's decision to load the truck. John Steinbeck didn't try. *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939) put the Joad family on Route 66 and let the reader argue about causes after meeting the people.

California's reception is the part we should remember with shame. In February 1936 the Los Angeles police chief sent officers to the state's border crossings to turn back migrants who looked indigent, a measure the newspapers called the bum blockade. California also had a law on its books making it a crime to bring an indigent nonresident into California, and it took until 1941, in *Edwards v. California*, for the Supreme Court to strike it down. These were American citizens, most of them white Protestants, met at a state line by men with badges who judged them a burden. The strangers of 1936 were not foreigners. They were us.

Gregory adds a detail the church should hear. Many of the migrants were Southern Baptists, Pentecostals and holiness people, often met coolly by the established congregations of the valley towns, so they planted their own, and he traces how much later Baptist and Pentecostal strength in the Central Valley grew from those small churches. The people treated as a problem at the border turned out to be the church arriving.

The causes were tangled then as now, and the people were neighbors anyway; nobody holding a cup of water at a roadside camp in 1937 needed to know what share of the Joads' ruin was drought and what share was the bank. The reflex to meet the displaced as a threat to be managed at a line is neither partisan nor modern, and American Christians acted on it against their own brothers and sisters in living memory.

## Famine and flight in Scripture

In Genesis, hunger moves God's people again and again. "Now there was a famine in the land. So Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there, for the famine was severe in the land" (Genesis 12:10, ESV). The verse comes only a few lines after God has promised Abram the land of Canaan, and the first thing the land does is fail him. Scripture doesn't treat his leaving as faithlessness; people went where the grain was. But it doesn't romanticize the migrant either. At the border Abram is afraid, and he asks Sarai to say she is his sister so that the Egyptians will not kill him to take her. The frightened stranger endangers his wife to save himself, and it is Pharaoh, the host, who ends up rebuking him. The text lets the displaced man be exactly what displaced people often are: vulnerable, compromised and dependent on the decency of someone who owes him nothing.

Isaac faces famine in his turn (Genesis 26:1), and in the next generation it drives the whole family south. Jacob's sons tell Pharaoh, "We have come to sojourn in the land, for there is no pasture for your servants' flocks, for the famine is severe in the land of Canaan" (Genesis 47:4, ESV), and they are given Goshen. The same famine that brought Jacob's household in as protected guests strips the Egyptians of everything they own: first their money, then their livestock, and finally they come to Joseph and say, "Buy us and our land for food, and we with our land will be servants to Pharaoh" (Genesis 47:19, ESV). By the end of the drought Pharaoh owns the land of Egypt, and its farmers owe him a fifth of every harvest. Walter Brueggemann, in his commentary on *Genesis* (1982), reads the passage as an account of famine becoming the occasion to concentrate land and people in the hands of the state; others read Joseph as a prudent administrator who kept a nation alive. Either way, the verse that follows is hard to miss: "Thus Israel settled in the land of Egypt, in the land of Goshen. And they gained possessions in it, and were fruitful and multiplied greatly" (Genesis 47:27, ESV). The favored refugees thrive while their hosts become servants, and a book later the power gathered in the famine is turned on them. "Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph" (Exodus 1:8, ESV). Israel's law would later forbid exactly what the famine produced in Egypt: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). Scarcity is when the strong buy up the weak, and a people who remember Egypt are commanded never to let it become permanent (the essay on [jubilee and the economy God commanded](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded) follows that law further).

Elimelech's household flees famine too, and the book of Ruth is honest about the cost. Naomi comes home saying, "I went away full, and the LORD has brought me back empty" (Ruth 1:21, ESV). She returns because "she had heard in the fields of Moab that the LORD had visited his people and given them food" (Ruth 1:6, ESV). The move was never meant to be permanent; it was a way to survive a bad decade. That is true of most people displaced by drought and flood now, and it matters for policy, because many would rather go home than be resettled if home can be made livable again.

Elisha treats famine flight as an ordinary act of survival. He tells the woman of Shunem, whose son he had restored, "Arise, and depart with your household, and sojourn wherever you can, for the LORD has called for a famine, and it will come upon the land for seven years" (2 Kings 8:1, ESV). She lives among the Philistines for seven years, comes home to find her house and field in other hands, and appeals to the king, who orders everything restored to her, along with the produce of the fields for all the years she was gone (2 Kings 8:3–6). The text assumes that one driven out by famine keeps her claim on what she left, and that a just ruler honors it.

When the prophet Agabus foretold a famine across the Roman world, the believers in Antioch answered with money rather than a debate: "So the disciples determined, every one according to his ability, to send relief to the brothers living in Judea" (Acts 11:29, ESV). The relief went to where the people were. Together these texts refuse a false choice the modern debate keeps offering: Scripture expects hosts to protect the one who flees and expects the wider church to carry aid to the one who stays. The laws binding Israel to the sojourner are treated at length in the essays on [what the Bible says about immigration and refugees](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-immigrants-and-refugees-in-depth) and on [the widow, the orphan and the stranger](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger). The famine stories add what the laws assume: that hunger moves people, that God's own family was moved by it more than once, and that the host's decency was never optional.

## Restitution, mercy and the argument over policy

The policy quarrel deserves both cases at full strength. The first, which many conservatives would sign, runs like this. Cheap energy is the greatest anti-poverty program in history; the coal and oil being indicted also powered the escape of billions from subsistence farming, and the people told to forgo them now are mostly poor. Bjørn Lomborg argues in *False Alarm* (2020) that climate change is real but alarm has pushed rich countries toward costly policies that do little, when the money would save more lives spent on adaptation, innovation and development. Seawalls, drought-tolerant seed, early warning and crop insurance are often what exposed communities ask for first, and aid delivered in place, as Antioch's was, may serve them better than resettlement. On this view the projections have been oversold before, and moralizing at the poor about the fuel that lifted the rich is a strange kind of compassion.

The second case, which many on the left would sign, begins with the ledger. The United States has put more carbon dioxide into the atmosphere over the industrial era than any other nation, while the atoll nations and much of sub-Saharan Africa have contributed a sliver and stand first in line for the consequences. The 1992 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change named the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, and in 2022 the nations meeting in Sharm el-Sheikh agreed to establish a fund for loss and damage. The philosopher Henry Shue sharpened the point in a 1993 essay distinguishing subsistence emissions from luxury emissions. Burning fuel to cook and stay warm is not the same moral act as burning it to fly to a second home, and a just arrangement protects the first while asking the second to carry the burden. On this view adaptation is necessary but not enough, and a rich country that pays for seawalls while refusing to cut its own emissions is selling the poor a bucket for a flood it keeps feeding.

A thoughtful Christian can hold much of both, and both, argued well, are arguments about restitution: who caused the harm, how much, and what is owed. Scripture takes that question seriously. "If fire breaks out and catches in thorns so that the stacked grain or the standing grain or the field is consumed, he who started the fire shall make full restitution" (Exodus 22:6, ESV). The law doesn't ask whether he meant harm; the fire got away from him, and the neighbor's field is still ash. That has real force against a rich church that would rather not look downwind. But it assumes a traceable fire and a known neighbor, and centuries of diffuse emissions by billions of people, a large share of it before the effect was widely understood, is harder to trace. Faithful Christians will weigh that difference differently.

Mercy is a different question, and it doesn't wait on the answer to the first. The lawyer in Luke 10 wanted the category settled in advance: "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29, ESV). Jesus told him about a man left half dead on the road and never said whether he'd been careless. He asked instead, "Which of these three, do you think, proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers?" (Luke 10:36, ESV), and then said, "You go, and do likewise" (Luke 10:37, ESV). The essay on [what the parable of the Good Samaritan is really about](/writing/good-samaritan-actually-arguing) traces that turn in full. The Samaritan did not file an attribution study before he knelt.

So order the questions. First-order and binding: the displaced person bears God's image, the stranger's claim on the people of God is not suspended because the causes of his need are tangled, and a wealthy church may not treat its comfort as morally neutral while knowing where its costs tend to fall. Second-order, where faithful Christians will disagree: carbon policy, the balance of adaptation and mitigation, whether to build new legal protections, how much a nation should admit, and what restitution, if any, one country owes another. The stewardship ground under all of it is laid out in the essay on [caring for creation](/writing/creation-care-not-optional).

Then judge right and left with one instrument: whether the displaced person stays a person or becomes an exhibit. The right's temptation is to argue the uncertainty of cause in order to escape the certainty of mercy, to quote God's promise after the flood that "seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease" (Genesis 8:22, ESV) as though a promise about the seasons of the whole earth were a promise that no field would fail, when Abram's famine arrives four chapters later. The left's temptation is to turn the displaced into evidence for a program, to preach a carbon policy as if it were the creed, and to lecture a poor country about the power plant it needs. Both have found a way to use the displaced without ever sitting at their table, and the church has done both, depending on the company.

## Though he was rich

When Paul asked the Gentile churches to give for the poor believers in Jerusalem, he didn't argue from guilt or from projections. He argued from the cross. "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). Then he named the principle: "For I do not mean that others should be eased and you burdened, but that as a matter of fairness your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness" (2 Corinthians 8:13–14, ESV). The rich church gives because it has been given everything by one who became poor for people with no claim on him. That is the ground under both restitution and mercy, and it asks for repentance before it asks for a program: repentance for the comfort we never questioned, for the neighbor we defined out of reach, for the argument we used to avoid a face.

A scene near the end of Genesis belongs beside every debate about the displaced. Joseph brings his old father, a famine migrant with nothing but flocks and family, before the most powerful king on earth, and the text says, "and Jacob blessed Pharaoh" (Genesis 47:7, ESV). The refugee blesses the king. Jacob tells him, "Few and evil have been the days of the years of my life" (Genesis 47:9, ESV), and then he blesses him again on the way out. Pharaoh had the grain and the power. The old man at the door had the promise of God. Egypt gave that family the best of the land in a famine, and a later king who did not know Joseph made that family's descendants slaves. The church in the rich world will spend this century deciding what it owes the people at its doors and the people who can't leave home at all. It will be tempted to think the question is how much to give. The harder one is whether we will remember, when the emergency has passed and the arguments have hardened, that we are Jacob's children before we are Pharaoh's, famine migrants taken in by grace, and that the one who took us in became poor to do it.
