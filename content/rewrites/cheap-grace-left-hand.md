Ask most people who quote the phrase what cheap grace means, and they'll tell you it is what happens when a church makes salvation too easy: forgiveness without repentance, an altar call with no aftermath, a man who got his fire insurance and went home unchanged. They are right. They have also, almost always, aimed it at exactly one target, the easy believism of the conservative church, and they have quoted Dietrich Bonhoeffer as if he wrote only to that side of the room.

This essay argues that cheap grace has two hands. One hand excuses: it hands out forgiveness to ourselves and our allies without requiring anything of them. The other hand withholds: it refuses forgiveness to our enemies no matter what they do. Both hands have made grace cheap, because both have made it cost the one who gives it nothing. Every tribe in the American church uses both hands. The right is practiced and visible with the first, and its critics have said so for years. The progressive church has its own version of both, and its version is harder to see because it doesn't look like grace at all. It looks like justice. I do not write this from across the aisle. I've signed checks with both hands.

## What Bonhoeffer meant, in the room where he wrote it

Bonhoeffer published *Nachfolge*, known in English as *The Cost of Discipleship*, in 1937, the same year the Gestapo shut down the underground seminary he had been leading at Finkenwalde. The first chapter opens with a sentence that has outlived its setting: "Cheap grace is the deadly enemy of our Church." He defined it with a surgeon's care. Cheap grace is the preaching of forgiveness without requiring repentance. It is "grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ, living and incarnate." And then the line that matters most for what follows: cheap grace is "the grace we bestow on ourselves."

The room he was writing in explains the charge. By 1937 most of the German Protestant church had made its peace with the Reich. The movement that called itself the German Christians had decided that the nation's rebirth under Hitler was a fresh revelation of God's will in history and that the church's task was to align with it. Bonhoeffer belonged to the Confessing Church, the minority that refused that claim, and he watched much of his church accept it with a clear conscience.

So Bonhoeffer's charge was never mainly about lazy individuals getting saved without changing. It was an indictment of a church that had granted itself absolution because it stood with what it believed was the rising cause of history. A church can cheapen grace by forgiving too easily. It can also cheapen grace by aligning so confidently with a movement that it forgets it is still standing in the dock.

There is one more fact about Bonhoeffer that the progressive reader rightly insists on. In 1930 and 1931 he spent a year at Union Theological Seminary in New York, and the place that shaped him most was not the seminary but Abyssinian Baptist Church in Harlem, where he worshiped and taught Sunday school. Reggie Williams argues in *Bonhoeffer's Black Jesus* (2014) that Bonhoeffer learned there to see Christ among the suffering and to read the gospel from below, and that he carried what he learned back to Germany. The progressive claim on Bonhoeffer is historically earned. He was not a comfortable conservative.

## The case for accountability at its strongest

Before any verdict about the left, its case deserves to be heard in the form its best defenders would give it.

It begins with a wound. For a very long time the word *forgiveness* has been handed to the wounded as a way of managing them. Women abused by their husbands have been told by pastors to forgive and go home. Survivors of abuse in churches have been told that pressing charges would be unforgiving and would harm the ministry. Black Americans were told, generation after generation, to be patient and forgive, while the thing they were asked to forgive went on happening. In his *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963), Martin Luther King Jr. wrote not to segregationists but to white clergy who agreed with his goals and deplored his timing, and he named their preference with painful precision: a moderate "who prefers a negative peace which is the absence of tension to a positive peace which is the presence of justice." King saw that a call for reconciliation can be a way of protecting the one who holds power.

James Cone pressed the same point to its theological root in *The Cross and the Lynching Tree* (2011). He argued that white American Christians sang about the cross every Sunday through the decades when Black men and women were lynched across the South, and never saw the resemblance between the crucified Jesus and the bodies hanging from trees. A grace that could look at a lynching and counsel patience was not the grace of the cross. It was the grace that protects the powerful.

And the progressive critic can point to the right's cheap grace in plain sight. In January 2018, Tony Perkins of the Family Research Council told an interviewer that evangelical voters had given the president a "mulligan," a do-over, for his past conduct. Whatever one's politics, that is forgiveness offered to a political ally without any request for repentance, the grace we bestow on our own side because we need it to win. The progressive who calls that cheap grace is quoting Bonhoeffer correctly.

All of this must be granted, and granted without hedging. The church has used a counterfeit forgiveness to muzzle real pain, and the anger against that counterfeit is closer to God than the counterfeit ever was. Any argument about the left's cheap grace that does not begin here is not worth making.

## The left hand

Hold all of that, and the left's own version comes into focus.

It has two movements. The first is mercy for categories and wrath for persons. Forgiveness flows freely toward the distant and the abstract, the marginalized in general, the oppressed as a class, the sympathetic figure who confirms our politics, and it freezes solid toward the particular person in front of us whose sin is the wrong kind. The relative who voted the way that frightens us. The pastor whose theology we find cruel. The man who said something unforgivable online a decade ago. We have built a public culture of denunciation with no rite of restoration, a confessional with no absolution, where the ledger of an ally is never reopened and the ledger of an enemy is never closed. It uses the warmest possible language about mercy, and it practices almost none toward the actual unforgiven.

The second movement is subtler, and it is the one I recognize in myself. It is grace bestowed on the self through identification with the right cause. Bonhoeffer's exact charge was that cheap grace is the grace we give ourselves, and the progressive version does not say *I am forgiven, so I can do as I please*. It says, underneath, *I am on the right side, so I am not the problem*. The cause launders the conscience. Standing with the oppressed becomes a way of standing outside the dock, of being one of the good people whom history will thank. Once I am one of the good people, the call to repent quietly passes me by, because repentance is for the complicit, the ones who have not yet seen what I have seen. That is grace without the cross, and it is precisely what Bonhoeffer saw in the German Christians, who were also sure they stood on the right side of history.

The shape is the same on the right, which excuses its own leaders and withholds mercy from the immigrant or the political enemy, and on the left, which excuses its own and withholds mercy from the unenlightened. A shared grammar does not mean equal consequences in every season, and it would be dishonest to pretend every failure weighs the same. But the grammar is shared, and it is worth naming, because the same instrument that convicts one side convicts the other.

Jesus told a parable about it that nobody wants attached to their politics. A servant is forgiven ten thousand talents, a debt no laborer could repay in many lifetimes, and walks out of his own pardon to seize a fellow servant who owes him a hundred denarii, choking him and demanding payment. The king's verdict is the most frightening sentence in the chapter: "And should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?" (Matthew 18:33, ESV). The horror is not simply that the man was a hypocrite. It is that he could not feel the contradiction. He held an erased fortune in one hand and throttled a man over pennies with the other, and the two never met in his conscience, because he had filed the other man under a different category from the one he had been forgiven under. I have done that. I have done it in the name of justice, which is the most respectable way to do it.

## Why justice without grace hardens

Two twentieth-century thinkers who had watched whole peoples consumed by righteous hatred, neither of them writing to defend the right, saw where this leads.

Hannah Arendt, a Jewish political philosopher who fled Nazi Germany in 1933 and was nobody's idea of a church apologist, gave the problem its sharpest secular statement in *The Human Condition* (1958). Human action, she argued, is irreversible: what is done cannot be undone, and its consequences run on far past anything the doer intended. The only remedy she could find for that predicament was forgiveness, the power to release one another from what we have done, and she credited Jesus of Nazareth with discovering its place in human affairs. Without it, she argued, each of us would be bound for life to the consequences of a single deed, unable ever to begin again. That is a precise description of a public culture that keeps the ledger of an enemy open forever. It isn't justice working too hard. It is a society that has lost the one faculty that lets people start over, and it will eventually discover that none of its own members can survive their worst day either.

Miroslav Volf wrote *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996) out of the wreckage of the Balkan wars. He tells of Jürgen Moltmann asking him, after a lecture, whether he could embrace a četnik, one of the Serbian fighters who had devastated his people. Volf's answer takes the whole book, and the heart of it is a distinction. The will to embrace the other, the settled refusal to write him out of the human family, comes before any judgment about him and does not depend on it. The embrace itself, a restored relationship, cannot happen without truth being told and justice being named. Sin, for Volf, begins not only with hating the wicked but with drawing the boundary of humanity so that the enemy falls outside it, after which whatever we do to him feels like cleansing. Exclusion almost always comes dressed as righteousness.

That distinction answers both hands at once. The right's cheap grace skips from the will to embrace straight to the embrace, with no truth told and nothing named: the mulligan. The left's cheap grace refuses the will to embrace altogether for anyone it has judged: the enemy with no way back. Costly grace holds both. It will not write the enemy out of the human family, and it will not pretend nothing happened.

## The cross holds both

This is where the theology has to slow down, because the easy answer, *the left should just forgive more*, is as shallow as the counterfeit it replaces, and Bonhoeffer would have despised it.

Forgiveness, reconciliation and impunity are three different things, and most of our confusion comes from treating them as one. To forgive is to give up my claim to repay the wrong myself. To be reconciled is to have trust restored, which requires truth and repentance and often takes years. Impunity is the absence of consequences, and forgiveness never requires it. A woman can forgive the man who abused her and still testify against him, still refuse to live under his roof, still be right to keep her distance for the rest of her life. Forgiveness never obliges anyone to return to danger. If you are in that danger now, in the United States the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, and no pastor who tells you otherwise speaks for Christ.

Paul holds these apart and together in a single passage. "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19, ESV). He does not say the wrong evaporates. He says I am not the collector. Then comes the unbearable next move, quoting Proverbs: "if your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he is thirsty, give him something to drink" (Romans 12:20, ESV), and "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:21, ESV). Walter Brueggemann, in *Praying the Psalms* (1982), shows where the rage goes in the meantime. The psalms of vengeance, the ones that cry "O God, break the teeth in their mouths" (Psalm 58:6, ESV), are not models for our conduct. They are the place where fury is spoken honestly and handed over to God, the only judge without sin, rather than carried out by our own hand. The rage is real enough to be prayed. Because it is prayed, it is taken out of my hands.

The church once knew how to practice this in public. In 390, after soldiers acting on the Emperor Theodosius's orders massacred thousands of people in Thessalonica, Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, wrote to the most powerful Christian in the world and told him he could not receive communion until he repented. Theodosius was the church's great ally, the emperor who had made Nicene Christianity the faith of the empire. Ambrose did not give him a mulligan. And when the emperor did public penance, the church restored him to the table. It neither excused its ally nor excluded him forever. That is the whole shape of costly grace in one story: the truth named in full, the powerful held to account, and the door opened, on the far side of repentance, to the one who did not deserve it.

Our own era has an example. In January 2018, at the sentencing of the former team doctor Larry Nassar, Rachael Denhollander, the first woman to go public with her accusation, gave a statement that did both things at once. She asked the court for the maximum sentence the law allowed, and in the same statement she spoke of the gospel's offer of forgiveness to him, if he would repent, and said she extended her own forgiveness to him as well. She did not trade accountability for forgiveness or forgiveness for accountability. She refused to let either become cheap.

The Apostles' Creed places "the forgiveness of sins" among the things the church believes, between "the communion of saints" and "the resurrection of the body." It is confessed as an article of faith, not offered as a political favor. It belongs to the whole communion, not to a tribe, and it is extended in hope of a resurrection that includes people we would not have chosen to see there.

## The elder brother

There is a son in one of Jesus's parables who never receives the grace, and it is because he refuses it, not because it is withheld.

The prodigal comes home and the father runs to him. The robe, the ring, the calf and the music all land on the son who wasted his inheritance, and the elder brother stands out in the field. "But he was angry and refused to go in. His father came out and entreated him" (Luke 15:28, ESV). His complaint is not wrong on the facts. He really did stay. He really did work. His brother really did squander everything. Every line of his grievance is true, and its truth is what hardens him, because he built his standing on being the one who did it right, and a father who throws a feast for the one who did it wrong has demolished the only economy he knew. He cannot enter the joy without surrendering his ledger.

The father's answer is tender and does not budge: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. It was fitting to celebrate and be glad, for this your brother was dead, and is alive; he was lost, and is found" (Luke 15:31–32, ESV). Notice that the father goes out to both sons. He runs to the one who sinned and he walks out into the dark to plead with the one who was right. The story ends without telling us whether the elder brother went in. It leaves the question with those of us who were, by every measure we trust, on the correct side.

Bonhoeffer could have stayed in New York in the summer of 1939, when friends had arranged a safe position for him. He went home to Germany within weeks, writing that he would have no right to share in the rebuilding of Christian life there after the war if he did not share the trials of this time with his people. He was hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945, two weeks before American soldiers reached the camp. He paid for the costly grace he preached in his own body. That is why his words still cut, and why it is a kind of theft to quote him only against the other side while keeping in our own hands the very thing he died calling the enemy of the church.

Jesus said it plainly enough that none of us can claim to have missed it: "But I say to you who hear, Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you" (Luke 6:27–28, ESV). And a few verses later, the line that should unsettle every tribe in the American church: "If you love those who love you, what benefit is that to you? For even sinners love those who love them" (Luke 6:32, ESV). Grace toward allies costs nothing. Grace toward the distant and the categorical costs nothing and earns applause from the people whose approval we want. The grace that costs is the grace owed to the particular person I have filed under *villain* so that I never have to file him under *neighbor*, the one whose hunger I am commanded to feed before I have finished deciding whether he deserves to eat.

Paul describes what happened at the cross in a sentence that refuses both hands at once. God put Christ forward, he writes, "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26, ESV). Just: the wrong was not excused, not waved through because the sinner was on the right team. It was named and borne in full. And the justifier: the one named guilty was not written out of the human family but brought home. Nobody on the right or the left bestowed that grace on himself. It cost the one who gave it everything, which is the only reason it isn't cheap. That is the grace every one of us has received, the right hand and the left, and it is the grace we are now asked to spend on someone who will not thank us for it. The father is still standing in the field, pleading. Whether we go in is the one part of the story he left for us to write.
