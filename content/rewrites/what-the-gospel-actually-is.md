Ask a room of churchgoers what the gospel is and you'll usually hear one of two answers. One sounds like advice: follow the example of Jesus, love your neighbor, become a kinder and more honest person. The other sounds like comfort: God loves you exactly as you are. Skeptics hear the same answers with less charity. Both hold something true. Neither is what the first Christians meant by the word.

Picture a messenger reaching a village in the middle of a long war. He doesn't hand out a manual on how to win it, and he doesn't assure the villagers that the war was over in their hearts all along. He reports something that happened somewhere else. The fighting has stopped. The enemy has surrendered. You can go home. Whatever the villagers do next depends on a report about an event they didn't cause and can't improve. That is the shape of the word gospel.

From outside the building the Christian message usually sounds like rules or like consolation. It rarely sounds like what it first was, a claim about history, the kind that could be checked and could therefore be false. The fault isn't only the listener's. I've preached advice from a pulpit and called it the gospel. Advice keeps a preacher useful, the expert with the steps. News leaves him nothing to offer but the announcement.

The gospel is an announcement of what God has done in Jesus the Messiah: that he died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and now reigns. And because it is news about a king who died in the place of his enemies, it asks for more than agreement. The demons, James says, agree. It asks for trust, and Jesus warned that a person can applaud every word he said and never give it.

## A word borrowed from the emperor and the prophets

The Greek word the New Testament uses, *euangelion*, wasn't a religious word when the church picked it up. It belonged to heralds, carried to a city when a battle had been won or a ruler born or crowned, and the herald's job was to report.

Around 9 BC the provincial assembly of Asia, acting on a proposal from the Roman proconsul Paullus Fabius Maximus, voted to begin the civic year on the birthday of Caesar Augustus. Copies were set up in several cities, and the best preserved was found at Priene, in western Turkey. The inscription praises Providence for sending Augustus as a savior who ended war and set the world in order, and it says that the god's birthday marked the beginning of the good tidings, *euangelia*, that came to the world through him. Adolf Deissmann brought it to New Testament readers in *Licht vom Osten* (1908), translated as *Light from the Ancient East*, because it shows how the word sounded in a Roman province: a royal announcement that a savior had come.

Luke knows how that sounds. He opens his account of Jesus' birth by naming the emperor, "In those days a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be registered" (Luke 2:1, ESV), and then sends a different herald to shepherds near Bethlehem. "For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Savior, who is Christ the Lord" (Luke 2:11, ESV). A birth, good news, a savior, a lord: the vocabulary spent on Augustus, handed to a child laid in a feeding trough. Some anti-imperial readings of the New Testament press further than the evidence allows, but in Thessalonica the charge against the first Christians was that they were "saying that there is another king, Jesus" (Acts 17:7, ESV).

The empire supplied the word; Israel's Scriptures supplied its meaning. The Greek translation of Isaiah uses the verb *euangelizomai* for the herald who runs ahead with news, and Isaiah 40 opens its comfort to exiles by calling for one: "Go on up to a high mountain, O Zion, herald of good news; lift up your voice with strength, O Jerusalem, herald of good news" (Isaiah 40:9, ESV). Twelve chapters later the herald arrives: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace, who brings good news of happiness, who publishes salvation, who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns'" (Isaiah 52:7, ESV). The news is that the exile is ending and the Lord is returning to Zion as king. What follows at once, from 52:13, is the song of the servant wounded for the transgressions of others, so that in Isaiah the announcement of God's reign and the suffering that makes it good news for sinners are never far apart.

Mark gives Jesus' own opening announcement: "Now after John was arrested, Jesus came into Galilee, proclaiming the gospel of God, and saying, 'The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel'" (Mark 1:14–15, ESV). The site's essay on [the kingdom of God](/writing/what-is-the-kingdom-of-god-and-why-it-changes-everything) follows that thread; the point here is the grammar. First comes the announcement, in the indicative: the time is fulfilled, the kingdom has come near. Then comes the response, in the imperative: repent and believe. The response matters enormously, and this essay ends there. But the response is not the news.

## What Paul says he handed on

The clearest summary of the gospel comes in Paul's first letter to Corinth, written in the mid-50s AD to a church where some were saying "there is no resurrection of the dead" (1 Corinthians 15:12, ESV). "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3, ESV), "that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:4, ESV), "and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:5, ESV).

"Delivered" and "received" were the ordinary terms for handing down a fixed tradition; this summary isn't Paul's own composition. Its compact clauses, each beginning with "that," read like a formula shaped for memory, and scholars of very different convictions, skeptical ones included, regard it as older than the letter and commonly date it to within a few years of the crucifixion. These are among the earliest Christian sentences we have.

The formula is a list of events. Died, buried, raised, appeared. It names witnesses, and Paul goes on to name more, including five hundred at once, most of whom he says were still alive and could be asked. He even hands the skeptic the terms on which the whole thing could be refuted: "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). I find that one of the most respectful sentences in the New Testament. It stakes the faith on a tomb outside Jerusalem around AD 30 and admits that if the claim is false, Christianity is false with it. The site's essay on [whether the resurrection happened](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen) weighs that claim; what matters here is the form. Advice can't be wrong in that way. Only news can.

But the events come with their meaning attached. Christ didn't simply die; he "died for our sins," and he died and rose "in accordance with the Scriptures," in line with Isaiah's servant. The small word "for" carries the weight of the report. The death was on our behalf, in our place, for sins that were ours. How that works is the subject of the site's essay on [why Jesus had to die](/writing/why-did-jesus-have-to-die). You don't need a finished theory of the atonement to hear the gospel. You do need the "for."

The church has had to rediscover this. In the preface to his German New Testament in 1522, Luther explained the word *Evangelium* with David and Goliath: the trembling Israelites heard that their champion had struck down the giant, and rejoiced over a victory they hadn't fought. Four centuries later, in *Christianity and Liberalism* (1923), J. Gresham Machen argued that the liberal Protestantism of his day had turned Christianity into moral exhortation, a religion in the imperative mood, when the New Testament begins instead by announcing something God has already done. The gospel starts with a report.

## Two ways of turning news back into advice

We keep turning it back into advice anyway, in two directions, each worth hearing at its strongest. The first says the heart of Christianity is the way of Jesus: love your neighbor, forgive your enemy, feed the hungry, live the Sermon on the Mount. Its defenders are often reacting, rightly, against a faith that talked about heaven and changed nothing about how its members treated the poor. A gospel that produces no changed life has something badly wrong with it. But follow the instinct to its end. If the gospel is fundamentally instruction, it isn't news, and it isn't good. It's a ladder, and most of us know how the ladder ends, because we've been climbing some version of it all our lives, and the rung we're on is starting to give. Advice assumes the listener can still win the war. The whole problem the New Testament describes is that he can't.

The second answer runs the other way: the gospel is simply that God loves you as you are, with no conditions and no demands. This guards something precious too: it refuses to let grace become a transaction, and it remembers that "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). The sociologist Christian Smith, interviewing American teenagers for *Soul Searching* (2005), found a version of this to be the working religion of the country. He called it moralistic therapeutic deism, in which God wants people to be nice and feel good and otherwise waits to be called on, something like a divine butler and cosmic therapist, in Smith's phrase. It is half of something true. Cut loose from the cross, though, that love becomes sentiment, a warmth that asks nothing and therefore changes nothing and cost nothing. The love of Romans 5:8 has a body, and the body has wounds. Reassurance that God thinks well of you is not the report that he has dealt with what is actually wrong with you, at the price of his Son's life.

Dallas Willard saw that these two answers share a hidden premise. In *The Divine Conspiracy* (1998) he described what he called gospels of sin management. On the right, the gospel had shrunk to forgiveness alone, a bar code on the soul that gets you past the checkout at death and claims little of life before it. On the left, it had shrunk to a program for removing social evils, with Jesus as its most admired advocate. Both had lost the center, a living Jesus who calls people to become his apprentices in God's reign. Willard's knife cuts in both political directions, which is why it can be trusted.

Neither the moralist nor the therapist is a stranger to me. The first is the sermon I'm tempted to preach, and I feel its pull every week, because advice is safe and news is dangerous. The second is the one most of us would rather hear on a bad week. Both make the gospel about us, our effort or our worth. Paul handed the Corinthians neither. He handed them a report about Jesus.

## King Jesus or justification by faith?

Once you agree the gospel is news, a harder question opens among people who agree on nearly everything else: what is the headline? Each side of the argument is protecting something real.

In 1545 Luther recalled that he had hated the phrase "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17 until he saw it as the righteousness God gives, by which he justifies the ungodly through faith, and then felt he had entered paradise through open gates. In the Smalcald Articles (1537) he wrote that nothing in this article could be surrendered even if heaven and earth fell. For centuries many Protestants summed up the gospel with that article: Christ died in your place, and you are declared righteous by trusting him, not by anything you do.

The challenge came in stages. In *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977), E. P. Sanders argued that the Judaism of Paul's day wasn't a religion of earning merit but what he called covenantal nomism, grace first and law as response. James Dunn's 1982 lecture "The New Perspective on Paul," published the next year, gave the movement that followed its common name, and N. T. Wright brought it to a wide audience. In *What Saint Paul Really Said* (1997) he argued that the gospel, for Paul, is not a system for how people get saved. It is the royal announcement that the crucified and risen Jesus is Israel's Messiah and the world's true Lord. Justification is not the gospel itself but something the gospel brings about: God's declaration of who belongs to his renewed people, Jew and Gentile together, on the basis of faith.

Scot McKnight carried the argument into the pews with *The King Jesus Gospel* (2011). American evangelicals, he argued, had built a salvation culture rather than a gospel culture, equating the gospel with a plan for getting individuals saved. The apostolic gospel, he argued from 1 Corinthians 15 and Acts, is the story of Jesus completing Israel's story. Matthew Bates pressed the point into the definition of faith. In *Salvation by Allegiance Alone* (2017) he argued that the Greek *pistis* often carries the sense of fidelity, and that saving faith is best summed up as allegiance to Jesus the king: assent to the gospel, a confessed loyalty to him and a life that embodies it. Bates insists that this allegiance answers grace rather than earning it, since the king has already won. At its strongest, this side notes that the four Gospels say little about justification and much about a king, and that a gospel shrunk to a transaction can be believed by people who never let Jesus rule their money, politics or enemies. A king has to be obeyed; a transaction only has to be completed.

The other side is just as serious. Greg Gilbert's *What Is the Gospel?* (2010) set the message out in four movements: God the righteous Creator, humanity in rebellion, Christ's death and resurrection for sinners, and the response of repentance and faith. The logic is simple: a king's arrival is terrible news for rebels, which all of us are, unless he has made a way to pardon his enemies. Paul ties the gospel to that pardon. He calls it "the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16, ESV), and he names God as the one "who justifies the ungodly" (Romans 4:5, ESV). Take that away, this side argues, and "Jesus is Lord" becomes a new law laid on people who couldn't keep the old one. D. A. Carson, with Peter O'Brien and Mark Seifrid, edited the two volumes of *Justification and Variegated Nomism* (2001 and 2004), arguing that Paul's Jewish world was more varied than Sanders's category allowed. John Piper's reply to Wright, *The Future of Justification* (2007), asked what becomes of the guilty person who needs to know he himself stands forgiven before a holy God. Wright answered in *Justification: God's Plan and Paul's Vision* (2009), in an exchange that remains a model of disagreement between brothers.

They share more than the argument suggests. Wright has never denied that Christ died for sins; his *The Day the Revolution Began* (2016) is a whole book on the cross. Gilbert and Piper don't deny that Jesus is king; they insist the kingship is good news only because of the cross. Both sides confess the creed Paul received, that Christ died for our sins, was raised bodily and reigns, and that belongs to the first order of Christian belief. Which part is the headline is a serious disagreement inside the family, not a line between faithful and unfaithful.

This essay doesn't settle which part is the headline. It holds two convictions and leaves the ranking open. The first is that the gospel's engine is the word "for": the death was in our place, and without it the king's arrival is a threat rather than a gift. The second is that the grace in that news claims the whole person, not a slice of the afterlife. Defenders of both accounts, at their best, would sign both sentences; they differ over which one leads. The strongest objection to the way this essay has put things comes from the king Jesus side. If the "for" is the engine, then Israel, the kingdom and the renewal of the world risk becoming scenery around an individual transaction, and the church goes on preaching how I get saved rather than who Jesus is. A reader who thinks the essay leans the wrong way is free to say so and remain a brother or sister.

## Even the demons believe

If the gospel is news, the obvious response is to believe it. But the New Testament insists there is a kind of believing that isn't enough, and Jesus ended the sermon the world admires most with a warning aimed at admirers. "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 7:21, ESV). These aren't scoffers; they prophesied and cast out demons in his name (Matthew 7:22). His answer is the sermon's most frightening sentence: "And then will I declare to them, 'I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness'" (Matthew 7:23, ESV). There was never a relationship beneath the activity.

Then come the two builders. "Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them will be like a wise man who built his house on the rock" (Matthew 7:24, ESV). "And everyone who hears these words of mine and does not do them will be like a foolish man who built his house on the sand" (Matthew 7:26, ESV). Both men hear. Through the dry season you couldn't tell the houses apart. Then "the rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew" (Matthew 7:25, ESV), and one house stands and the other falls, "and great was the fall of it" (Matthew 7:27, ESV). The storm doesn't create the difference; it reveals a foundation that was always rock or sand. Matthew adds that "the crowds were astonished at his teaching" (Matthew 7:28, ESV), and the sermon has just told them that astonishment was never the test.

James makes the point in plainer terms. "What good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him?" (James 2:14, ESV). Then he reaches for the sharpest example available: "You believe that God is one; you do well" (James 2:19, ESV). Even the demons believe, he adds, and they shudder. Their creed is orthodox. An unclean spirit in Mark names Jesus more accurately than the disciples yet could: "What have you to do with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God?" (Mark 5:7, ESV). The demons have all the information they need.

The church spent centuries naming what they lack. Augustine, preaching on John's Gospel, distinguished believing Christ, taking his word as true, from believing into him, the trust by which a person loves him and is joined to him; medieval theologians drew from Augustine a threefold formula of believing that God is, believing God and believing into God. The Lutheran and Reformed teachers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries set out the distinction teachers still use, and Francis Turretin's *Institutes of Elenctic Theology* (1679–1685) is one of its standard statements. Faith, *fides*, has three parts: *notitia*, knowing what the gospel says; *assensus*, judging that it's true; and *fiducia*, the personal trust by which a person rests the whole weight of himself on Christ. The demons have *notitia* and *assensus*, which is why they shudder. They lack *fiducia*: they know who Jesus is and won't entrust themselves to him.

The old Protestant and Catholic argument lives in that distinction. In its decree on justification in 1547, the Council of Trent taught that justification is not only the forgiveness of sins but the sanctification and renewal of the inner person, and it condemned the teaching that justifying faith is nothing but confidence in God's mercy, fearing a trust that would leave people confident and unchanged. Calvin answered in the same year that faith alone justifies, yet the faith that justifies is not alone. On the Protestant reading, Paul and James aren't at war. When Paul says "one is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28, ESV), he refuses to let anything we do ground our standing with God. When James says "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (James 2:24, ESV), he refuses to call bare assent faith, and "justified" in his argument is often read as something like shown to be in the right. Both are describing *fiducia* from different sides: it rests on nothing but Christ, and it is never idle.

The site's essay on [whether you can lose your salvation](/writing/can-you-lose-your-salvation) takes up the question every sensitive reader of Matthew 7 eventually asks: is my faith the real thing? One word belongs here, because these warnings can crush a tender conscience. *Fiducia* isn't a quality you inspect in yourself until you find enough of it. It is trust that looks away from itself to Christ. The father in Mark 9 had about as little of it as a person can have, and what he said was "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24, ESV). Jesus healed his son. What Matthew 7 warns against is not weak faith. It is a religious life with no trust in it at all, busy and admiring and fluent and never once handed over.

## Cheap grace and the cost of the throne

The man who gave this problem its lasting name was a Lutheran writing to Lutherans. In 1937 Dietrich Bonhoeffer was running an illegal seminary for the Confessing Church at Finkenwalde, which the Gestapo closed that autumn. That year he published *Nachfolge*, now translated as *Discipleship*. Cheap grace, as he describes it, is grace treated as a principle rather than a living word: forgiveness preached without repentance, grace without discipleship, without the cross, without the living Jesus Christ. Costly grace costs a person his life and gives him the only life worth having. He wasn't saying Luther had been wrong, but that Luther's discovery had been handed down to comfortable heirs as a formula that excused them from following anyone. He paired two sentences to name the remedy: only the one who believes obeys, and only the one who obeys believes. Obedience doesn't earn grace. It is what trust looks like when it's real, the way trusting a rope means putting your weight on it.

It's tempting to aim Bonhoeffer only at other people. Conservatives hear "cheap grace" and think of congregations that have quietly dropped repentance, where love means affirmation. That has force: a grace that never tells anyone they're wrong has no one to forgive. Progressives think of a revival culture that reduced salvation to a prayer prayed once, or of a civil religion in which Christianity is a badge of national belonging and the cross hangs beside the flag without either making demands on the other. That has force too, because a gospel of Jesus as king announces a king who judges every nation, including ours. The two critiques are one critique. In each case we've kept the words and let go of the claim, and what's left is assent without *fiducia*, a faith the demons could hold.

I have to put myself inside this. My working life is words about Jesus, and it's entirely possible to explain and admire them every day and be, underneath the fluency, a man building on sand. What's in question, for me as much as for any reader, isn't whether I agree with the Sermon on the Mount but whether the agreeing has become a life with a different shape, and I don't always know until the weather turns.

This is where the gospel proves more demanding than the moralist's program. The ladder asks only for improvement, a better version of the self that stays in charge. The gospel asks for that self's death. "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me" (Mark 8:34, ESV). Paul holds gift and claim in one breath: "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8, ESV), and "we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Ephesians 2:10, ESV). The gift comes first and comes free, and the life it makes is what the gift is for. Cheap grace is easy because it leaves you on the throne. The real thing costs you the throne.

## What the herald leaves with you

Here is the gospel as plainly as I can put it, for the reader who doesn't believe it. A Galilean Jew named Jesus, executed under Pontius Pilate around AD 30, died for the sins of others, as Israel's Scriptures had foreshadowed. He was buried. On the third day God raised him bodily from the dead, and he was seen alive by people who could be named and questioned. By that resurrection God declared him Lord of the world. Paul admitted that if the history is false, the whole is empty. You're free to weigh it and find it wanting. What you're owed is the claim itself, not a moral program or a reassurance with the cross removed, and no one is owed your agreement by a deadline.

The claim cuts two ways. The herald doesn't ask the soldiers to win the war. He tells them it has already been won, outside the city, by someone else who paid for it himself. To the soldier who is exhausted and out of ammunition, that is the best thing he has ever heard. To the soldier who was sure he was winning, it sounds like treason. The gospel has always been both, and which one it is to you says a good deal about where you're standing.

For those of us who say we've believed it, Jesus left the harder word. The two houses looked alike until the storm. Hearers and doers sat in the same crowd. Everyone gets the words, and sooner or later everyone gets the weather: the diagnosis, the betrayal, the graveside, and at last our own. The storm never cares how sincerely we admired the blueprint.

The war was ended by a king who took the loss that belonged to his enemies and walked out of his own tomb on the third day. That is the one foundation the storm can't reach. The question the gospel leaves in our hands was never whether we agree with it. It is whether we've stopped fighting, and whose weight we're resting on when the rain comes.
