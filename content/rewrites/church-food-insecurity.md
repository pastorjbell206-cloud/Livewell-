Near a great many American churches, often within a few minutes' drive, a line forms once a week outside a side door. The people in it are often grandparents raising grandchildren on a fixed income, workers whose hours were cut, parents whose paychecks run out before the month does. Many have prayed the prayer the volunteers inside have prayed: give us this day our daily bread. The people handing out the boxes and the people carrying them to the car are asking God for the same thing in the same words, and most weeks they never sit down to eat together.

What does the Bible say about hunger? The quick answer is true: feed the hungry. The church, to its real credit, has done it more steadily than almost any institution in Western history. But Scripture says something slower and stranger about bread than "give it away." From the manna to the upper room, bread in the Bible is rarely only a commodity moved from people who have it to people who don't. It's the stuff of a table, and a table is where strangers are made into a household. The church has grown very good at distribution and much less practiced at communion, and the distance between those two words is the argument that follows.

I write that as a pastor. For years I preached on daily bread without letting the hunger a short drive from my own church into the sermon. The indictment starts at the pulpit.

## Hunger in the Richest Country on Earth

Start with what can be counted. The Economic Research Service of the U.S. Department of Agriculture publishes an annual report, *Household Food Security in the United States*, drawn from a supplement to the Census Bureau's Current Population Survey. In its report on 2022, published in 2023, ERS found that 12.8 percent of American households were food insecure at some point during the year, which comes to roughly 44 million people living in those households. In the ERS definition, a food-insecure household is one that, at times during the year, was uncertain of having, or unable to get, enough food for everyone in it because it lacked money or other resources. That isn't famine, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. It looks like parents skipping meals so the children can eat, and a choice in the last week of the month between the power bill and the groceries. It is a different hunger from the siege in Lamentations, and in one way a more damning one, because it happens in the presence of abundance.

"Food desert" needs the same care. In 2009, at the direction of the previous year's farm bill, ERS reported to Congress in *Access to Affordable and Nutritious Food* that about 23.5 million Americans lived in low-income areas more than a mile from a supermarket or large grocery store. The working definition that grew from that study is narrower than popular use: a low-income census tract where a substantial number or share of residents lives more than a mile from a supermarket or large grocery store in a city, or more than ten miles in the country. The agency now generally speaks of "low-income, low-access" tracts instead.

The concept is contested from two directions, and both critics are partly right. The economists Hunt Allcott, Rebecca Diamond, Jean-Pierre Dubé and their colleagues, studying supermarket openings and household purchases nationwide in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (2019), found that a new supermarket changes what nearby households buy very little. By their estimate, giving poorer households the same stores and prices as richer ones would close only about a tenth of the nutrition gap; the rest comes from differences in what people choose to buy, which they linked in part to education and nutrition knowledge. From the other side, the Bronx gardener and farmer Karen Washington, who is widely credited with the term "food apartheid," objected that "food desert" is an outsider's word that sugarcoats the problem, and that bringing in a supermarket won't change it. Her term asks about the whole food system, including race, geography and economics. The history bears her out, from the federal appraisal maps of the 1930s to the disinvestment that followed. Put the critiques together and something honest comes out. Hunger in America is mostly a matter of money rather than miles, and the money has a history.

The church's response has taken a particular shape. In Phoenix in 1967, John van Hengel, a soup-kitchen volunteer who had seen how much edible food grocers were throwing away, founded St. Mary's Food Bank, usually counted as the first food bank. Through the 1980s, as public assistance tightened, church pantries and soup kitchens multiplied. The sociologist Janet Poppendieck, in *Sweet Charity?* (1998), traced how that emergency response, meant to be temporary, settled into a permanent second system of food provision run largely by volunteers, many in church basements. It was good work, and it still is. Poppendieck asked what it had quietly taught the country to accept.

## Manna, Daily Bread, and the Economy of Enough

Scripture's first long lesson about food is given to a hungry people where nothing grows. A month out of Egypt, Israel complains, and God's answer is not a lecture on gratitude. It is breakfast. "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I am about to rain bread from heaven for you, and the people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not'" (Exodus 16:4, ESV). The bread comes first, and its meaning is learned afterward, an order that matters for any church tempted to offer the hungry a sermon in place of a meal.

The manna comes with rules, and the rules are the lesson. Each household gathers for a day, and what is kept overnight breeds worms. When it was measured, "whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack" (Exodus 16:18, ESV). The wilderness economy makes hoarding pointless and shortage impossible, and Paul reached for that verse when he asked the prosperous Corinthians to send money to the poor believers in Jerusalem, so that "your abundance at the present time should supply their need" (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV). When Moses later told Israel that the manna had taught them that man does not live by bread alone (Deuteronomy 8:3), he wasn't ranking spiritual food above physical food. God taught them to trust his word by feeding them, daily, with bread that couldn't be stockpiled. The dependence was learned through the stomach.

The prayer Jesus taught keeps the wilderness in it. "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matthew 6:11, ESV). The word translated "daily," *epiousios*, is so rare that Origen, in his treatise *On Prayer* in the 230s, thought the evangelists had coined it, and scholars still argue whether it means bread for today, for tomorrow, or simply the bread we need. Every option asks for enough and no more, the manna's measure. And the prayer is plural. Jesus didn't teach anyone to pray for *my* bread. A Christian who prays it alone in a comfortable kitchen is still praying for a household that includes people whose cupboards are empty. That doesn't turn the prayer into a policy platform, but it makes it impossible to pray honestly while believing my neighbor's bread is none of my business.

Isaiah widens the picture into an invitation. Speaking to exiles, God calls out like a vendor in the market, except that nothing is for sale. "Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price" (Isaiah 55:1, ESV). Then comes the question under this whole essay: "Why do you spend your money for that which is not bread, and your labor for that which does not satisfy?" (Isaiah 55:2, ESV). The prophet means first the covenant mercies promised to David, but he doesn't treat bread as a mere symbol. A people who forget God will get food wrong too, and the feast God offers is free because it is a gift before it is anything else.

That word is where the theologian Norman Wirzba begins in *Food and Faith* (2011). Eating, he argues, is where theology becomes unavoidable, because each meal either receives the world as God's gift, given through soil, rain, animals and other people's labor, or treats it as a commodity acquired at the lowest price and consumed without thought. The line outside the side door is one of the places where our forgetting of the gift shows.

## Jesus at Table With the Wrong People

Apart from the resurrection, the feeding of the five thousand is the only miracle all four Gospels record. In Mark the disciples are sensible: the hour is late, the place remote, so send the crowd to the villages to buy food. Jesus's reply makes no logistical sense: "You give them something to eat" (Mark 6:37, ESV). The disciples answer in purchasing power, two hundred denarii, most of a year's wages for a laborer. What he does next is easy to read past. "Then he commanded them all to sit down in groups on the green grass" (Mark 6:39, ESV). He doesn't form a line. He seats the crowd in companies, as guests are seated at a banquet, and then takes, blesses, breaks and gives, the four verbs Mark will use again at the Last Supper. "And they all ate and were satisfied" (Mark 6:42, ESV). The crowd isn't processed. It's hosted.

In John's sequel the crowd follows him across the lake for more loaves, and he offers himself: "Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst'" (John 6:35, ESV). Jesus fed the hungry without first checking their motives, then told them the truth about a deeper hunger without taking the bread back. The loaves were not bait, and they were not the whole gift.

His meals scandalized people for a reason we tend to miss. In the ancient Mediterranean world a shared table meant shared standing, and the seating showed everyone where they ranked. So the complaint that followed him was a complaint about social order: "And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them'" (Luke 15:2, ESV). At another dinner he told his host to change the guest list: "But when you give a feast, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind" (Luke 14:13, ESV), precisely because they couldn't repay. A meal given to people who can't return the invitation breaks the ancient economy of favors, in which every table was also a ledger.

Christine Pohl's *Making Room* (1999), a history of Christian hospitality, argues that the early church understood its welcome of the poor in this way. A place at a household table, rather than provisions handed out at the door, told a person of low status that he was a guest worth seating. Pohl traces how much of that hospitality later moved into institutions, gaining scale while losing the table where rich and poor ate the same food.

The risen Jesus kept the pattern. Two disciples walked to Emmaus with a stranger who explained the Scriptures all the way, and they still didn't know him. Back in Jerusalem they told it this way: "Then they told what had happened on the road, and how he was known to them in the breaking of the bread" (Luke 24:35, ESV). He had been explained to them all afternoon. He was recognized at a table.

## Where the Church First Failed at Table

Luke describes the earliest church in terms of food. "And day by day, attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes, they received their food with glad and generous hearts" (Acts 2:46, ESV). Four chapters later the table has cracked.

"Now in these days when the disciples were increasing in number, a complaint by the Hellenists arose against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution" (Acts 6:1, ESV). The daily work had quietly sorted itself along a line of language and culture, so that Greek-speaking widows were missed. Nobody is accused of malice; it was the ordinary neglect that happens when those running a distribution mostly resemble one group of those receiving it. The apostles didn't simply add volunteers. The community chose seven men "of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom" (Acts 6:3, ESV), and all seven named carry Greek names. Many readers take that as a sign that oversight of the table passed to people from the neglected community. If so, and it is at least a reasonable reading, the first church answered a failure of distribution by moving power across the table rather than by improving logistics on one side of it.

Corinth shows a sharper failure. The church met in homes and ate the Lord's Supper as part of a real meal. Gerd Theissen, in the essays gathered in English as *The Social Setting of Pauline Christianity* (1982), argued that the conflict in 1 Corinthians 11 ran between richer and poorer members: the wealthier, who supplied the food, ate their own meal with more and better fare, while the poor had little. Many readers add that laborers and slaves, who didn't control their own hours, arrived after the others had already eaten. Paul refuses to call it the Lord's Supper at all: "When you come together, it is not the Lord's supper that you eat" (1 Corinthians 11:20, ESV). He explains why: "For in eating, each one goes ahead with his own meal. One goes hungry, another gets drunk" (1 Corinthians 11:21, ESV). Then his temper shows: "What! Do you not have houses to eat and drink in? Or do you despise the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing?" (1 Corinthians 11:22, ESV). They had turned the meal meant to make them one body into a display of who mattered. When Paul warns against eating "without discerning the body" (1 Corinthians 11:29, ESV), whatever else the phrase has come to carry, in its setting it includes failing to see the hungry brother at the same table as part of Christ's body.

This is the distinction the essay turns on. Charity as distribution moves food from those who have it to those who don't. It can be generous and life-saving, and it leaves giver and receiver where they were, on two sides of a counter. The table as communion seats them together, eating the same food under the same host, and makes them one household for as long as the meal lasts. Acts 6 shows distribution going wrong inside a communion; Corinth shows communion corrupted into a stage for inequality. Wirzba presses the point from the sacrament: the Eucharist, he argues, sets the pattern for all Christian eating, because those who receive Christ's self-giving life are drawn into it and become nourishment for others.

For a long time the church kept something of the two together. During the Cappadocian famine of about 368, Basil of Caesarea gathered the hungry of the region, and Gregory of Nazianzus, in his funeral oration for his friend, remembered him not merely funding the relief but serving the famished with his own hands. Yet the separation had already begun. By the third century the Eucharist was generally celebrated apart from a full meal, and the love feast, or agape, carried on beside it as a charity supper. A regional synod at Laodicea, usually dated to about 363 or 364, then ruled in its twenty-eighth canon that love feasts were not to be held in churches. Over time the Supper became a rite without a meal, care for the hungry became a ministry beside the rite, and the two drifted into the separate rooms where most of our churches keep them now.

## Why Charity Is Necessary, and Why It Is Not Enough

None of that is an argument against the food pantry, and its case deserves full strength first. A family that can't make it to Friday needs groceries on Thursday, and no theology of the table feeds them in the meantime. Jesus's picture of the judgment doesn't praise the righteous for their analysis. "For I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me" (Matthew 25:35, ESV). They are surprised to learn whom they served; they had simply fed people. James is plainer still: "and one of you says to them, 'Go in peace, be warmed and filled,' without giving them the things needed for the body, what good is that?" (James 2:16, ESV). A church that sneers at the pantry as a bandage has forgotten that bandages stop bleeding. The volunteers who unload the truck have obeyed a direct command while the rest of us talked about systems.

The limits are real, too. Poppendieck argued that emergency food is often insufficient, unstable and nutritionally thin, that it frequently costs its users their dignity, and, harder still, that because charity satisfies the giver it can serve a whole society as a kind of moral safety valve, letting comfortable people feel hunger is handled while its causes go unexamined. Steve Corbett and Brian Fikkert, writing for evangelical churches in *When Helping Hurts* (2009), distinguished relief, the right answer to an emergency, from the rehabilitation and development chronic need requires, and warned that relief offered long after the emergency has passed confirms the helper's pride and the receiver's shame. Robert Lupton, in *Toxic Charity* (2011), argued that one-way giving repeated indefinitely erodes the recipient's agency, and commended food cooperatives that neighbors pay into and help run. These critiques can be overstated; no hungry child is dignified by being denied a sandwich while adults debate development theory. But they name what distribution can't do alone. It moves calories across a counter. It doesn't make a household.

The argument also has to face the two ways American Christians usually frame hunger. The framing more common on the right says hunger is best met close to home, by people who know the person in front of them, and that large public programs can weaken work, family and voluntary institutions; there is a real principle there, the old conviction that care belongs first to the nearest competent hands. The framing more common on the left says hunger in a rich country is a policy outcome, produced by wages, prices, housing costs and the design of public assistance, and that is also true; decisions made far from the pantry line, from the appraisal maps of the 1930s to the welfare reform law of 1996, shaped who stands in it.

Apply one instrument to both, the question Paul put to Corinth: do they discern the body? The right-leaning framing is tempted to treat the hungry neighbor as a test of personal responsibility, kept at the far end of a transaction that displays the giver's virtue. The left-leaning framing is tempted to treat him as evidence for a structural argument, kept at the far end of a debate that displays the advocate's righteousness, often while looking down on pantry volunteers it has never joined. One manages the poor through charity, the other through policy, and both can do it without ever sharing a meal. That's the Corinthian failure, and it isn't partisan.

So sort the disagreement honestly. First-order, binding on every Christian: the hungry bear God's image, and a church able to feed its neighbor and unwilling to has left the floor of the faith. Second-order, where faithful Christians divide: how much of the answer belongs to markets, government and church. Third-order: work requirements, benefit levels, subsidy designs. Christians can argue the second and third for a lifetime and remain family, but no one may use the difficulty of the second as an anesthetic for the first.

In an ordinary congregation, faithfulness can look modest. Some churches have made their pantries client-choice pantries, arranged like a small store, so people pick their own food instead of taking a box packed by strangers. Some have planted community gardens where neighbors who need food and neighbors who don't work the same beds. Some have opened the Wednesday supper to anyone, so that the cooks and the people who couldn't afford groceries that week sit down together and nobody can tell from the plates who is which. None of these replaces emergency food or public assistance. Each moves a little from distribution toward communion. If you need food yourself, calling 211 in most of the United States will connect you with help nearby, and there is no shame in using it.

## Who Decides What Grows

Underneath the pantry line lies a question most American congregations never ask, and the world's rural poor asked it first. La Vía Campesina, an international movement of peasants, small farmers and landless workers formed in 1993, put forward the idea of food sovereignty at the World Food Summit in Rome in 1996. In a form its defenders would sign: "food security" asks whether people have enough to eat, while food sovereignty asks who controls the system that decides what grows, by whose hands and for whose table. It claims for communities and nations the right to shape their own food and farming policy, to protect local growers from being undersold by subsidized imports, and to keep land, water and seed with the people who work them. The case most often cited is Haiti, which in the mid-1990s, under pressure tied to international lending, slashed its tariff on imported rice; subsidized American rice flooded in and Haitian rice farming was badly undercut. In March 2010 Bill Clinton told the Senate Foreign Relations Committee that pressing that policy on Haiti, which he had backed as president, had been a mistake.

The strongest objection deserves the same fairness: the modern agricultural system has fed more people than any arrangement in history. Norman Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 for the high-yield wheat at the heart of the Green Revolution, which is widely credited with sparing hundreds of millions from famines once predicted for South Asia. Critics argue that romanticizing small farms is a luxury of the well fed, and that tying the rural poor to low-yield subsistence could trap them in the very hunger the movement opposes. That is a serious reading of the famine record, not greed in disguise.

The economist Amartya Sen helps both sides see further. In *Poverty and Famines* (1981) he argued that starvation is a matter of some people not having enough food to eat, not of there being too little food, and showed that famines have struck where food was available because some group lost its ability to command food, through lost wages or shifting prices. His subject was famine, not the American pantry line, but the logic of access over supply describes that line closely.

A Christian can grant much to food sovereignty and still question its central word. Scripture gives sovereignty over food neither to the market nor to "the people." "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof, the world and those who dwell therein" (Psalm 24:1, ESV). Food is first his gift: "You cause the grass to grow for the livestock and plants for man to cultivate, that he may bring forth food from the earth" (Psalm 104:14, ESV). We are guests at that table, never its owners, which cuts against the commodity logic that treats food as one more thing to sell and against any movement that makes a nation or class the final judge of the earth's use. Who decides is the right question. The Christian answer is that the table has a Host, and every human arrangement is judged by whether his guests are fed.

Wendell Berry brought the question home to the ordinary eater in "The Pleasures of Eating" (1989). Eating, he insisted, is an agricultural act, and the industrial food economy profits when eaters become passive consumers who neither know nor care where their food comes from or what it cost the land. His counsel was concrete: grow some of your food, cook it, learn where the rest comes from, buy from local farmers where you can, and find that the pleasure of eating deepens as you understand the lives and places that feed you. A church that plants a garden won't change a trade regime, but it will train a people to see that food is given before it is sold, and that those who eat are joined to those who grow and those who go without.

## The Supper and the Line Outside

Come back to the side door. The same building usually holds two tables. On Sunday there is the Lord's Table, where the congregation remembers that Jesus "took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, 'This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me'" (Luke 22:19, ESV). On Thursday there is a folding table in the hall with boxes on it. Most of us call the first worship and the second outreach. The Bible won't let those tables stay apart. The bread on the Sunday table is the body of a man accused of eating with sinners, who seated a hungry crowd on the grass as guests, and who was broken so that people with nothing to offer could be fed without price. Corinth can happen in any church that serves the second table faithfully and never seats its guests where the members eat.

Repentance begins with people like me. We have been generous in the way that keeps us in charge, giving what we could spare, at hours that suited us, across a counter that kept everyone in place, and we have called it the whole of obedience because it was real obedience as far as it went. It didn't go to the table. Dorothy Day, who founded the Catholic Worker with Peter Maurin in 1933, opened its houses of hospitality during the Depression and lived among the people she served, ended her memoir *The Long Loneliness* (1952) by saying, in effect, that she had come to know God and her neighbors in the breaking of bread. She was describing a meal, not a program.

The prophets saw where the story ends. "On this mountain the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food, a feast of well-aged wine, of rich food full of marrow, of aged wine well refined" (Isaiah 25:6, ESV). That feast will have no line outside a side door and will not sort the guests by what they brought. Every Lord's Supper is a small, stubborn rehearsal of it, a meal where the only qualification for a place is hunger for what the Host alone can give. The church that eats that bread is saying, whether it means to or not, that this is the household it belongs to.

The pantry should stay open, and the policy arguments should go on in good faith. The question that remains is smaller and harder than either. It's whether the people who go without will ever be seated with us, not as a project and not as an audience, but as guests of the same Lord at the same table. The prayer Jesus taught us is plural. Somewhere near your church this week, the rest of the "us" will be standing in line.
