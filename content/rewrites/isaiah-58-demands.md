Every January a good number of American churches call their people to a season of prayer and fasting, and plenty of Americans who never enter a church start fasting anyway, skipping breakfast on a schedule and counting the hours on an app. From inside a kitchen the two fasts look much alike. Someone in the house gives something up, counts the days and waits for a result: a clearer head and a looser belt in one case, an answered prayer in the other. Both are usually sincere, and both tend to assume that a fast is something you do to yourself in order to get something.

Isaiah 58 was spoken to people who assumed exactly that, and it's the most searching word about fasting in Scripture. The people in it aren't frauds. They keep the appointed days, bow low and wear sackcloth, and they're bewildered that nothing happens: "Why have we fasted, and you see it not? Why have we humbled ourselves, and you take no knowledge of it?" (Isaiah 58:3, ESV). God's reply is usually heard either as a lesson in fasting with the right attitude or as a dismissal of religious practice, as though fasting were the problem and activism the cure. The chapter says something harder than either.

It turns on a play of words English can't quite carry. "Humbled ourselves" is the old liturgical phrase for afflicting the soul, the language of the Day of Atonement. A few verses later God tells them to "satisfy the desire of the afflicted" (Isaiah 58:10), and "afflicted" comes from the same root. They had afflicted their own souls. God asked them to relieve the afflicted soul of someone else. **The fast God chooses isn't less than the fast they kept but a fast turned outward, in which what the body gives up becomes someone else's release, and the whole chapter, down to the Sabbath verses at its end, is about the difference between those two kinds of hunger.**

Many people who have left the church could tell you why this matters. They learned early that the most devout person they knew was also the hardest to work for, or never forgave a debt, or spoke of the poor at dinner with contempt. Isaiah 58 takes their side before it takes anyone else's. It doesn't take their conclusion that the practices are empty. It asks what the practices were for.

## A fast in a city that hadn't been rebuilt

In 1892 Bernhard Duhm argued in *Das Buch Jesaia* that chapters 56 to 66 of Isaiah came from a later hand than chapters 40 to 55, and most critical scholars still call them Third Isaiah, addressed to the community that came back from Babylon after Cyrus let the exiles return in 538 BC. Conservative scholars such as J. Alec Motyer (*The Prophecy of Isaiah*, 1993) and John Oswalt (1998) defend the book's unity under Isaiah of Jerusalem, hearing in these chapters the prophet speaking to his own people and, beyond them, to generations he would not see. The creeds don't settle the question, and faithful readers differ. This essay reads the chapter against the returned community because that is where the Bible's own record of disputed fasts lies, in Zechariah and Nehemiah, and the fit is close whichever view of authorship a reader holds: a struggling people, living among ruins in a small province of the Persian Empire.

That community fasted, and we know why. The Babylonians had burned the temple in the fifth month of 587 BC (2 Kings 25:8-9), and the governor Gedaliah was murdered in the seventh month (2 Kings 25:25), and the survivors kept those anniversaries with fasting for generations. In 518 BC, with the second temple going up again, a delegation from Bethel asked the priests and prophets whether they should still mourn and abstain in the fifth month (Zechariah 7:1-3). God first asked whether those fasts had ever been for him (Zechariah 7:5), a question the essay on [Lent and fasting](/writing/why-lent) takes up. Then he repeated what the earlier prophets had said.

"Thus says the LORD of hosts, Render true judgments, show kindness and mercy to one another," (Zechariah 7:9).

"do not oppress the widow, the fatherless, the sojourner, or the poor, and let none of you devise evil against another in your heart." (Zechariah 7:10).

A community asks about its fasts, and God answers about its neighbors. Zechariah goes on to promise that the fasts will one day become feasts (Zechariah 8:19), but the road from the fast to the feast ran through the widow and the sojourner.

The social facts behind that answer are plain enough. Harvests were poor (Haggai 1:6), and Persian taxes were due regardless. Around 445 BC Nehemiah found families mortgaging fields and vineyards to buy grain, borrowing to pay the king's tax, and handing their children over as debt slaves to their own countrymen. Their protest contains a phrase worth keeping: "Now our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers, our children are as their children." (Nehemiah 5:5). Nehemiah made the nobles stop exacting interest and give back the fields (Nehemiah 5:6-13). Whatever the exact date of Isaiah 58, it speaks into this world, where those who fasted most visibly were often the ones holding the notes.

Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic* (1975) read Third Isaiah as the literature of a bitter struggle inside the restored community. His reconstruction of the parties claims more than the evidence can bear, but he saw rightly that these chapters speak to insiders. The section opens: "Thus says the LORD: 'Keep justice, and do righteousness, for soon my salvation will come, and my righteousness be revealed.'" (Isaiah 56:1). The next verse blesses the one "who keeps the Sabbath, not profaning it, and keeps his hand from doing any evil" (Isaiah 56:2). From its first lines Third Isaiah binds justice, worship and the Sabbath together. Chapter 58 is where the binding is tested.

## The complaint of a sincere people

The chapter opens by telling the prophet to lift up his voice "like a trumpet" and declare the people's sins (Isaiah 58:1). The trumpet is the *shofar*, and its most solemn blast is set in the law: "Then you shall sound the loud trumpet on the tenth day of the seventh month. On the Day of Atonement you shall sound the trumpet throughout all your land." (Leviticus 25:9). That trumpet announced the Jubilee, the year of release, on the day Israel fasted for its sins. No one can prove Isaiah 58 was written for that day, but the law had already joined the fast and the release, and the chapter joins them again.

What follows is one of the fairest portraits of religious people in the prophets. "Yet they seek me daily and delight to know my ways, as if they were a nation that did righteousness and did not forsake the judgment of their God; they ask of me righteous judgments; they delight to draw near to God." (Isaiah 58:2). The words "as if" carry the verdict, but notice what God concedes first. They seek him every day and enjoy being near him. The problem isn't that their devotion is fake. It's that their devotion is real and doesn't reach the rest of their lives.

Then their complaint and God's answer share a single verse: "'Why have we fasted, and you see it not? Why have we humbled ourselves, and you take no knowledge of it?' Behold, in the day of your fast you seek your own pleasure, and oppress all your workers." (Isaiah 58:3). Three words carry the weight.

"Humbled ourselves" is the Day of Atonement's phrase: "And it shall be a statute to you forever that in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, you shall afflict yourselves and shall do no work, either the native or the stranger who sojourns among you." (Leviticus 16:29). The statute joined the fast to a rest from work that covered everyone in the household, the foreigner included. The fasters kept the first half and broke the second. "Pleasure" means your own business, what you want, and it will return at the chapter's end. And "oppress" is the verb Exodus uses for Pharaoh's taskmasters (Exodus 5:6) and Deuteronomy for a creditor pressing a debtor, who in the year of release "shall not exact it of his neighbor, his brother" (Deuteronomy 15:2). The noun for "workers" is rare, and some older translations read it as the labors owed rather than the laborers, but either way someone is being driven to produce or to pay on the day of the fast. The people complaining that God doesn't see them are acting like the Egyptians God once saw very clearly: "Then the LORD said, 'I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings,'" (Exodus 3:7). God hadn't stopped seeing. He was looking at the workers.

The fast itself had become a quarrel: "Behold, you fast only to quarrel and to fight and to hit with a wicked fist. Fasting like yours this day will not make your voice to be heard on high." (Isaiah 58:4). Then God mocks the posture: bowing the head like a reed, spreading sackcloth and ashes, and calling it a day acceptable to the LORD (Isaiah 58:5). A reed bends in the wind and springs back when it passes. The posture was right, and nothing behind it had moved.

The fasters weren't lazy about God; they were busy about him, and the busyness had become a place where the neighbor's claim couldn't reach them. I've preached this chapter, and I've led a congregation in worship while poverty and injustice went on just outside our walls, untouched by what we did inside them. The fasters' question is one the church keeps asking in its own way: we did the religious thing, so why hasn't it worked? The answer is embarrassing because it's so close. God was paying attention the whole time. He was looking at the people we weren't.

## Bonds, straps and yokes: the language of debt

Then God names the fast he wants, in two verses quoted so often their edges have worn smooth.

"Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke?" (Isaiah 58:6).

"Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house; when you see the naked, to cover him, and not to hide yourself from your own flesh?" (Isaiah 58:7).

The first verse is often preached as a picture of inner freedom, and the Hebrew won't let that be the whole meaning. The word for "bonds" is rare and means tight cords or fetters; by itself it says nothing about debt, and no one should build a sermon on its etymology. The "straps of the yoke" fastened the bar to a working animal's neck, and the yoke was Israel's plainest image of forced labor, from Egypt (Leviticus 26:13) to Solomon's heavy yoke on his workers (1 Kings 12:4). Most precise of all, "let the oppressed go free" joins the verb "send" to the word *chofshi*, "free," which is the legal formula for releasing a debt slave: "If your brother, a Hebrew man or a Hebrew woman, is sold to you, he shall serve you six years, and in the seventh year you shall let him go free from you." (Deuteronomy 15:12).

The Greek translators of Isaiah, working probably in Egypt in the second century before Christ, heard it the same way. Where the Hebrew says to undo the straps of the yoke, the Septuagint speaks of loosing the knots of coerced contracts; where it says to break every yoke, the Greek says to tear up every unjust bond, the written note of debt. Moshe Weinfeld showed in *Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East* (1995) that ancient Near Eastern kings proclaimed edicts of release, cancelling debts and freeing debt slaves, and that Israel's law belongs to that world while changing its logic. In Babylon release was a king's occasional favor. In Israel it was written into the calendar by the God who owned the land. The seventh year and the Jubilee have [an essay of their own](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded).

So verse 6 asks creditors to release debtors and masters to free the bound, and verse 7 moves from the structure to the table: bread, a roof, clothing. The first undoes what should never have been done, and the second gives what no law could compel. The essay on [charity and justice](/writing/charity-is-not-justice-the-difference) draws that distinction at length. Isaiah refuses to choose between them.

The last clause is the one most readers skip. In Hebrew idiom "flesh" means kin. Judah, urging his brothers to sell Joseph, says "he is our brother, our own flesh" (Genesis 37:27), the family at its worst, selling its own flesh for silver. Nehemiah's debtors were making the same claim: the people you are pressing are your family. The phrase widens to the whole covenant people and finally to every bearer of God's image, but it starts close. The fast God chooses begins at home.

The promises then answer the complaint. The people who asked to be noticed are promised light breaking like the dawn and the glory of the LORD as their rear guard (Isaiah 58:8), the exodus glory on every side. "Then you shall call, and the LORD will answer; you shall cry, and he will say, 'Here I am.' If you take away the yoke from your midst, the pointing of the finger, and speaking wickedness," (Isaiah 58:9). God answers their complaint with "Here I am," and beside it stands "the pointing of the finger," literally "sending out the finger," the same verb as "let the oppressed go free." You can send the oppressed out into freedom, or you can send out the finger in contempt, and the chapter counts the second as a yoke too, one that costs a creditor nothing and still binds.

Verse 10 completes the wordplay: "If you pour yourself out for the hungry and satisfy the desire of the afflicted, then shall your light rise in the darkness and your gloom be as the noonday." (Isaiah 58:10). Where the ESV has "pour yourself out," the Hebrew has "your soul": offer your soul to the hungry. The fasters afflicted their own souls and asked God to notice; he asks them to pour their souls out for the afflicted. It's the same self-denial with a different address. And the promise that follows was meant literally for people living in rubble: "And your ancient ruins shall be rebuilt; you shall raise up the foundations of many generations; you shall be called the repairer of the breach, the restorer of streets to dwell in." (Isaiah 58:12). The walls would be rebuilt by a people who had stopped pressing each other, and the name God gives them is a builder's name.

## Why a chapter about justice ends with the Sabbath

Then, in its last two verses, the chapter turns to the Sabbath.

"If you turn back your foot from the Sabbath, from doing your pleasure on my holy day, and call the Sabbath a delight and the holy day of the LORD honorable; if you honor it, not going your own ways, or seeking your own pleasure, or talking idly;" (Isaiah 58:13).

"then you shall take delight in the LORD, and I will make you ride on the heights of the earth; I will feed you with the heritage of Jacob your father, for the mouth of the LORD has spoken." (Isaiah 58:14).

Many critical commentators have judged these verses a later addition, and the change of subject is real. But someone put them here, and Brevard Childs argued in his commentary (2001) that Isaiah should be read in the shape its final editors gave it. Read that way, the Sabbath verses are the chapter's conclusion, and the vocabulary shows it. Verse 3 accused the fasters of seeking their "pleasure" on the fast day; verse 13 uses the word twice. The chapter opens and closes on the same charge, a holy day turned into your own business. And the pleasure they sought for themselves gives way to a delight received: "call the Sabbath a delight," "take delight in the LORD."

The deeper link is what the Sabbath was for. Deuteronomy gives the command a household's reason: no one works, not the son or daughter, the servants, the animals or the sojourner, "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (Deuteronomy 5:14), because you were slaves in Egypt and the LORD brought you out (Deuteronomy 5:15). The Sabbath was the weekly form of the release the seventh year and the Jubilee carried out on a larger scale. Once a week nobody in the house belonged to anyone's schedule but God's. A people who kept it honestly couldn't grind their workers for six days and call the seventh faithfulness, and a people who kept the fast of verse 6 would find the Sabbath of verse 13 the natural shape of their week.

The returned community broke the link as easily as we do. Nehemiah found Jerusalem treading winepresses and trading fish on the Sabbath, and shut the gates at dusk to stop the market (Nehemiah 13:15-22), as Amos had heard merchants wishing the Sabbath over so they could return to their rigged scales (Amos 8:5; see [the essay on Amos](/writing/amos-american-church)). Both the Sabbath and the fast of justice refuse to let the market decide what a person is worth. The fuller theology of the day, and the honest Christian disagreements about how it binds us, are in [What Is the Sabbath](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it).

The final promise is about land. "The heritage of Jacob" is the *nachalah*, the family inheritance held in trust from God, the very thing the poor had lost to the rich in Nehemiah 5. The heritage of Jacob can only feed a people who have stopped taking it from one another.

## The line Jesus added in Nazareth

Luke tells us that Jesus, not long after forty days of fasting in the wilderness, stood up in the Nazareth synagogue on the Sabbath and read from Isaiah (Luke 4:1-2, 16-17).

"The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty those who are oppressed," (Luke 4:18).

"to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor." (Luke 4:19).

Most of this is Isaiah 61. The reading stops before "the day of vengeance of our God" (Isaiah 61:2), and one line comes from somewhere else: "to set at liberty those who are oppressed" is Isaiah 58:6 in its Greek wording. Whether Jesus joined the passages as he read or Luke joined them in the telling, the manifesto of Jesus' ministry has the fast God chooses sewn into it. Then he sat down and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing." (Luke 4:21).

The thread is the Greek *aphesis*, release, used twice in verse 18. In the Greek Old Testament it names the Jubilee and the seventh-year release of debts; in Luke it is also the word for the forgiveness of sins (Luke 1:77; 3:3; 24:47). John Howard Yoder argued in *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) that Nazareth announced a Jubilee, a social program his followers were meant to live. His critics answer, fairly, that Luke never shows Jesus cancelling a debt, that the church is not a state, and that Luke's *aphesis* leans toward forgiveness. The Lord's Prayer refuses to make them choose: "and forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone who is indebted to us." (Luke 11:4). Forgiveness of sins and release of debts are one release, received from God and passed on. The [kingdom Jesus announced](/writing/what-is-the-kingdom-of-god-and-why-it-changes-everything) starts there.

The early church kept the fast this way. Aristides, defending the Christians to a Roman emperor in the second century, said they would fast two or three days when one of their number was poor and they had nothing to spare, so that he could eat. The Shepherd of Hermas, written in Rome in the same century, counsels eating only bread and water on a fast day, reckoning what the day's food would have cost, and giving that to a widow, an orphan or someone in need. In Lent of 387, with Antioch waiting in dread for the emperor's reprisal after a riot, John Chrysostom told his people that the honor of fasting lies not in going without food but in going without sin, since nothing is gained by abstaining from meat while biting and devouring one another, Paul's image from Galatians 5:15. Leo the Great, preaching in Rome between 440 and 461, repeatedly bound the church's seasonal fasts to almsgiving and its collections for the poor.

The synagogue reads the chapter the same way. The Talmud (Megillah 31a) names the passage beginning "For thus says the One who is high and lifted up" (Isaiah 57:15) as the prophetic reading on the morning of Yom Kippur, and synagogues read on through 58:14, so that the people hear, while fasting, that God rejects a fast that oppresses workers. They take it not as a reason to stop fasting but as the fast's conscience. The Mishnah (Taanit 2:1) describes public fasts in drought when an elder reminded the people that Scripture says God saw not the sackcloth and fasting of Nineveh but its deeds, how they turned from their evil way (Jonah 3:10).

## Fasting now: discipline, diet or technique

One popular reading of Isaiah 58 ends fasting altogether: God wants justice, not an empty stomach. But God never tells the fasters to stop. He tells them what the fast is. Jesus said "when you fast" (Matthew 6:16), and the church in Acts fasted before sending out its first missionaries (Acts 13:2-3). Using the chapter to abolish the practice keeps the neighbor and loses what the fast was teaching.

That teaching deserves its strongest defense. Richard Foster's *Celebration of Discipline* (1978) did as much as any book of its generation to return fasting to ordinary Protestant life. Foster insisted that fasting must center on God, and he argued that it brings to the surface what controls us: when food or comfort is withdrawn, the anger and anxiety it was covering come up where they can be confessed. The fasters of Isaiah 58, quarreling on the fast day, had been shown what was in them and hadn't looked. What Isaiah adds is direction. The fast that reveals what controls me should end by releasing someone I've been controlling.

The modern pull runs toward the fast as technique. One form is the diet, whose health claims are a matter for doctors; when a Christian fast drifts into it, the self stays at the center and the day's savings go to the self. The other is spiritual: the fast sold as a method for getting a breakthrough, as if God's attention were a lock and hunger the key. That's the fasters' own theology in verse 3. Scot McKnight's *Fasting* (2009) argues that in Scripture people fast in response to something grievous, not to make something happen. Isaiah asks, further, where the fast goes.

Both our tribes have a version of the rejected fast. Churches call days of prayer and fasting for national revival while members' wages and talk about the poor go untouched, and the pointing finger of verse 9 is at home in sermons that treat poverty as a failure of character. Others keep the language of justice, drop the humbled soul and the Sabbath as relics, and pursue their causes with a contempt that looks like "to quarrel and to fight." The failures aren't identical, but the chapter was written to insiders, and it has to be read first against our own.

This isn't medical advice. Anyone with a history of eating disorders shouldn't fast from food, and anyone pregnant, diabetic, on medication, frail or young should ask a doctor first. The fast of Isaiah 58 can be kept without skipping a meal. If fasting has become a way to punish yourself, or you're thinking of harming yourself, tell someone and call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

## The fast at our own table

For a household the chapter's questions are concrete. The cleaner, the sitter and the yard crew are the "workers" of verse 3 in a modern kitchen, and the fast asks what they're paid and when (the essay on [fair wages](/writing/theology-fair-wages) makes the biblical case). A family loan can become a yoke carried through every holiday long after the money stopped mattering. The "flesh" we hide from may be a parent in a care home or a brother who embarrasses us, and the children at the table learn about the pointing finger from how the adults talk about the poor. Hermas's rule is still open to any family: eat simply one day a week and give what it saved, preferably to someone whose name you know.

I count myself among the fasters. We aren't hypocrites in the cartoon sense; we're sincere people whose sincerity has found a room the neighbor can't enter. The chapter's first gift is to name that room.

Its second gift is a few chapters back. "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth." (Isaiah 53:7). "Oppressed" is the taskmaster's verb from 58:3, and "afflicted" is the root of the fasters' humbled souls. The Servant stands where the workers stood, afflicted not to win God's notice but for others, and he "poured out his soul to death" (Isaiah 53:12). The Hebrew verb differs from 58:10, but the picture is the one the fast was asking for.

Christians confess that Servant is Jesus, who fasted forty days, read the line about the oppressed in Nazareth, and carried on a cross every yoke we'd fastened on each other and every debt we owed God. That is why the chapter isn't only a verdict. We don't keep the true fast to be freed; we keep it because we've been freed, as Israel's servants rested because Israel remembered it had been a slave in Egypt. And the fast isn't forever. Zechariah promised the fast days would become feasts, and the resurrection was the first of them.

Until then the fasters' question hangs over every church that fasts: why have we fasted, and you see it not? God has seen all along. He has been looking at the worker unpaid on the fast day, the brother whose note was never torn up, the relative no one visits, the person pointed at across a table. The ruins are still there, and so is the name offered to anyone who picks up a stone. Most of us already know where the nearest breach is.
