Most people who use the word "mystic" now mean nearly the opposite of what the church meant by it for most of its history. The word has come to describe someone who has gotten past religion to a raw experience of the sacred that every tradition shares and none of them owns. For some skeptics that settles the matter: the experiences are the real thing, and the doctrines, sacraments and churches are husks around them. For some Christians it settles the matter the other way: mysticism is what people reach for when they've stopped believing anything in particular.

Both readings grab something true. There are people who use the mystics to get free of the creeds, and there are churches that treat any talk of God's presence as a slide toward the vague. But both share an assumption neither has examined, which is that "mystical" means a private, extraordinary experience that stands on its own. That meaning is a few centuries old in a tradition of twenty, and it has hidden the mystics from most of the people who now talk about them.

I write as a Protestant pastor, a member of the tradition that misplaced them. For most evangelicals the mystics aren't taught in Sunday school and aren't quoted from pulpits, including, for too long, mine. We have been rich in arguments for God and poor in any language for waiting on him, and I don't exempt myself from that sentence. Read in their own settings, and given their strongest critics a full hearing, the great Christian mystics turn out to be something other than escapees from doctrine. They are its hardest school, and the sternest critics of what our age calls mysticism.

## What "mystical" meant before it meant private experience

The word comes from the Greek *mystikos*, kin to *mysterion* and to a verb for closing the eyes or lips. In the pagan world it belonged to the mystery cults, whose initiates kept silent about the rites. The New Testament turned the word inside out. For Paul a mystery is not a secret kept from outsiders but a secret God has now told: "the mystery hidden for ages and generations but now revealed to his saints" (Colossians 1:26, ESV). The next verse names it: "To them God chose to make known how great among the Gentiles are the riches of the glory of this mystery, which is Christ in you, the hope of glory" (Colossians 1:27, ESV). The mystery is public, addressed to Gentiles, which is to say to anybody. What is hidden about it is not its content but its depth.

That is how the early church used the adjective. The French Oratorian Louis Bouyer traced the word through the fathers in an essay first published in 1949 and translated as "Mysticism: An Essay on the History of the Word" (1980), and returned to it at length in *The Christian Mystery* (French 1986). He found that "mystical" first described things held in common, and in three senses: biblical, liturgical and spiritual. There was the mystical sense of Scripture, the meaning of the Old Testament that comes to light in Christ, which Origen and his successors labored to draw out. There was the mystical reality of the sacraments, Christ present under the water of baptism and the bread and cup of the Eucharist. And from these two came the third, the believer's own knowledge of God, which the fathers expected to grow from hearing the Scriptures and sharing the table.

Bernard McGinn, in *The Foundations of Mysticism* (1991), the first volume of the standard history in English, prefers to speak of a mystical element within Christianity rather than of mysticism as a thing on its own, and he describes that element as whatever in Christian belief and practice concerns preparing for, becoming conscious of, and responding to the immediate presence of God. The key word is presence, not unusual states, because so many of the mystics distrusted unusual states. The noun "mysticism," naming a special kind of experience and a special kind of person, came late. Michel de Certeau showed in *The Mystic Fable* (1982) that it was in seventeenth-century France that *la mystique* hardened into a field of its own, a discourse about extraordinary inner states somewhat apart from word and sacrament.

The Reformers still spoke the older language. John Calvin wrote in the 1559 *Institutes* (III.11.10) that he gave the highest importance to the joining of Head and members, Christ dwelling in our hearts, which he was content to call a mystical union. He wrote it while answering Andreas Osiander, who taught that we are righteous by the indwelling of Christ's divine "essential righteousness," and Calvin insisted on a union without any mingling of God's being with ours. He meant the union every believer has with Christ by the Spirit through faith, not an experience reserved for an elite.

This is the distinction everything else turns on. There is *the mystical* in the old sense: the hidden depth of what God has given openly to the whole church in Scripture, sacrament and the life of faith. And there is *mysticism* in the modern sense: an inner experience treated as its own warrant, available apart from any church, and often taken to be the true core all religions share. The great Christian mystics belong to the first. Most arguments about them, for and against, are really arguments about the second.

## Dionysius, the Cloud and the way of unknowing

The strand of the tradition most often called mystical grew from one scriptural image. At Sinai, "The people stood far off, while Moses drew near to the thick darkness where God was" (Exodus 20:21, ESV). Gregory of Nyssa, in *The Life of Moses* (around 390), read that climb as the shape of the Christian life: the soul begins in light when it first turns from falsehood, and the nearer it comes to God the darker things grow, because it is nearing what no mind can contain.

Around the year 500 a Syrian Christian gave that instinct its most influential form. He wrote under the name of the Athenian convert in Acts: "But some men joined him and believed, among whom also were Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris and others with them" (Acts 17:34, ESV). The West read him as Paul's own disciple for a thousand years, until humanists such as Lorenzo Valla and Erasmus doubted it; he is now called Pseudo-Dionysius. A writer steeped in late Neoplatonism had dressed his work in apostolic clothing.

His short treatise *The Mystical Theology* sets out two ways of speaking about God. The way of affirmation, called cataphatic, says God is good, wise, living and light, because Scripture says so. The way of negation, called apophatic, says God is not good as a creature is good, nor even "being" as a thing is. The second way does not cancel the first. It follows and purifies it: every true name for God is true, none is adequate, and the soul that has learned both falls silent before the God beyond both. Dionysius sets the whole treatise at Sinai, opening with Moses leaving the crowd behind and entering the darkness where God is. His title keeps the old sense: the hidden theology, concealed in Scripture and liturgy, which silence finally honors.

This can sound like sophisticated agnosticism. The apophatic way assumes that God has spoken. It refuses only to let our words about him become an idol we can handle. A God we could define exhaustively would be one more thing in the world, and the second commandment forbids exactly that. The apophatic tradition is the second commandment applied to concepts.

Some nine centuries later an anonymous English priest wrote this strand's most practical book. *The Cloud of Unknowing*, from the later fourteenth century, was written for a young man drawn to contemplative prayer, and its author very likely also made the English version of Dionysius known as *Deonise Hid Diuinite* ("Dionysius' Hidden Divinity"), a fair rendering of "mystical theology." Put everything, even your holiest thoughts, beneath a cloud of forgetting, he says; above you is a cloud of unknowing that no thought can pierce, and only love can reach God there. So he counsels a simple, naked intention of love toward God himself rather than his gifts, gathered if need be into one short word such as "God" or "love."

What modern readers miss is how fenced the book is. Its prologue asks that it not be passed to the merely curious, and that whoever reads it read the whole. The author assumes a reader who confesses his sins, receives the sacraments, knows the Scriptures and lives under direction. The cloud is not a place you reach by technique in an afternoon; it is the darkness around a life already formed by the church's ordinary means. The older story behind all this, the desert monks and their discipline of stillness, is told in [Who Were the Desert Fathers and Mothers, and Why Do They Matter?](/writing/why-people-fled-to-the-desert) and [What the Desert Fathers Teach About Silence and Solitude](/writing/silence-and-solitude). What the way of unknowing adds is a theology of why the silence is not empty. It answers a God whose self-disclosure is too full for any sentence to hold.

## Julian and Eckhart: a vision under the cross and a warning at the edge

Julian of Norwich was an anchoress, enclosed for life in a cell attached to the church of St Julian in Norwich; we don't know her birth name. In May 1373, at thirty and a half by her own account, she fell so ill that everyone expected her to die. A priest held a crucifix before her face, and as she looked at it she received sixteen showings, the first of them blood running down from under the crown of thorns. She wrote a short account and then pondered the showings for some twenty years before writing the long version we call *Revelations of Divine Love*, the earliest surviving book in English known to have been written by a woman.

Everything in Julian begins at the cross, though she is often read as a teacher of generic reassurance. In one early showing she saw something the size of a hazelnut in her palm and understood that it was all that is made. She wondered how it could last, and understood that it lasts because God made it, loves it and keeps it. That is the doctrine of creation seen rather than argued: the world does not hold itself up.

The famous line comes later and in a harder place. In the thirteenth showing Julian is looking at sin, wondering why God in his wisdom did not prevent it. The answer she receives is that sin was "behovely," an old word for fitting within God's purpose, and then the line T. S. Eliot set into "Little Gidding" in 1942: "All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well." It is spoken to a woman staring at the worst thing in the universe, and it doesn't explain sin away. Julian presses the obvious question: the church teaches that some will be lost, so how can all be well? She doesn't resolve it. She holds to the church's teaching and understands that God will do a great deed at the last day, whose manner will stay hidden until it is done. Read in context, the line is not a slogan. It is a promise about what God will do, received from the wounds of the crucified by someone who refused to pretend she could see how.

Meister Eckhart is a different case. A German Dominican born around 1260, master of theology at Paris and a celebrated preacher, he spoke in his vernacular sermons of the ground of the soul where God and the soul meet, of a detachment that lets go even of willing God's will in one's own way, and of the birth of the eternal Word in the soul. His phrasing was deliberately extreme; he could say that the eye in which he sees God and the eye in which God sees him are one eye. Read charitably and alongside his Latin works, much of this restates traditional teaching on grace boldly. Heard in a crowded church, some of it seemed to erase the line between Creator and creature.

The archbishop of Cologne opened proceedings against him in 1326. Eckhart appealed to the pope and died before the case was decided. On 27 March 1329 John XXII issued the bull *In agro dominico*, condemning twenty-eight propositions, some as heretical and others as suspect, among them the claim, which the bull attributed to him, that something in the soul is uncreated. The bull also records that he had submitted to the church's judgment before he died. Scholars still dispute how fairly the propositions represent him. But the episode shows what the modern romance of the mystic forgets: the tradition did not assume that anyone who spoke of union with God was right. It tested what was said against the faith, and it was willing to say no.

## Teresa, John and the tests the mystics set themselves

If Eckhart shows the church testing a mystic, Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross show mystics testing themselves, often more severely than their critics do. Teresa, born in 1515, spent nearly two decades in the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation in what she later called a divided and mediocre life of prayer. In 1562 she founded the small, poor convent of St Joseph's in Ávila to keep the Carmelite rule more strictly, and spent the rest of her life founding houses, fighting superiors and writing, while the Inquisition examined her autobiography. In 1970 Paul VI named her, with Catherine of Siena, among the first women Doctors of the Church.

In 1577 she wrote *The Interior Castle*, picturing the soul as a castle made of a single diamond or very clear crystal, with seven dwelling places and God at the center. The door is prayer. The book moves inward through quieter prayer and honestly described trials to what she calls the spiritual marriage, and it is as much a map of how easily we mistake a feeling for God.

Two of her tests deserve to be better known than her raptures. The first concerns Christ. In her *Life* and again in the sixth dwelling of the *Castle*, she recalls being taught that advanced souls should set aside every bodily image, even the humanity of Jesus, to rest in pure divinity. She tried it and came to count it a serious error: the sacred humanity is the door through which every blessing comes, and no one grows too spiritual for the man who was crucified. The second concerns the neighbor. We can't be sure whether we love God, she says in the fifth dwelling, but we can be much surer whether we love our neighbor, and that is the surest sign. The point of the spiritual marriage, she writes at the end, is that good works be born from it. John the apostle said the same: "No one has ever seen God; if we love one another, God abides in us and his love is perfected in us" (1 John 4:12, ESV).

John of the Cross, twenty-seven years younger, joined her reform and paid for it. Friars of his own order seized him in December 1577 and held him in a cell in Toledo until he escaped in August 1578. His dark night, treated with care in [Why Does God Feel Silent? Faith When God Seems Absent](/writing/when-god-is-silent-and-the-room-is-empty), meant a work of God stripping the soul of its dependence on felt consolation, which he distinguished from lukewarmness and from illness.

Less known is how hard John was on the experiences people now go to the mystics to find. In *The Ascent of Mount Carmel*, begun around 1579, he tells his readers not to seek visions, voices or revelations and not to rest in them when they come, since they can be counterfeited and even true ones become attachments. His reason is Christological. In giving his Son, his one Word, God has said everything at once, so asking him for new revelations offends him by looking away from Christ. John was reading the opening of Hebrews: "but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son, whom he appointed the heir of all things, through whom also he created the world" (Hebrews 1:2, ESV).

The mystic of the modern imagination goes past doctrine to experience. Teresa and John went through experience and came back insisting on Christ's humanity, on the neighbor, and on the Word already spoken. Denys Turner argued in *The Darkness of God* (1995) that modern readers impose an interest in special experiences on medieval writers who were critics of that interest, and that the apophatic tradition is closer to a discipline for distrusting our experiences of God than a technique for producing them. Teresa and John fit his case better than they fit the bookstore shelf.

## Is all mysticism one? William James, Huxley and Katz

The modern idea that all mystics secretly say the same thing begins with a great book. William James gave the Gifford Lectures in Edinburgh in 1901 and 1902 and published them as *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902). He proposed four marks of mystical states: they are ineffable, noetic (felt as knowledge), transient and passive. He concluded, carefully, that such states carry authority for those who have them but lay no duty on anyone else to accept their revelations uncritically.

Aldous Huxley went further. *The Perennial Philosophy* (1945) gathered Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist and Sufi sayings to argue that beneath the doctrines lies one teaching: a divine Ground of all being, knowable directly by the loving, pure in heart and poor in spirit. W. T. Stace gave the idea philosophical rigor in *Mysticism and Philosophy* (1960), arguing for a common core of experience that each tradition interprets in its own vocabulary.

The perennialist case is not foolish, and its motives are often generous. The resemblances are real. Dionysius's negations echo the Upanishads' "not this, not this"; Eckhart's detachment sounds like Zen, as D. T. Suzuki argued in *Mysticism: Christian and Buddhist* (1957). If one summit is reached by many paths, the religious wars were fought over maps, and humility means honoring the summit and holding the paths loosely. A skeptic drawn to that picture isn't being lazy. He is trying to be fair to the whole human race.

The strongest answer came from philosophy. In 1978 Steven Katz published "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism" in *Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis*, a volume he edited, arguing that there are no pure, unmediated experiences. The concepts, texts and practices a mystic brings shape not only the report but the experience itself. A Jewish mystic trained to expect God's unbridgeable holiness does not have a Buddhist experience and describe it in Jewish words; he has a Jewish experience. On this view the common core is an artifact of lifting descriptions out of their settings. Critics answered in turn: Robert Forman and his colleagues argued in *The Problem of Pure Consciousness* (1990) that some contemplative states empty the mind of content and so escape that shaping. The debate is still open.

Katz wasn't defending Christianity, and his argument cuts against any Christian who wants experience to prove God independently, since no experience comes uninterpreted. But it fits the older Christian sense of the mystical remarkably well. Julian's showings were of the crucified Christ, received while looking at a crucifix. Teresa's prayer passed through the humanity of Jesus. John measured every darkness by the Word spoken in the Son. None of them claimed to have gotten beneath the faith to a neutral core. They claimed that the God confessed in the faith had drawn them deeper into it. The perennialist wants the experience without the tradition; the great Christian mystics would have said that without the tradition there is no telling what the experience is of. Two people kneeling in two different houses can look exactly alike. Who they kneel to is the whole question.

## The Protestant case against mysticism, at full strength

My own tradition has a serious quarrel with the mystics, and some of the sharpest Protestant theologians read them closely and still said no. Luther is the complicated case. He published the *Theologia Germanica*, an anonymous fourteenth-century German mystical treatise, in 1516 and more fully in 1518, and praised it highly. By 1520, in *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church*, he was warning that Dionysius was more Platonist than Christian. When radicals of the 1520s claimed revelations of the Spirit that let them set the written Word aside, he hardened. In the Smalcald Articles (1537) he wrote that God gives his Spirit and grace to no one except through or with the external Word that comes first, and he traced every appeal to a Spirit apart from the Word back to the serpent in Eden. Paul grounded faith the same way: "So faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (Romans 10:17, ESV).

The twentieth century sharpened the charge. Emil Brunner's *Die Mystik und das Wort* (1924), aimed at Schleiermacher's theology of religious feeling, set mysticism and the Word against each other: one seeks God in the depths of the self, the other receives him as an address from outside it. Anders Nygren's *Agape and Eros* (1930 to 1936) read much of the mystical tradition, Dionysius among its chief examples, as Platonic eros baptized, the soul climbing toward God by stages, set against agape, God's unmotivated love descending to sinners. Karl Barth, in §17 of *Church Dogmatics* I/2 (1938), on revelation as the abolition of religion, treated religion as the human grasp at God that revelation judges, and read mysticism, alongside atheism, as religion trying to overcome its contradictions from within while remaining religion.

Put together, the case is strong. The gospel is news of what God did outside us, in history, and a spirituality of turning inward reverses the direction of grace. Ascent smuggles works back in under the name of prayer. The One beyond being owes more to Plotinus than to the God of Israel, who is known because he speaks. And private experience, once granted authority, will sooner or later sit in judgment over Scripture.

The critique is right about more than the mystics' admirers admit, and wrong about the best mystics themselves. Notice who agrees with it: John refusing new revelations, Teresa refusing a prayer that left Christ's humanity behind, Julian holding to the church's teaching past what her showings could explain, the Cloud's author assuming confession and the sacraments. The critics were largely arguing with mysticism in the modern sense, and the best of the tradition was arguing with it too.

Protestantism also has its own mystical tradition, though it seldom uses the name. John Owen's *Of Communion with God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost* (1657) is a treatise on the believer's distinct communion with each person of the Trinity, drawing freely on the Song of Songs. Jonathan Edwards preached in "A Divine and Supernatural Light" (published 1734) that the Spirit gives not new information but a new sense of the heart, the difference between believing honey is sweet and tasting it. In his *Personal Narrative*, written around 1739, he recalled reading Paul's doxology (in the King James wording), "To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen" (1 Timothy 1:17, ESV), and a sense of God's glory entering his soul unlike anything he had known. The same Edwards wrote *Religious Affections* (1746), the most careful Protestant treatise on testing religious experience, concluding that the chief sign of grace is not a feeling's intensity but a life of Christian practice. That is mystical in the old way: the hidden sweetness of the Word, tasted by the Spirit and proved in obedience.

## Scripture, creed and church: what the mystics can still teach us

The one time Paul describes what later ages would call a mystical experience of his own, he tells it almost against his will. Pressed by rivals in Corinth who boasted of visions, Paul speaks in the third person of a man caught up into the third heaven fourteen years before, who "heard things that cannot be told, which man may not utter" (2 Corinthians 12:4, ESV). He refuses to build on it. What follows is a thorn God would not remove, and the answer he got instead: "But he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.' Therefore I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (2 Corinthians 12:9, ESV). The canon's own mystic ends in weakness, and offers no method for reaching the third heaven.

The guardrails the mystics need are mostly the ones the best of them set for themselves. Scripture governs experience, never the reverse. The creeds mark the floor: the God met in the dark is the Father, Son and Spirit confessed in the Nicene Creed, the Word made flesh who rose bodily from the grave, not an impersonal Ground to which Christ is one door among many. And the church tests and holds the one who prays. Where those three are kept, the mystics are among the church's best teachers. Where they are dropped, what remains has only borrowed their vocabulary.

Kept there, they teach that knowing about God and knowing God differ, and that a church can do the first brilliantly for generations while neglecting the second. They teach that experience is the least reliable part of the spiritual life, a strange lesson from mystics and the one our age most needs. That falls hardest on people like me. We have filled our services so there is no silence to fear and given our people a great deal of true information about God, and very little help being still before him long enough to be changed.

To the skeptic, one honest word. You're right that experience cannot prove God, and the mystics agree, which is part of why they are worth reading. They do not claim a feeling in the dark establishes anything. They claim the God who made himself known in a man who could be touched and a death that can be dated is deeper than our arguments can hold.

The deepest darkness in the Christian story is not on Sinai or in a Carmelite cell. "And when the sixth hour had come, there was darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour" (Mark 15:33, ESV). Every Christian mystic worth reading ends there, where God was most hidden and most given at once, and Julian's "all shall be well" was spoken from that hill. Paul put it plainly: "For God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6, ESV). The light is in a face, and the face was crucified. We have made a great deal of noise about that God, much of it true. Whether we have ever waited in the dark long enough to be found by him is a question no essay can settle for anyone.
