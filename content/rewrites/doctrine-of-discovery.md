## A word in a Supreme Court footnote

In 2005 the Oneida Indian Nation of New York lost a tax case. It had bought back parcels of its old reservation, lost two centuries earlier, and argued they were exempt from the city of Sherrill's property taxes. In *City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York*, Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg, writing for the Court, held that too much time had passed for the nation to revive its sovereignty there. But in a footnote on the background of the case, the opinion explained how title to Indian land had worked from the start: under what it called the doctrine of discovery, ownership of the lands Native peoples occupied when the colonists arrived became vested in the sovereign, first the discovering European nation and later the states and the United States.

The doctrine of discovery is a legal principle with a theological birth certificate. It holds that a Christian sovereign whose subjects first reach lands not held by a Christian ruler acquires a superior title to them, and that the peoples already living there keep, at most, a right to occupy what they may no longer freely sell or govern. Its roots run through papal bulls issued between 1452 and 1493, through the patents of Protestant kings who rejected the pope and kept the principle, and into an 1823 opinion by Chief Justice John Marshall that still anchors the law of Native land in the United States.

Two accounts of this history circulate. One says the bulls belong to a vanished world, the churches have renounced them, and the Christian in the pew didn't sail with Columbus. The other says the doctrine shows what Christianity always was underneath its hymns, a religion of chosen peoples and promised lands. The first is right that guilt isn't inherited and that the repudiations are real. The second is right that the doctrine was no accident attached to the faith from outside. It was theology, written by churchmen and argued from Scripture.

What both accounts miss is a distinction on which the whole history turns. There is a difference between the authority to proclaim and the right to possess. The risen Christ gave his church the first, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (Matthew 28:18, ESV), and sent it to make disciples and to teach. He kept the second for himself. The doctrine of discovery was the moment the church mistook the one for the other: it took an authority that belongs to Christ alone, handed a share of it to a pope, and let the pope hand the earth to princes. That confusion is the sin to be named. It is also why the remedy isn't less Christianity but an older one.

## What the popes granted, 1452 to 1494

The story begins before 1492, in the canon law of the crusades. The legal historian Robert A. Williams Jr., in *The American Indian in Western Legal Thought* (1990), traces it to a thirteenth-century argument over whether unbelievers could lawfully own anything. Pope Innocent IV, writing as a canonist, held that they possess real dominion and property by natural law, though the pope might intervene when they violated that law. His contemporary Hostiensis, the canonist Henry of Segusio, argued that with the coming of Christ lawful jurisdiction passed to the faithful. The gentler view carried the schools. The harder one, Williams shows, kept surfacing whenever a Christian prince wanted a coast.

Portugal wanted a coast. By the 1440s its ships were bringing captives home from the Atlantic shore of Africa. The royal chronicler Gomes Eanes de Zurara described the sale of enslaved Africans at Lagos in 1444, grieving over the separation of families even as he thanked God for the souls who would now be saved. Willie James Jennings opens the first chapter of *The Christian Imagination* (2010) with that scene because it holds the whole problem in one frame: real Christian feeling, a theology of salvation and a slave market, with the theology blessing the market instead of stopping it.

The papacy gave the enterprise its legal form. In *Dum Diversas* (18 June 1452), Pope Nicholas V granted King Afonso V of Portugal authority to attack and subdue Saracens, pagans and other enemies of Christ, to seize their lands and goods, and to reduce their persons to perpetual servitude. In *Romanus Pontifex* (8 January 1455) he confirmed Portugal's exclusive claim to the African coast it had reached and what lay beyond, and forbade other Christian powers to enter those waters without Portuguese leave. The bull's language is mission. Its provisions concern trade, shipping and slaves.

When Columbus returned in 1493, Spain needed the same protection. Pope Alexander VI supplied it in several bulls that year, chief among them the second *Inter caetera* (4 May 1493), which granted Ferdinand and Isabella the lands found or yet to be found west of a line a hundred leagues beyond the Azores and Cape Verde islands, wherever no Christian prince already ruled. It expressed the hope that barbarous nations would be subdued and brought to the faith, and it bound the monarchs to send good and learned men to instruct them. Portugal objected, and in the Treaty of Tordesillas (7 June 1494) the two crowns moved the line themselves, to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese. Nobody on the far side of the line was asked anything.

The documents' defenders deserve their best case. The popes saw themselves as arbiters keeping Catholic kingdoms from war over the oceans, and the 1494 settlement was reached by treaty, not by fleets. The grants carried a real obligation to evangelize, which some friars honored at the cost of their lives. The medieval world assumed political authority needed a spiritual warrant, and the pope was its obvious source. None of this was written by men who thought themselves wicked.

That is exactly the difficulty. Christ claimed all authority and sent his disciples to teach and baptize; he gave them no title to anyone's field. The bulls ran that claim through a different chain, from Christ to Peter, from Peter to his successor, from the successor to a king and from the king to his captains, and at the end of it the commission to teach had become a license to possess. Baptism had become a property qualification. Whatever canonical label one gives it, that is a first-order error, because it rations the image of God by baptismal status (Genesis 1:27) and contradicts what Scripture says about who owns the earth: "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof, the world and those who dwell therein" (Psalm 24:1, ESV). The psalm puts the earth and its peoples under one owner. The bulls divided them, and gave the earth to some of the people.

The Requerimiento, read to uncomprehending villages from 1513, belongs to the same world and is told in [the essay on missions and colonialism](/writing/colonialism-and-missions). The logic did not stay Catholic.

## Rejecting the pope, keeping the grant

Protestants like to tell this as a Catholic story. England's kings ignored a papal line that left them nothing, and after 1534 they rejected the pope. They kept the principle and rewrote its chain of authority. In March 1496 Henry VII issued letters patent to John Cabot and his sons to sail under the English flag east, west and north, pointedly not south where the papal grants lay, and to find the lands of heathens and infidels so far unknown to Christians, which they were to occupy and possess as the king's vassals. The one qualification that mattered echoed the bulls: the lands were to be ones unknown to Christians.

The first charter of Virginia (1606) licensed colonies where no Christian prince or people yet held possession, and commended the venture as a way of bringing the faith to peoples living in darkness. The seal of the Massachusetts Bay Company, chartered in 1629, showed an Indigenous man saying "Come over and help us," words borrowed from Paul's vision at Troas:

> "And a vision appeared to Paul in the night: a man of Macedonia was standing there, urging him and saying, 'Come over to Macedonia and help us.'" (Acts 16:9, ESV)

In Acts the plea sends Paul across the sea with nothing but the gospel, to a beating and a jail in Philippi. On the seal it became a company's letterhead, spoken for people who had asked for nothing, to justify a charter for land.

Puritan divines supplied the theology. John Cotton, preaching to Winthrop's departing fleet in 1630 on God's promise to plant his people Israel in a place of their own, taught that God makes room for a people in several lawful ways, one being to find land empty and take it by improving it. Winthrop had already argued in writing, in 1629, that the Native peoples held only a natural right to land they did not enclose or improve with settled habitation and cattle, so that the English, if they left them enough for their use, might lawfully take the rest as ground never replenished or subdued, in the terms of Genesis 1:28. Historians know the argument by the Latin tag *vacuum domicilium*, an empty dwelling. In 1689 John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* made labor mixed with land the origin of property and offered the illustration "in the beginning all the World was America." Rome had made baptism a property qualification. The Protestants added agriculture.

Steven T. Newcomb, a Shawnee and Lenape scholar, presses the charge further in *Pagans in the Promised Land* (2008). The whole framework, he argues, rests on the Old Testament pattern of a chosen people entering a promised land, and American law still thinks inside it. It is the critic's strongest case, because it reaches past the popes to the Bible itself. Jennings, writing from inside the faith, locates the failure more exactly. The church forgot that it had been grafted into Israel's story as a guest and began to imagine itself as Israel's replacement. A church that thinks it has inherited Israel's promises without Israel can think it has inherited Israel's land-claims too, and then any coast can become Canaan.

That diagnosis begins the answer to Newcomb. In Scripture the conquest of Canaan is no portable template. It is a bounded judgment on particular nations, deferred for generations because "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Genesis 15:16, ESV), and hard enough to need [its own honest treatment](/writing/did-god-command-genocide). Even then the land never became Israel's property in Winthrop's or Locke's sense: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). Israel was warned that the land could expel them as it had expelled the nations before them:

> "lest the land vomit you out when you make it unclean, as it vomited out the nation that was before you." (Leviticus 18:28, ESV)

The promised land was held on the harshest terms in the canon, by tenants under an owner who was not one of them. The doctrine of discovery read the conquest narratives and skipped the lease.

## How discovery became American law

The United States inherited the doctrine through a lawsuit with no Native party in it. In 1773 and 1775 speculators bought vast tracts north of the Ohio River directly from the Illinois and Piankeshaw nations. The new nation refused to honor such purchases and later sold land in the region to settlers, among them William M'Intosh. The heirs of one buyer, Thomas Johnson, sued. Lindsay G. Robertson, working through the land companies' own papers in *Conquest by Law* (2005), argues that the investors arranged the case themselves, on an agreed statement of facts, hoping the Court would confirm their purchase. It did the opposite.

Marshall's opinion for a unanimous Court in *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (1823) held that the tribes could not have sold what they did not fully own. Discovery, he reasoned, gave the Christian nation whose subjects made it title against every other European power and the sole right to acquire the soil from its inhabitants. The Native nations kept a right of occupancy, but their sovereignty was, in his word, diminished, and their power to sell the soil to whomever they pleased was denied. The United States had inherited Britain's title. To show that England had adopted the principle, Marshall cited the commission Henry VII gave to Cabot.

Read fairly, Marshall makes the case harder, not easier. He conceded with visible unease that the pretension of turning the discovery of an inhabited country into conquest might seem extravagant, and that the rule might be opposed to natural right. But a court, he held, could not undo a rule on which the property of a whole country rested: "Conquest gives a title which the Courts of the conqueror cannot deny." That is a judge who believed himself bound by history, not a zealot. It is also a system that had turned a theological claim into a settled fact, so that no one inside it needed to believe the theology for the dispossession to continue.

The principle outran its author's attempt to limit it. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) Marshall read discovery more narrowly, as a rule governing claims among the European powers. Georgia ignored the ruling, the federal government did not enforce it, and under the Indian Removal Act of 1830 the Cherokee were driven west in 1838 on the road their descendants call the Trail of Tears. In *Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States* (1955) the Court held that Native title resting on aboriginal occupancy was not property the government had to pay for when it took Tlingit timber in Alaska.

Robert J. Miller, a law professor and citizen of the Eastern Shawnee Tribe of Oklahoma, showed with Jacinta Ruru, Larissa Behrendt and Tracey Lindberg in *Discovering Indigenous Lands* (2010) that the same doctrine structured Native land law in Canada, Australia and New Zealand. Miller breaks it into about ten recurring elements, among them first discovery, actual occupancy, the discoverer's exclusive right to buy, diminished Native title and sovereignty, the fiction of empty land, and Christianity and civilization as the tests of rightful ownership. Laid out that way, it stops looking like a relic and looks like a working machine whose parts were forged in theology.

Scripture has a story about that kind of machine. When King Ahab wanted the vineyard beside his palace, its owner refused him in words that treat land as an inheritance rather than a commodity:

> "But Naboth said to Ahab, 'The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers.'" (1 Kings 21:3, ESV)

Jezebel then did everything by the book: letters under the king's seal, a fast proclaimed, two witnesses, a trial and a stoning, after which the vineyard passed lawfully to the crown. Every form was followed, the religious ones included. God sent Elijah to meet Ahab in the vineyard with a question that ignores the paperwork: "Have you killed and also taken possession?" (1 Kings 21:19, ESV). The story doesn't make every legal taking a murder, and neither should we. It says that procedure doesn't sanctify a taking, and that the one who benefits is addressed even when someone else wrote the letters. Ahab didn't write the letters, and the nation that has lived on *Johnson* for two centuries didn't write the bulls. Elijah's question still went to the man standing in the vineyard.

## What the repudiations settle, and what they don't

The churches have not been silent. In 2009 the General Convention of the Episcopal Church repudiated the doctrine. The executive committee of the World Council of Churches denounced it in 2012, and several American denominations followed, among them the United Methodist Church in 2012 and the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America and the Presbyterian Church (USA) in 2016. Those who pressed for them, often Native Christians, deserve better than the claim that nothing has been said.

The weightiest statement came from Rome. On 30 March 2023 the Dicastery for Culture and Education and the Dicastery for Promoting Integral Human Development issued a joint note on the doctrine of discovery. It acknowledged that the papal documents did not adequately reflect the equal dignity and rights of Indigenous peoples, that competing colonial powers manipulated them for political ends to justify immoral acts, and that church authorities sometimes failed to object. It repudiated the concepts that fail to recognize the inherent rights of Indigenous peoples, including the legal and political doctrine of discovery. And it drew a distinction that carries its argument: the bulls were historical documents tied to political questions and have never been considered expressions of the Catholic faith, while the doctrine as a legal theory was the work of later jurists and courts. As evidence that the church had taught otherwise, it cited *Sublimis Deus* (1537).

Many Indigenous leaders welcomed the note and named what it did not do. It did not formally rescind the bulls. Its talk of manipulation placed the fault on colonial powers and later jurists, when the bulls, read plainly, authorized conquest and perpetual servitude before anyone twisted them. And a canonical distinction between a papal grant and church teaching can sound, to a dispossessed people, like an institution explaining why it isn't responsible for words still at work in a courtroom.

Both sides have a point. Catholic theology does distinguish solemn teaching on faith and morals from a pope's political and juridical acts, and the bulls were acts of the second kind; no council ever defined that faith confers title. But the distinction cuts in a direction its defenders may not want. If the doctrine was never taught as dogma, then it did its damage without needing to be, through grants, charters, sermons, seals and court opinions, and through an imagination that simply assumed the baptized were the earth's rightful owners. That is where the error lived, and where repentance has to reach. A repudiation can retire a proposition. It can't undo an imagination, and it can't return a field.

The strongest objection from inside the church has texts. Scripture refuses inherited guilt: "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (Ezekiel 18:20, ESV). The Christian in the pew didn't draft *Inter caetera* or argue *Johnson*, and any account that treats guilt as a stain passed down in the blood has left Christian ground.

But guilt isn't the only question. Three are tangled here, and they weigh differently. Whether the theology was false is first-order, and a Christian has no second opinion available: the claim that faith confers title to other people's land contradicts creation, the prophets and the Lord who made a despised Samaritan the neighbor in his parable. What the living church now owes is second-order, and the right word for it is obligation rather than guilt. If my grandfather stole a field and I inherit it, Ezekiel clears me of his sin; it doesn't deed me the field. Whether that obligation means advocacy, partnership with Native churches and nations, or returning land a denomination holds, faithful Christians will answer differently, and the biblical case on restitution is weighed in [the essay on reparations](/writing/theology-of-reparations). How courts should treat *Johnson* is a third, legal question, where the church has no special competence. The chronic failure has been to let the difficulty of the second and third questions excuse silence about the first.

Daniel, carried into exile as a youth, with no hand in the kings' sins that brought Jerusalem down, still prayed, "we have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled, turning aside from your commandments and rules" (Daniel 9:5, ESV). He kept the two apart, naming the ruin as coming "for our sins, and for the iniquities of our fathers" (Daniel 9:16, ESV). He was not taking his fathers' guilt as his own. He was refusing to stand outside his people's history while living inside its consequences.

## The witnesses the church already had

For a Christian, the most important fact about the doctrine is that the church contradicted it from the start, in its own pulpits and lecture halls, and was overruled.

On an Advent Sunday in 1511, in Santo Domingo on Hispaniola, the Dominican friar Antonio de Montesinos preached to colonists who held the Taíno in forced labor. His text was the Baptist's:

> "He said, 'I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, "Make straight the way of the Lord," as the prophet Isaiah said.'" (John 1:23, ESV)

Bartolomé de las Casas later recorded the substance of the sermon. Montesinos told the colonists they were in mortal sin for their cruelty to an innocent people and asked by what right they held them in such servitude. Were these not human beings with rational souls, and were the colonists not bound to love them as themselves? When the colonists demanded a retraction, he preached again the next Sunday and made it worse. The protest reached Spain and produced the Laws of Burgos (1512), which regulated the system without ending it.

In lectures at Salamanca in 1539, later published as *De Indis*, the Dominican Francisco de Vitoria took apart the conquest's legal titles. The pope has no temporal lordship over the whole world, he argued, and cannot grant what he does not hold. The peoples of the Americas were true owners and true rulers before the Spanish came, and unbelief no more cancels ownership than sin does. Discovery gives no title to lands that already have owners; in substance, it gave the Spanish no more right than those peoples would have gained by discovering Spain. He did not escape his age, since he still allowed war when Spaniards were barred from trading and preaching, but on the central question he was plain, forty-six years after *Inter caetera*, and right.

Paul III's *Sublimis Deus* (1537) declared that the peoples of the Indies, and any peoples later discovered, were truly human, were not to be deprived of liberty or property even though they were outside the faith, and were to be invited to Christ by preaching and the example of a good life. The Vatican was right to cite it, and it is also sobering. A companion brief, *Pastorale officium*, threatened with excommunication anyone who enslaved or despoiled the Indians. The emperor Charles V objected, and in 1538 Paul III annulled that brief and its penalties. The truth was spoken at the top of the church, and the machine kept running.

Behind these witnesses stands an older one. Paul told the Athenians that God "made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place" (Acts 17:26, ESV). The nations' dwelling places are God's allotment to them, not blank space awaiting a Christian claimant. Paul came to Athens to proclaim. He did not come to possess.

This is the ground Mark Charles and Soong-Chan Rah stand on in *Unsettling Truths* (2019). Charles is Navajo, Rah a Korean American theologian, and both write as Christians. They trace the doctrine behind the bulls to a distorted theological imagination they locate in Christendom itself, the long fusion of church and empire that taught Christians to treat political power as the gospel's natural instrument, and follow it into American national identity. Charles has argued for a national process of truth-telling that he calls conciliation rather than reconciliation, on the ground that there was never a prior harmony to restore. Some readers will think they press the line from Constantine to Columbus harder than the evidence bears. But notice where they stand. They aren't demolishing Christianity to make room for something else. They are digging beneath Christendom to the faith that preceded it, the faith Montesinos preached and Vitoria defended. That is reconstruction, and it is the only honest response to this history.

## Strangers and sojourners on someone's land

I am not outside this history. I have led worship on ground whose history I never asked about, and I have preached the justice of God in general terms without knowing who lived on the land before the building, or how it came to be for sale. That ignorance wasn't innocent. It was comfortable, a choice repeated across generations not to know what is hard to know. A church that doesn't know its history can't confess it, and a church that can't confess it keeps living inside the imagination that produced it.

So repentance begins with something unglamorous, which is knowledge. It means a congregation learning the history of the land it meets on, which in this country almost always runs through a treaty, a cession or a removal, and saying it aloud among its own people. It means teaching the doctrine by name, and seeking out Native Christians and churches, who have carried the faith through everything described here, to listen before deciding what repair should look like. And where a church holds land whose history it knows, it means asking the second-order question out loud, with Zacchaeus in view, and letting the answer cost something.

The deeper change is in the imagination. The doctrine taught Christians to see themselves as owners. Scripture teaches them to see themselves as tenants and guests. The patriarchs, Hebrews says, "died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13, ESV). And the one to whom all authority in heaven and on earth was given owned no land at all:

> "And Jesus said to him, 'Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.'" (Matthew 8:20, ESV)

He held every title there is and claimed none of them. He was stripped at his execution and laid in another man's tomb. The logic of discovery runs from authority to possession, and the cross is God's refusal of that logic in his own flesh. When the risen Christ sent his disciples to the nations, he sent them with an authority won by being dispossessed, an authority to teach, to baptize and to serve. The church that sailed under the bulls carried his name and forgot his way.

The earth will be inherited. Jesus said so, and he said who will inherit it: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matthew 5:5, ESV). The promise is to the meek, and it is an inheritance, which means it is received from the owner and not seized from the neighbor. The doctrine of discovery was the church's attempt to take early, and by force, what had been promised to the meek as a gift at the end. The peoples who lost their land under it are not a footnote to that promise. Some of them are its heirs.

Somewhere in your county there is a treaty, and on the land it ceded, in all likelihood, a church. The papers were drawn up long ago by other hands, and the courts have long since said they cannot deny the title. Elijah's question was never addressed to the courts. It was addressed to the one standing in the vineyard, and on Sunday morning that is us.
