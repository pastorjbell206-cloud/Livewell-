I have five sons. Every argument about what one generation owes the next turns concrete when the next generation is made of people you know by name, who will live inside whatever you leave behind. But the question isn't finally about my house. It is one of the oldest questions in Scripture and one of the hardest in modern moral philosophy, and we tend to meet it in pieces, which is part of why we answer it so badly.

Watch how the pieces are distributed. One side of American public life talks constantly about the planet our grandchildren will inherit and grows quiet when the conversation turns to the federal debt they will also inherit. The other side can recite the deficit to the billion and treats talk of a warming climate as a costume for someone else's politics. Churches, meanwhile, worry about whether their children will keep the faith and rarely ask what kind of faith those children watched them practice. Each of us sees the inheritance that indicts the people we already disagree with. That suggests most of us don't actually have a principle about the future. We have a grievance about the present that we have dressed in the language of our grandchildren.

The Bible offers something better than a grievance. It offers a grammar, worked out across a thousand years of Israel's life, for how sin, consequence, blessing and faith travel down a family line. That grammar makes a distinction we badly need: guilt belongs to the one who sins, consequences run down the generations, and mercy runs farther than either. Held together, those three claims give the church one instrument for weighing every inheritance we are leaving, whichever tribe it embarrasses, and they place the whole question inside a people that already lives across time.

## Why the Future Became Easy to Ignore

The temptation is older than any modern theory. When the prophet Isaiah told King Hezekiah that the treasures of his house would one day be carried off to Babylon, and that some of his own descendants would serve in a foreign palace, the king called the word good. The narrator gives his reason with a bluntness that still stings: "For he thought, 'There will be peace and security in my days'" (Isaiah 39:8, ESV). By the standard of the books of Kings, Hezekiah was one of the best rulers Judah ever had, which is what makes the verse frightening. A faithful man, told of a disaster that would fall on his descendants, felt relief that it would not fall on him. Most moral failure between generations looks like that. It is the quiet comfort of knowing the bill comes due after we are gone.

What the modern West did was give Hezekiah's relief a philosophy. On 6 September 1789, writing from Paris as the French Revolution was beginning, Thomas Jefferson sent James Madison a letter arguing that "the earth belongs in usufruct to the living." A usufruct is the legal right to use and enjoy property you don't own, and Jefferson meant the word seriously: the dead have no rights over the world they leave, and no generation may bind the next. Using the mortality tables of his day, he reckoned a generation at about nineteen years and concluded that laws, constitutions and public debts ought to expire on roughly that schedule. There is something admirable in the letter. Jefferson did not want his country's children paying for its parents' wars.

Madison's reply, dated 4 February 1790, is the wiser document. He granted Jefferson's worry about debt and then pressed on the principle. Much of what the living enjoy, he pointed out, was paid for by the dead: improvements made to the land, a conquest repelled, independence won at a cost that fell on those who did not live to see its benefits. Such improvements, he argued, form a kind of debt the living rightly owe, and a generation that simply cancelled every obligation it inherited would be claiming the harvest while refusing the mortgage. That reply will return below as a practical test.

That same year, 1790, Edmund Burke published his *Reflections on the Revolution in France*, and in it gave the answer to Jefferson that has echoed ever since. Burke conceded that society is in some sense a contract, but he insisted it is not a trading agreement between the people who happen to be alive, to be dissolved whenever their interests change. Because the great goods of a civilization take many generations to achieve, he wrote, society becomes a partnership "between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." He described the living as something like life-tenants of an estate, holding for a season what they received from their fathers and owe to their heirs, forbidden to act as if they were its absolute masters. Burke was not a theologian, and his defense of inherited order could shade into a defense of inherited privilege. But on this point he had recovered something the Bible never lost.

G. K. Chesterton gave Burke's partnership its most memorable phrase more than a century later. In *Orthodoxy* (1908) he argued that tradition simply extends the franchise, giving votes to our ancestors, the most obscure class of all. He called it "the democracy of the dead," and he meant that people who happen to be walking around at a given moment have no special right to outvote everyone who came before. Run the logic forward and the unborn get a vote too. A democracy that polls only the living is an oligarchy of the present.

By the late twentieth century that oligarchy had become a way of life. Christopher Lasch argued in *The Culture of Narcissism* (1979) that the defining loss of his era was the sense of historical continuity, the feeling of belonging to a succession of generations that began before us and will continue after. Without it, he said, people learn to live for themselves and for the moment, neither honoring their ancestors nor planning for their posterity. Our institutions reinforce the loss. The elected official thinks in two- and four-year cycles, the public company in quarterly earnings, the household in monthly payments, and none of those horizons reaches the child born in 2060. I don't write that from above it. Pastors think in budget years and building campaigns, and churches can spend down what earlier believers built as readily as any corporation. Hezekiah's relief is the default setting of creatures who die, and it takes something outside us to break it.

## The Sins of the Fathers, Read Carefully

The Bible's first word on generations is one many readers wish it had left out. It comes inside the Ten Commandments, attached to the prohibition of idols: "You shall not bow down to them or serve them, for I the LORD your God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and the fourth generation of those who hate me" (Exodus 20:5, ESV). Skeptics have fairly pressed it: if God punishes great-grandchildren for what their ancestors did, he is less just than a decent county judge.

The objection deserves an answer and not a shrug, and the answer starts with reading the sentence to its end. It does not stop at the fourth generation. It continues: "but showing steadfast love to thousands of those who love me and keep my commandments" (Exodus 20:6, ESV). The grammar is built on an asymmetry. Iniquity travels three or four generations; steadfast love, the covenant word *hesed*, travels to thousands. Moses makes the scale explicit later in Deuteronomy, where the faithful God "keeps covenant and steadfast love with those who love him and keep his commandments, to a thousand generations" (Deuteronomy 7:9, ESV). Three or four generations is roughly the reach of a single household, the span a grandfather might live to see under his own roof. A thousand generations is longer than recorded history. The commandment is not a statement that God is harsh. It is a statement that consequences are real, and bounded, and that mercy is unbounded by comparison.

The pattern returns at the most important moment in the book of Exodus. Israel has made the golden calf, Moses has broken the tablets, and God agrees to go on with this people. When he passes before Moses on the mountain, he proclaims his own name: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6, ESV). And then: "keeping steadfast love for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, but who will by no means clear the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children and the children's children, to the third and the fourth generation" (Exodus 34:7, ESV). The rest of the Old Testament returns to this passage again and again, and it is spoken in the aftermath of Israel's worst betrayal. God will not pretend that sin has no afterlife in a family or a nation, and he will not let that afterlife have the last word.

Then, centuries later, came the correction. The exiles in Babylon had turned the old teaching into a fatalism that excused them. Their proverb put it plainly, and Ezekiel quotes it back to them: "What do you mean by repeating this proverb concerning the land of Israel, 'The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge'?" (Ezekiel 18:2, ESV). Lamentations records the same mood from inside the ruins: "Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities" (Lamentations 5:7, ESV). God refuses the proverb under oath, and then gives the principle that will govern Israel's reading of Exodus from then on: "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (Ezekiel 18:20, ESV). The law had long barred Israel's judges from putting children to death for their fathers (Deuteronomy 24:16). Ezekiel carries the principle out of the courtroom and applies it to Israel's standing before God.

Ezekiel does not contradict Exodus. He clarifies what it never meant. Side by side, the texts yield the distinction this essay turns on. Guilt is personal. No child stands condemned before God for a father's sin, and no generation is damned for its ancestors. Consequences are generational. A father's drinking, a nation's idolatry, a king's broken treaty, a people's debts and wars all go on shaping the lives of children who had no part in them, because human beings are not sealed units but members of households and nations. And mercy is larger than both. Ezekiel gave the exiles something harder than their proverb and better: you are not guilty of your fathers' sins, you do live among their consequences, and the God of the thousand generations is calling you to turn. "Why will you die, O house of Israel?" (Ezekiel 18:31, ESV).

That distinction clears away two errors live in American churches: the instinct that says "I didn't do it, so it isn't mine," which Ezekiel permits about guilt but never about consequences, and the habit of assigning inherited stain to whole groups, which he forbids outright. Nehemiah shows the middle path in prayer. Hearing in Susa that Jerusalem's walls lay broken, walls he had not torn down, he confessed "the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6, ESV). He claimed no guilt that wasn't his. He claimed a responsibility that was, because he had the power to rebuild. The site's essay on [what the Bible says about restitution and reparations](/writing/theology-of-reparations) follows that same distinction into the history of American housing; the principle belongs here, applied to everything a generation hands down.

## What Israel Was Told to Hand Down

If the commandments teach how sin travels, the rest of the Torah teaches how faith is meant to travel, and the method is strikingly ordinary. Deuteronomy is itself a generational document. Moses preaches it on the plains of Moab to the children of the people who stood at Sinai, most of whom are buried in the wilderness. The audience is already once removed from the events, and the obligation has not thinned. At its center stands a command still recited with the Shema: "And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7, ESV). The first inheritance is a memory, carried in the speech and rhythms of a household's day, and it has to be on the parent's heart before it can reach the child's ears.

Psalm 78 takes that command and stretches it across four generations in the space of a few lines. "We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD, and his might, and the wonders that he has done" (Psalm 78:4, ESV). God commanded the fathers to teach, the psalm continues, "that the next generation might know them, the children yet unborn, and arise and tell them to their children" (Psalm 78:6, ESV). The purpose follows: "so that they should set their hope in God and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments" (Psalm 78:7, ESV). Count the links: the fathers, their children, the children yet unborn, and the children those will teach in turn. It is the same span as the visitation of iniquity in Exodus, three or four generations, now running in the other direction. What a household can transmit for harm it can also transmit for good. And then the psalm spends some sixty verses telling the story of generations that forgot, which makes it less a comfort than a warning. Forgetting compounds.

The warning had already come true once. After Joshua's death, the book of Judges records the sentence every parent and every pastor should fear: "And there arose another generation after them who did not know the LORD or the work that he had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10, ESV). Nobody had abolished the faith. It simply failed to cross a single generational line. Against that, the psalmist sets the ordinary liturgy of a living people: "One generation shall commend your works to another, and shall declare your mighty acts" (Psalm 145:4, ESV). Faith survives by being spoken, praised and practiced in front of people younger than we are.

Sociology has confirmed what Deuteronomy assumed. When Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton published *Soul Searching* (2005) from the National Study of Youth and Religion, they found that American teenagers largely reproduced the religious lives of their parents, and that what most of them had absorbed was not historic Christianity but a softer creed they named moralistic therapeutic deism: a distant God who wants people to be nice and to feel good about themselves. That indicts the adults more than the teenagers. Our children inherit the faith we actually practice, and a generation that treated God as a therapist should not be surprised when its children see no reason to keep the appointment. The practical side belongs to the site's essay on [raising kids in the Christian faith through rhythms rather than milestones](/writing/parenting-family-rhythms-that-form-faith). The theological point is that faith is the first and heaviest thing one generation owes the next, and the whole congregation owes it, not only parents.

The Torah does not stop at memory. It also legislates material inheritance, and it does so in a way that limits the present owner. "A good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children," Proverbs says (Proverbs 13:22, ESV), and the horizon is deliberately set at the grandchildren. The land law of Leviticus puts a ceiling on what any generation may do with its fields: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). Every family held its plot as a trust from God for its descendants, which is why Naboth could refuse a king: "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1 Kings 21:3, ESV). He was refusing to sell what was not wholly his, because his grandchildren had a claim on it that Ahab's money could not buy out. The site's essay on [the Year of Jubilee, land and debt](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded) follows those laws in detail. What matters here is the posture they build. The living are trustees, and the measure of what they do with the land is what is left for those who come after. That is Burke's life-tenancy long before Burke, with one difference: Israel's trust rested not on the wisdom of ancestors, which can be foolish, or the rights of descendants, which are hard to define, but on the ownership of God. Every generation holds on loan.

## The Philosophers' Problem, and the Longtermist Answer

Modern moral philosophy came to the same question late and found it harder than expected. In *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Derek Parfit posed what he called the non-identity problem. Which people come to exist depends on which policies a society chooses, because those policies change who meets whom and when children are conceived. Imagine, Parfit suggested, a society choosing between depleting its resources and conserving them. Depletion raises the standard of living for a couple of centuries and lowers it afterward. Yet the people alive in that later, poorer world would not have existed under conservation; different people would have been born. Their lives are still worth living, and their only alternative was never existing. So whom has depletion harmed? Parfit was sure it was wrong and spent years looking for a principle that could say why, without claiming to have found one.

Hans Jonas came at it from another side. In *The Imperative of Responsibility* (English edition 1984) he argued that technology had extended human action beyond the reach of the old ethics of neighbor and present moment, and he proposed a new imperative: act so that the effects of your action are compatible with the continuation of genuine human life on earth. His model for this responsibility was the plainest one available, a parent before a newborn, whose mere existence makes a claim no contract created.

The most ambitious recent answer is longtermism, set out for a general audience in William MacAskill's *What We Owe the Future* (2022). Its argument, in MacAskill's own terms, is short: future people count, there could be a great many of them, and we can make their lives go better or worse. From those premises he concludes that shaping the long-term future well, above all by reducing the risk of catastrophe, is among the chief moral priorities of our time. The case is serious. Distance in time, like distance in space, does not make suffering less real. A child who will be born in 2150 will feel hunger and fear exactly as a child in 2026 does. Any ethic that ignores her because she can't vote yet has simply confused power with worth.

Christians should grant most of that gladly. But the frame has blind spots. Its future is a sum, a vast aggregate of possible welfare, and sums invite a kind of moral arithmetic in which enormous speculative numbers can outweigh the poor person at your door. Its future also rests on us. If the fate of trillions depends on this generation's decisions, the living carry a messianic burden no generation can bear, and it's hard to see what keeps that weight from either crushing people or justifying almost anything done in the future's name.

The biblical grammar answers Parfit's puzzle from another direction. Our obligation to those after us depends neither on their being particular people who can be harmed nor on our calculating their total welfare. It rests on the God who is Lord of every generation. "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations" (Psalm 90:1, ESV). The land, the children and the future are his, and the living answer to him for what he lent them. That is covenant rather than contract. A contract needs two parties present to consent. A covenant can bind a people to those not yet born because the one who holds it has already met them. It also relieves the messianic burden without licensing Hezekiah's shrug. We are not the saviors of the future. We are its trustees, and trustees can be faithless.

## One Instrument for Every Inheritance

Madison's reply to Jefferson supplies the test, and the Bible sharpens it. Some burdens passed forward are just, because the heirs also receive what the burden bought: the drained field, the won independence, the school built on borrowed money that will educate the children who repay it. Other burdens are simply consumption billed to people who cannot object. The question to put to any inheritance is whether those who receive it would ratify it if they could stand in the room, whether they are being handed an asset along with the bill or only the bill.

Apply that instrument honestly and it cuts in every direction. Federal debt held by the public now roughly equals the size of the whole American economy, a level last reached in the years after the Second World War, and the Congressional Budget Office's 2025 long-term budget outlook projected it passing that postwar record within a few years and climbing from there. The Social Security trustees' annual reports have for several years projected the main retirement trust fund exhausted in the early 2030s. Some of that borrowing bought things our children will use. Much of it paid for present spending and tax cuts that neither party was willing to fund, and both parties have done it. The Christian who treats the deficit as a sin only when the other party is in power has not yet understood the sin.

The same instrument falls on the atmosphere. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change stated in 2021 that it is "unequivocal" that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land. Because much of the carbon already emitted lingers for centuries, a large share of the cost will fall on people not yet born. The objections of Christians wary of climate policy deserve a fair hearing: cheap energy has lifted billions out of poverty, the costs of rapid transition also land on working families, and a church has no special competence in carbon markets. Those are real questions of prudence. But they are questions about how to answer, not whether the bill exists. Roger Scruton, no progressive, argued in *Green Philosophy* (2012) that conservatism rightly understood is the most natural home for environmental care, because it begins from Burke's partnership and from love of a particular home. Wendell Berry, in "Two Economies" (collected in *Home Economics*, 1987), argued that every human economy lives inside a larger one it did not make and cannot fully comprehend, and he was willing to call that larger economy the kingdom of God. The site's essay on [what the Bible says about caring for the environment](/writing/creation-care-not-optional) sets out that theology, including Berry's earlier contrast between the exploiter and the nurturer. The point here is that the national debt and the atmosphere are the same kind of question, and a Christian who can see only one of them is reading with a party's eyes.

Institutions and trust are inheritances too, and so is the church itself. We received congregations, schools, hospitals and mission agencies built by believers who gave sacrificially for people they would never meet. Some of our generation has spent that capital on buildings meant to comfort us, on scandals covered to protect reputations, on a public witness so bound to partisan causes that our children associate the name of Christ with a party. I am inside that account. I have kept silent when silence was easier and benefited from arrangements I never examined, and a pastor's caution about controversy has served as my reason to avoid conversations that would have cost me something. That confession doesn't settle anything. It only marks where the obligation starts.

## A Great Cloud of Witnesses

The church is the one institution on earth that already lives the way Burke described, a partnership of the living, the dead and the unborn. Hebrews 11 walks through that partnership name by name, Abel and Abraham, Moses and Rahab, and says of them all that they "died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar" (Hebrews 11:13, ESV). Then comes the strangest sentence in the chapter: "And all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect" (Hebrews 11:39-40, ESV). The dead are waiting on the living. Their story is incomplete without ours, and ours without those who come after us. "Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us" (Hebrews 12:1, ESV).

That is the answer Christianity gives to Parfit's puzzle and to Hezekiah's relief. We owe the future because we belong to it, as the saints in Hebrews belong to us, and because the God who holds all of it has promised that his mercy reaches a thousand generations. Our failures toward those who come after do not have the last word, because the cross bore the iniquity that runs downhill through every family line, and because the risen Christ is the firstfruits of a future no generation can spend down. That hope is not permission to be careless. It is the only ground on which repentance for what we are handing on becomes bearable.

When Babylon's armies were outside Jerusalem and the city was about to fall, the prophet Jeremiah bought a field. He paid, signed the deed before witnesses, and had it sealed in an earthenware vessel so that it would last for a long time, because "thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: Houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (Jeremiah 32:15, ESV). Nothing suggests he ever walked that field in peace. He bought it for people he would never meet, on a promise he would not see kept.

Every generation of the church is holding a deed like that. The ones who come after us will open the jar and read what we signed: the debts we ran up and the ones we paid, the land we kept and the land we used, the faith we practiced in front of them, and the faith we only mentioned. They are not here yet to ask what we were thinking. We will be among the witnesses when they are.
