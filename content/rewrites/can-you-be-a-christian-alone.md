Most people who type this question into a search bar already know what the church is going to say, and many have heard it in a tone that made church sound like a debt collector. Some still pray and read the Gospels on their own, and find Jesus more compelling there than he ever seemed from a pew. Some were hurt badly enough that walking into another congregation feels like walking back into the room where it happened. Some drifted after a move, a newborn or a spring when every church went online, and found that nobody seemed to notice. And some never went at all, and wonder whether the Jesus they've started to take seriously comes attached to an institution they have no wish to join.

You deserve an honest answer, which means neither of the two you've probably heard. One says you can't be a real Christian without regular attendance, and it arrives as an accusation. The other says faith is between you and God and the rest is packaging, and it arrives as a relief. The first confuses the church with a turnstile. The second sounds more like our century than like the New Testament.

The answer this essay gives turns on one distinction. Christian faith is personal, and it is never private. It is personal because no one can believe for you. No parent's faith, no pastor's and no denomination's will stand in for your own trust in Christ. But a private faith is something else: held apart from others, answerable to no one and received from no one. The New Testament insists on the first and cannot imagine the second. You can believe alone; the thief on the cross did, and Jesus received him. Whether you can go on being a Christian alone is a different question, and the answer has less to do with rules than with what kind of thing a Christian is.

## Who Is Asking the Question

In October 2012 the Pew Research Center published a report that carried a sociologists' word far beyond sociology: "'Nones' on the Rise." The share of American adults claiming no religious affiliation had grown in five years from just over 15 percent to just under 20 percent, and among adults under thirty it was about a third. About two-thirds of the unaffiliated said they believed in God, more than a third called themselves spiritual but not religious, and roughly one in five prayed every day. Most told Pew that religious organizations were too concerned with money and power, too focused on rules and too involved in politics. These were not, for the most part, people who had argued their way out of belief. They had kept some version of it and set the institution down.

By 2024 Pew put the unaffiliated at 28 percent of American adults, and its profile of them that year found that most nones describe their religion as "nothing in particular" rather than atheist or agnostic. The fuller account of why people left is in [Why Are People Leaving the Church?](/writing/why-people-are-leaving-the-church). What matters here is the person left holding the question.

Jim Davis and Michael Graham, working with the political scientist Ryan Burge, estimated in *The Great Dechurching* (2023) that some forty million American adults who once attended church regularly no longer do. Their most useful finding concerned how people left. A smaller group was harmed, and the authors take that seriously. The larger share simply drifted. Life moved and Sunday didn't move with it. Many of those who drifted still hold much of what they once believed, and a good number said they would consider going back under the right conditions.

So the person asking is usually not a skeptic of God but someone who has come to think of church like a gym membership: good in principle, not required for the thing itself. That view rests on real arguments and real wounds, and the church did a great deal to teach it.

## The Case for Leaving, in Its Strongest Form

Start with the wounds, because for many people they come first. People have been abused by pastors and youth leaders and then watched the institution protect the abuser. Others were shunned for asking a question or pushed out for telling the truth. If that is your story, you are owed safety and the truth before you are owed any argument, and nothing here asks you to go back into the room that hurt you. The longer answer to that wound is in [Church Hurt: How to Heal and Whether to Go Back](/writing/when-the-church-is-what-hurt-you). If you are being harmed now, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, and the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline answers a call or text at 988.

Then the institutions. The complaint Pew heard was not invented by the church's critics. American churches have spent half a century lending their name to political causes on the right and the left, raising money for buildings while neighbors went without, and defending leaders they should have removed. A person can conclude from that record, with no hostility toward Jesus, that the institution has become an obstacle to him.

Then a quieter complaint. Much of American church life has been built as a service: good music, a sermon that fits your schedule, programs for your children and almost no claim on your life. Robert Bellah and his colleagues drew the relevant line in *Habits of the Heart* (1985). A community, they argued, is bound by a shared history and by obligations that outlast anyone's preferences. A lifestyle enclave is a group that gathers around shared tastes and leisure and comes apart when the tastes change. A great many churches have run as lifestyle enclaves with a steeple, and when the service disappoints, the customer leaves. The people who walk out are not leaving a family. They were never offered one.

Finally there is a theological argument, and serious Christians make it. Salvation is by grace through faith, not by attendance. Protestantism was born insisting that no priest or institution stands between the believer and God, and Hebrews invites every believer to "with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" (Hebrews 4:16). Jesus withdrew to pray alone, "very early in the morning, while it was still dark" (Mark 1:35), and told his disciples, "when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret" (Matthew 6:6). His hardest words were for the religious establishment. And the thief beside him on the cross was saved with no baptism, no membership and no sermon. If that man could be with Christ in paradise, why should anyone think the church necessary?

Much of this is true, and the church should say so first. Attendance saves no one, the institutions have failed often, and the consumer church is thin. I'm a pastor, which puts me inside that indictment rather than outside it; the church many people left is one my profession built. But notice what the theological argument has quietly done. It has taken texts that make faith personal and read them as though they made it private. That difference is the whole question, and the second reading has a history of its own.

## How Faith Became a Private Matter

For most of Christian history the question would have been hard to form. Around the year 155 Justin Martyr explained to the Roman emperor what Christians did on the day called Sunday: those in the cities and the countryside gathered in one place, heard the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets read, prayed, and shared the bread and cup, and afterward the deacons carried a portion to those who were absent (*First Apology* 65–67). The early church's answer to a member who couldn't come was not private worship. It carried the gathering to him.

The Reformation made faith unmistakably personal. When Martin Luther refused at the Diet of Worms in 1521 to recant unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason, declaring his conscience bound to the word of God, he gave the West an image it has never let go of: one man standing alone before the emperor. But Luther did not build a faith of one. After ten months hidden at the Wartburg he went back to the congregation at Wittenberg, and in *On the Councils and the Church* (1539) he set out the marks by which anyone could tell where the church was. Personal, yes. Private, never.

The privatizing came later. American revivalism, from Whitefield in the 1740s to Finney's demand for an immediate decision in the 1830s, put the individual conversion at the center, and the church began to look like whatever came afterward. By 1902 William James could define religion, for the purposes of *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, as "the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine." The faith an ordinary believer receives from others by tradition he set aside as secondhand. A century later Charles Taylor argued in *Varieties of Religion Today* (2002) that James had described our world better than his own.

In *A Secular Age* (2007) Taylor traces how that happened. Since the 1960s, he argues, the West has lived in an age of self-expression, in which each person is expected to find his own way of being human rather than conform to a pattern handed down by church, class or nation. In the religious world Taylor calls post-Durkheimian, belonging to God no longer implies belonging to a church or a people, and the test of any practice is whether it speaks to me. Taylor does not call this simple loss, since it freed many people from a conformity that had passed for faith. But it produced a new default, in which joining anything feels like a compromise of the self.

Bellah's team had met the default in person. Among the Americans they interviewed was a young nurse they called Sheila Larson, who believed in God, couldn't remember the last time she'd been to church, and named her faith after herself: "It's Sheilaism. Just my own little voice." They took her as an emblem of expressive individualism, the conviction that the truest self is found within and expressed outward, with every institution judged by whether it serves that self, and they noted that on this logic there could be as many American religions as Americans.

The church was not the only casualty. Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone* (2000) documented the thinning of lodges, unions, parent-teacher associations and bowling leagues across the last third of the century, and estimated that congregations had been the setting for something like half of the nation's memberships, giving and volunteering. When Americans stopped joining things, the church was the largest thing they stopped joining. Then came 2020, and millions learned that a service could be watched from the couch.

Evangelicals spent the late twentieth century preaching that Christianity is not a religion but a personal relationship with Jesus. We meant something true. But we said it so often, and said so little about the body, that a generation heard "private" when we meant "personal." We invited people to a decision and neglected to tell them they were being added to a people. The spiritual-but-not-religious believer did not invent the private faith. In many cases we handed it over.

## Personal but Never Private: The Grammar of the New Testament

Modern English uses one word for "you" whether it means one person or many, and American readers tend to hear the singular. The apostles mostly wrote the plural. Paul tells the Philippians to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you" (Philippians 2:12–13), and the command is plural, given to a congregation he has just urged to be "of the same mind" (Philippians 2:2). "Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16) pairs a plural "you" with a singular temple; the Spirit's dwelling is the gathered church. Even the shut room of Matthew 6 is not private in our sense. Jesus sends his disciples in to pray in secret and gives them the words to use there: "Pray then like this: 'Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name'" (Matthew 6:9). The prayer for the closet begins with a plural.

Paul's controlling image is the body, and it was not new. Livy tells how Menenius Agrippa, in 494 BC, talked the plebeians who had walked out of Rome into returning with a fable about limbs that rebelled against the belly, a story meant to keep the lower orders in their place. Paul turns it inside out. "For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ" (1 Corinthians 12:12). Then he addresses the strong: "The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you,' nor again the head to the feet, 'I have no need of you'" (12:21). The parts that seem weaker "are indispensable" (12:22), and "if one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (12:26). Paul's body is not a device for keeping the weak quiet. It makes them indispensable and lays the first obligation on the strong, which matters to anyone who was told, in effect, that the church had no need of them.

Then comes the sentence that holds this essay's distinction together: "Now you are the body of Christ and individually members of it" (1 Corinthians 12:27). *Individually*: each member is distinct, known and irreplaceable. *Members*: none exists apart. To be joined to Christ is to be joined to his body. A severed foot does not become a freer foot. Joseph Hellerman argues in *When the Church Was a Family* (2009) that the apostles' sibling language, in a world where the bond between siblings was among the strongest a person had, reassigned a believer's first allegiance. You don't attend a family. You belong to it.

Ephesians carries the image from a body to a house. "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone, in whom the whole structure, being joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord. In him you also are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:19–22). The setting is Jews and Gentiles made one by Christ, who broke down "the dividing wall of hostility" (Ephesians 2:14), and the verbs are passive and plural. A brick in a field is not a smaller temple. It is a brick waiting for the wall. Peter adds that believers "like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood" (1 Peter 2:5). The priesthood of believers, often cited against the church, is in Peter's words one priesthood made of many stones.

The strongest answer to the theological case for leaving sits in the letter that supplied its best verse: "since we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh, and since we have a great priest over the house of God, let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (Hebrews 10:19–22). The letter has already told us who that house is: "we are his house" (Hebrews 3:6). And the next sentences read: "Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful. And let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near" (Hebrews 10:23–25). The direct access is the reason for the gathering, not a substitute for it, and no one can stir himself up the way a brother or sister can.

## What One Person Cannot Do: Water, Bread and Word

Ask what the New Testament says Christians do, and how much of it can be done alone. At Pentecost, "those who received his word were baptized, and there were added that day about three thousand souls" (Acts 2:41). *Added* is the verb that matters. Baptism marks your own death and rising with Christ, and it is also something no one has ever done to himself. Paul says believers were "baptized into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13). Christians disagree in good faith about who should be baptized and how, but whether an infant is carried to the font or a believer walks into the water to confess faith, no tradition has made self-baptism its practice. Even the Ethiopian official in Acts 8, the nearest thing the New Testament offers to a solitary convert, could not do it for himself: "they both went down into the water, Philip and the eunuch, and he baptized him" (Acts 8:38).

Then the table. "Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread" (1 Corinthians 10:17). Paul wrote that to a church failing badly at it. In Corinth the wealthy ate before the poor arrived: "each one goes ahead with his own meal. One goes hungry, another gets drunk" (1 Corinthians 11:21). Paul's remedy was not to abandon the table: "So then, my brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another" (1 Corinthians 11:33). His answer to a church that humiliated its weakest members was to mend the table, and the mending began with the strong.

The Reformers, who had every reason to prize personal faith, defined the church by exactly these acts. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 called the church the assembly of saints in which the gospel is purely taught and the sacraments rightly administered. Calvin's *Institutes* (1559) held that wherever the word is purely preached and heard and the sacraments administered as Christ instituted them, a church of God undoubtedly exists. The Belgic Confession of 1561 added discipline. Luther's list of 1539 ran to seven marks and ended with the one we would rather forget, the holy cross a faithful church bears. Every one of these marks happens between people. The word is preached by one and heard by others; the sacraments are given and received; discipline is one believer going to another. The marks of the church are, among other things, a list of what cannot be done alone.

Calvin opened his account of the church with an image borrowed from a North African bishop thirteen centuries dead: the church as the mother through whom believers are conceived, born, nourished and kept to the end of their lives. The image is usually quoted without its history. Cyprian became bishop of Carthage around 248. In 250 the emperor Decius ordered the empire's inhabitants to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a certificate proving it. Many complied; others refused and were imprisoned or killed. Afterward the church had to decide what to do with the lapsed. In Carthage a faction readmitted them quickly on the confessors' authority, bypassing the bishops. In Rome, Novatian led a rigorist party that refused them altogether and set up a rival church to keep itself pure. Into that crisis, in 251, Cyprian wrote *On the Unity of the Church*, with the sentence that outlived him: "He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother."

The setting changes how the line reads. Cyprian was not writing to the wounded or the lonely. He was writing against people building churches of their own design, one more lenient than the church and one more severe. The argument beneath the line was about whether failures could come home, and Cyprian's answer, worked out with his fellow bishops, was yes, through repentance and the church's patient restoration rather than around it. He sealed the argument with his life, beheaded in 258 under Valerian. The line has since been swung as a club, and Protestants would not say membership saves anyone. But the image survived the Reformation because it names something true. A mother is not a gatekeeper. She is the one through whom life is given and fed, and who takes you back.

## The Word You Cannot Speak to Yourself

Dietrich Bonhoeffer learned why this matters under pressure. In 1935 the Confessing Church, which refused the Nazi-backed church government, asked him to lead one of its seminaries, which settled at Finkenwalde in Pomerania. The Gestapo closed it in 1937, and the next year he wrote down what that shared life had taught him, published in 1939 as *Life Together*.

Its central argument is one the solitary believer rarely hears. The Christian needs another Christian, Bonhoeffer says, because the word that saves comes from outside. Left to myself I excuse what I ought to confess and condemn what God has already forgiven. So God puts his word in another person's mouth, and Bonhoeffer argues that the Christ in my own heart is weaker than the Christ in my brother's word, because my heart is uncertain and my brother's word is sure. A faith with no outside voice slowly becomes a conversation with oneself, drifting gently toward a gospel shaped like its owner's preferences, and no one is close enough to say, *that isn't Christ, that's you.* That is the hidden cost of Sheilaism. My own little voice can never tell me anything I don't already want to hear.

The same holds for sin. In his chapter on confession Bonhoeffer observes that the person alone with his sin is utterly alone, and that sin wants to stay hidden because hiddenness is its strength. James had given the remedy long before: "Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16). A private faith can confess to God, and it should. What it cannot do is hear another human being, who knows what was done, say aloud that it is forgiven in Christ.

Bonhoeffer was not romantic about community, which is why the wounded can trust him. He warned that whoever loves his dream of Christian community more than the actual community becomes its destroyer, and that God often has to shatter the dream before real fellowship can begin. Nor would he set solitude and community against each other. The one who cannot be alone should beware of community, he wrote, and the one who is not in community should beware of being alone. A disciple needs the shut room and the shared table. He called the physical presence of other believers a gift of grace that prisoners and the sick long for. Four years after the book appeared he was in prison himself, and in April 1945 he was hanged at Flossenbürg.

## Believing Alone and Being Held

So can you be a Christian without going to church? You can believe alone. Many people do for a season, and God does not lose track of any of them. But believing alone and being a Christian alone are different claims, because being a Christian is not only a belief you hold. It is a body you have been joined to, a family you have been adopted into, a house you are being built into. You can hold the faith by yourself for a while. The New Testament never expects it to hold you that way.

The first word of response belongs to the church, and it is repentance. If the people who left were offered a service instead of a family and a party platform instead of a body, the obligation to change lies with us before it lies with them. Paul's first demand at Corinth fell on the strong. A church that wants the dechurched back has to become a place where the weak are indispensable, where abuse is reported and not managed, where no party writes the sermon, and where a hard question isn't handled. None of that works as a strategy for filling seats; the people who left can smell marketing.

To the reader who was hurt: no one is asking you to walk back into the room where it happened. There are other rooms, and some of them are quietly faithful; [How Do You Find a Church Worth Joining?](/writing/how-to-find-a-church-worth-joining) is one place to begin. Go slowly. You recognized what was wrong in the church that hurt you because you carry some picture of what a church was meant to be, and a counterfeit only disappoints someone who knows the real currency. To the reader who drifted, nothing here is an accusation, but it's worth asking whether the faith you still claim is being fed by anything outside yourself. And to the reader who never went, no church should ask you to join before you believe. But if you want to know whether any of this is true, it has always been most visible at a table where people who would never have chosen one another share one loaf.

The thief deserves one more look, because he is the case everyone cites. He did believe alone, with no church and no water, in the last hours of his life. But listen to what Jesus promised him: "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). *With me.* Even the most solitary conversion in Scripture ends in company, and the company grows. When John is shown the end of the story, he sees "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). That crowd is what Christ died for. "Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25), and he did it on a night when every one of his friends ran. He loved the body while it was failing him. The church in your town is failing him too, and I include my own failures in that. It is still where people who would never have picked each other are learning, slowly and by grace, to stand in the crowd they will stand in forever.

Somewhere near you this Sunday, in a room plainer than it ought to be, a few such people will pass bread down a row and say to one another words none of them could say to himself. You are not required to be there for God to love you. The question you carry out of this essay is a quieter one: whether you are willing to be loved the way he has chosen to do it, through hands that are not your own.
