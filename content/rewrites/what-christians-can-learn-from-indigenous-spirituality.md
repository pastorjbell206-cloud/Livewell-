## Two pictures painted by someone else

Most American Christians meet Native American religion in one of two versions, and neither was painted by Native people. In the older version, the one many missionaries carried west, the ceremonies of the peoples they met were heathen darkness, the drum was a summons to spirits, and conversion meant leaving all of it behind along with the language it was spoken in. In the newer version, which arrived with the environmental movement, the Native elder became a saint of ecology, the keeper of a wisdom the industrial West had thrown away. Its most famous image is a public service advertisement that Keep America Beautiful first aired in 1971, in which a man in buckskin paddles a canoe past smokestacks and floating trash, steps onto a littered shore, and turns to the camera with a single tear on his cheek. The actor, who called himself Iron Eyes Cody and played Indians in films for decades, was the son of Sicilian immigrants.

The two pictures look like opposites, but they share a grammar. Both make Native people into a symbol that serves someone else's argument, and both assume that there is one thing out there called Native spirituality, waiting to be condemned or admired. There isn't. The United States recognizes more than 570 tribal nations, and there are others it doesn't recognize. The Lakota of the northern plains, the Diné of the Southwest, the Haudenosaunee confederacy of what is now New York and Ontario, the Anishinaabe peoples around the Great Lakes, the Tlingit of the Alaskan coast and the Hopi of the high mesas have their own languages, their own accounts of how the world began, their own ceremonies and their own ways of speaking about the sacred. Many of their members don't use the word "religion" for these ways at all. They speak of instructions, of a way of life, of what the old people taught. An essay that asks what Christians can learn from all of that at once has already stopped listening. So this one names particular peoples and practices only where it can describe them as their own members would, and says less wherever that is the honest thing to do.

There is a second flattening beneath the first, and it matters more. Both pictures assume that Native people stand outside the church, so that the question becomes what one religion, Christianity, might borrow from another, as if a Christian sat on one side of a table and a Native person on the other. For a great many Native people the table runs through the middle of their own families and their own lives. There are Lakota Episcopalians descended from priests, Cherokee Baptists who sing hymns in Cherokee, Kiowa Methodists with hymns of their own composing, Mohawk Catholics who honor a saint of their own people, and Native Pentecostals and evangelicals across the continent, many of whom came to faith through Native preachers. The faith has been Native, for some peoples, for longer than the United States has existed. Any honest answer to the question in this essay's title has to begin with them.

That changes who the teachers are. The best guides to what Native traditions can teach the church are not the ones on the posters. They are Native Christians who have carried the faith through everything done to their peoples in its name, and Native critics, some of them raised in the church, who have told it the truth about itself. I write as a pastor in Michigan, on ground that was home to the Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi, the Anishinaabe peoples of the Great Lakes, and to other nations besides, long before it held a single church, and as a member of a Christian tradition that did a great deal of damage on this continent and very little listening. The history of that damage, from the papal bulls behind [the doctrine of discovery](/writing/doctrine-of-discovery) to the residential schools, is told in [the essay on missions and colonialism](/writing/colonialism-and-missions), and it won't be retold here. This essay asks a different question: what the church, having done that, might still receive.

One distinction has to govern everything that follows. Learning from a people is different from taking from them. The market in borrowed ceremony is old and ugly: weekend vision quests, sweat lodges run for paying customers by people with no standing to run them, and in October 2009 a self-help retreat near Sedona, Arizona, where three people died in a makeshift sweat lodge led by a motivational speaker. In 1993 a gathering of Lakota, Dakota and Nakota leaders formally condemned the exploitation of Lakota ceremony by outsiders who sold imitations of it. Christians have no business adding a devotional wing to that market. What we can do is harder and slower. We can let Native Christians and Native critics show us what Scripture already says and what we stopped hearing, and we can let them tell us where we have mistaken our own culture for the gospel. Learning of that kind doesn't add a practice to our lives. It takes an assumption away.

## The Christians who were already there

In 1759 the Presbytery of Suffolk, on Long Island, ordained a Mohegan man named Samson Occom to the gospel ministry. He had been born in 1723 in Mohegan country near New London, Connecticut, and came to faith as a teenager in the revivals of the early 1740s. He studied with the Congregationalist minister Eleazar Wheelock at Lebanon, Connecticut, and spent roughly a decade among the Montaukett people of eastern Long Island as schoolmaster and preacher. In 1765 Wheelock sent him to Britain to raise money for a school for Native students, and Occom preached across England and Scotland and raised a great deal. Wheelock used much of it to move his work to New Hampshire, where in 1769 it became Dartmouth College, a school that educated mostly white young men. Occom wrote to his old teacher with open bitterness about what had been done with the gifts he had gathered.

He left his own account of the other slights. In *A Short Narrative of My Life*, written in 1768, Occom set down that the missionary societies had paid him far less than white missionaries doing the same work, and said in substance that he could find no reason for it except that he was an Indian. In 1772 he preached at the execution of a Native man named Moses Paul, and the sermon, printed that year, went through many editions and became one of the most widely read works published by a Native author in the eighteenth century. Then, in the 1780s, he helped lead Christian families from several Native communities in southern New England and on Long Island to land offered by the Oneida in New York, where they founded a town called Brothertown in 1785. It was meant as a place where Native Christians could live as Native people and as Christians without being slowly dispossessed by the colonial towns around them. Occom died in 1792.

His life should reset the question of this essay. Occom was not the object of anyone's mission when he did his most important work. He was an ordained minister of the gospel and a critic of the church from inside it. The faith was his. The institutions were not, and they made sure he knew it. What he suffered was not, in the first place, the destruction of his culture, though that pressure was real. It was the church's refusal to let a Native Christian be a full brother, paid as one, trusted as one and allowed to lead as one. That failure is less dramatic than a massacre and more durable, and most churches in this country have never repented of it because they have never noticed it.

The pattern ran through the colonial centuries. John Eliot, the Puritan minister at Roxbury, gathered Native converts into so-called praying towns beginning with Natick in 1651, and in 1663 he published a complete Bible in the Massachusett language, the first Bible printed in the Americas, produced with Native collaborators whose skill made it possible. When King Philip's War broke out in 1675, Massachusetts authorities rounded up many of those same Native Christians and confined them on Deer Island in Boston Harbor, where a great number died of hunger and cold that winter. The colony had translated the Scriptures into their language, and then it treated them as enemies because of their faces. In the Catholic north, a young Mohawk woman named Kateri Tekakwitha, who lived from 1656 to 1680, was baptized, left her village under pressure from relatives who resented her faith, and died at a Christian mission near Montreal, and her own people remembered her as holy long before Rome did. She was canonized in 2012.

The critics belong in this family too. Vine Deloria Jr. (1933 to 2005), a member of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe, was the son and grandson of Episcopal priests who served among the Dakota and Lakota, and he trained in theology at a Lutheran seminary before turning to law. He knew the church from the rectory. In 1969 his *Custer Died for Your Sins* skewered anthropologists, bureaucrats and missionaries alike, and in 1973, the year of the armed occupation at Wounded Knee, he published *God Is Red*, still one of the most serious Native critiques of Christianity written in English. Its central argument is a distinction. Christianity, Deloria argued, is a religion of time: its God acts in history, in a sequence of events that can be told anywhere, and so it travels light and can be carried into any country. The traditions of the peoples he knew are religions of space: their sacred is bound to particular places, particular mountains and rivers and springs, and to the particular people who belong to those places. A religion of time, he argued, can leave the land behind, and so it can treat land as real estate. A religion of space cannot.

A Christian who wants to learn from Native traditions should read Deloria first, because he refuses to be flattered or patronized. He also wasn't entirely right, and the place where he was wrong turns out to be one of the most useful things he taught.

## Land as gift, not inventory

Deloria was right about Western Christianity and wrong about the Bible, and the difference is the first lesson. Four years after *God Is Red*, the Old Testament scholar Walter Brueggemann published *The Land* (1977), arguing that land is a central theme of biblical faith, perhaps the central one, and that modern theology had badly misread Scripture by treating Israel's faith as a matter of history and inner decision that floated free of any ground. He traced the story as a drama of land: Abraham called toward a country he would never own, Israel landless in Egypt and the wilderness, the land received as gift, grasped as possession, lost in exile and promised again. The Bible, read on its own terms, is saturated with place. Its God is remembered by the places where he met people.

Jacob, running for his life, sleeps with his head on a stone at a place he doesn't know, dreams of a ladder, and wakes to a discovery about the ground beneath him:

> "Then Jacob awoke from his sleep and said, 'Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it.'" (Genesis 28:16, ESV)

He sets up the stone, pours oil on it and names the place Bethel, the house of God. Moses, tending sheep in the wilderness, turns aside to look at a bush that burns and is told, "take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground" (Exodus 3:5, ESV). Israel's law assumes that the people will know their land field by field, that its boundary stones are sacred, that it must rest every seventh year, and that no one may sell it forever because it belongs to God. And when God came to us in person, he came to a place: "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14, ESV). He grew up in one Galilean village and was executed outside the walls of one city. The incarnation is the most local event in history.

So Deloria's distinction between time and space describes a Christianity that forgot its own Scriptures. It is a fair description of the Christianity that crossed the Atlantic. The theologian Willie James Jennings argues in *The Christian Imagination* (2010) that the colonial encounter tore Christian identity loose from place. The settler's faith was carried in the settler's body and imagined as portable and universal, while the peoples whose identity was bound to particular lands were displaced from them, and land itself was remade into property, measured, surveyed and sold. Jennings's point isn't that Christians happened to be greedy. It's that a way of thinking about God took shape in which place no longer mattered to faith, and once place doesn't matter, land becomes inventory. The legal side of that story belongs to [the essay on the doctrine of discovery](/writing/doctrine-of-discovery). The spiritual side is ours to examine now.

I have to be careful here, because I am describing my own habits and those of the churches I know best. We choose church sites by traffic counts and demographic studies. We buy and sell sanctuaries like any other commercial property. Most of us couldn't name the people who lived on the land beneath our buildings, or the treaty by which it passed into American hands. We speak of heaven as though the point of the gospel were to get out of here. None of that is deliberate heresy. It is a faith that has stopped noticing where it stands.

Native traditions, in their different ways, notice. Traditional Haudenosaunee gatherings open and close with a long recitation known in Mohawk as Ohén:ton Karihwatéhkwen, usually translated as the Words That Come Before All Else, and in English as the Thanksgiving Address. The speaker greets and gives thanks, in order, to the people gathered, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants, the food plants, the medicine plants, the animals, the trees, the birds, the winds, the thunderers who bring rain, the sun, the moon, the stars, the teachers who were sent, and the Creator. Each section closes with words meaning that now our minds are one. Compare the command Moses gave Israel on the edge of the land: "And you shall eat and be full, and you shall bless the LORD your God for the good land he has given you" (Deuteronomy 8:10, ESV). The verses that follow warn that fullness breeds forgetting. The Thanksgiving Address is, among other things, a discipline against that forgetting, and most of our table graces are not.

The romance has to be refused here too. In *The Ecological Indian* (1999), the anthropologist Shepard Krech III argued that the image of Native peoples as natural conservationists living in perfect balance is a myth, one that erases real peoples who, like every people, changed and sometimes damaged the places they lived. His critics, Native scholars among them, answered that he read the evidence selectively and missed where the ethic actually lives, in relationships of obligation to particular places and creatures rather than in a record of flawless outcomes. Both sides make a point worth keeping. No people is innocent, and the Native elder on the poster is as much a caricature as the heathen in the missionary tract. What Christians can learn is not an innocence but a posture: land received as a gift and a relative, owed gratitude and care, rather than land held as a commodity owed nothing.

That posture is ours by right, and we lost it. Genesis puts the first human in the garden to work it and keep it (Genesis 2:15). Paul says that "the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now" (Romans 8:22, ESV), and that the blood of the cross reconciles "all things, whether on earth or in heaven" (Colossians 1:20, ESV). The biblical case is laid out at length in [the essay on caring for creation](/writing/creation-care-not-optional). The church didn't need Native peoples to invent a theology of land. It needed them to remind it that it already had one, and for five centuries it wasn't listening.

## Relatives, generations and the drum

The second lesson concerns kinship. Lakota prayers often close with the words *Mitákuye Oyás'iŋ*, usually rendered "all my relatives," a phrase that places the one praying inside a web of relationship reaching to family, people, animals, the land and the generations before and after. Ella Deloria, Vine's aunt, a Dakota linguist and the daughter of an Episcopal priest, described Dakota society in *Speaking of Indians* (1944) as organized around a single demand: that a person be a good relative, keeping the obligations kinship laid on every relationship. Randy Woodley, a theologian of Cherokee descent, argues in *Shalom and the Community of Creation* (2012) that many Native peoples share a vision he calls the Harmony Way, a life of right relation with the Creator, with other people and with the rest of creation, and that it stands remarkably close to the Hebrew *shalom*, which means far more than the absence of conflict. Western Christianity, Woodley contends, shrank shalom to a private peace between the soul and God.

He has a point, and Paul makes it before him. The church is a body in which "the eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you'" (1 Corinthians 12:21, ESV), and the believer is surrounded by "so great a cloud of witnesses" (Hebrews 12:1, ESV) who died in faith before us. Israel was taught to think in generations. When the people crossed the Jordan, Joshua set up twelve stones from the riverbed "that this may be a sign among you. When your children ask in time to come, 'What do those stones mean to you?'" (Joshua 4:6, ESV). A place, a story and a question from the grandchildren: that is how memory was meant to work. The principle widely associated with the Haudenosaunee, that a decision should be weighed by its effect on the seventh generation, would have made sense to Joshua. It makes little sense to a church that plans in fiscal years and treats faith as a decision made alone.

The third lesson is about story and ceremony, and here Richard Twiss is the essential witness. Twiss (1954 to 2013), a Sicangu Lakota enrolled in the Rosebud Sioux Tribe, came to faith in Christ as a young man and spent his life insisting that he did not have to stop being Lakota to follow Jesus. In *Rescuing the Gospel from the Cowboys*, published in 2015 after his death, he argued that generations of missionaries required Native converts to abandon the drum, the dance, traditional song and regalia as pagan, while treating the organ, the hymn tune, the pew and the Sunday suit as though they were neutral. The result taught Native people that Christianity was the white man's religion, and Native Christians to be ashamed of their grandparents. He called for worship of Christ in Native forms, what he called the Jesus Way.

The missiologist Paul Hiebert, who had served as a missionary in India, explained why the older approach failed in an essay titled "The Flaw of the Excluded Middle" (1982). Western missionaries, he argued, carried an Enlightenment picture of the world with two floors: religion above, dealing with God and eternal salvation, and science below, dealing with natural causes. The middle floor, where most people in most cultures actually live, the realm of spirits, ancestors, blessing, curse, sickness and healing, had no place in it. So converts heard a gospel with nothing to say about their daily fears, kept it for Sunday, and went back to the old practitioners on Monday. Hiebert admitted he had been blind to that middle himself. Much of what missionaries called syncretism, he suggested, was the fruit of a gospel preached too small. The Bible has no such blind spot; it is full of powers and principalities, healings and the dead. Its faith is also carried chiefly in story and in embodied acts, water, bread, wine, oil and hands, as [the essay on physical things in worship](/writing/why-faith-uses-physical-things) argues. Native traditions that teach through story and through ceremony involving the whole body can remind a church of words and ideas what kind of book it holds.

## Where the confessions part

Honesty requires saying where the ways divide, and saying it without contempt. The Christian confession is that God has one mediator: "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). Christians pray to the Creator through Christ and by the Spirit, and not through spirits, ancestors or powers. They honor the dead in Christ as a cloud of witnesses, not as intercessors to be addressed. Many traditional practitioners will hear that claim as the same arrogance that outlawed their ceremonies, and they have reasons: the federal Code of Indian Offenses of 1883 banned the Sun Dance and other rites, and not until the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 did Congress declare that Native religious practice deserved protection. A claim once enforced by federal agents can't expect a hearing as good news on the first telling.

But the claim itself isn't contempt, and Paul shows why. In Lystra he told pagans that God "did not leave himself without witness, for he did good by giving you rains from heaven and fruitful seasons, satisfying your hearts with food and gladness" (Acts 14:17, ESV). In Athens he began with their altar "To the unknown god" and said, "What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you" (Acts 17:23, ESV). Then he called them to repent. The Christian confession is not that Native peoples knew nothing of God before the ships came. It is that the Creator they knew in rain and harvest has shown his face in a crucified Jewish man, and that this same man judges every culture, beginning with the one that carried his name here.

Two debates follow, and both deserve fair statement. The first concerns the Native American Church, incorporated in Oklahoma in 1918, whose all-night prayer meetings gather around a fire and a crescent-shaped altar, with peyote received as a sacrament that members understand as medicine and a means of prayer. Some of its ways speak of Jesus and use the Bible; others far less. Many members credit it with bringing them out of alcohol, which it forbids. When Oregon denied unemployment benefits to Alfred Smith, a Klamath member fired for sacramental peyote use, the Supreme Court upheld the state in *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990), and Congress protected the practice by statute in 1994. Christians who confess one mediator have to ask whether the sacrament is understood as a second channel of divine power alongside Christ, and members themselves answer that differently. An outsider should ask it without pretending to answer for everyone.

The second is the contextualization debate that Twiss and Woodley opened among evangelicals. Critics, many of them Native pastors who had left ceremonial life at real cost, argued that the drum, the smudge and the sweat lodge carry meanings that don't wash out, and that Paul himself warned: "what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God" (1 Corinthians 10:20, ESV). Twiss's defenders answer that the same chapter tells believers to "Eat whatever is sold in the meat market without raising any question on the ground of conscience" (1 Corinthians 10:25, ESV), because the earth is the Lord's. Paul held both. Hiebert's essay "Critical Contextualization" (1984) proposed a method between rejection and absorption: a congregation studies its own customs and the Scriptures together and decides, under the Word, what to keep, what to transform and what to leave.

The order of the questions matters. Christ as the one mediator is first-order, part of the confession itself. Which cultural forms may carry worship of Christ is second-order, a matter on which faithful churches differ. This essay lands with Twiss: there is no theological basis for treating European forms as neutral and Native forms as contaminated, and every form, the organ included, must pass the same test under Scripture. The strongest objection hasn't been fully answered. Forms aren't empty, and a Native believer who gave up ceremony for Christ deserves not to be told his sacrifice was prejudice. The Potawatomi writer Kaitlin Curtice, in *Native* (2020), shows the other side of that cost, describing how reclaiming her people's identity became bound up with finding God again, and how much it costs when a church asks Native people to shed who they are. The people best placed to decide are Native churches themselves. Those of us outside owe them room to disagree and remain brothers and sisters.

## The glory of the nations

At the end of the canon John sees the holy city come down from heaven, and he says of it: "They will bring into it the glory and the honor of the nations" (Revelation 21:26, ESV). Richard Mouw, reading that vision alongside Isaiah 60 in *When the Kings Come Marching In* (1983), argued that the cultural goods of the peoples are not simply destroyed at the end. They are judged, cleansed and carried in. If he is right, Lakota kinship, Haudenosaunee thanksgiving and Kiowa hymns are not rubble the gospel has to clear. They may be among the gifts the nations carry through the gate, purified as every European gift will have to be purified, and by the same Lamb.

That hope runs through the cross and not around it. The reconciliation of all things comes "by the blood of his cross" (Colossians 1:20, ESV), and no circle of relatives, Native or European, restores without it what sin has broken. But the cross also judges the church that carried it onto this continent as a banner of possession.

Joshua's stones are still the right question. Our churches are stones set on land, and our children will ask in time to come what they mean. An honest answer will have to include Samson Occom, underpaid and preaching anyway; Native Christians starving on an island in Boston Harbor at the hands of fellow Christians; Lakota believers told that their drums belonged to the devil; and a land that others thanked field by field while we measured it for sale. Those Native Christians and their critics were telling us what our own book said. We are late in hearing it. Whether the church can still become a good relative on this ground isn't something an essay can settle. It will be settled, if at all, in the places where we stand.
