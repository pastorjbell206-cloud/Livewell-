Open your banking app and look at the number. Something moves in you before you've decided what you think about it: relief, a small flush of pride, or the low hum of not-enough that follows so many people around like a draft under a door. The number is only a number, and yet it produced a feeling. Money is never just arithmetic. It is one of the places where the heart is most exposed, which is why most of us would rather talk about anything else.

Christians usually come to this subject wanting a ruling. Is it wrong to be rich? How much should I give? This essay will reach those questions, but Scripture answers them from underneath. It keeps asking a prior question. Is money a tool, a neutral instrument that takes its moral color from whoever holds it? Or is it a master, a power with claims of its own, offering what only God can finally give? Most of us live as if the first answer were obviously true. Jesus spoke as if the second were.

**The claim of this essay is that the Bible treats wealth as a genuine gift, a serious danger and a rival lord, all at once, and that the Christian life with money depends on one distinction: money as a tool that serves the love of God and neighbor, and money as a master that promises security. The same coins can be either. What decides it is not the size of the balance but what we are trusting it to do.**

I write as a pastor who pays the same bills and feels the same pull as anyone reading. This is not financial advice. It is an argument about worship; for budgets, debts, taxes and investments, talk with a qualified professional.

## A gift that comes with a warning

Start where Israel started, on the edge of a land it had not yet farmed. In Deuteronomy 8 Moses looks past the wilderness toward the houses and herds to come, and he tells the people where their wealth will come from and what it will tempt them to believe: "Beware lest you say in your heart, 'My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth.' You shall remember the LORD your God, for it is he who gives you power to get wealth, that he may confirm his covenant that he swore to your fathers, as it is this day" (Deuteronomy 8:17–18, ESV). The blessing and the warning arrive in the same sentence, and the danger Moses names is forgetting. Full barns, he says, will tempt the heart to "be lifted up," and then "you forget the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 8:14, ESV). The wilderness had taught dependence one day at a time. The land would tempt Israel toward independence one harvest at a time.

The wisdom books keep both halves. Proverbs does not blush at plenty, yet it sets two refuges side by side and lets the reader see the difference: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous man runs into it and is safe. A rich man's wealth is his strong city, and like a high wall in his imagination" (Proverbs 18:10–11, ESV). The wall is real enough to build and imaginary as protection. Then comes the prayer of Agur: "give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me, lest I be full and deny you and say, 'Who is the LORD?' or lest I be poor and steal and profane the name of my God" (Proverbs 30:8–9, ESV). Agur fears both ends of the scale for the same reason. Each can pry a person loose from God, one through the arrogance of having and the other through the desperation of lacking.

The New Testament scholar Craig Blomberg took his title from that prayer. In *Neither Poverty Nor Riches* (1999) he walked the whole canon and found something more textured than either the prosperity preacher or the earnest ascetic wants to hear: wealth is a good gift and a spiritual danger at once, and the danger rises with the gift. Abraham was rich, and Jesus' own ministry was carried by "Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod's household manager, and Susanna, and many others, who provided for them out of their means" (Luke 8:3, ESV). Scripture never says simply that wealth is evil. It asks whether, having wealth, you will remember who gave it.

## "You cannot serve God and money"

Jesus pressed on that ancient balance in the Sermon on the Mount: "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys and where thieves do not break in and steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:19–21, ESV). Notice the direction of the last line. We assume the heart leads and the money follows. Jesus says the heart follows the treasure. Put your security somewhere, and your love will move in after it.

Then the verdict: "No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money" (Matthew 6:24, ESV). Behind "money" stands *mammon*, a plain Semitic noun for what a person owns. In Jesus' mouth the plain noun becomes a proper name, a lord standing across from God, and the service he describes is a slave's, the kind that cannot be split between owners. He does not say you should not serve both. He says you cannot. Matthew then hinges the passage on a single word: "Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life" (Matthew 6:25, ESV). Mammon and worry share a paragraph because worry is the wage mammon pays. Money promises to make tomorrow safe, and whoever believes it spends a lifetime guarding the thing that was supposed to bring peace.

Mark tells of a man who had kept the commandments from his youth and knelt to ask Jesus how to inherit eternal life. "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me.' Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:21–22, ESV). Jesus found the place where the man's security actually rested and asked for it. The disciples were amazed, likely because they assumed, as much of their world did, that wealth was a mark of God's favor, and Jesus widened the saying rather than softening it: "Children, how difficult it is to enter the kingdom of God!" (Mark 10:24, ESV). When they asked who could be saved, he answered, "With man it is impossible, but not with God. For all things are possible with God" (Mark 10:27, ESV).

Luke sets his hardest story inside a quarrel over money. Someone asks Jesus to make his brother divide the inheritance, and Jesus answers the man who feels shortchanged with a warning to everyone listening: "Take care, and be on your guard against all covetousness, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15, ESV). Only then does he tell of the farmer whose land "produced plentifully," who resolved to build larger barns and tell his soul to relax for many years (Luke 12:16–19, ESV). Nothing says he cheated anyone. What condemns the plan is what it was for. The barns were a wall against the future, and the future came that night: "Fool! This night your soul is required of you, and the things you have prepared, whose will they be?" (Luke 12:20, ESV). A few verses later Jesus names the alternative, and it is a Father: "Fear not, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom" (Luke 12:32, ESV).

Luke also shows that the camel can get through. Soon after "What is impossible with man is possible with God" (Luke 18:27, ESV), Jesus invites himself into the house of Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector grown rich on collaboration, before Zacchaeus has promised anything. Only afterward comes the response: "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8, ESV). Fourfold was what the law exacted for a sheep stolen and slaughtered (Exodus 22:1), far beyond the added fifth it required of someone who had defrauded a neighbor (Leviticus 6:5). Zacchaeus holds himself to the harsher standard, gladly. Jesus, who asked the man in Mark for everything, asks Zacchaeus for nothing more and says, "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9, ESV). Grace came through his door first, and the open hand followed.

## Tool or master: the fathers' long argument

The church has argued about these texts from the beginning. Around the year 200, Clement of Alexandria wrote *Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?* for wealthy Christians who had heard Mark 10 read aloud and despaired. His answer is the tool view at its best. Jesus, he argued, was not commanding the man to throw his property away but to root out of his soul the passions that cling to property. Wealth is an instrument, and an instrument can be used well or badly. Clement adds a point that is hard to dismiss: if everyone gave away everything at once, who would be left to feed the hungry or shelter the stranger? Much of this is right. Jesus did not ask Zacchaeus or Joanna to sell everything, and Paul told Timothy to instruct the rich, not to stop being rich, but to be "rich in good works" (1 Timothy 6:18, ESV). Nor was Clement a flatterer; he insisted that the rich be poor in spirit and generous in fact.

The trouble is that the tool view is exactly what a heart ruled by money would like to believe. During the famine that struck Cappadocia around 368, Basil of Caesarea preached on the rich fool in the homily known by the farmer's words, "I Will Tear Down My Barns." Basil fastens on the farmer's question, "What shall I do?" (Luke 12:17, ESV), and marvels that he had to ask, since the answer stood hungry outside his gate. He compares wealth to a well: drawn from, the water stays sweet and flows freely; left alone, it goes stagnant. He also lets the rich man make his defense, that he wrongs no one by keeping what is his own, and he refuses it. Basil does not deny that wealth can be used well. He denies that the man holding it is a reliable judge of whether he is using it well, because the heart that owns the barns is the one issuing the verdict.

John Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch in the late fourth century, took the argument to the story of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31). In his sermons on that parable he likens wealth and poverty to the masks actors wear in the theater: one player comes on as a king and another as a beggar, and when the evening ends the masks come off and each is only what he is. Death ends the play. What the rich man had worn as his identity was only ever a costume, and it stayed behind when he left the stage. Preaching like that helped set the imperial court against him, and it ended, years later in Constantinople, in exile in 404.

In 1954 the French sociologist and lay theologian Jacques Ellul gave this old argument a modern name. In *Money and Power* (English translation 1984) he argued that Jesus' personification of mammon is no figure of speech. Money functions as a power, bending those who handle it toward its own logic, in which everything can be bought and every relationship priced. Ellul's counsel was neither to worship it nor to run from it. Christians are to desacralize it, and the instrument he names is the gift that expects nothing back, because a gift is the one transaction money cannot put a price on.

Put Clement beside Ellul and the distinction this essay turns on comes into focus. The tool view is true about the coins and often false about the heart. A tool makes no promises, so the test is not how much money you have or how carefully you manage it, but what the money is promising you. When it begins to promise safety, standing, freedom from the chances of a fragile life, a self worth being, it has stopped acting like a tool and started acting like a lord. A hammer has never told anyone that tomorrow would be safe.

## What Paul told the rich and the rest of us

Paul's clearest words on money come in 1 Timothy: "But godliness with contentment is great gain, for we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world. But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content. But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (1 Timothy 6:6–10, ESV). Paul never said that money is the root of all evil. The love of it is a root of all kinds of evils, and his warning is aimed less at the rich than at "those who desire to be rich," which describes nearly everyone who has felt the hum of not-enough. The danger lives in the one who wants long before it reaches the one who has.

Then Paul turns to those who already are rich, and steers between two errors: "As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy. They are to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share, thus storing up treasure for themselves as a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of that which is truly life" (1 Timothy 6:17–19, ESV). Enjoyment is permitted. What is forbidden is asking wealth to be the foundation it can never be, and the real foundation is laid by giving it away.

The virtue at the center of the passage is contentment, and it's easy to mistake. It isn't resignation, and it certainly isn't complacency; the comfortable are often complacent and rarely content. Paul calls it something learned: "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content" (Philippians 4:11, ESV). The Puritan Jeremiah Burroughs, in *The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment* (1648), called it a mystery the world cannot teach, and observed that the Christian reaches it less by adding to what he has than by subtracting from what he craves. An economy built on stimulated desire cannot afford to let anyone learn that arithmetic, because "He who loves money will not be satisfied with money" (Ecclesiastes 5:10, ESV).

Paul's fullest teaching on giving comes in 2 Corinthians 8 and 9, written to gather money for poor believers in Jerusalem, and he begins with the poor. The Macedonian churches, "in a severe test of affliction, their abundance of joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part" (2 Corinthians 8:2, ESV). The ground of their giving is one sentence: "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). The measure is proportion: "it is acceptable according to what a person has, not according to what he does not have" (2 Corinthians 8:12, ESV). The goal is fairness, "your abundance at the present time should supply their need" (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV), and the spirit is freedom: "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7, ESV).

The biblical scholar Gary Anderson has shown how concretely the first readers heard this. In *Sin: A History* (2009) he traces how Second Temple Judaism pictured sin as a debt and almsgiving as treasure deposited in heaven, and in *Charity* (2013) he takes up the proverb "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD, and he will repay him for his deed" (Proverbs 19:17, ESV), and argues that for ancient Jews and Christians a gift to the poor was an act of faith in the kind of world God made, a world whose Creator is generous and can be trusted. When Jesus promised the rich young man "treasure in heaven," he was telling him his accounts were held in the wrong bank.

## Wesley's three rules and the problem of success

No Protestant took Paul more practically than John Wesley. In 1760 he published "The Use of Money," a sermon on Jesus' strange counsel to "make friends for yourselves by means of unrighteous wealth" (Luke 16:9, ESV). Like Clement, he refuses to despise money; the fault, he argues, lies in those who use it, and in the hands of God's children it is food for the hungry and clothing for the naked. His counsel comes in three rules: "Gain all you can," "Save all you can," "Give all you can." The first is fenced by what it may not cost: health of body, soundness of mind, or harm to a neighbor. The second is not a license to hoard; Wesley meant cutting off the spending that feeds the desire of the flesh, the desire of the eye and the pride of life. The third is the purpose of the other two. A man who gains and saves and stops there, Wesley warns, has done nothing, and money merely laid up might as well be thrown into the sea. His biographers tell how, as a young fellow at Oxford, he fixed what he would live on and gave away every increase in his income. When the excise commissioners asked in 1776 for a return of his household silver, he answered, in substance, that he had a few silver teaspoons and would buy no more while so many around him wanted bread.

Wesley also saw the trap in his own success. In "Thoughts upon Methodism" (1786) he worried that true religion produces diligence and frugality, that these produce riches, and that as riches increase so do pride, anger and love of the world, until the form of religion remains while its spirit drains away. His people had learned the first two rules well. The third kept slipping. Max Weber quoted that passage in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) as a confession at the center of his thesis: the disciplined Protestant helped build the modern economy, and once built, the economy no longer needed the faith that produced it. What had been worn as a light cloak became, in his translator's phrase, an iron cage.

In 1714 Bernard Mandeville published *The Fable of the Bees*, subtitled *Private Vices, Publick Benefits*, arguing that greed and vanity drive public prosperity and that a hive of honest bees would be a poor one. We did not reject Paul's diagnosis. We inverted it, and came to treat the craving he called a root of evils as the fuel of the common good.

That inversion has two faces, and they need to be judged with the same instrument. The first is consumer comfort, the quiet belief that whatever a careful life has accumulated was earned by prudence and testifies to it. It rarely boasts. It builds larger barns and calls them responsibility. The second face is envy, and it belongs to no single party. Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, question 36), defined envy as sorrow at another's good, perceived as a loss to oneself, and it wears the clothes of justice as easily as comfort wears the clothes of prudence. That is why, in Luke 12, Jesus warned against "all covetousness" in answer to the brother who felt cheated, before he told the story of the man who had too much. None of this reduces the Bible's concern for the poor to envy; the prophets are full of that concern, and it binds every Christian. But the heart that believes someone else's portion would finally make it whole serves the same master as the heart that trusts its own barns. One worships money it has. The other worships money it lacks.

I can't write that from a safe distance. I have a family. I have five sons. The reaching wears the costume of love and is sometimes only fear, and I've dressed up anxiety about the future as prudence more often than I'd like to admit. The instrument that measures the comfortable and the envious measures the preacher first.

## Debt, the tithe and the practices of freedom

Two practical questions remain. The first is debt, and Scripture is realistic about it: "The rich rules over the poor, and the borrower is the slave of the lender" (Proverbs 22:7, ESV). It also assumes that borrowing will happen. The law forbade charging interest to a needy neighbor (Exodus 22:25), and the Psalms judge the borrower not for borrowing but for failing to repay: "The wicked borrows but does not pay back, but the righteous is generous and gives" (Psalm 37:21, ESV). Paul's line is often read as a ban on all borrowing, but its setting is a command to pay what is owed: "Pay to all what is owed to them... Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (Romans 13:7–8, ESV). He isn't legislating against a mortgage. He is saying that the only debt a Christian carries forever is love.

The useful distinction is between debt that serves a household's real needs and debt that buys present comfort by pledging future freedom. The second is mammon's promise running in reverse: it offers today now and makes the borrower answerable for years he has not yet lived. If you are carrying debt you can't see your way out of, the shame usually weighs more than the numbers, and the first step is to put the full amount in front of someone you trust and in front of a reputable nonprofit credit counselor who can help you build a plan. Again, this is not financial advice. And if money trouble has brought you to despair, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

The second question is the tithe, and here faithful Christians genuinely disagree. It is a second-order question. It divides sincere, orthodox churches without touching the creeds, and each view deserves its strongest form.

The case that the tithe still binds begins before Sinai. Abraham gave a tenth to Melchizedek (Genesis 14:20) and Jacob vowed a tenth at Bethel (Genesis 28:22) centuries before the law of Moses, so the practice cannot be dismissed as merely ceremonial. Malachi rebuked Israel for withholding it: "Bring the full tithe into the storehouse, that there may be food in my house" (Malachi 3:10, ESV). Jesus, rebuking the Pharisees for neglecting justice and mercy, did not abolish their tithing: "These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others" (Matthew 23:23, ESV). Hebrews 7 recalls Abraham's tithe to Melchizedek, whose priesthood Christ now holds. And a fixed proportion guards a disciple against the self-deception this essay has been describing. "Give as you decide" easily becomes "give what's left," and the tithe keeps the first portion first.

The case for grace-giving beyond the tithe answers that Israel's tithes were several, not one (Numbers 18:21; Deuteronomy 14:22–29), tied to the land, the temple and the Levites, and together came to more than a tenth. Jesus spoke Matthew 23 to Pharisees under the law before the cross, and Hebrews 7 is an argument about priesthood, not a rule for giving. Most tellingly, when Paul raises money in 2 Corinthians 8 and 9, and when he tells the Corinthians to set something aside "on the first day of every week... as he may prosper" (1 Corinthians 16:2, ESV), he never mentions the tithe. He grounds giving in Christ's grace and measures it by proportion and freedom. Irenaeus, in *Against Heresies* (c. 180), contrasted Israel's consecrated tithes with the liberty of Christians who set all their possessions at the Lord's disposal. The tithe returned later as church law: the Council of Mâcon in 585 threatened excommunication for withholding it, Charlemagne made it compulsory in 779, and in much of Europe it remained a tax until France abolished it in August 1789. Those who hold this view worry that a ten percent rule becomes a burden for the poor and a ceiling for the comfortable.

Each side carries an objection it hasn't fully answered. The tithe advocate must explain why the apostle most concerned with collections never used the obvious argument. The grace-giving advocate must reckon with how often "freedom" has meant giving less than a tenth, not more. What they share matters more than where they part: giving that is regular, proportionate, first rather than last, cheerful, and never less than the old covenant asked of people who had far less. Christians land differently here and remain brothers and sisters.

## The one who became poor

Every practice in this essay is a way of refusing the promise money keeps making, and none of them works as a technique. You cannot budget your way out of serving mammon. The grip loosens from another direction.

Paul named that direction when he grounded the whole collection in Christ, who "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). The Son of God had nowhere to lay his head and died with his garments divided among the soldiers who crucified him. He built no wall against the future. He entrusted himself to the Father, and the Father raised him from the dead. That is the answer to mammon's promise. The security money offers is real the way a high wall in the imagination is real, and the security it cannot offer has already been given, at the cost of the only one who was ever truly rich. Giving does not purchase that safety. It practices it, and each gift is a small act of trust in a Father who has already provided the one thing money could never buy.

Pastors learn at gravesides that money keeps its promises and breaks them in the same motion. It delivers the house. It does not deliver the person who can live in it. Luke 12 ends the farmer's story with a verdict rather than a moral: "So is the one who lays up treasure for himself and is not rich toward God" (Luke 12:21, ESV). Chrysostom's theater closes on everyone. The balance stays behind with the costume. What leaves is a person who was rich toward God or was not.

So look at the number again. The question it puts to you was never how much you have or how much you need. It is whom you serve, and the answer is written less in what you say about God than in what you are counting on that number to do. Mammon will take you on any terms and never tell you when you have enough. Christ has already paid for you in full. That settles your worth. It does not settle what you will do with the money this month, or with the knock at the gate from someone who needs what you have and is already waiting for your answer.
