Say the word *reparations* in most churches and the room sorts itself before anyone opens a Bible. One person hears a demand to pay for sins he never committed. Another hears the first honest sentence about American history anyone has spoken in the building. Both reactions come at the speed of reflex, and the reflex is partisan rather than scriptural. That is worth noticing, because the Bible says more about restitution, in more careful detail, than about most questions American evangelicals have organized their politics around.

Two questions hide inside the one people type into a search bar. The first is moral: does a people owe anything for wrongs whose authors are dead, when the results are still on the books? The second is political: should the federal government pay, and if so whom, how much and through what program? Scripture speaks with unusual precision to the first and leaves the second largely to prudence, and its precision supplies the distinction the argument turns on. Restitution answers a concrete, traceable harm: a named wrong, an identifiable party, a measurable loss, something still held that ought to be returned. Diffuse guilt is a mood of inherited stain attached to a group, which no payment can discharge and no confession can end. The Bible commands the first and forbids the second, and much of our quarrel comes from confusing them.

The argument lands in four places. Restitution is a biblical obligation, not a borrowed slogan. The history is real, recent and still compounding. The design of any national remedy is a second-order, prudential question on which faithful Christians divide in good conscience. And the most honest place for the church to begin is not Congress. It is the church's own ledgers.

## What the law of restitution actually required

What most scholars take to be the oldest body of civil law in the Bible, the Book of the Covenant in Exodus 21 to 23, is less interested in punishing thieves than in making victims whole. When a man stole, the remedy was paid to the person he robbed, and the law set the rate with care: "If a man steals an ox or a sheep, and kills it or sells it, he shall repay five oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep" (Exodus 22:1, ESV). Three verses later the scale drops: "If the stolen beast is found alive in his possession, whether an ox or a donkey or a sheep, he shall pay double" (Exodus 22:4, ESV). The difference is traceability. An animal still in the thief's pen can be handed back with a penalty added. An animal slaughtered or sold has vanished into someone else's economy. The ox's higher rate is usually explained by its work: its theft took the next season's plowing too. The law priced in what the loss went on costing. It understood compounding before anyone had a word for it.

The chapter then turns from theft to negligence without letting the obligation go: "If fire breaks out and catches in thorns so that the stacked grain or the standing grain or the field is consumed, he who started the fire shall make full restitution" (Exodus 22:6, ESV). No multiple is added, because no one intended the fire. But the grain is gone, and the man whose carelessness burned it still owes it.

Leviticus 6:1 to 5 takes up the case nobody caught. A man has cheated his neighbor over a deposit, taken something by robbery or oppression, or kept lost property and lied about it. No court has convicted him; he simply comes to realize his guilt. The law calls his wrong against his neighbor a breach of faith against God, and it sets the remedy: he returns the whole, adds a fifth, and pays it to the owner on the day he acknowledges the wrong, before he brings his offering to the altar. Jacob Milgrom, in his commentary *Leviticus 1-16* (1991), drew attention to the gap between the rates. A thief caught with the goods pays double; a man who comes forward pays the principal and a fifth. Milgrom read the difference as deliberate. The law makes confession cheaper than exposure, so that repentance itself reduces what is owed. God wanted the wrongdoer to come forward.

Numbers 5 restates the law and makes the order explicit. The one who has broken faith "shall confess his sin that he has committed. And he shall make full restitution for his wrong, adding a fifth to it and giving it to him to whom he did the wrong" (Numbers 5:7, ESV). Then comes a clause that bears directly on the modern objection that everyone involved is dead: "But if the man has no next of kin to whom restitution may be made for the wrong, the restitution for wrong shall go to the LORD for the priest, in addition to the ram of atonement with which atonement is made for him" (Numbers 5:8, ESV). The Torah anticipated the victim's death and refused to let the debt die with him. The payment passes to the kinsman, the *go'el*, and failing a kinsman, to God's house. The limit should be stated too: in Numbers the wrongdoer is still alive. The text settles that a debt survives its creditor. It doesn't settle whether it survives its debtor.

So restitution answers a named wrong to a traceable party, measured by what was lost and what the loss went on costing, graduated by intent, made cheaper by confession, and not cancelled by the victim's death. Zacchaeus understood the shape. His pledge contains two acts, and he kept them apart. Half his goods went to the poor, generosity toward a need no one could trace to him. And "if I have defrauded anyone of anything" (Luke 19:8, ESV), he would repay fourfold, the Exodus rate for a stolen sheep that couldn't be returned. Charity and restitution are different acts, and the first has never discharged the second.

## A debt that outlived the king who incurred it

The hardest question sits exactly where Numbers stops. Every American who held a slave is dead, and so is every person held. Scripture's answer to that fact is usually given in one verse, and it deserves its full weight: "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (Ezekiel 18:20, ESV). Deuteronomy 24:16 had already made the principle law for Israel's courts: children are not to be put to death for their fathers. No Christian may teach that a white child born in 2010 stands guilty before God for what a planter did in 1850. People who resist reparations on this ground are defending something true.

But there is a stranger text in the canon, and it has to be read alongside Ezekiel rather than hidden from him. Second Samuel 21 opens with a famine that won't lift: "Now there was a famine in the days of David for three years, year after year. And David sought the face of the LORD. And the LORD said, 'There is bloodguilt on Saul and on his house, because he put the Gibeonites to death'" (2 Samuel 21:1, ESV). The background is in Joshua 9, where Israel's leaders swore by the LORD to let the Gibeonites live. Saul later tried to wipe them out "in his zeal for the people of Israel and Judah" (2 Samuel 21:2, ESV). It was a state act, done by a king in the nation's name, and it broke a sworn national oath. Saul is dead when the famine comes, and David had no hand in the killing. Yet the debt surfaces in David's reign, and David must answer it.

He asks the survivors a question that could hang over every American hearing on this subject: "What shall I do for you? And how shall I make atonement, that you may bless the heritage of the LORD?" (2 Samuel 21:3, ESV). Their first reply is worth learning from: "It is not a matter of silver or gold between us and Saul or his house" (2 Samuel 21:4, ESV). What was broken was a promise and a people's standing, and a check alone can't mend that. What they asked for instead is harder. They demanded seven of Saul's descendants, and David handed them over to be killed, exactly the transfer of punishment from father to son that Deuteronomy forbids. The narrator doesn't commend it. He turns to Rizpah, the mother of two of the dead, who spread sackcloth on a rock and kept the birds and beasts from the bodies from harvest until the rains came. When David is told of her vigil, he goes to gather the bones of Saul and Jonathan, and the bones of the seven are gathered too, and Saul and Jonathan are buried in the tomb of Kish, Saul's father. Only then does the text speak of healing: "And after that God responded to the plea for the land" (2 Samuel 21:14, ESV).

Read with care, the passage teaches two things. A nation's broken promise doesn't expire when the administration that broke it leaves office; the obligation fell on whoever held the throne. And the payment must never land on the bodies of individual descendants as punishment; the canon closes that door. What remains is the obligation of the institution that made the promise and broke it, answered from what the institution still holds.

Christian moral theology drew a matching distinction long ago. Augustine, writing to the imperial official Macedonius around 414, gave the rule the medieval church kept: where stolen goods can be returned and aren't, repentance is feigned rather than real, and the sin isn't forgiven. Thomas Aquinas, treating restitution as an act of commutative justice in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, question 62, article 6), distinguished two roots of the obligation: the taking, and the thing taken. A man who received another's goods without any wrong, as when they were left with him on deposit, owes nothing by reason of how he got them. He still owes the goods themselves for as long as he has them. Ezekiel forbids inherited guilt, and Aquinas agrees. Neither suggests that inherited possessions are clean because the heir is. An heir can be wholly innocent and still be farming land that was never the seller's to sell.

## How the map was drawn

The American question has a concrete beginning, and the name of the bill at the center of today's debate points to it. On January 12, 1865, General William T. Sherman and Secretary of War Edwin Stanton met in Savannah with twenty Black ministers and church leaders and asked what the freed people needed. The answer was land. Four days later Sherman issued Special Field Order No. 15, setting aside coastal land in South Carolina and Georgia for freed families in plots of up to forty acres, and tens of thousands settled on it. By that autumn President Andrew Johnson had ordered the land returned to its former Confederate owners, and most of the families farming it were removed. The United States made a promise and broke it within months. The next century added interest.

Richard Rothstein's *The Color of Law* (2017) corrects the usual account of what followed, which calls residential segregation in the North and West *de facto*, the sum of private prejudice, preference and income. Rothstein argues from the documentary record that it was *de jure*, created and enforced by explicit public policy. The Home Owners' Loan Corporation, established in 1933, drew "residential security maps" in the later 1930s, shading many Black neighborhoods red. Economic historians still argue about how much those particular maps steered lending. The record of the Federal Housing Administration, created in 1934, is harder to dispute. Its underwriting manuals treated Black residents as a threat to property values, recommended racially restrictive covenants, and kept the insurance that built the American suburb largely out of Black hands. In 1941 in northwest Detroit, a developer built a concrete wall roughly half a mile long between his planned white subdivision and an existing Black neighborhood so that the FHA would insure the loans. The wall is still standing.

Ira Katznelson's *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005) shows the same pattern in the GI Bill of 1944, whose mortgage and education benefits were administered through local officials and banks in ways that largely shut Black veterans out, especially in the South. The Supreme Court made restrictive covenants unenforceable in *Shelley v. Kraemer* in 1948, and the Fair Housing Act outlawed housing discrimination in 1968. By then the suburbs had been built. Families allowed to buy in 1950 watched their equity compound for two generations; families kept out bought later, bought less, or bought on predatory terms. Ta-Nehisi Coates's "The Case for Reparations" (*The Atlantic*, 2014) built its argument around one such life: Clyde Ross, who came north from Mississippi and could buy a house in Chicago's North Lawndale only "on contract," from a seller who kept the deed and could evict him for a missed payment.

The result can be measured, though honestly. The Federal Reserve's 2019 Survey of Consumer Finances found median wealth of $188,200 for white families and $24,100 for Black families. Housing policy isn't the only cause, and scholars dispute how much weight belongs to income, education, family structure, inheritance and discrimination. But no honest account leaves housing out, because for most American families the house was the wealth. A gap built by law was later explained as a difference in character, and that substitution is its own injustice.

The church was inside all of this. Some white congregations organized to keep Black families out; far more followed their members to suburbs the policies had cleared for them and never asked how the suburb came to be white. Black congregations stayed in the redlined neighborhoods and held them together while banks, stores and city services withdrew. One church carried the cost of the map while another collected its dividends, and both learned to call the arrangement normal. I'm a white pastor in a denominational stream with its own history on these questions. I don't stand outside the story I'm telling.

## The case for reparations, and the case against

The case for reparations deserves the form its defenders would sign, and Coates gave it its most influential modern statement. The plunder of Black Americans, he argued, wasn't a single ancient crime but a continuous chain of policy running from slavery through Jim Crow, redlining and contract selling into living memory; many of those defrauded in North Lawndale were alive when he wrote. Coates insisted that reparations meant more than a check: a national reckoning in which the country accepts its whole biography, the shameful chapters with the celebrated ones. His concrete proposal was modest: he backed H.R. 40 and the national inquiry it would open.

John Conyers, the congressman from Detroit, first introduced that bill in 1989 and reintroduced it in every Congress until he left office in 2017. Its number recalls the forty acres. It authorizes no payment; it would create a commission to study slavery and the discrimination that followed and recommend remedies. In 2021 it cleared the House Judiciary Committee for the first time; the full House has not voted on it. Defenders note a precedent: the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, signed by Ronald Reagan, formally apologized to Japanese Americans interned during the Second World War and paid $20,000 to each surviving internee. William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen, in *From Here to Equality* (2020), took on the practical objections directly, proposing a federal program aimed at closing the wealth gap, with eligibility tied to descent from people enslaved in the United States. Earlier still, in *Why We Can't Wait* (1964), Martin Luther King Jr. pointed to the GI Bill as proof that the nation knew how to compensate a class of citizens, and he proposed a Bill of Rights for the Disadvantaged, framed to reach the white poor as well.

The case against deserves the same courtesy, and many of its strongest voices are Black. On June 19, 2019, at a House Judiciary subcommittee hearing on H.R. 40, Coates testified and so did Coleman Hughes, then a young writer and student. Hughes argued that the people owed for slavery are no longer alive and the living aren't entitled to collect their debts; that reparations would make Black Americans victims without their consent, including the many who opposed the idea; that putting a price on their ancestors' suffering would insult many of them; and that the effort would divide the country further and make it harder to build the coalitions that present problems require. What Black Americans needed, he said, was not another apology but safer neighborhoods, better schools, a less punitive criminal justice system and affordable health care. John McWhorter had pressed related points in "Against Reparations" (2001), arguing that the design was unworkable and the energy better spent on what helps people now. The practical questions aren't evasions. Who pays, when tens of millions descend from later immigrants or from Union soldiers? Who receives, when ancestry is mixed and many enslaved ancestors left no record? And would a payment be treated as a receipt, the account closed and the subject shut, while schools and lending and courts went on as before?

Notice what separates the Civil Liberties Act from the larger proposal. In 1988 the victims were alive, the wrongdoer was one government acting through documented orders, and the harm could be traced to the person. The distinction from Numbers doesn't settle the national question, but it gives both sides a fair test to put to any proposal: how near it stays to that shape. Advocates can point to specific federal programs, documented exclusions, a government that is the same legal party now as then, and victims of mid-century housing policy, some still living; Darity and Mullen's lineage requirement is an attempt to keep eligibility tied to a traceable wrong. Critics answer that lineage is still not injury, and that a program large enough to close a national gap will inevitably pay and charge people by category.

So the question has to be ordered. It is first-order, binding on anyone under Scripture's authority, that restitution is a biblical obligation and not a partisan idea, that the history happened and compounded, and that the church may not tell a comfortable story about it. It is second-order, and genuinely contested among faithful Christians, whether the right response is a federal program, a commission, targeted remedies for traceable harms, broad policy aimed at present disadvantage of the kind Hughes and King each urged from different directions, or chiefly the church's own repair. A Christian who opposes H.R. 40 on prudential grounds while taking the first order seriously is a brother in an honest argument, and so is one who supports it. This essay asks the reader to take neither side of the bill. And it faces an objection it can't fully answer: the further any national remedy moves from traceable harm toward racial category, the harder it becomes to tell restitution from the diffuse guilt Ezekiel forbids. Advocates believe careful design can hold that line, and critics doubt any national program can. This essay can't settle which of them is right.

## Two ways to keep what isn't ours

Measure the right and the left with the same instrument, and let it be the one Numbers supplies: confess the specific wrong, and restore it to the one it was done to. Each side keeps half of that sentence and drops the other half.

The characteristic failure on the right is the innocence defense. "We didn't do it" is true, and Ezekiel backs it. But it's often deployed as permission to keep everything the doing produced, a careful reading of Ezekiel 18 in service of a balance sheet no one intends to open. It also has a consistency problem. Conservatives rightly believe in institutional continuity. We honor a Constitution written in 1787, hold the government to treaties signed by presidents long buried, and teach our children pride in what earlier Americans achieved. A nation that claims its inheritance on the Fourth of July can't disclaim it when the bill arrives, and Second Samuel 21 is the Bible's own witness that continuity cuts both ways. The right keeps the plea of innocence and drops the restoration.

The characteristic failure on the left is diffuse guilt. It turns a traceable debt into a permanent racial stain, something a white person carries by being white and can never set down. It prefers the statement to the payment and the workshop to the audit, asking for confession without end while saying little about what should be restored, by whom, to whom. Guilt of that kind can't be discharged, because it was never attached to an act. So it leads to paralysis, or to contrition performed without anything changing, and eventually to backlash. The left multiplies the confession and loses the restitution just as surely.

Ezekiel 18, read in its setting, answers both. The exiles in Babylon had a saying: "What do you mean by repeating this proverb concerning the land of Israel, 'The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge'?" (Ezekiel 18:2, ESV). Their fathers' sins served them two ways. They excused the exiles, since the disaster was someone else's fault, and they paralyzed them, since a fate already decided left nothing worth doing. God demolishes inherited guilt precisely in order to demand present obedience. The righteous man in that chapter is defined by what he does with his hands this week: he "does not oppress anyone, but restores to the debtor his pledge, commits no robbery, gives his bread to the hungry and covers the naked with a garment" (Ezekiel 18:7, ESV). And the chapter ends with an invitation: "For I have no pleasure in the death of anyone, declares the Lord GOD; so turn, and live" (Ezekiel 18:32, ESV). Ezekiel removes the guilt that paralyzes so the living are free to restore what is in their own hands.

Restitution for traceable harm is finite and payable. It names what happened and who was harmed, and it can be finished, which is why it is merciful to both parties. Diffuse guilt never names a creditor and never issues a receipt, so it breeds despair or defiance and seldom repair. The right calls the harm untraceable; parts of the left call it so total that no particular act could answer it. Either way, nothing is given back.

I've preached repentance more readily than repair. I've led services of lament that moved a room and asked nothing of a budget, and I let the feeling stand in for the ledger. The grief was genuine, but grief costs less than a signature on a check, and I allowed the cheaper offering to be called a reckoning. One failure refuses the reckoning and the other counterfeits it, and in both cases the neighborhoods stay as the maps left them.

## Starting with the church's own books

Whatever one concludes about Washington, the church needn't wait for Congress, and some Christian institutions haven't.

In 1838 the Jesuits of the Maryland province, under Thomas Mulledy, a former president of Georgetown, sold 272 enslaved men, women and children to plantations in Louisiana, and part of the proceeds relieved Georgetown's debts. The records survived, and genealogists have since traced thousands of living descendants. In 2016 the university offered descendants the admissions consideration it gives the children of alumni, and in 2017 Georgetown and the Jesuits formally apologized and renamed two buildings, one for Isaac Hawkins, the first person listed in the sale documents. In 2021 the Jesuits of the United States and Canada pledged $100 million toward a foundation formed with the descendants. Princeton Theological Seminary published a historical audit in 2018 finding that it had benefited from slavery through its investments and donors, and in 2019 it committed $27.6 million, chiefly to scholarships and programs for present and future students. That same year Virginia Theological Seminary established a reparations endowment of $1.7 million, and from 2021 it made direct payments to descendants of Black people who labored on its campus, enslaved and later under Jim Crow.

All three can be defended, and they aren't the same act. Georgetown and Virginia Seminary moved toward the shape of Numbers 5: a documented wrong, identified people, payment to the heirs of those wronged. Princeton's response was broader, nearer to generosity toward a class than restitution to a family, though it rested on an honest reckoning with specific facts. Each institution opened its own archives and read the entries aloud before it paid anything. That is the order of Leviticus 6. The confession came first, and it was specific.

Every congregation has books: minutes, deeds, building records, a history of who was welcome and when. Many older deeds still carry racial covenant language, unenforceable since 1948 and never struck out. Some churches moved to the suburbs in the very decade the FHA made that move easy for some families and impossible for others, and the minutes record the reasons given. Repair begins by reading those books and saying plainly what they contain. Where a harm can be traced to people still living or to their heirs, Numbers 5 points restitution toward them. Where it can't, the same chapter offers a principle: when no kinsman could be found, the payment went to the LORD, not back into the offender's pocket. For a church, that may mean directing repair toward the Black-led congregations and institutions that carried the cost of the map, in partnerships that submit to their leadership rather than borrowing their presence for a photograph. None of this is legal or financial advice; a congregation weighing changes to deeds, endowments or restricted funds needs counsel from people who know that law. But the counsel concerns how to pay, not whether the question may be asked.

None of this is the price of grace. Grace comes first, and that order is what makes restitution bearable. At the cross the question of guilt before God is settled in full for everyone who comes to Christ, whatever his ledger holds, and that includes me. A church that believes this has no need to protect its innocence by keeping what isn't its own. The gospel doesn't cancel the debts we owe our neighbors. It removes the terror that keeps us from reading them.

In the end it's hard to get past Rizpah. She had no power to repair anything. She had sackcloth, a rock and a season, and she spent the season keeping the birds off her sons until word of it reached the king and he did what honor required. The text doesn't say the land was healed when the men were hanged. It says God answered after someone in authority finally went back for the bones and gave the dead their due. Some in the American church have kept that watch for a very long time, many in the congregations the maps were drawn to contain. The debt still stands on the books. Whether the church answers it like David after the vigil, or like a court that let three years of famine pass before it thought to ask, is being decided in ordinary budget meetings, in churches like ours, this year.
