Of everything in the Bible, angels and demons were the easiest part to mock. An invisible traffic of spirits behind the visible world seemed like the part of religion a grown man was supposed to have outgrown. I could argue against the resurrection. The unseen order I dismissed with a smile, and a smile is worse than an argument, because a smile doesn't have to think. I've come to believe the man smiling understood this subject less than almost anything else he was sure of. But he had a case, and it deserves a full hearing.

The question usually arrives from an odd angle. A grandmother dies and someone at the funeral says she has gained her wings. A video of a deliverance service, all shouting and stage lights, circulates among people already sure the church has lost its mind. Some Christians hardly think about the unseen, and some think of little else. Both, and the skeptic watching them, are owed the same thing: a patient account of what the Bible says, what it declines to say, and what the centuries have added in its name.

## The Case for an Empty Sky

Here is the reasonable person's objection, and it is reasonable. For most of human history, the space we now fill with germ theory, meteorology and neurology was filled with beings. Illness was a curse, a storm was a mood in the heavens, a seizure looked like possession, and a stillborn child sent a village looking for the witch who had done it. Then we found the bacterium under the plague and the misfiring neuron under the fit, and one by one the spirits were evicted from the rooms they had occupied. That is most of what a modern person means by progress, and it has saved an incalculable number of lives.

Max Weber gave the change its name in "Science as a Vocation," a lecture delivered in Munich in 1917: the disenchantment of the world. Its core is not knowledge but an assumption: that, in principle, no mysterious incalculable forces are at play, everything can be mastered by calculation, and no one needs to plead with spirits the way our ancestors did. That assumption sets the limits of the believable before anyone has argued anything.

Rudolf Bultmann carried that assumption into the New Testament. In his 1941 essay "New Testament and Mythology," he argued that the biblical writers lived in a three-storied universe, heaven above, earth in the middle, the underworld below, with the middle floor constantly invaded from the other two. His best-known line, in paraphrase, is that no one can use electric light and the radio and modern medicine while also believing in the New Testament's world of spirits and wonders. But Bultmann was no village atheist. He was a Lutheran preacher and a member of the Confessing Church that resisted the Nazi capture of the German churches, and his worry was pastoral. Asking modern people to swallow an ancient cosmology before they could hear the gospel, he thought, put a false stumbling block in their way, so that they rejected Christ over a picture of the universe the gospel never required. Remove the mythological frame and the true offense, the cross and its summons to decision, would stand where it belonged. Bultmann wanted the skeptic scandalized by the right thing.

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), described how this feels from the inside. The older self was porous, open to blessings and curses that could cross into it from outside. The modern self is buffered, sealed at the skin, with meaning inside the mind and only nature beyond it. I was that buffered man, and I didn't experience my disenchantment as a position. I experienced it as adulthood.

And the church earned the smile. In 1976 a young German woman named Anneliese Michel, who had been treated for epilepsy, died of malnutrition and dehydration after months of exorcisms, and in 1978 a court convicted her parents and two priests of negligent homicide. There is a deliverance industry that stages possession as theater, and a market in books and conferences that teaches anxious believers to read a flat tire as enemy fire. There is the long habit of naming a demon where there was an untreated illness and leaving the sufferer ashamed on top of sick. When the skeptic points at this, he isn't being cynical. He is being accurate. The church has to answer for the counterfeit before it says a word about the original. But my smile was aimed at the counterfeit, and I had never once looked hard at what the text actually claims.

## What Scripture Says, and How Little

Start with what the Bible doesn't say. It doesn't teach dualism. The picture most of us absorbed, God and the devil as matched opponents in a contest whose outcome hangs in doubt, is closer to a film plot than to anything in the canon. God has no equal and no opposite. Every being in the unseen order is a creature, dependent at every moment on borrowed existence. Augustine argued in *The City of God* (426) that evil has no nature of its own; it is the corruption of a good thing, a defection rather than a substance. The powers of darkness are good creatures gone wrong. That distinction reorganizes the subject. Superstition runs on the fear of a power that might win. The Bible's unseen order is not a balanced war. It is a rebellion inside a creation that has one Lord.

In one sense the Bible is itself a disenchanting book. Genesis 1 declines even to name the sun and moon, which were gods to Israel's neighbors; it calls them the greater and lesser lights and gives them work. But Scripture de-divinizes the world without emptying it. The heavens are drained of gods and then shown to be full of servants.

The Hebrew and Greek words translated "angel" both mean messenger. Angels in the Bible are not the souls of the dead or the soft infants of greeting cards. They are dispatched, given errands, sent to speak, to guard and to carry out judgment, and when one appears, the first thing it usually has to say is some form of "Fear not." Hebrews sums up their role in a question: "Are they not all ministering spirits sent out to serve for the sake of those who are to inherit salvation?" (Hebrews 1:14). Their weight is borrowed. The Son is not one of them, which is why Paul warned the Colossians against the worship of angels (Colossians 2:18) and why the angel in Revelation refused John's attempt to fall at his feet, calling himself a fellow servant (Revelation 22:8-9).

Then there is a stranger strand that most churchgoers have never been taught. Psalm 82 opens in heaven: "God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment" (Psalm 82:1). The psalm condemns these "gods" for failing the weak and the fatherless and announces that they will die like men. Some interpreters, reading the psalm alongside Jesus' use of it in John 10, take them to be human judges who bear a divine title because they judge in God's name. Others, including the late Old Testament scholar Michael Heiser in *The Unseen Realm* (2015), argue that the psalm describes spiritual beings God set over the nations who abused their charge.

That second reading leans on a verse with a real textual question behind it. The ESV renders Deuteronomy 32:8 this way: "When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he divided mankind, he fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God." The King James Version, following the standard Hebrew text, ends the verse with "the children of Israel." But a Hebrew copy of Deuteronomy found among the Dead Sea Scrolls reads "sons of God," the ancient Greek translation points the same way, and several modern translations follow them. If they are right, the Song of Moses pictures the nations placed under heavenly overseers while Israel is kept as the Lord's own portion, and the "prince of the kingdom of Persia" in Daniel 10 begins to make more sense. This is a third-order question. Nothing in the creeds depends on it, faithful scholars disagree about the text and the reading, and a Christian who thinks Psalm 82 is about corrupt judges has surrendered nothing essential.

The figure English Bibles call Satan appears in the Old Testament more rarely than most people assume, and in a surprising role. In Job, "there was a day when the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and Satan also came among them" (Job 1:6). The Hebrew has the definite article, *ha-satan*, "the satan," which reads more like a title than a name: the adversary, the accuser, a prosecutor in the heavenly court. His whole case is a slander, that Job's faith is only a transaction. Zechariah sees the same office: "Then he showed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, and Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him" (Zechariah 3:1). The LORD rebukes the accuser, calls Joshua "a brand plucked from the fire," and orders his filthy garments exchanged for clean ones. Whether this accuser is the devil of the Gospels, the Old Testament doesn't settle, but the New Testament plainly thinks the line runs through, since Revelation calls the devil "the accuser of our brothers" (Revelation 12:10). The oldest biblical picture of the enemy is not a monster with horns. It is a voice in court, making the case against us with just enough truth to be devastating.

## Jesus and the Unclean Spirits

The Gospels are where the skeptic's case presses hardest, because there is no honest way to remove the exorcisms from the story of Jesus. Historians who share none of the church's faith generally agree that Jesus was known in his lifetime as an exorcist and healer. His first public scene in Mark ends with the crowd in Capernaum amazed that "He commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him" (Mark 1:27).

The contrast with deliverance theater is sharp. Jesus uses no formulas and no hours of shouting. He speaks, and it is done. And he says what it means: "But if it is by the finger of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20). The exorcisms are signs that a stronger man has entered the strong man's house (Mark 3:27). When the seventy-two return thrilled that the demons submitted to them, Jesus answers, "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (Luke 10:18), and then turns them away from the spectacle: "Nevertheless, do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20). Even at the height of the conflict, he won't let the unseen become the main subject.

The Gospels also keep a distinction the church has too often forgotten. Matthew's summary of Jesus' early ministry lists the people brought to him in separate groups: the diseased and those in pain, those oppressed by demons, those having seizures, and paralytics (Matthew 4:24). The evangelist did not think every illness was a demon or every seizure a possession. Most of the healings involve no spirit at all; the blind and the lame are simply sick, and Jesus treats them as sick. The New Testament knew the difference between a body that is failing and a person under assault.

This has to be said plainly, because people have been badly hurt. Mental illness is not demon possession. Depression, anxiety, intrusive thoughts, psychosis, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia are illnesses of a real body and a real brain, and they respond to real treatment. A Christian who hears voices, or who is tormented by thoughts she hates, needs a physician and often a psychiatrist, and the church's job is to help her get there, not to shame her out the door. Even the tradition that kept the rite of exorcism has long urged caution: the Roman Ritual of 1614 told the priest not to believe too readily that anyone was possessed, and the Catholic Church's revised rite still asks that medical and psychiatric causes be ruled out first. If you are frightened by what is happening in your own mind, see a doctor. If you are thinking about ending your life, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, today. This is not medical advice, and nothing here replaces a doctor who can actually examine you.

None of that makes the Gospels' claim disappear. The church has done great harm by seeing demons where there was illness, and the New Testament still says, without embarrassment, that Jesus confronted personal evil and won. The man at Gerasa who lived among the tombs and cut himself with stones ends the story sitting "clothed and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15). The striking thing in that scene is not the violence of the spirits. It is the quiet at the end: a man dressed, restored, and sent home to tell his friends how much the Lord had done for him.

## What We Added

If the skeptic believes too little about the unseen, popular Christianity believes a great deal the Bible never taught, and much of it arrived through films, poems and paintings.

The most common belief, the one spoken at funerals, is that the people we love become angels when they die. The grief behind it deserves gentleness. But it isn't biblical, and it quietly shrinks the Christian hope. Angels and human beings are different kinds of creatures. Jesus told the Sadducees that in the resurrection people "neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (Matthew 22:30), like them in one respect, not turned into them. The dead in Christ wait not for wings but for bodies, raised like their Lord's. The idea of earning wings owes more to Clarence in *It's a Wonderful Life* (1946) than to the New Testament, and it gives the grieving a ghost with a halo when the creed promises them the resurrection of the body.

Guardian angels are a harder case, because there is a real thread to pull. "For he will command his angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways" (Psalm 91:11). Jesus warned against despising children, because "in heaven their angels always see the face of my Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 18:10). From these texts the church drew a widely held teaching, and Thomas Aquinas argued in the *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274) that every human being is given a guardian angel from birth. It is a reasonable inference that many faithful Christians hold. But it is an inference. Scripture never explains the assignments, never names the angels, and never invites us to pray to them or seek contact with them. And where Psalm 91 is quoted in the Gospels, it is the devil who quotes it, urging Jesus to jump from the temple and test the promise (Matthew 4:6). Angelic protection can become presumption, and Jesus refused it.

The nine ranks many Christians assume are biblical come from a writer of around 500 who took the name of Dionysius the Areopagite, the Athenian Paul converted in Acts 17:34. In *The Celestial Hierarchy* he arranged the angels in three triads: seraphim, cherubim and thrones; dominions, powers and authorities; principalities, archangels and angels. The names are biblical. Paul speaks of "thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities" (Colossians 1:16). But Paul's lists are doxology, piling up every title of power to say that whatever exists, Christ made it and outranks it. Pseudo-Dionysius turned the doxology into an organizational chart. Because the work was thought to come from Paul's own convert, it carried near-apostolic weight for about a thousand years, and Aquinas built on it. This is tradition, serious and often beautiful. It is not Scripture.

Our picture of the devil owes even more to the poets. Dante's *Inferno* puts Satan at the bottom of hell, frozen to the chest in ice, weeping from six eyes and chewing Judas, Brutus and Cassius, a devil so pathetic and mechanical that the portrait is itself a theology: evil, in the end, is sterile and stuck. Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667) gave us the opposite, a Satan of magnificent speeches and wounded pride, so compelling that William Blake, in *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* (c. 1790), remarked in effect that Milton wrote freely of devils and in fetters of God and angels, being of the devil's party without knowing it. Against all this, the Bible's reticence is striking. It gives no physical description of Satan and no map of hell's administration. Its interest is in what the enemy does, which is to lie, accuse, tempt and destroy, and in what God has done about him.

C. S. Lewis caught the balance in his preface to *The Screwtape Letters* (1942). There are, he said in effect, two equal and opposite errors about devils: to disbelieve in them, and to believe in them with an excessive and unhealthy interest, and the devils are equally pleased by both, welcoming the materialist and the magician alike. The skeptic and the deliverance showman look like opposites. They are making mirror-image mistakes.

## The Powers and the Century That Named Them

The New Testament's most sustained language about the unseen isn't about possession. It is about what Paul calls rulers and authorities. "For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 6:12). It is usually heard as a call to private spiritual combat, and it is partly that. But its first claim is the one we most often ignore. The person across the table, across the aisle, across the border, is not the darkness.

In the twentieth century some Christian thinkers began to read Paul's powers as more than invisible individuals. The Dutch theologian Hendrikus Berkhof, in *Christ and the Powers* (1953; English 1962), argued that Paul's language names the structures that order human life, tradition, law, the state, moral and economic systems, which God made to hold the world together and which, in rebellion, demand a loyalty that belongs to God alone. Berkhof wrote in a Europe that had just watched an ideology seize a whole people.

Walter Wink developed the reading most fully in a trilogy: *Naming the Powers* (1984), *Unmasking the Powers* (1986) and *Engaging the Powers* (1992). His case deserves its strongest form. Every institution, Wink argued, has an outer form and an inner spirit, a corporate personality that is real and shapes the people inside it more than they shape it. He pointed to the letters of Revelation 2 and 3, each addressed to the "angel" of a church, as Scripture's own witness that a congregation has a collective spirit that can be rebuked and called to repent. When that inner spirit demands ultimate loyalty, it becomes demonic, and Wink named the whole web of such powers the Domination System, ruling by violence and the threat of it. On this reading Christ's victory frees people from institutions that claimed to be gods.

Wink saw something the church had lost. Anyone who has watched a decent person become cruel inside a cruel system, or seen a lie outlive every liar who built it, knows evil has a dimension larger than individual choices. Bultmann declared the world of spirits unbelievable in 1941, in the very years his own country was showing how a nation can be seized by something none of its people could name. The irony is not at his expense; he stood against the regime. The century simply became its own counterargument.

But Wink's reading has a cost. He did not think the powers were independent personal beings; for him they were the inner side of the outer structures and had no existence apart from them. That fits the angels of the churches reasonably well. It fits the Gospels poorly. The man in the tombs at Gerasa was not suffering the spirit of an institution, and Jesus addressed what tormented him as someone, not something. Job opens with an accuser standing before God before any empire or bureaucracy exists. Paul seems to hold both at once: "the rulers of this age" who crucified the Lord of glory (1 Corinthians 2:8) are human authorities and the powers behind them, and he refuses to pull them apart. So does this essay, with the older church. There is a personal malice behind the structures, and the structures are also real. A Christian who keeps only one half has read only half of Paul.

The same instrument has to be turned on both sides of the American church. On the right, spiritual warfare has too often become a way of naming political opponents as demonic, as if the other party were the darkness Paul meant. On the left, the powers have too often become only the structures one already opposes, with no demon in one's own movement and no accuser in one's own heart. Both do exactly what Ephesians 6:12 forbids. Both make the enemy flesh and blood, and both are comforting, because both locate the darkness somewhere other than here. The doctrine of the unseen, rightly held, is the one thing that could have pulled me back from the easiest sin of the political animal, which is to believe my opponent is the darkness.

The Western debate also needs a check from outside the West. The historian Philip Jenkins, in *The Next Christendom* (2002) and *The New Faces of Christianity* (2006), showed that the center of gravity of world Christianity has moved to Africa, Asia and Latin America, and that believers there often read the New Testament's world of spirits, healing and deliverance not as an embarrassing ancient cosmology but as a description of the world they live in. Their pastors would put the case this way: Jesus never treated deliverance as an embarrassment, the Gospels already tell sickness from oppression, and a church that sends the ill to doctors and still prays over the tormented in the name of a risen Lord has not abandoned reason. It has refused to shrink the gospel to what a Western seminar room can feel. That world has its own abuses, including terrible accusations of witchcraft against children, and none of it should be romanticized. But it exposes an assumption. The buffered self is not the universal human condition finally reached. It is one local way of experiencing the world, largely Western and largely recent, and most of the world's Christians don't share it. When the Western reader finds the unseen unbelievable, he should at least ask whether he has discovered a truth or described his neighborhood.

## The Accuser Has Lost His Case

If the New Testament wanted its readers afraid, this is where it would press. It does the opposite. Read rather than quoted in the dark, its tone is a settled, almost startling confidence, and the reason is one event. Of the cross, Paul says: "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Colossians 2:15). The image is a Roman triumph, a general leading his defeated enemies through the streets so the whole city could see the war was over. And it happened on an instrument of execution, in what looked to every witness like total defeat. John puts it bluntly: "The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8).

Go back to the courtroom, where the Bible first shows us the enemy. The accuser's case against Joshua the high priest had evidence behind it; the filthy garments were not a slander. What silenced the accuser was not Joshua's innocence but the Lord's rebuke and the Lord's gift of clean clothes. Revelation announces that "the accuser of our brothers has been thrown down" (Revelation 12:10), and Paul asks the question that ends the trial: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Romans 8:33). The deepest defeat of the devil is not an exorcism. It is justification. The voice that says you are only what you have done, that you will never be clean, has lost its standing in the only court that finally matters, because the Judge has already clothed the accused.

That is why the Christian posture toward the unseen is sober rather than fevered. "Be sober-minded; be watchful. Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour" (1 Peter 5:8). The lion is real, but no one is told to spend a life screaming at the grass. "Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7). The order matters. The Christian preoccupied with demons has reversed it and put the enemy at the center, and the Christian who laughs the subject off has decided in advance that the lion is a housecat.

The skeptic who has read this far isn't required to agree. He may still think the unseen order is the last piece of an ancient cosmology rightly retired. But he should see that the biblical claim is not the one he was mocking. It is a creation with one Lord, messengers who serve and refuse worship, an accuser who has lost his case, and Paul's confidence that "neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers" can separate God's people from his love (Romans 8:38). That claim might be false. It is not childish.

The Christian who has read this far has something harder to carry. Most of us have been buffered too, believing in the unseen in principle while living as though the room were empty, then startled into fear when something breaks through. We have mapped the darkness onto our opponents and excused it in ourselves, and we have preferred the thrill of warfare to the slower work of repentance. The Bible's answer is not more information about demons. It is a cross where the powers were disarmed, an empty tomb where the last enemy was beaten, and a Lord who told his friends, flushed with their authority over spirits, to rejoice instead that their names were written in heaven.

When the king of Syria surrounded Dothan to take Elisha, the prophet's servant woke to an army on the hills and despaired. Elisha told him not to be afraid, and then he prayed, "O LORD, please open his eyes that he may see" (2 Kings 6:17), and the young man saw the mountain full of horses and chariots of fire. The prayer was not for more fear or less. It was for sight. The man I used to be thought the room was empty because he had sealed the windows himself. The Christian who fills the room with terrors has painted over the same windows from the other side. What remains for both is the servant's prayer, and the long work of learning to live in a world fuller than we thought and more safely held than we feared.
