You pray, and nothing comes back. Not a refusal, which would at least be an answer, but nothing. For some people it arrives after a funeral, somewhere in the fourth month, when the meals have stopped and a second, larger quiet settles over the house. For others it comes with no event at all. The creed is intact; you'd sign it tomorrow. But the sense of being heard has drained out of prayer, and one night you type into a search bar the question you can't ask anyone at church: why does God feel silent, and where has he gone?

The church has mostly given one answer, less by teaching than by reflex. If God is quiet, the fault is on your end. There's a sin you haven't confessed, a faith that's grown thin, a habit of prayer you let slide. So the grieving learn to add guilt to their grief and the dry add shame to their dryness. The answer survives because it's useful to the rest of us. If the silence is your fault, it can't come for me.

I came to faith from atheism, and an atheist isn't troubled by the silence of heaven. For him it's the premise. The silence of God becomes unbearable only to people who believe God speaks, so the complaint is itself a strange testimony: no one grieves the silence of a friend he never had. That settles nothing. It does tell you the question lives inside faith, and the Bible puts it there deliberately.

This essay turns on one distinction. There is a silence that means no one is there. And there is a silence that belongs to the cross and to the long Saturday between the crucifixion and Easter, the silence of a God who has gone down into the dark instead of calling to us from above it. From the inside, the two feel the same. They aren't, and the Christian answer to the hiddenness of God depends on telling them apart without pretending the first is impossible. The skeptic's case deserves to be heard at full strength, and it will be. But first the Bible has to be allowed to say what it says.

## A God who hides himself

The phrase the tradition gave this subject comes from Isaiah: *Deus absconditus*, the hidden God. "Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior" (Isaiah 45:15, ESV). Its setting matters. The chapter opens with the Lord calling Cyrus of Persia his anointed, a pagan king who doesn't know him, and announcing that through him Israel will go home from exile. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC, and the return followed. The confession of hiddenness comes out of that astonishment. Nobody in Babylon looked at a Persian conqueror and saw the Savior of Israel at work. The hiddenness in Isaiah isn't God's absence from history. It's his presence in a form nobody expected.

And four verses later the same God says the thing a person in the silence most needs to hear: "I did not speak in secret, in a land of darkness; I did not say to the offspring of Jacob, 'Seek me in vain'" (Isaiah 45:19, ESV). The hidden God and the God who refuses to be sought in vain are one God in one chapter. We tend to let one swallow the other: either God is plainly available, and your silence is your fault, or God is simply gone. The prophet will allow neither.

Martin Luther made this the hinge of his theology. At Heidelberg in April 1518, six months after the ninety-five theses, he drew a line between two kinds of theologian. The theologian of glory looks for God in visible power and success. The theologian of the cross knows that God has chosen to be found in the last place anyone would look, in a crucified man. Luther's twenty-first thesis says the first calls evil good and good evil, while the second calls a thing what it actually is. God hides, for Luther, under his opposite: strength in weakness, life in death, his yes inside what looks like a no. To the eye of glory the cross proves God is absent. To faith it's where he has made himself known.

Seven years later, arguing with Erasmus in *The Bondage of the Will* (1525), Luther named a second and darker hiddenness. Beyond what God has shown in his Word there is God in his naked majesty, who works all things and has not explained himself. Luther's counsel is blunt: don't pry into that majesty. Hold to God as he has clothed himself in his Word, which means hold to Christ. The historical theologian B. A. Gerrish, in a 1973 essay on Luther and Calvin in the *Journal of Religion*, distinguished the two as hiddenness in revelation and hiddenness outside it. The first is the hiddenness of the cross, where God is concealed and found in the same place. The second is the terror of a God behind what he has disclosed. Most people in the silence are afraid of the second. Luther knew that fear from long experience of what he called *Anfechtung*, assault. He refused to let it have the last word about who God is, and sent the frightened back to the place where God chose to be seen, which happens to be a place of dereliction.

Blaise Pascal carried the verse into the age of the new science. In the notes published after his death as the *Pensées* (1670), he quotes Isaiah's line in Latin and argues that hiddenness is not an embarrassment for Christianity but one of its predictions. God, he reasoned, wished to be found by those who seek him with their whole heart and hidden from those who flee him, and so gave enough light for those who want to see and enough obscurity for those who don't. He went so far as to say that a religion which doesn't call God hidden isn't true. The skeptic will call that convenient, since a theory that predicts both evidence and its absence is hard to test. The objection is fair, and every defender of Pascal since has had to answer it.

## The prayers Israel refused to edit

Before the argument, the prayers. The reflex that blames the silent believer isn't invented from nothing. Deuteronomy warns that if Israel breaks covenant the Lord will hide his face (Deuteronomy 31:17), and Isaiah tells a faithless generation why their prayers go unheard: "but your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God, and your sins have hidden his face from you so that he does not hear" (Isaiah 59:2, ESV). A person in the silence should be free to ask whether something needs confessing. Sometimes it does.

But Scripture refuses to let that be the whole story. Psalm 44, a national lament after defeat, complains precisely that the people have not been unfaithful: "All this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you, and we have not been false to your covenant" (Psalm 44:17, ESV). Then it turns on God with a question the guilt reflex doesn't permit: "Why do you hide your face? Why do you forget our affliction and oppression?" (Psalm 44:24, ESV). Hiding can be judgment. It can also fall on the faithful with no explanation, and then Israel is permitted to protest. The reflex that reads every silence as a verdict on the sufferer has taken one thread of Scripture and cut off the rest.

The lament psalms are where Israel learned to speak into that silence, and they aren't polite. Psalm 13 asks "how long" four times and then demands an answer: "Consider and answer me, O LORD my God; light up my eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death" (Psalm 13:3, ESV). Two verses later he turns: "But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation" (Psalm 13:5, ESV). Nothing in the psalm says the silence has ended. The turn is made from inside it, on the strength of a love remembered and a rescue not yet seen.

Psalm 22, the psalm Jesus prayed on the cross, names the experience exactly: "O my God, I cry by day, but you do not answer, and by night, but I find no rest" (Psalm 22:2, ESV). It goes deep into humiliation, the mockers and the lots cast for clothing, and then late in the psalm makes a claim that reverses what the sufferer felt: "For he has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, and he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him" (Psalm 22:24, ESV). The one who cried "you do not answer" was heard. The psalm doesn't say he was wrong to feel what he felt. It says the feeling and the truth were not the same, and the truth came out on the far side.

Psalm 88 doesn't come out on the far side. It is the darkest lament in the Psalter, and it never turns. "O LORD, why do you cast my soul away? Why do you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 88:14, ESV). The psalm answers its question with nothing, and its last word in Hebrew is darkness. Israel kept it anyway, with every chance to soften it or leave it out. And every line, even the worst, is addressed to the "LORD, God of my salvation." The man in the pit has lost the sense of being heard. He hasn't stopped speaking to the one who should hear.

Job is the long form of the same prayer. His friends bring the tidy explanation most of us were raised on and defend it through round after round of speeches. Job refuses it as a man hunting for God, not fleeing him: "Behold, I go forward, but he is not there, and backward, but I do not perceive him" (Job 23:8, ESV). His sharpest question is about the silence: "Why do you hide your face and count me as your enemy?" (Job 13:24, ESV). When God speaks from the whirlwind he answers none of Job's questions. He gives himself. He rebukes Job for darkening counsel without knowledge (Job 38:2), and then tells the friends they haven't spoken rightly of him, as Job has (Job 42:7). For all the rebuke, the man who accused God of hiding is vindicated over the men who defended him.

Lamentations, written over the ruins of Jerusalem after 586 BC, says what every silent believer has felt: "You have wrapped yourself with a cloud so that no prayer can pass through" (Lamentations 3:44, ESV). Its famous line about mercies new every morning is spoken in the same chapter, with the city still in ruins, and the book ends not on that line but on an open question. Scripture never treats the silence of God as unreal, and never as the end of the conversation. Its grammar for divine silence is addressed speech. The faithful complain to God about God. They accuse him of hiding, demand that he answer, and keep calling him "my God" while they do it. That is not the absence of faith. It is what faith sounds like when it refuses both to lie about the dark and to let the dark have the last word.

## The strongest case that silence means absence

For most of Christian history the silence of God was a pastoral problem. In 1993 the Canadian philosopher J. L. Schellenberg made it a philosophical one. His *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason* argued that hiddenness is itself evidence that there is no God, and the argument has shaped the field ever since.

It runs roughly like this. A perfectly loving God would always be open to a personal relationship with any creature capable of one. Relationship requires, at minimum, believing the other exists. So such a God would make sure no one capable of relationship with him was left without belief through no fault of their own. But there are such people. The 1993 book spoke of reasonable or inculpable nonbelief; in later work Schellenberg calls them nonresistant nonbelievers: people who aren't running from God, who would welcome him if he were there, and who have looked honestly and can't find him. If they exist, a perfectly loving God doesn't.

The force of the argument is its picture of love. No loving mother hides for years from a frightened child calling for her in the dark, least of all to build the child's character. If she could make herself known and chose not to, we wouldn't call her mysterious. We'd call her cruel.

Christians should concede what is true here before answering it. Nonresistant nonbelievers exist. The church has too often insisted that every unbeliever is secretly in rebellion, a claim it can't know, and one that insults people who have searched longer and more honestly than many believers. And the argument is stronger than the usual replies; "God respects your freedom" doesn't explain why he would hide from someone begging to be found.

The skeptic's case also has a witness no philosopher can match. Elie Wiesel was fifteen when he was deported from Sighet to Auschwitz in 1944. His memoir *Night*, first written in Yiddish and then published in French in 1958 and in English in 1960, remembers the first night in the camp as the night the flames consumed his faith, and among the things he vows never to forget is the silence of that night. The Yiddish version carried a title that could stand over this whole subject: *And the World Remained Silent*. Wiesel's silence is doubled, God's and humanity's, and he lets neither off. The first reply a Christian owes that book isn't an argument. It is to stop talking.

The philosophical replies are still worth hearing, because they show which premise is doing the work. Pascal's is the oldest: a God who overwhelmed every mind with evidence would compel assent but not win trust. Paul Moser develops this in *The Elusive God* (2008), arguing that God's evidence is purposively available, offered to invite the surrender of the will rather than the curiosity of a spectator. The weakness is plain. That fits the resistant better than the nonresistant, and Schellenberg's argument is about the nonresistant.

Michael Rea's reply in *The Hiddenness of God* (2018) goes deeper. Rea argues that the argument quietly assumes divine love must look like the love of an ideal human parent, which is exactly what a transcendent God need not be. Rea adds that God can be present without being felt, and he points to Scripture and the church's liturgy as places where God makes himself available to anyone who comes. The tradition never promised felt presence on demand. The Lord's Table belongs on the same list, and it doesn't depend on anyone's feelings to be what it is.

None of this makes the argument disappear, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What the replies show is that it rests on a premise Christians have reason to question: that love must mean felt presence on terms we would recognize. They question it because of where their own story puts God. If the deepest disclosure of divine love is a man crying out to a God who doesn't answer, then Christianity has already said that love and felt absence can occupy the same hour. That doesn't refute Schellenberg. It does change the question from whether a loving God could be silent to what kind of silence the cross is.

## The dark night, stated accurately

Before turning to the cross, one phrase needs rescuing. "The dark night of the soul" now means almost any hard season, and the looseness has cost it the precision that made it useful. It comes from John of the Cross (1542 to 1591), a Spanish Carmelite friar seized in December 1577 by brothers of his own order who opposed the reform he supported, and held in a cramped cell in Toledo until he escaped in August 1578. The poem that begins "on a dark night" is usually dated to the months just after his escape; the commentaries, *The Ascent of Mount Carmel* and *The Dark Night*, followed in the 1580s.

What John describes isn't grief or doubt in general but a particular work of God in a person long given to serious prayer. He distinguishes a night of the senses, in which the consolations that made prayer sweet are taken away, from a deeper night of the spirit, in which the person's very ideas of God are stripped. And he insists the darkness comes not from God's distance but from his nearness. Borrowing from Pseudo-Dionysius, he calls contemplation a ray of darkness: the divine light is so much brighter than the soul can bear that it registers as night, the way full sunlight blinds a weak eye.

John was careful in a way his modern borrowers aren't. He gives signs for telling this night apart from lukewarmness, where a person has simply stopped caring, and from what he called melancholy or some bad humor, which we would recognize as a disorder of body and mind. He didn't baptize every darkness as a spiritual gift. That matters for anyone reading this in the worst of it. If the silence has come with a loss of sleep and appetite, a heaviness that colors everything and not only prayer, or any thought that your life isn't worth continuing, it may not be a dark night in John's sense at all, and no one should tell you it is. Depression is a real illness with real treatments, and seeking them is not a failure of faith. This isn't medical advice, and it can't replace a doctor. If the dark has pressed you anywhere near the edge, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the United States, today.

Grief has its own darkness, and it isn't the dark night either. C. S. Lewis had written a calm, ordered book on suffering, *The Problem of Pain* (1940). Then in July 1960 his wife, Joy Davidman, died of cancer, and the journal he kept afterward, published under a pseudonym as *A Grief Observed* (1961), describes going to God in desperate need and finding what felt like a door bolted from the inside, and then silence. Lewis noticed that the real danger wasn't that he would stop believing in God but that he would come to believe terrible things about him. That is Luther's second hiddenness, the dread of a God behind the silence who isn't the God of the cross. Lewis kept writing to the silence, as the psalmists did.

What John, the psalmists and Lewis share is the distinction this subject depends on: the absence of the feeling of God is not the absence of God. The two seemed identical only because nearness had come with warmth attached. We have been formed to treat feeling as the test of what's real, so when the warmth goes we conclude that what it pointed to has gone too. The tradition answers that the feeling was never the faith. It was weather.

## Saturday, the day God kept silent

We have a theology of Good Friday and a theology of Easter. We have almost nothing for the day between, which is the day most people in the silence are living. The Gospels say almost nothing about it either, and their reticence is its own testimony. Matthew records only that the authorities secured the grave: "So they went and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone and setting a guard" (Matthew 27:66, ESV). Luke gives the women a single verse: "Then they returned and prepared spices and ointments. On the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment" (Luke 23:56, ESV). That is the whole of it. The Messiah was dead, heaven said nothing, and the women kept the Sabbath because the law required it, with spices waiting for a body. There was a sealed stone and a commandment kept in grief.

The disciples didn't know it was Saturday. We read the story from the far side of Easter and can't unknow the morning that's coming. For them it wasn't the middle day of a drama. It was the day after the end. Alan Lewis, a Scottish Reformed theologian whose *Between Cross and Resurrection* (2001) appeared after his death from cancer, argued that the church has to read the three days twice. The first reading follows the story as the disciples lived it, with no knowledge of the ending, so that the cross is really defeat and the grave really the end. The second reads it in the light of Easter. Read only the second way, and we rush past the grave and lose the God who lay in it; only the first, and we are left in the tomb. Lewis thought the church had largely neglected the first reading, and Saturday with it.

The creed doesn't let Saturday be a blank. It confesses that Christ was crucified, died, was buried and descended to the dead. On the silent day, then, God the Son was not hovering above death but in it. Hans Urs von Balthasar, in *Mysterium Paschale* (first published in German in 1969 as part of the dogmatics *Mysterium Salutis*; English 1990), pressed further than most theologians would. He argued that Holy Saturday is not a triumphant march through the underworld but the Son's utter solidarity with the dead, being dead with the dead, sharing in obedience the condition of those cut off from God, so that even the Father's silence toward the dead Son falls inside the love of the Trinity.

That reading is contested, and the contest should be stated fairly. Alyssa Lyra Pitstick, in *Light in Darkness* (2007), argued that Balthasar's account can't be reconciled with the church's historic teaching, which from the Fathers through Aquinas pictured the descent as Christ going in glory to the righteous dead to free them, a victory and not a further suffering, and that the departure reaches into his doctrine of Christ and of the Trinity. A reader who holds that view holds the weight of the tradition. For Pitstick, writing as a Catholic, the matter is settled church teaching. Across the wider church, how Christ descended isn't a first-order question; the creeds bind us to his real death, burial and descent, not to one account of the descent. This essay needs only what both sides confess: that on Saturday the Son of God was truly dead, that God's own story now includes a grave, and that the silence of that day was the silence of a God who had gone to the place of the dead, not a God who had left.

Shusaku Endo's novel *Silence* (1966) puts the question into the life of a seventeenth-century Portuguese Jesuit smuggled into Japan during the persecution. He watches Japanese believers die slowly for their faith while God says nothing, until the silence becomes unbearable. At the climax he is pressed to trample a *fumie*, a worn image of Christ, to spare others torture, and he hears, or believes he hears, the face on the image tell him to trample, because Christ came to be trampled on and to share their pain. God had not been silent, the novel suggests. He had been suffering alongside them. Readers still argue whether the ending is grace or collapse, and the martyrs who refused to trample aren't diminished by the priest's choice. But Endo saw what the cross implies: the silence of God and the suffering presence of God can be one silence, seen from different sides of the grave.

Here the distinction lands. A silence that means absence has no one inside it. The silence of Saturday has someone inside it, and he is dead, and he is God. On Friday the Son prayed Psalm 22 as his own, the psalm that goes on to say God had not hidden his face after all. Saturday is the long pause between the cry and the answer. From within, the two silences feel identical. They aren't, because one is empty and the other is the tomb of Christ.

## What the silence asks of us

If that's true, the church owes the silent something it has mostly withheld. We can't stand Saturday, so we hurry people through it. We expect the grieving to be functioning by a certain month and the dry to be warm again by a certain retreat. We quote the resurrection to people still in the tomb. We call it encouragement. More often it is fear that someone else's silence says something about our own faith.

I'm a pastor, and I've done this. Earlier in ministry I handed explanations to people who needed company, because an explanation gave me something to say and I couldn't bear having nothing. Job's friends had the right instinct for seven days, when they sat on the ground with him and said nothing because his suffering was very great (Job 2:13). Then they opened their mouths, and the comfort ended. The hardest thing a church learns is to sit in a silent room and let it be silent, without breaking it and without leaving. It is the one thing the disciples managed on Saturday.

For the person inside the silence, the tradition's counsel is plain. Keep speaking to God, even if all you can say is the psalm that doesn't resolve. Keep the practices you built when prayer was warm, because they were never about the warmth. Eugene Peterson borrowed a phrase from Nietzsche, of all people, for the title of *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* (1980), and it names faithfulness when the view is gone: the same road, walked without scenery. Come to the Lord's Table when you feel like a hypocrite for coming; its promise doesn't flicker with your mood. And tell someone who has been through the dark. If the silence has turned into a despair that tells you your life is over, don't wait for the dark to lift. Call or text 988.

None of this makes the silence end. It may last a season or years, and the honest thing and the kind thing are the same here: there is no promised date. The skeptic is right that the quiet is real, and no answer should be offered over the smoke Wiesel remembered. What the Christian faith offers is not an explanation of the silence but a claim about who is in it. The Son of God cried into the same silence on Friday and lay inside it on Saturday. He was the one in the grave.

So the question you carry out of this isn't whether you can feel God. You may not, for a long time. It's whose silence this is. There is a silence with no one in it, and a great many honest people have concluded that is the only kind. And there is the silence of a sealed stone on the day the women rested according to the commandment, with the spices ready and the body cold and nothing yet said from heaven. You can't tell the two apart by listening harder. You can only decide which grave you are sitting beside, and keep your place there, and say "my God" into the dark the way the psalmist did, until the one who lay in it speaks.
