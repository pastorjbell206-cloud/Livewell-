## A law written for the year things go wrong

A great many people now carry a debt that has outlived the reason they took it on: the loan for a degree never finished, the hospital bill from the year the insurance failed, the credit card that carried a family through a layoff and then kept carrying them. A debt is more than a number owed. It is a claim on your future labor, held by someone who doesn't need to know your name, and when it can't be paid down it quietly decides what you're allowed to become. Nothing here is financial or legal advice; a household facing debts it cannot pay needs a qualified counselor and sometimes a lawyer. But the experience is far older than our instruments, and the Bible says more about it than most Christians have been told.

Leviticus 25 is where it says the most, and the church has mostly filed that chapter under abolished or admired it from a safe distance as a metaphor for the soul. I have preached these texts and felt how much weight a comfortable congregation wants the word *metaphor* to carry. The chapter won't bear it. It deals in fields, houses and years, and it assumes what economic optimism tends to hide: harvests will fail, families will sell land they need, and some people will sell their own labor to survive. So it builds a series of floors under the fall.

Walk down them in order. First, "If your brother becomes poor and sells part of his property, then his nearest redeemer shall come and redeem what his brother has sold" (Leviticus 25:25, ESV). The redeemer, the *go'el*, is the nearest kinsman, whose duty is to buy back what the family has lost so that the land stays in the clan. If there is no redeemer, the man may buy it back himself when he recovers (25:26–27). If he cannot maintain himself, his neighbors must support him, and the command is plain: "Take no interest from him or profit, but fear your God, that your brother may live beside you" (Leviticus 25:36, ESV). If he sells himself, "you shall not make him serve as a slave" (Leviticus 25:39, ESV); he works as a hired man until the Jubilee. And beneath every fall stands the fiftieth year, announced by the trumpet on the Day of Atonement, when Israel was to "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants," and "each of you shall return to his property and each of you shall return to his clan" (Leviticus 25:10, ESV).

The law even sets the price. A buyer counts the years to the next Jubilee and pays accordingly, "for it is the number of the crops that he is selling to you" (Leviticus 25:16, ESV). What changed hands in Israel was a lease on harvests, never the ground. The reason is the sentence the whole chapter turns on: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). The same logic governs persons. An Israelite cannot be held as permanent property because "it is to me that the people of Israel are servants. They are my servants whom I brought out of the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 25:55, ESV). God owns the land and the people, and so neither can be owned forever by anyone else.

Two details keep this from becoming a slogan. A house inside a walled city could be redeemed for only a year, and then it passed to the buyer for good (25:29–30). The Jubilee protected farmland, the means by which a household fed itself. It aimed at livelihood, not at wealth as such. The second detail is harder, and a skeptical reader will find it whether I name it or not: the same chapter permits Israelites to buy slaves from the surrounding nations and hold them permanently (25:44–46). The release ran to the edge of the covenant people and stopped. That limit is the plainest evidence that this law cannot simply be lifted into another society, and the gospel would carry the logic of release much further, until Paul could ask a slaveholder to receive his runaway "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 16, ESV).

What the chapter finally draws is a distinction our economy has blurred, between credit and bondage. The Torah does not forbid lending. It commands it, generously (Deuteronomy 15:8). What it forbids is the conversion of one bad season into a permanent condition, the loan that becomes a lien on a man's grandchildren. Debt as a tool is permitted. Debt as a master is not.

## The release, the edges of the field and the redeemer

Deuteronomy 15 works on a shorter clock. "At the end of every seven years you shall grant a release. And this is the manner of the release: every creditor shall release what he has lent to his neighbor. He shall not exact it of his neighbor, his brother, because the LORD's release has been proclaimed" (Deuteronomy 15:1–2, ESV). The foreigner, the *nokri*, is excepted (15:3), and a common scholarly reading takes him to be a trader passing through rather than a resident neighbor, which would mean the release covered loans of distress between neighbors and left commercial credit alone. Between its hope that there be no poor in the land and its realism that there always will be, the chapter shows it knows exactly how a lender thinks: "Take care lest there be an unworthy thought in your heart and you say, 'The seventh year, the year of release is near,' and your eye look grudgingly on your poor brother, and you give him nothing, and he cry to the LORD against you, and you be guilty of sin" (Deuteronomy 15:9, ESV). The law anticipates its own evasion. It names the calculation any sensible creditor would make, and it calls that calculation sin.

The same chapter frees the Hebrew debt-servant in the seventh year and forbids sending him out empty-handed (15:12–14), and the reason given is memory: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you; therefore I command you this today" (Deuteronomy 15:15, ESV). Israel's economics are an extension of its exodus. A people once owned by Pharaoh may not build a Pharaoh's economy among themselves.

The gleaning laws apply that memory to the harvest. The farmer is not to reap to the edges of his field or gather what falls; it is left "for the poor and for the sojourner" (Leviticus 19:10, ESV). The sheaf forgotten in the field "shall be for the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow" (Deuteronomy 24:19, ESV), and the reason is again the memory of Egypt (24:22). Two things about this law are easy to miss. It limits ownership from inside, so that the owner's right stops short of his own field's edge. And it presumes work. The poor are not handed a sack of grain. They walk the rows and gather it themselves, which is why the gleaning law embarrasses both the reader who thinks the poor have no claim and the reader who thinks their claim can be met without their labor.

The book of Ruth puts all of this into a story, and it has to be read whole, because its economics and its love story are the same plot. It opens with famine in Bethlehem, the house of bread, and a family that leaves its land for Moab. The men die, and Naomi comes home with her Moabite daughter-in-law, telling the women of the town, "I went away full, and the LORD has brought me back empty" (Ruth 1:21, ESV). She is exactly the widow Deuteronomy wrote for, and Ruth is exactly the sojourner. They arrive at the barley harvest, and Ruth goes out to glean, which is to say she walks through the one door the law had left open for a woman with no land, no husband and no standing.

The field belongs to Boaz, and Boaz does more than the law asks. He tells his workers to let her glean among the sheaves and to pull out stalks from the bundles for her on purpose (2:15–16). He prays that she will be rewarded by "the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge" (Ruth 2:12, ESV). Naomi recognizes him as "one of our redeemers" (Ruth 2:20, ESV), and on the threshing floor Ruth hands his prayer back to him: "Spread your wings over your servant, for you are a redeemer" (Ruth 3:9, ESV). The word for wings also means the corner of a garment. She is asking him to become the answer to what he prayed.

Then the book turns on a man who kept the law exactly. There is a nearer redeemer, and at the city gate he is glad to buy Elimelech's field until he learns that the widow comes with it, and that a son born to her would carry the dead man's name back onto the land. "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I impair my own inheritance" (Ruth 4:6, ESV). Scholars still debate how the *go'el*'s duty related to the levirate law of Deuteronomy 25, which bound brothers, but the moral point doesn't wait on that debate. The nearer redeemer does nothing illegal. He calculates, the calculation is sound, and the book does not preserve his name. Boaz accepts the cost to his own inheritance that the other man refused, to restore a family not his own, marrying a Moabite whom a strict reading of Deuteronomy 23:3 would have kept outside the assembly. At the end the women say to Naomi, "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer" (Ruth 4:14, ESV), and the genealogy runs from their son to David.

Ruth forces a second distinction here. Law can define a duty and set a floor, and it can be kept by a man who protects his own inheritance at every step. What law cannot supply is the love that spends itself past the duty. Ruth needs both: a gleaning law that keeps her alive before anyone loves her, and a redeemer who does what no statute required.

## Did Israel ever keep the Jubilee?

The honest answer is that we don't know. Some scholars read Leviticus 25 as a late, utopian priestly program never enforced; others think it preserves an old institution rarely kept on schedule. What is not in doubt is that debt release was a real practice in Israel's world. Moshe Weinfeld set out in *Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East* (1995) how Mesopotamian kings issued edicts of *misharum*, "equity," often on taking the throne, cancelling certain debts and freeing people held in debt servitude. The Akkadian term for that release, *andurarum*, is cognate with the Hebrew *deror*, the "liberty" of Leviticus 25:10. The most fully preserved decree, the edict of Ammi-saduqa of Babylon in the seventeenth century BC, cancelled arrears and ordinary interest-bearing loans of grain and silver while expressly exempting silver advanced as a commercial investment for trade.

The parallels show that Israel's law was no fantasy, and they show what was different about it. In Babylon release depended on the king's pleasure. In Israel release was fixed to the calendar and grounded in God's ownership of the land. No king proclaimed it, no king could withhold it, and the king himself sat under the law (Deuteronomy 17:18–20). Royal discretion became covenant obligation, which is the difference between a pardon and a right.

The evidence for the seven-year release is firmer than for the Jubilee. Under Zedekiah, with Babylon at the walls, the people freed their Hebrew slaves and then took them back when the danger eased, and Jeremiah's answer is savage: "Behold, I proclaim to you liberty to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine, declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 34:17, ESV). After the return Nehemiah's community swore to forgo the collection of debts in the seventh year (Nehemiah 10:31), and First Maccabees records a town surrendering for lack of food in a sabbatical year (1 Maccabees 6:49). For the Jubilee itself no account survives of one being proclaimed, and rabbinic tradition later held that it lapsed once the tribes no longer lived on their allotments. The Chronicler read the exile as the law enforced from heaven: the land lay desolate "until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths" (2 Chronicles 36:21, ESV).

The prophets show what happened when these laws were ignored. When Ahab wanted Naboth's vineyard, Naboth answered with the theology of Leviticus 25: "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1 Kings 21:3, ESV). He was killed on false testimony. Isaiah pronounced woe on "those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room, and you are made to dwell alone in the midst of the land" (Isaiah 5:8, ESV), and Micah saw the appetite from inside: "They covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away; they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance" (Micah 2:2, ESV). The mechanism is simple: a bad harvest, a loan, a pledge of land, the land lost, and a free household working as tenants on ground its grandfather had farmed.

Nehemiah 5 shows the spiral in one scene. Families had mortgaged their fields in a famine or borrowed to pay the Persian king's tax, and now their children were being sold, "but it is not in our power to help it, for other men have our fields and our vineyards" (Nehemiah 5:5, ESV). The creditors were fellow Jews. Nehemiah made them return the fields (5:11), in effect applying the Jubilee as an emergency measure without waiting for the fiftieth year.

Walter Brueggemann argued in *The Land* (1977) that land is a central theme of biblical faith, and that Israel's peculiar temptation was to receive the land as gift and then hold it as possession, as though it were earned and could be secured by good management. The Jubilee was the law's defense against that forgetting, the prophets are the record of its failure, and the exile is what the forgetting cost. The land kept the Sabbath its tenants would not.

## The year of the Lord's favor, and what the church did with it

Israel's failure pushed the Jubilee forward into hope. Isaiah 61 borrows its vocabulary for the restoration God himself would bring: the anointed messenger comes "to proclaim liberty to the captives" and "to proclaim the year of the LORD's favor" (Isaiah 61:1–2, ESV), and the word for liberty is *deror* again. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the text called 11QMelchizedek reads Leviticus 25, Deuteronomy 15 and Isaiah 61 together and expects a final Jubilee in which a heavenly deliverer, Melchizedek, proclaims liberty to the captives and releases them from the debt of their iniquities. For some Jews before Jesus, the Jubilee had already become a way of speaking about the last things.

That is the background to Nazareth, where Jesus read Isaiah 61, stopped at "to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:19, ESV), and said the scripture was fulfilled that day. The Greek Old Testament rendered the release of Leviticus 25 with *aphesis*, which Luke's Isaiah uses twice in one verse and which is also Luke's ordinary word for the forgiveness of sins (Luke 24:47). Gary Anderson traces in *Sin: A History* (2009) how, in the centuries before Jesus, Jewish speech came to picture sin as a debt, so that the Aramaic word for debt could simply mean sin. Matthew's Lord's Prayer asks, "and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (Matthew 6:12, ESV), and Luke's keeps both words side by side: "forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone who is indebted to us" (Luke 11:4, ESV).

This is where the spiritualizing reading goes wrong, and it goes wrong by being half right. Jesus did fill the Jubilee with a depth no transfer of land could hold; the captivity he broke is older than any mortgage. But the prayer he taught does not trade one release for the other. It binds them, so that a disciple asks for the cancellation of his debt to God in the same breath that he confesses the cancellations he owes. When the Spirit fell, the Jerusalem church shared its goods freely (Acts 5:4) until "there was not a needy person among them" (Acts 4:34, ESV), echoing the Greek of Deuteronomy 15:4 down to its uncommon word for the needy. What the Torah promised and Israel missed had begun in a congregation.

The church's own history with the Jubilee is a long lesson in how the economic half gets lost. For more than a thousand years it kept part of the Torah's quarrel with interest alive. The Council of Nicaea in 325 forbade clergy to lend at interest, and the Third Lateran Council in 1179 denied Christian burial to open usurers. Thomas Aquinas argued in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, question 78) that charging for a loan of money sells something that does not exist, since money's use is its spending. That settlement loosened as commerce grew, and Calvin, among others, made room for moderate interest, though never exacted from the poor. Meanwhile the name Jubilee had gone elsewhere. In 1300 Boniface VIII proclaimed the first Holy Year, promising a plenary indulgence to pilgrims who visited the basilicas of Saints Peter and Paul in Rome. The word survived; the fields and debts were gone from it. Release had become a spiritual transaction administered by the institution.

Yet the economic Jubilee kept breaking back in. In *Tertio Millennio Adveniente* (1994), John Paul II asked Christians to mark the Great Jubilee of 2000 by considering a substantial reduction, if not an outright cancellation, of the international debt that burdened poor nations. By the late 1990s a coalition of churches and charities in Britain calling itself Jubilee 2000 was campaigning to cancel the unpayable debts of the poorest countries, and in May 1998 tens of thousands of people formed a human chain around the G8 summit in Birmingham. The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative of the IMF and World Bank, begun in 1996, was enlarged after the Cologne summit in 1999, and in 2005 the G8 agreed the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative, which cancelled much of what qualifying countries still owed the IMF, the World Bank and the African Development Fund.

A chapter of Leviticus had moved finance ministers, and the results were real and mixed. Critics on the left called the attached conditions punitive. William Easterly, in *The Elusive Quest for Growth* (2001), warned from the other side that forgiving debts can reward the governments that ran them up and do little for their poorest citizens unless the habits behind the debt change. Some of the same countries borrowed heavily again, and in 2020 Zambia defaulted on its bonds. A release is not a cure. It is a mercy that must recur because the conditions that produce bondage recur, which is exactly why the Torah put it on a calendar instead of leaving it to a season of public feeling.

## Israel is not America, and markets are not Pharaoh

Two objections deserve their strongest form. The first is that the Torah's economic law was given to one people under one covenant, in a land allotted by tribe and clan, and does not bind a modern pluralist state. The Reformed tradition said so with care. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) teaches that God gave Israel "sundry judicial laws, which expired together with the State of that people; not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require" (19.4). A Christian who campaigns to legislate Leviticus 25 has forgotten verses 44 to 46.

The second objection is stronger, because it concerns the poor themselves. Deirdre McCloskey argued in *Bourgeois Equality* (2016) that what she calls the Great Enrichment, the unprecedented rise in ordinary income since about 1800, came chiefly from a change in how societies regarded trade, invention and commercial life. By the World Bank's measures the share of humanity in extreme poverty fell from more than a third in 1990 to around a tenth by the late 2010s, much of it as China, India and others opened to markets. Credit is part of that story, and a society that makes lending risky makes it scarce, which hurts the borrower first.

The Jewish tradition learned that lesson from inside the Torah. The Mishnah records that Hillel instituted the *prosbul*, a document transferring a loan to the court so that the sabbatical year would not cancel it, because he saw that people had stopped lending as the seventh year approached (Mishnah Shevi'it 10:3). Deuteronomy 15:9 had come true. Hillel's answer was an evasion of the law or a mercy that kept credit flowing to the poor, and probably both. Either way it concedes that a debt release works only among people who fear God more than they fear a loss.

The objections are true, and they don't settle the matter, because they answer a question the Torah was not asking. Christopher Wright, in *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* (2004), distinguishes between applying Israel's law directly and reading Israel as a paradigm, in the grammarian's sense: the model verb a student learns so that he can conjugate verbs he has never seen. Israel's social order is God's worked example of a redeemed society in one time and place, and its laws teach principles to be translated, not transplanted. The Westminster divines' "general equity" points the same way. The statute expired. The equity did not.

Read as paradigm, these chapters yield something definite. Property is real but held under God, never absolute. Productive credit is good, and debt that hardens into inherited bondage is a wrong a just order must limit. Protection for those who fall belongs in the structure, not in the mood of the powerful. And the aim is to restore households to providing for themselves, not to equalize outcomes. None of that yields a bankruptcy code, a policy on sovereign debt or a cap on interest rates. Those are second-order questions of prudence and evidence, and Christians who agree about Leviticus may argue about them and remain brothers. The paradigm judges every answer we give.

It judges two readings in particular, with the same instrument. The libertarian reading runs close to Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974): holdings are just if justly acquired and freely transferred, and any pattern imposed afterward violates the rights of owners. Its defenders rightly note that the Jubilee has no central office and restores land to families, not the state, and that Nozick's own theory makes room for rectifying past injustice. But Leviticus 25 restores the land whether or not the sale was unjust. It voids the very freedom of transfer that the theory treats as sacred. A sale in Israel was never final, because the seller never owned what a modern deed conveys. The socialist reading hears in Acts 4 the music of Karl Marx's formula in the *Critique of the Gotha Program* (1875), from each according to ability and to each according to need, and it rightly notes that the Torah refuses to let market outcomes stand as the last word. But the Jubilee returns land to the household and the clan, not to the collective, and Acts 5:4 insists that Ananias's field remained his own until he chose to give it. The sentence that cuts both readings is the same: "the land is mine." It denies the owner's absolute title, and it denies the state's. Both readings want Scripture to settle a quarrel between individual and collective ownership. Leviticus places ownership with neither.

I cannot swing that instrument from a safe distance. I have lent and expected a return. I have given to food banks and organized coat drives, and far less often asked whether the arrangements I benefit from build bondage into other people's lives. The eye that looks grudgingly on a borrower as the year of release draws near is not a villain's eye. It is the ordinary prudence of anyone with something to lose, which includes most people who will read this and the man who wrote it.

## The trumpet on the Day of Atonement

The detail that stays with me is the date. The Jubilee did not begin at the new year or the harvest. "On the Day of Atonement you shall sound the loud trumpet throughout all your land" (Leviticus 25:9, ESV). That was the day the high priest laid Israel's sins on the head of a goat and sent it into the wilderness (Leviticus 16:21–22). The release of debts and the release of guilt were proclaimed on the same afternoon, by the same God, to the same people. When the trumpet sounded, the man who had just been forgiven everything was told to go home and forgive his neighbor's loan.

We have learned to want the second release and to decline the first. That is the long temptation of Christian readings of the Jubilee, from Boniface's indulgences to the sermon that turns Leviticus 25 into a meditation on inner freedom. But the gospel does not dissolve the Jubilee. It carries it down to where the debt was deepest. God forgave us, Paul says, "by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross" (Colossians 2:14, ESV). The one who cancelled it was the true *go'el*, the near kinsman who took on flesh to redeem what his brothers had sold, and who did what the nearer redeemer in Ruth would not: he impaired his own inheritance to restore ours.

That changes the question without softening it. The debt I owed God is gone, and the prayer Jesus taught ties my standing in that forgiveness to the debts I hold over others. Some are money. More are the grudge kept on account, the person whose one bad season I have decided will define him, the church margin harvested to the edge because the budget was tight, and the national arrangement that lets one generation's misfortune become the next one's permanent address.

Naomi came home saying the LORD had brought her back empty, and she ended the story holding a grandson while the women of Bethlehem blessed God for not leaving her without a redeemer. Between those scenes stood a law that left grain at the edge of a field and a man who went past the law. The church now lives between the trumpet that sounded at Nazareth and the final Jubilee it announced, when the land and its people return to their true owner. Until then we are tenants. The field we call ours has edges, the notes we hold have a date on them, and each of us stands where the nearer redeemer stood, deciding whether to protect an inheritance or spend it. The trumpet has sounded over everyone who has been forgiven. What we release shows whether we heard it.
