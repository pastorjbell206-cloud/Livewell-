Most people who type this question into a search box aren't asking about political philosophy. They're holding a bill, or deciding whether the ache in their side is worth a visit they can't pay for, or stretching a prescription by taking it every other day. Some come from the other side of the counter: a physician who now spends much of the week arguing with insurers, or a small-business owner watching premiums swallow the raise she meant to give her employees. The question is abstract only for people it hasn't reached yet.

When it reaches a pastor's study, it usually comes in a quieter form. People describe a symptom they watched for months because they couldn't work out how to pay to learn what it was, and by the time they were examined the thing had grown. They want to know whether being poor made the difference, and whether God meant it to. The first answer is sometimes, honestly, yes. The second is no.

The American church has mostly treated health care as a political question, which has been a polite way of declining to treat it as a moral one. Some of that caution is honorable; nobody is ordained to write insurance law. But its cost falls on the people least able to carry it. This essay argues that care for the sick sits near the center of Christian obedience, that the church's history proves it, and that the fight over how a nation pays for that care is a real argument in which faithful Christians differ, though it can never excuse silence about those it leaves behind. The site takes up [disability](/writing/disability-dignity-and-the-image-of-god), [grief that doesn't lift](/writing/the-weight-that-stays) and [prayer and medicine in depression](/writing/mental-health-and-the-church-beyond-pray-about-it) elsewhere. Nothing here is medical, legal or financial advice.

## The healer who touched the leper

The Gospels don't treat healing as a side ministry Jesus performed between sermons. Matthew summarizes the whole Galilean ministry in one sentence:

> "And he went throughout all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every affliction among the people." (Matthew 4:23, ESV)

Teaching, proclaiming and healing are one announcement, and the healings are the part a sick person could feel. When John the Baptist sent from Herod's prison to ask whether Jesus was the one to come, Jesus answered with bodies: "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them" (Matthew 11:4-5, ESV). He was claiming that the age Isaiah promised (Isaiah 35:5-6) had begun. The kingdom arrives as restored eyes and legs before it arrives as a doctrine.

The manner matters as much as the fact. Leprosy in Israel was a sentence of exile; the law required the leper to live outside the camp and cry "Unclean" to anyone who came near (Leviticus 13:45-46). When a leper knelt and said that if Jesus was willing he could make him clean, Jesus could have healed him with a word from a safe distance.

> "Moved with pity, he stretched out his hand and touched him and said to him, 'I will; be clean.'" (Mark 1:41, ESV)

The touch came before the cure. For a man no one had touched in years, it may have been the larger mercy. That order, presence before remedy, runs through the whole Christian tradition of caring for the sick, and it's the thing most at risk in a system organized around billing.

Mark also records what the medicine of the day did to a woman who had bled for twelve years:

> "and who had suffered much under many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was no better but rather grew worse." (Mark 5:26, ESV)

Her illness had made her unclean and her treatment had made her poor. Jesus restored her to her body and to her people, calling her "Daughter" in front of the crowd (Mark 5:34).

In the parable of the sheep and the goats, the King welcomes the righteous for what they did for him unawares:

> "I was naked and you clothed me, I was sick and you visited me, I was in prison and you came to me." (Matthew 25:36, ESV)

In context it is harder than its use on church banners. The verb means to look in on someone and care for them, not to pay for their care from a distance. And interpreters have long divided over who "the least of these my brothers" are (Matthew 25:40, ESV). Many careful readers take them to be Jesus's own disciples, sent out poor and received or refused in his name (Matthew 10:40-42); others read the phrase as reaching every needy person. The narrower reading lets no one off, because the same Jesus told the parable of the Samaritan to explode the question of who counts as a neighbor (Luke 10:29), and he told it in medical terms:

> "He went to him and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine. Then he set him on his own animal and brought him to an inn and took care of him." (Luke 10:34, ESV)

The next morning he paid the innkeeper and promised to cover whatever more it cost (Luke 10:35). He did the binding himself, and he paid an institution to finish what he couldn't. The site's essay on [what the Good Samaritan was actually arguing](/writing/good-samaritan-actually-arguing) takes the parable further.

Ezekiel had already indicted Israel's shepherds, the rulers who fed themselves on the flock, and the first three failures in his charge sheet are medical:

> "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them." (Ezekiel 34:4, ESV)

Ezekiel assumes that the way a people's rulers treat its sick is something God will judge. That assumption, more than any single verse, makes health care a moral question for Christians and not merely a technical one.

## What the church did when the plague came

A skeptic may fairly ask whether any of this ever left the page. It did, when leaving the page could kill you. From about 250 an epidemic moved through the Roman Empire for more than a decade. It is often called the plague of Cyprian, after the bishop of Carthage who wrote *On Mortality* around 252 to steady a frightened church. In Alexandria the bishop Dionysius described the plague in an Easter letter that Eusebius preserved in his *Ecclesiastical History* (VII.22). Most of the Christians, he wrote, did not spare themselves. They visited the sick without fear, served them for Christ's sake and caught the disease from them, and many who nursed others back to health died themselves, including some of the best of the presbyters, deacons and lay people. The pagans of the city, he says, did the reverse: pushed away the sick at the first symptom, fled from their own families, and threw the dying into the streets. Dionysius was writing to his own flock and drew the contrast sharply, and a fair historian discounts some of it. But he was writing about his own dead to people who had buried them.

The sociologist Rodney Stark, in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), took accounts like this seriously as evidence. He argued that ordinary nursing, water, food, warmth and someone who stayed, would have sharply lowered the death rate in an age without antibiotics, so that Christians who nursed one another and some of their neighbors survived at higher rates than those who fled, and that over generations the difference helped turn a small sect into a large part of the empire. His figures are reconstructions, and historians debate them. The mechanism is less disputed. A people who believed the sick body was worth tending, even at the risk of their own, kept more people alive, and the watching world noticed.

By the fourth century the instinct had become an institution. Around 369 to 372 Basil of Caesarea built outside his city a complex for the poor, the sick and travelers, with physicians attached. Timothy Miller, in *The Birth of the Hospital in the Byzantine Empire* (1985), traced how hospitals in the Christian East grew from foundations like Basil's into serious medical institutions staffed by physicians. Gary Ferngren's *Medicine and Health Care in Early Christianity* (2009) draws the distinction that keeps the Christian claim honest. The church did not invent medicine. Greek physicians had long practiced it, and most early Christians used their doctors without embarrassment. What the church added was the conviction that the sick poor had a claim on the community, and an institution built to honor that claim: care given to strangers who could never pay, as a religious duty rather than a patron's favor.

The pattern held for a thousand years. In 1113 Pope Paschal II formally recognized a brotherhood that ran a hospital for pilgrims in Jerusalem, the order that became the Knights Hospitaller, whose members were taught to serve the sick as their lords. Guenter Risse's *Mending Bodies, Saving Souls* (1999) traces the line from such houses to the modern hospital. Many American hospitals still carry the names of the saints, orders and denominations that built them.

The honest record has other entries. The Hospitallers who served the sick as their lords also took up the sword. Some Christians have treated medicine as a failure of faith, and children have died of treatable illnesses because their parents refused a doctor. The science that makes modern medicine powerful came mostly from laboratories, not cloisters. A Christian who claims the whole of medicine for the church claims too much. But what the church did claim, that a stranger's sick body lays a demand on whoever sees it, whether or not he can pay, was not the default of the ancient world. Allen Verhey argued in *Reading the Bible in the Strange World of Medicine* (2003) that Jesus's healings were signs of God's coming reign, and that Christian care for the sick should be read the same way, as a token of that reign and never a replacement for it. So the church need neither despise medicine nor expect it to save us.

## How America built a system no one designed

The American arrangement is easier to judge once you see that nobody planned it.

For most of the nineteenth century medicine was cheap because it could do little. Paul Starr's *The Social Transformation of American Medicine* (1982) tells how that changed: as medicine became effective it became expensive, and the question of who pays moved to the center. The answer America drifted into began in 1929, when Baylor University Hospital in Dallas offered the city's schoolteachers hospital coverage for small monthly payments, the seed of Blue Cross. During the Second World War, federal wage controls kept employers from competing for workers with higher pay, but health benefits were treated differently, so companies offered insurance instead, and the tax code later confirmed the habit by excluding employer-paid premiums from taxable income. Americans came to get coverage through their jobs because of wartime wage policy and a tax rule, not because anyone argued it was wise or just. Losing a job came to mean losing a doctor.

Other nations chose differently. Germany under Bismarck created compulsory sickness insurance in 1883, run through regulated funds. Britain founded the National Health Service on 5 July 1948. In November 1945 Harry Truman asked Congress for national health insurance, and the plan died after the American Medical Association campaigned against it as socialized medicine. America built a patchwork instead. Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare and Medicaid into law on 30 July 1965. In 1986 Congress required hospitals that take Medicare and run emergency departments to screen and stabilize anyone who comes in, able to pay or not, which made the emergency room the country's last net and one of its most expensive. In 2003 Congress created health savings accounts. In March 2010 Barack Obama signed the Affordable Care Act, which expanded Medicaid, subsidized private insurance and barred insurers from refusing people for preexisting conditions. Christians have stood on both sides of most of these fights.

The economist Kenneth Arrow saw why it would be hard. In "Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care" (1963) he argued that medical care is unlike most goods: no one knows when illness will strike or what it will cost, the patient can rarely judge what he's buying, and the doctor knows far more than the buyer. So the ordinary market fails in predictable ways, and societies build other institutions, professional ethics, licensing, nonprofit hospitals and insurance, to fill the gap. That doesn't settle which remedy is best. It explains why none works as cleanly as promised.

The results are what people bring into the pastor's study. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau reported in 2022 that medical bills were the most common debt in collections on Americans' credit reports. Mark's woman who "had spent all that she had" is not an ancient curiosity. A good number of the people in our pews are her.

## Is health care a right? Three answers at full strength

The Christian claim that health care is a right didn't begin in an American party platform. In *Pacem in Terris* (1963), John XXIII listed among the rights of every person the right to life and bodily integrity and to the means needed for a proper human life, naming medical care among them, along with security when sickness or old age leaves someone unable to provide for himself (§11). He grounded them in the dignity of the person and paired each with a duty.

The same tradition had already built in a counterweight. In *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931), Pius XI set out subsidiarity: a larger body does wrong when it takes over what smaller communities can do, though it must help when they can't. Abraham Kuyper reached a Protestant cousin of the idea in his 1880 address on sphere sovereignty, opening the Free University of Amsterdam: family, church, school and the work of society each hold their own authority under God, which the state exists to protect, not swallow. Both traditions affirm that the sick have a claim on the community. Neither assumes the national government must be the one to pay it.

That leaves a distinction most of the American shouting ignores. To say health care is owed to the sick is to say that access to treatment should not finally depend on wealth, and that a society which lets it do so has done wrong. To say a particular program is the way to meet that obligation is a second claim, about means. The first belongs to the church's moral teaching. The second belongs to prudence, where faithful people have always differed.

The case for universal coverage through a single public payer, or something near it, runs like this. Illness is the purest example of a risk no one chooses, and the fairest way to carry an unchosen risk is to spread it across everyone. T. R. Reid, in *The Healing of America* (2009), found that the other wealthy democracies all cover everyone, by quite different means: Britain's government-run service, Canada's single public insurer, the regulated private funds of Germany and Japan. They spend less per person than America does. A single payer can bargain down prices, strip out billing overhead, and end the cruelty of losing coverage with a job. Christians who hold this view reach for Paul's image of the body: "If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (1 Corinthians 12:26, ESV). Paul was describing the church, not a nation, and honest advocates grant it. Their point is that people formed by that image should find pooled risk natural rather than suspect.

The case for a market-based, consumer-driven system is just as serious, and its Christian defenders are often among the most generous people in any congregation. John Goodman and Gerald Musgrave argued in *Patient Power* (1992) that American care is expensive because patients rarely see prices; when a third party pays, no one has reason to economize. Put the money in patients' hands, through savings accounts and insurance kept for true catastrophes, and prices would fall and quality would compete. A right to care is a claim on someone's labor, the nurse's, the surgeon's and the taxpayer's, and Christians should be wary of coercion hiding inside compassionate language. American returns have paid for a large share of the drugs and devices the world now uses, and killing those returns would cost lives no one would ever count. Public systems ration too, by waiting list rather than wallet. And Paul, who told the Galatians, "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2, ESV), added three verses later, "For each will have to bear his own load" (Galatians 6:5, ESV). Responsibility and mutual care belong together, and a system that erases the first will starve the second.

Between the two sit the mixed arrangements where most real systems live: Germany's since Bismarck, the Swiss and Dutch systems of mandatory private insurance, the Affordable Care Act's subsidies and regulated markets. Each keeps private providers and insurers while trying to leave no one out. Defenders say they capture what is true in both other cases; critics say they combine the costs of the market with the bureaucracy of the state. The Samaritan, who bound the wounds himself and then paid an institution to finish the work, is a fair picture of what they reach for.

Christians don't divide neatly even within one tradition. In March 2010 the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops opposed the final Affordable Care Act, chiefly because they judged it would allow federal funding of abortion and lacked adequate conscience protections, while the Catholic Health Association, which represents Catholic hospitals, supported it. Both affirmed that every person should have access to care; they disagreed about what the law would do and what conscience could accept. Christians argue not only about who pays but about what counts as care. Since its first centuries the church has held that the child in the womb is a human life owed protection, which is why what a health system pays for has never been a neutral question for Christians; the site takes that conviction up in [its essay on Christians and abortion](/writing/christians-and-abortion).

Many Christian families rely on health-sharing ministries, whose members, usually after affirming a statement of faith and a standard of conduct, pay a monthly share toward other members' eligible medical bills. The Affordable Care Act exempted members of ministries sharing continuously since the end of 1999 from its individual mandate. But they are not insurance, and the better ones say so plainly. They usually make no legal promise to pay any particular bill; they can limit or exclude preexisting conditions, cap what they share, and decline expenses they judge contrary to their standards; and they generally aren't regulated or backed as insurers are. A family considering one should read every guideline, ask what happens with a catastrophic diagnosis, and seek independent advice. They are real mutual aid, their limits fall hardest on the sickest, and they could not carry a nation.

## What binds every Christian, and what doesn't

Some things bind every Christian conscience. The body is good: God made it, the Son took it on, and the resurrection promises to raise it. The sick are to be cared for, and Christ counts that care as done to him. A person who can't afford a diagnosis bears the same image of God as one who can. A church that shrugs at the preventable suffering of the poor has set aside something the prophets and the Gospels put near the center. Whether the months people spend waiting to learn what is wrong with their bodies grieve God is not a contested question.

The mechanism is genuinely open: single payer, regulated markets, subsidized private insurance, mutual aid on a serious scale, or some mixture no one has tried. The Christian who reads Ezekiel 34 and concludes that markets serve the poor better than governments is not less serious about the sick than one who concludes the reverse. But the open question can't be used to close the first one. In much of the American church the difficulty of the policy argument has worked as a permission slip for silence about the moral one, as though not knowing the best mechanism excused anyone from calling the present outcome unacceptable. A man who sees his neighbor's house on fire and can't decide between hoses hasn't thereby earned the right to go back to bed. And the charitable tradition, beautiful as it is, isn't sufficient at the present scale. Free clinics and benevolence funds do real good, and they cannot carry tens of millions of uninsured and underinsured Americans.

That judgment has to be applied with the same instrument in both directions, or it's only a tribe in borrowed robes. To the Christian right: "It's not the government's job" has too often become, in practice, "It's no one's job." Subsidiarity was never a doctrine of abandonment, and a church that invokes it against public programs while funding nothing at the scale its alternative would require has kept the half of the teaching that costs it nothing. The market's defenders are right that prices matter and coercion is real. They are wrong when they speak as though the woman in Mark 5 simply made poor purchasing decisions.

To the Christian left the charge is just as sharp. It's easy to vote for compassion and let the vote stand in for it. The advocates of public coverage are right that no one chooses illness and that pooling an unchosen risk is a mercy. They are wrong when they speak as though a government program discharges what Christ asked of his own people's hands. A church that has handed the sick to the state can lose the habit of visiting them, and Matthew 25:36 says "visited," not "insured." Public systems have their own cruelties, and a Christian who treats every critic of single payer as heartless hasn't reckoned with patients abroad who wait months for treatment.

Both sides share a deeper assumption. Each has made the question about which system to trust, the state or the market, and neither says much about the church's own hands. Stanley Hauerwas argued in *Suffering Presence* (1986) that medicine at its heart is a practice of being present to people in pain, and that it can't be sustained without communities that have learned not to abandon the suffering. Whatever a nation decides about paying for care, it can't legislate someone into the chair beside the bed.

The indictment reaches the pulpit too. I have preached Ezekiel 34 and let it stay safely in ancient Israel, because letting it cross the street would have sounded political and cost me something. The theology was never unclear. What was missing was nerve, and the cost of that is measured in diagnoses that came a stage too late, in mothers who didn't survive births that should never have been dangerous, in children carrying untreated illness into adulthood like a debt they never signed for.

## The chair beside the bed

The letter of James holds two scenes in one chapter, and the American church has usually read them in different rooms. The first indicts rich landowners:

> "Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts." (James 5:4, ESV)

An economy in which someone can work two jobs and have no realistic way to learn what is growing in her body isn't the fraud James condemns. But he would recognize the family resemblance: the labor taken, the laborer's body left without what it needs. The cries are reaching God's ears. Whether they reach ours is the open question. Ten verses later the same letter moves to the sickbed:

> "Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord." (James 5:14, ESV)

The elders come to the house, and whatever else the oil signified, it meant someone from the church had put hands on a sick body. James saw no conflict between the prophet's anger at a system that crushed the poor and the elder's patience at a single bed; he expected each to keep the other honest. Anger at the system with no one at the bedside hardens into ideology. Presence at the bedside with no grief over what put the patient there softens into sentiment.

Christians ought to say one more thing the argument avoids. Every treatment postpones. The best system in the world, however it is funded, will eventually lose every patient. Medicine can't save us, and a people who expect it to will spend without limit and still despair (the site treats [how to die well](/writing/how-to-die-well) on its own). The Christian hope is not a longer life but a raised one, and the last book of the canon promises what no hospital can: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:4, ESV).

That promise doesn't make the argument about health care small. It makes it serious. If the body will be raised, what we do to bodies now is not a matter of indifference, and the question a society answers in its budgets, its hospitals and its churches is whose bodies it is willing to lose early and whose it will fight to keep. The resurrection doesn't answer that question for us. It sets the standard by which our answer will be judged. In the parable the King says "I was sick and you visited me" to people who never knew they had been visiting him.

A skeptic can believe none of this, and that's fair. What he shouldn't be able to say is that Christians spent sixteen centuries building hospitals and then went quiet when the sick in their own towns couldn't pay. The policy argument will go on, with Christians on every side of it. The bedside won't wait for it to finish. Somewhere near every church in America tonight, someone is watching a symptom they can't afford to name. Mark says Jesus was moved with pity, stretched out his hand and touched the man, and only then spoke the word that made him clean. None of us can speak that word. All of us can reach the bed, and we will be asked whether we did.
