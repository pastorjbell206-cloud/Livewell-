### The talk that never comes

Most Christian parents are waiting for a conversation. We call it the talk, and we've scheduled it for some future birthday when the child will be old enough, which usually means when we expect to feel less mortified. That age doesn't arrive. The parent never feels ready, and long before the appointed day the child has already received a substantial education from the screen, the school bus, the older cousin, the search bar and the culture itself, which teaches early and relentlessly and never asks anyone's permission.

Some of the instinct behind the single talk is right; a six-year-old doesn't need information meant for a sixteen-year-old. But the idea that sex can be handled in one conversation mistakes what children are actually learning. They're absorbing less a set of facts than a story about what the body is, what desire is for, and whether this subject can be spoken of in their own home. A child assembles that story out of a thousand small signals, and the most powerful signal a parent sends is the one sent without words: the flinch, the nervous laugh, the sudden interest in the dishes. Each of those teaches the child that this is the one room in the house he'll have to enter alone.

So the first thing to surrender is the belief that you control the timing. You don't. What you control is whether you're one of the teachers. A child who learns about the body from his parents in a hundred unremarkable moments grows up with a framework. A child who learns about it everywhere except at home grows up with the framework of everywhere, which in practice means the market and whatever reached him first.

Scripture assumed this long before we forgot it. "And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:6–7, ESV). Moses doesn't describe a lecture delivered once at the proper age. He describes a household in which the truth about God is spoken in the ordinary traffic of the day, at bedtime and at breakfast. The truth about the body belongs to that same traffic, a conversation kept open for eighteen years and beyond.

In practice that means starting earlier and smaller than feels comfortable. A four-year-old who learns the correct names for his body parts the same way he learns the word elbow has begun, without ceremony, to understand that his body isn't shameful or unspeakable. A six-year-old who asks where babies come from can be given a true answer sized to a six-year-old: God made mothers and fathers able to make a new person together, and the baby grows inside the mother's body until it's ready to be born. A child approaching puberty needs to hear what is about to happen to his body before it starts, from someone who loves him, rather than after, from someone who doesn't. A young teenager needs to hear what sex is for and why the church has always asked what it asks. Each is a small deposit into an account the child will draw on when a question terrifies him and he has to decide whom to ask. He'll go to the parent who spent ten years proving that this subject doesn't produce panic in this house.

There's a part of this that fathers in particular tend to abdicate, partly from embarrassment and partly from the old assumption that this is a mother's territory. I was raised without a father, and I have felt the hole where that instruction should have been. A boy who never hears his father speak plainly and without shame about sex, about what it's for and what it costs and how to treat a woman as a person rather than a body, won't therefore learn nothing. He'll learn everything, from sources that don't love him. The silence doesn't protect a son. It hands him over. I have five sons, and I refuse to leave the same hole in them by being too awkward to speak. Daughters need their fathers here too; a girl who has heard a good man speak of her body with reverence has a measure for every man who speaks of it otherwise.

### What the body is for

Before a parent can tell a child what to do with his body, the parent has to know what the body is. Most of us carry a loose Christian inheritance mixed with modern assumption, and our children can hear the seams.

The Bible's first word about human beings is a word about bodies. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The image of God is spoken over creatures who are male and female, whom God blesses and tells to be fruitful and fill the earth (Genesis 1:28), and at the end of that day God looks at all of it, flesh included, and pronounces it "very good" (Genesis 1:31, ESV). "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18, ESV), God says, and the answer to that loneliness isn't an idea but another body, a woman, and a union so complete that the text can only call it one flesh: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24, ESV). Then the quiet sentence that tells you what was lost: "And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25, ESV).

Shame enters the story only with sin, when the first couple sew fig leaves together and hide. And the first thing God does for two ashamed people, after the judgment has been spoken, is make them garments and clothe them (Genesis 3:21). God answers human shame by covering it, not by exposing or mocking it. A great deal of Christian teaching about sex would have gone differently if it had started there.

The Psalms press the same truth down to the level of the individual child. "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb. I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (Psalm 139:13–14, ESV). And again: "Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them" (Psalm 139:16, ESV). David isn't praising God for a soul that later took up residence in flesh. He's praising God for the flesh, for being known and made before he could know or make anything himself. That is the foundation of the Christian account of a person: you were given to yourself.

Paul brings all of this to bear on sex in Corinth, a busy first-century port where, for a man, sex with a prostitute was an ordinary transaction. Some of the Corinthian believers had apparently developed a theology to match. Paul quotes their slogan back to them: "'All things are lawful for me,' but not all things are helpful. 'All things are lawful for me,' but I will not be dominated by anything" (1 Corinthians 6:12, ESV). Their reasoning seems to have been that only the spirit counts, so what you do with the body doesn't touch the real you. Paul answers with a higher view of the body than they could imagine. "And God raised the Lord and will also raise us up by his power" (1 Corinthians 6:14, ESV). The body matters because it's going to be raised, and because it's already joined to Christ. And so: "Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body. Or do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God? You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:18–20, ESV).

That passage turns on a distinction every parent needs, between a body that is given and a body that is made. There are, roughly, three ways to think about the body. It can be raw material, the stuff a self edits into whatever it has decided it is. It can be a prison, the old dualist suspicion that the real person is trapped inside the flesh, a suspicion the church condemned in the Gnostics and then quietly absorbed in its own embarrassment about the body. Or it can be a gift, which is what Scripture says it is: you, given to you, knit together on purpose, destined for resurrection and held in trust for the God who made it.

To modern ears "you are not your own" sounds like a restriction. To a child it's also a shelter. If your body belongs to God, it doesn't belong to the boy pressuring you, the adult who wants a secret, or the industry that wants your attention. The sentence that asks for chastity also forbids anyone from taking what isn't theirs.

### How the church made sex strange, and how the West unmade it

Christian parents often assume that the ethic they want to pass on is simply what decent people believed until recently. The history is stranger and more useful. When the church first taught it, it was the odd view, and knowing that changes how you teach it to a child who already feels odd for holding it.

The historian Kyle Harper tells this story in *From Shame to Sin* (2013). Roman sexual morality, he argues, was organized around status and honor. What counted was who you were and whose body you used. The chastity of freeborn women was guarded fiercely, while a free man's use of slaves and prostitutes carried little moral stigma, because the bodies of the unfree were treated as available. Shame in that world was social, a matter of reputation and rank. The Christian movement, beginning with Paul's teaching on sexual immorality, replaced that system with something Rome found baffling: sin, a morality in which every person, slave or free, stood before God as a responsible moral agent, and the same demand fell on the senator and the slave girl alike. Christians, on Harper's account, made the bodies of the powerless matter in a world that had built its pleasures on them.

The early Christians knew how peculiar this made them. The anonymous second-century *Epistle to Diognetus*, written to a pagan inquirer, describes the believers as ordinary in their language, dress and customs, yet strange in a few telling ways: they marry and have children like everyone else, it says, but they don't abandon their newborns, and they share their table with anyone but not their bed. In an empire where exposing an unwanted infant was legal and unremarkable, those two refusals were a public argument about what a person is worth.

Then the church won, and something was lost in the winning. For more than a thousand years the ethic was the official morality of the West, honored in the breach about as often as in the keeping; men were excused for what women were ruined for. Christendom enforced it as a social code, and a social code can be kept by people with no idea why it exists. That helps explain how quickly it collapsed once the social pressure was withdrawn.

The withdrawal came in stages. The first oral contraceptive was approved in the United States in 1960, loosening the old link between sex and children. In 1965 the Supreme Court, in *Griswold v. Connecticut*, placed contraception inside a constitutional right to privacy. By 2015, when *Obergefell v. Hodges* extended marriage to same-sex couples, the law had caught up with a change in the culture's imagination that had been under way for generations.

But the laws and the pill were the proximate causes. The deeper change was in how we understand a self. The sociologist Philip Rieff argued in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966) that the West was trading religious man, who found himself by submitting to something larger, for psychological man, whose highest goal is his own well-being. Robert Bellah and his colleagues, in *Habits of the Heart* (1985), gave a name to the resulting outlook: expressive individualism, the belief that each person has a unique core of feeling and intuition that must be expressed if his life is to be his own. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), traced how this became the common sense of the West after the 1960s, a culture in which each of us is thought to have his own way of being human, found within rather than received.

Carl Trueman brought these threads together in *The Rise and Triumph of the Modern Self* (2020). He begins by asking how a sentence like "I am a woman trapped in a man's body" came to strike so many people as coherent, and answers by tracing a line from Rousseau's turn toward the inner self, through the Romantic poets, to Freud, who made sexuality central to what a human being is, and on to the New Left thinkers such as Wilhelm Reich and Herbert Marcuse, who made sexual liberation a political cause. Trueman's summary terms are the sexualization of psychology and the politicization of sex. Once sex is understood as the core of identity, and identity is understood as something the inner self declares, a sexual ethic that says no to a desire can only be heard as a no to a person.

This is the most important thing for a Christian parent to grasp. When your teenager hears you say that sex belongs within the marriage of a man and a woman, he may not hear a claim about acts at all. He may hear a claim about whether certain people, possibly including his friends, possibly including himself, are allowed to exist. You can't have a real conversation until you understand why it sounds that way, and it sounds that way because of centuries of history that shaped you as much as him.

And it did shape us. Evangelical Christians spent the last half century denouncing the sexual revolution while importing its central assumption. We told our children to follow their hearts. We made our peace with easy divorce with little of the resistance we brought to other questions, which made our sexual ethic look selective to everyone watching. Taylor's diagnosis describes the water all of us swim in, the church included, and I'm not standing on dry ground while I say so.

### What purity culture got wrong, and what it didn't

Many parents reading this grew up inside what is now called purity culture, which peaked in the 1990s, and some are still carrying it. The True Love Waits campaign, launched among Southern Baptists in 1993, invited teenagers to sign pledge cards promising abstinence until marriage. Joshua Harris's *I Kissed Dating Goodbye* (1997) became a bestseller and urged young people to give up dating altogether. There were rings, rallies and youth-group object lessons a generation still remembers with a wince: the rose passed around the room until its petals were gone, the piece of tape that loses its grip after being stuck to too many arms.

The parents and pastors who built that culture deserve a fair hearing. They were frightened, with reason, raising children in the years of the AIDS crisis and a rapidly sexualizing mass media. They believed, correctly, that sex outside of marriage does real harm and that the historic Christian ethic is true, and they wanted to give their children something firm to stand on. None of that was foolish, and much of it was love.

But the teaching failed. Linda Kay Klein's *Pure* (2018) gathers the testimony of women raised in evangelical youth culture who described carrying fear and shame about their bodies long into adulthood, including into marriages where the thing they'd been told was sinful was suddenly supposed to be good. Girls were often made responsible for boys' lust, as though a young woman's body were a hazard to be managed rather than a gift to be honored. Harris himself later said the book had done harm, and in 2018 he announced that the book would no longer be published. Rachel Joy Welcher, in *Talking Back to Purity Culture* (2020), writes as someone who still holds the Christian sexual ethic and argues that the movement betrayed it, in part by selling chastity as a transaction: wait now, and God will reward you with a wonderful marriage and wonderful sex. That promise had the same logic as the prosperity gospel. It turned obedience into an investment, and when the return didn't come, or a young person had already fallen, it had nothing left to say.

The deepest failure was the confusion of guilt with shame. Guilt says, I have done something wrong. Shame says, I am something ruined. The Christian gospel has a great deal to say about guilt, because guilt can be confessed and forgiven. Purity culture too often taught shame instead, and shame can't be forgiven, only hidden. The rose doesn't grow its petals back. Those object lessons taught children that sexual sin, and sometimes sexual harm done to them by someone else, left a permanent stain that grace could manage but never wash away.

The New Testament says the opposite. Right after listing the sexual sins the Corinthians had practiced, Paul writes, "And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11, ESV). Washed, not stained. And the letter to the Hebrews describes Jesus as the one "who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2, ESV). The God who clothed Adam and Eve in the garden is the same God who was stripped of his garments at the cross so that the ashamed could be covered.

Notice what the sexual revolution and purity culture have in common, because it's the thing you most need not to pass on. The revolution said that sex is who you are. Purity culture said, in effect, that your sexual history is what you're worth. Both made sex ultimate. Both loaded it with more meaning than any created good can carry. The Christian ethic, rightly taught, does something else. It honors sex as good and powerful enough to need boundaries, and it refuses to let sex be the final word about any human being, because that word belongs to God.

So the answer to bad teaching of a true ethic is better teaching of the same ethic. The failures of the 1990s show that we taught it with fear where we should have taught it with hope, and with shame where we should have taught it with grace. Your child deserves the ethic without the shame, which is harder to give than either the rules alone or no rules at all.

### The curriculum your child is already enrolled in

For a child with a connected device, the question about pornography is no longer whether he'll encounter it but when, often by accident. It's free, endless and built to escalate, and it hands him an account of sex cut loose from love, from covenant and from the personhood of the other, before his own desire has fully woken. Pornography isn't only a temptation your child will someday face. It's a curriculum he is already enrolled in, and it teaches desire what to want before he has any say.

Delay it as long as you can, with filters, devices kept in shared rooms and a first smartphone later rather than sooner. But also prepare him for it by saying, before it happens, that it will. Tell him it's a counterfeit, that sex is so good an entire industry was built to fake it, the way counterfeiters only copy real currency. Tell him that when he sees it, he can come to you and there won't be an explosion. A child told this in advance can come back. A child who stumbles into it alone, certain you'd be devastated, is cut off from you on the subject where contact matters most.

Consent and body safety belong in the same conversation, and they follow directly from "you are not your own." Because your child's body belongs to God, no one else has a right to it. Teach the correct names of body parts, because a child who has words can tell. Teach the difference between privacy and secrecy: privacy is the ordinary modesty of a family, while a secret an adult asks a child to keep about touching is the one thing an abuser needs. Tell your child that no one may touch the parts a swimsuit covers except to keep him healthy, with you there, and that if anyone ever does, or asks him to keep it quiet, he can tell you and you will believe him. And teach him to honor other people's bodies, including their no.

The church can't teach this with clean hands. The Boston Globe's 2002 reporting on abuse in the Catholic Archdiocese of Boston and the 2022 independent investigation into how the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee handled abuse allegations exposed the same sin in different robes, institutions protecting their reputations instead of children. "Whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him if a great millstone were hung around his neck and he were thrown into the sea" (Mark 9:42, ESV). ### When your child tells you who he is

Many parents eventually face a harder conversation: a child who says he's attracted to people of the same sex, or a child in real distress about being a boy or a girl. This essay is not medical advice. Questions about a child's mental health or about any medical treatment belong with qualified physicians and counselors, and no parent should try to settle them alone or from something they read.

The historic Christian ethic is that sex belongs within the lifelong marriage of a man and a woman, and that every other Christian is called to chastity. That calling binds the straight teenager exactly as much as the gay one, and the church's habit of treating one boy's attractions as a scandal while shrugging at another boy's pornography has been an injustice. Few people experience their attractions as chosen; what each of us chooses is what to do with them. Wesley Hill, a New Testament scholar who experiences same-sex attraction and holds the church's historic teaching, wrote in *Washed and Waiting* (2010) about the real cost of that faithfulness and the church's duty to make it bearable through friendship and belonging. A child who holds that ethic should find in his family and his church a home, not a quarantine.

On gender identity, the parent who affirms deserves to be heard in the form he'd sign. He sees his child suffering. He's been told by people he trusts that affirmation is what protects a child. He fears, above everything, losing his child, to despair or to estrangement, and he has watched the church answer people who didn't fit with mockery, silence and exclusion. Refusing to affirm, to him, is refusing the child. That fear isn't foolish. Despair in a young person is real, and a parent who takes it seriously is right to. Honor the feeling without ever agreeing with the despair, and if your child speaks of wanting to die, call or text 988 that day.

This essay's conviction is the one the Christian story gives. The body isn't raw material for the self to revise, and it isn't a prison the real self must escape. It's you, given to you, known and knit together before you had a single opinion about yourself. That account is more dignifying than the one that says you are whatever you currently feel and that the burden of assembling yourself rests on your own shoulders. The cultural story sounds like freedom and often lands like crushing weight.

But no Christian conviction requires contempt, and nothing in this ethic permits a parent to reject a child or put him out of the house. Speak fairly of the other view with your child; the moment he catches you caricaturing it, he'll stop trusting you on everything. Grieve the church's cruelty out loud so he knows you aren't defending it. A teenager who hears his parents speak with contempt about people wrestling with these questions learns that their faith makes people harder.

### Love through everything

The question that decides the rest is whether your child believes you'll stop loving him if he tells you the truth. That fear drives a child underground, and underground is the one place you can't reach him. Jesus told of a son who took his inheritance to a far country, and of a father who did not follow him there, yet saw him coming from far away. "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20, ESV). Paul puts the same love at the center of the gospel: "but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). God's love for us was never his approval of us. It came first, and it cost him a cross.

That is the difference a child needs to have lived inside. Not approval of everything. Love through everything. None of us gives it cleanly. We flinch, we lecture, we let fear speak where grace should have, and repentance in a household sometimes starts with a parent saying, I handled that badly, and I'm sorry. But the child who knows he has that love without condition is a child who keeps talking, and as long as he's talking to you, nothing is finished. What he'll believe in the hardest conversation was mostly settled in the thousand easy ones before it, in whether you flinched or held steady. Some of those are already behind you. The rest are waiting at your kitchen table, probably sooner than you'd choose.
