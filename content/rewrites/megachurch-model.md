Most Americans who go to church on a Sunday go to a large one. That sounds wrong until you hear how the sociologist Mark Chaves, who directs the National Congregations Study, describes the pattern in *American Religion: Contemporary Trends* (2011). Most congregations in the United States are small, many of them well under a hundred regular participants. But most churchgoers aren't in most congregations. They have gathered, decade by decade, into the largest ones. The typical church is a small room, and the typical churchgoer sits in a big one.

So the question in the title isn't academic. You may have found Christ in an auditorium of four thousand and never stopped being grateful. You may have slipped out of one after years in which nobody noticed you were gone. Or you may not go to church at all, and what you know of the megachurch is what the news told you when Willow Creek and Mars Hill came apart, which left you wondering whether the whole enterprise was a show with a building attached. The question tends to be asked in one of two tones, a sneer or a defense, and neither tone has helped anyone see much.

I'm a pastor, and I have borrowed more from the megachurch model than I'd like to admit. That's the honest place to start, because the megachurch is not a strange growth on the side of American Christianity. It is American Christianity with the volume turned up, and what it did well and badly the rest of us do too, only more quietly. This essay makes a simple claim that takes some time to earn. The megachurch asked a real question, how do we reach the people who aren't here, and it answered with real love and real skill. Its failures were not mainly failures of size. They came from treating a crowd as though it were already a body, and from building institutions in which both the man on the stage and the person in the seat could stay unknown.

## How the Megachurch Was Built

The megachurch didn't begin in 1975, and it didn't begin with a theater in the Chicago suburbs. Its roots run back to the early American republic, when the church lost its state support and had to win its people. Nathan Hatch's *The Democratization of American Christianity* (1989) tells how Methodist circuit riders, Baptist farmer-preachers and a crowd of independent revivalists won the new nation between the Revolution and about 1830 by speaking the language of ordinary people and trusting them to judge a preacher for themselves. Hatch honors what that gave the poor and the unschooled. He also names the cost. The audience became sovereign. A minister who couldn't hold a crowd lost it to one who could, and the American congregation learned to think of itself as the judge of what it was offered.

Charles Finney gave that sovereign audience a method. His *Lectures on Revivals of Religion* (1835) treated revival as something a church could bring about by the faithful use of means, much as a farmer brings in a harvest, and he answered the critics of his new techniques by pointing to the converts they produced. Finney was sincere, and many came to faith under his preaching. What outlived his revivals was the test he applied to them: a method proves itself by what it produces. If results are the test, why not study them scientifically?

That is what the church-growth movement set out to do. Donald McGavran was born in India to missionary parents and spent decades there as a missionary himself, watching some mission stations stagnate while whole families and villages elsewhere came to faith together. In *The Bridges of God* (1955) he argued that the gospel moves most naturally along the lines of kinship and community, through people movements rather than isolated individual converts pulled out of their world. In *Understanding Church Growth* (1970) he pressed the point into what became known as the homogeneous unit principle: people like to become Christians without having to cross barriers of race, language or class. McGavran became the founding dean of the School of World Mission at Fuller Theological Seminary in 1965, and his student and colleague C. Peter Wagner brought the ideas home to American congregations in *Your Church Can Grow* (1976). McGavran's burden was evangelistic. But his method taught a generation to ask what makes churches grow, to measure the answer, and to organize ministry around whatever the measurements said.

The American applications were already under way. In 1955, the same year as *The Bridges of God*, Robert Schuller began holding services at a drive-in theater in Orange County, California, preaching from the roof of the snack bar to families who stayed in their cars. His ministry would culminate in the Crystal Cathedral, dedicated in 1980. Schuller's genius, and his danger, was that he began from what the listener already wanted and worked backward to the gospel.

Twenty years later came the movement's founding story. Before launching Willow Creek Community Church in 1975, a young youth pastor named Bill Hybels and his team went door to door in the suburbs northwest of Chicago asking people who didn't attend church why they stayed away. The answers were consistent enough to become a design brief: church was boring, it seemed irrelevant to real life, it was always asking for money, and it left people feeling guilty. Willow Creek began in a rented movie theater in Palatine, Illinois, and built its weekend services to remove those objections, with contemporary music, drama, plain language and sermons aimed at the questions ordinary people were already asking. A midweek service carried the heavier teaching for believers. The name for this approach became seeker-sensitive, and Willow Creek grew into one of the largest churches in the country. In 1992 it formed the Willow Creek Association to share the model, and thousands of congregations around the world took their cues from it.

In 1980 Rick and Kay Warren started Saddleback Church in southern Orange County after a similar season of listening to the unchurched people around them. Warren built his ministry around a composite portrait of the local unchurched man, whom he called Saddleback Sam, and in *The Purpose Driven Church* (1995) he gave pastors a clear and portable method. Then came *The Purpose Driven Life* (2002), which sold tens of millions of copies.

The Hartford Institute for Religion Research, where the sociologist Scott Thumma has led the study of large congregations for decades, defines a megachurch as a Protestant congregation with a sustained average weekly attendance of two thousand or more. By that measure they were rare in 1970. Thumma's research with Warren Bird, published through Hartford and Leadership Network, tracked their rise to well over a thousand by the early twenty-first century, spread across denominations and nondenominational networks and concentrated in the growing suburbs of the South and West. The count kept climbing through the 2010s while American church attendance as a whole declined, which is Chaves's pattern again: fewer people in church, and more of them in the largest rooms.

## The Strongest Case for the Megachurch

Before any criticism, the megachurch deserves the defense its own people would make, and it is a strong one.

Begin with the people. Millions of Americans who would never have walked into a traditional church walked into a megachurch, and many of them met Christ there. They came because a coworker invited them to a place that looked like somewhere normal people gathered, where nobody expected them to know when to stand, where the music was in a style they recognized and the sermon addressed the marriage or the debt or the grief they were actually carrying. The founders of the movement believed that the church bears responsibility for every needless barrier between a stranger and the gospel, and they were right to believe it. Paul put the principle in a sentence: "I have become all things to all people, that by all means I might save some" (1 Corinthians 9:22, ESV). He wrote it about eating and customs and the manner of his life, and the seeker church read it as a charter for adapting everything around the message so that the message itself could be heard.

Nor is a large congregation a modern novelty. The church's first sermon ended with a crowd: "So those who received his word were baptized, and there were added that day about three thousand souls" (Acts 2:41, ESV). Luke goes on to describe a church that met in two kinds of places, in the great public space of the temple courts and around tables in ordinary houses, and the megachurch has always pointed to that pattern as its own. Charles Spurgeon, a Baptist, opened the Metropolitan Tabernacle in London in 1861 with seating for thousands, and nobody thinks of him as a church-growth consultant. Size, by itself, is not an innovation or an offense.

The best megachurches also knew the danger of the crowd better than their critics often admit. Rick Warren's *The Purpose Driven Church* argued that the key issue for churches was health rather than growth, and that a healthy church grows as a byproduct, the way a healthy body does. He described a series of concentric circles, from the surrounding community to the crowd that attends, then to the congregation of members, the committed and finally the core of servants and leaders, and the whole method was designed to move people inward from the edge. The goal was a church that grows larger and smaller at the same time, as newcomers are drawn in on Sunday and folded into small groups during the week. Warren insisted, then and later, that the methods change while the message stays the same.

Scott Thumma and Dave Travis made a related case in *Beyond Megachurch Myths* (2007). They argued that several things Americans assume about megachurches are false or overstated. Most do not preach the prosperity gospel. Many run extensive systems of small groups and serious classes. Many are more racially mixed than the average American congregation. And the stereotype of the megachurch as a one-man show, they argued, ignores the large staffs and lay leaders who carry most of the actual ministry.

There is also the matter of what a large church can do that a small one usually can't. It can staff a children's ministry with trained people and run it with safety procedures a congregation of sixty may never get around to. It can fund recovery ministries, counseling centers, food pantries and relief work at a scale that changes a region. Celebrate Recovery, a Christ-centered recovery program begun at Saddleback in 1991, now meets in thousands of churches of every size. In the early 2000s Kay Warren led a campaign to wake American evangelicals up to the HIV and AIDS crisis, at a time when many churches preferred not to talk about it.

A fair critic has to concede all of this, and I do. The megachurch was, among other things, an act of love toward people the older churches had stopped expecting to see. It asked who wasn't in the room, and it grieved over the answer.

## What Went Wrong: Consumers, Celebrities and Reveal

The criticism began early, and the most serious of it came from people who shared the movement's convictions. In *Dining with the Devil: The Megachurch Movement Flirts with Modernity* (1993), Os Guinness took his title from the old proverb that whoever sups with the devil needs a long spoon. He didn't reject the tools of management, marketing and the social sciences, but he argued that they are not neutral, that they carry assumptions about what people are and what success means, and that a church which learns to be relevant on modernity's terms will slowly find itself captive to them. A year later, in *God in the Wasteland* (1994), the theologian David Wells described a God who had become weightless in the evangelical church: still affirmed, still sung about, but resting so lightly on the life of the congregation that he made little difference to it. Wells laid much of the blame on the logic of marketing, in which the needs of the consumer set the agenda and the church succeeds by meeting them.

When a church begins by asking what the unchurched dislike about church, it has already accepted the unchurched person as the judge of what church should be. That can be a missionary's humility. It can also be a salesman's reflex, and over time the difference is hard to keep. A service engineered around preference trains preference. It teaches people, week after week, to evaluate the music, the preaching, the parking and the children's wing, and to go elsewhere if something better comes along. Critics noted early that much megachurch growth was transfer growth, Christians leaving smaller congregations nearby rather than people coming to faith, and that the instinct that drew them in could carry them out again. The movement had meant to reach the world with the church. In practice it often reached the church with the world's way of choosing.

The most honest evidence came from Willow Creek itself, which deserves real credit for it. In 2007 the church published *Reveal: Where Are You?*, by Greg Hawkins, its executive pastor, and Cally Parkinson, reporting on surveys of its own congregation and others. Greater participation in church activities did not, by itself, predict greater love for God and neighbor. A significant share of the most committed people described themselves as stalled in their faith or dissatisfied with how the church was helping them grow. The report concluded that the church needed to help mature believers take responsibility for their own spiritual lives rather than depend on programming, and Hybels acknowledged publicly that the findings had forced a hard look at the model. Few institutions study themselves and publish the result. But the result said what Guinness and Wells had said from outside. A church can be very good at gathering people and much less good at forming them.

Then there was the stage. The megachurch concentrates attention, and attention concentrates on a face. The senior pastor becomes the voice of the brand, the reason people visit, the author of the books and the figure on the screen at every campus, and the institution's finances and reputation come to depend on him. Paul met an early version of this in Corinth, where the congregation had split into admiration societies: "What I mean is that each one of you says, 'I follow Paul,' or 'I follow Apollos,' or 'I follow Cephas,' or 'I follow Christ'" (1 Corinthians 1:12, ESV). His response was to make himself smaller. "So neither he who plants nor he who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:7, ESV). The church-growth movement took its name from a word Paul reserved for God.

The two most visible collapses of the era followed the pattern Paul warned against, and they can be told briefly. In April 2018 Bill Hybels retired early from Willow Creek after several women, including former staff, alleged sexual misconduct, which he denied. The church's elders at first defended him, then apologized to the women, and resigned that summer along with the lead pastor who had succeeded him. An independent advisory group reported in 2019 that it found the allegations credible. In Seattle, Mark Driscoll had built Mars Hill Church from a small gathering in 1996 into a network of campuses across several states, most of them receiving his sermons by video. In 2014, after former elders and staff described a culture of domineering and harsh leadership, Driscoll resigned, and within weeks the church announced it would dissolve, its campuses becoming independent churches or closing. The site's essay on [sexual abuse in the church](/writing/sexual-abuse-crisis-in-the-church) deals with abuse and cover-up directly, and if a church leader has abused you or someone you know, that is a crime, and it belongs first with the police, not with the church's own process. If a child is in danger, call 911. If you are still carrying what a church did to you, [this essay on church hurt](/writing/when-the-church-is-what-hurt-you) is written for you.

These stories don't prove that every large church is corrupt. Small churches have hidden abusers too, often with fewer people in a position to notice. But in both of these cases the structures meant to hold the leader accountable had grown up around the leader and were slow to believe what they were told. The larger the platform, the more there is to protect, and the more people depend on the man at the center staying where he is. That is not a law of nature. It is a temptation, and the megachurch model too often built it into the foundation.

## A Crowd Is Not a Body

The Gospels draw a distinction the church-growth movement tended to blur. Jesus drew crowds wherever he went, and the evangelists always distinguish the crowd from the disciples. The crowd follows for a while, for the bread or the healing or the spectacle. The disciples are the ones he calls, knows and sends. And at least once Jesus deliberately thinned his own following. After the feeding of the five thousand he taught hard things about eating his flesh and drinking his blood, and John records that even many who had been called his disciples proved to be part of the crowd: "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him" (John 6:66, ESV). No church-growth consultant would have advised that sermon. Jesus let them go, and then he turned to the ones who were left: "So Jesus said to the twelve, 'Do you want to go away as well?'" (John 6:67, ESV).

A crowd is a group of people facing the same direction. A body is a group of people joined to one another, so that each depends on the others and is known by them. That distinction, rather than any number, is the real question about the megachurch. When Paul wrote that the church is a body, he was not using a figure of speech for an organization. The "members" of 1 Corinthians 12 are limbs, not names on a roll. "The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you,' nor again the head to the feet, 'I have no need of you'" (1 Corinthians 12:21, ESV). Paul goes further and insists that the parts that seem weakest are the ones the body can't do without, and he describes a mutual exposure no audience can have: "If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (1 Corinthians 12:26, ESV).

It matters who Paul was writing to. The Corinthian church met in households. Jerome Murphy-O'Connor, working from the floor plans of excavated Roman houses in *St. Paul's Corinth* (1983), estimated that a single household assembly could have held only a few dozen people. So the body Paul described was, in one sense, a small church. And the small church was failing the test. The previous chapter describes the Lord's Supper in that congregation, where the wealthy ate their fill before the poor arrived: "For in eating, each one goes ahead with his own meal. One goes hungry, another gets drunk" (1 Corinthians 11:21, ESV). A room of forty was perfectly capable of being a crowd with a head table. Size did not make Corinth a body, and it would be a mistake to think size alone unmakes one.

What size does is make anonymity easy. That is the distinction worth carrying away from the megachurch debate. The problem was never that too many people came. The problem was a structure in which two kinds of people could remain unknown. At the top, a leader could become a figure on a screen, visible to everyone and known by almost no one, accountable in practice only to people he had chosen. At the bottom, an attender could come for years, consume the preaching and the music, and never be known, needed or corrected by anyone. The letter to the Hebrews assumes the opposite on both counts: "Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account" (Hebrews 13:17, ESV). Leaders who will give an account for souls have to know whose souls they are. Jesus describes the good shepherd the same way: "The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out" (John 10:3, ESV).

This is why Mark Dever's *Nine Marks of a Healthy Church* (2000) placed such weight on things the seeker model had quietly set aside: expositional preaching, a clear account of conversion, and above all meaningful membership and church discipline. Dever wasn't arguing for small churches; he was arguing that a church is a covenanted people who take responsibility for one another, and that a congregation where no one can be missed, admonished or restored has stopped being a church in the full sense, however many people attend. Warren's circles, fairly read, were an attempt to answer the same problem, and many large churches have answered it well, with elders who actually shepherd small flocks within the larger whole. The honest verdict is that a megachurch can be a body, and some have been, but it can go on for years without noticing that it isn't one, because the crowd keeps coming.

## Multisite, the Small Church and the Temptations of Every Size

The movement has not stood still. Since the 1990s many large churches have grown by going multisite: one church meeting in many locations, often with a local campus pastor and a sermon delivered by video from the main campus. Geoff Surratt, Greg Ligon and Warren Bird made the case in *The Multi-Site Church Revolution* (2006). Multisite lets a growing church reach new neighborhoods without building ever-larger auditoriums, keeps congregations closer to where people live, and raises up local leaders. Those are real goods. The objection, put most carefully by Jonathan Leeman in *One Assembly* (2020), is that the New Testament word for church, *ekklesia*, means an assembly, and a church that never assembles is not one church in any sense the apostles would recognize. On that reading, a multisite church is several congregations sharing a name, and often sharing a preacher most of their members will never meet. Multisite churches with strong local elders can answer part of that objection, but not all of it, and Mars Hill showed what the unanswered part can mean when the man on the screen fails: the whole network came apart with him.

Alongside multisite, a quieter counter-movement has grown up. Karl Vaters's *The Grasshopper Myth* (2012) argued that small churches are not failed big churches and that faithfulness in a congregation of seventy is not a consolation prize. Michael Horton's *Ordinary* (2014) pushed back against the restlessness that is always looking for the next innovation, and pointed the church back to the ordinary means God has promised to use: the word preached, baptism, the Lord's Supper and prayer. Kevin DeYoung and Ted Kluck, in *Why We Love the Church* (2009), defended the plain, institutional local congregation against a generation that found it disappointing. Much of this is a needed correction.

But the small church has its own temptations, and they deserve the same honesty. A small congregation can be run by one family for three generations and be as unaccountable as any celebrity. It can be closed to strangers and call its closedness intimacy. Small churches like to quote Jesus's promise, "For where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (Matthew 18:20, ESV), as comfort for low attendance, but in context the promise belongs to a passage about confronting sin and restoring a brother. It is a word about accountability, and a small church can avoid accountability as skillfully as a large one. Dietrich Bonhoeffer warned in *Life Together* (1939) that the person who loves his dream of community more than the actual community of believers will end by destroying the community. The dream of the small, warm, close-knit church can be exactly that kind of wish, and it can make a Christian as much a consumer as any megachurch did, shopping this time for intimacy.

## What the Crowd Cannot Carry

So are megachurches good for Christianity? Some have been very good for a great many people, and some have done grave harm, and most have done both at once, which is true of every church, including the ones we love. The better question is the one the megachurch era forced on all of us. The founders asked who wasn't in the room, and the church still needs to ask it. The question they asked too late was who in the room was actually known.

That question doesn't let anyone off. It doesn't let the pastor off who wants a crowd more than he wants a flock, and every pastor, whatever the size of his church, knows that want. It doesn't let the churchgoer off who chose a large room because nobody there would ask how his marriage was, or a small one because nobody there would ask him to change. The megachurch did not invent the consumer or the celebrity. It gave them a stage and a parking lot, and it showed the rest of us what we look like when the lights are brighter.

It is worth remembering where the church began. Jesus was welcomed into Jerusalem by a crowd and condemned before one, and when he died, the crowds went home and most of his disciples had already scattered. The church was built on a body broken and raised rather than on a crowd, and on a few frightened people who were found again by the one they had abandoned. Only after that did the numbers come, and Luke is careful to say whose numbers they were: "And the Lord added to their number day by day those who were being saved" (Acts 2:47, ESV).

The growth was his. It still is. What is left to us is the slower work that no stage can do and no small room guarantees: to be known and to know, to be missed when we are absent, to let someone who has watched our lives tell us the truth about them. You can find that in a congregation of five thousand, and you can miss it entirely in a congregation of fifty. Wherever you sit this Sunday, the question the megachurch leaves behind is not how many people are in the room with you. It is whether any of them would notice, and grieve, if you were gone, and whether you would notice them.
