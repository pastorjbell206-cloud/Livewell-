Ask a certain kind of church a hard question about sin and you won't get silence. You'll get fluency. We can name sin at a scale our grandparents rarely managed: in systems and structures, in the long arithmetic of history, in the way an economy can grind the poor without any single villain pulling the lever. That's a real skill, and much of what it describes is true. But listen to the word that has gone quiet underneath it. Sin in the singular. Sin in the first person. Sin as something I did this week, and would rather not say.

I'm inside the church I'm describing, and I have helped the word go quiet. So this essay has to begin fairly, with the case my own side of the argument tends to skip, and it has to end with the one person it can't exempt.

The distinction that matters here isn't between personal and structural sin. Scripture holds both, and the church needs both. It's the distinction between naming sins and confessing sin. You can name sins in the third person indefinitely, with great accuracy and at great volume, and never once say the sentence that the gospel actually asks for. A church can become expert in the first and forget the second. Progressive Christianity recovered the Bible's language for the sins of systems, which was a genuine gain, and then quietly lost the confession with a first-person pronoun in it, which was a catastrophe. The recovered half, cut loose from the lost half, has become one of the most respectable forms of self-righteousness the modern church has produced. And the conservative church, which likes to think it never lost the word, had shrunk it long before.

## What the progressive church got right

Start with the strong version of the progressive case, because it deserves to be heard in the form its defenders would sign.

For a long stretch of American Christianity, sin meant a short list of private vices: what you drank, what you watched, whom you slept with. Meanwhile the public sins went unnamed from the pulpit. The segregated congregation, the underpaid worker, the war sanctified from the pulpit without a tremor of conscience: none of these made the list. The word had been shrunk until it fit inside other people's bedrooms and never reached the church's own balance sheet. And the language of personal sin was often used as a weapon rather than a mirror. It was aimed at women more than men, at the poor more than the comfortable, at the body more than the bank account. It told victims to examine their own hearts and left the powerful alone. People raised under it learned to hear the word "sin" as a tool for keeping them small.

So a generation pushed back, and the pushback was largely right. They insisted that sin is bigger than private habits; that it builds itself into laws and markets and customs and outlasts the people who built it; that a society can be guilty in ways no individual intends. They had the prophets on their side. Amos stood in a prosperous nation and indicted an entire economy, not a few greedy men. Recovering that wasn't a fad. It was obedience. The progressive Christian also saw that preaching shame at people who were already crushed did not produce holiness; it produced despair, or hiding. A defender of that recovery would say: we didn't stop talking about sin. We started talking about the sins the church had spent a century ignoring.

That's true, and I don't want to give any of it back. The trouble lies in what went out the door while everyone was looking at what came in.

## How the word went quiet the first time

This has happened before, almost beat for beat, and the last time it did not end the way the optimists expected.

The American mainline didn't abandon the doctrine of sin in one act of unbelief. It mislaid it slowly across the long Protestant afternoon between the Civil War and the First World War, and for reasons that felt like maturity. The educated West had learned to read the human story as a climb, and the harsher parts of the old faith (wrath, judgment, the bondage of the will, the corruption native to every heart) came to seem like the theology of a frightened age the species had outgrown. Sin, where it survived in the preaching, softened into ignorance or maladjustment, a residue of bad circumstances that better schools and better laws would wash out.

H. Richard Niebuhr, looking back in 1937 in *The Kingdom of God in America*, fixed the whole drift in one sentence that still draws blood. Liberal Protestantism, he wrote, had come to preach a gospel in which "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross." Every clause is something quietly removed. No sin, so no need of a cross. Niebuhr wasn't sniping from outside. He was a churchman grieving from inside his own tradition, naming what it had done to itself while believing all along that it was growing up. The danger in that drift was not only that sinners would stop hearing about sin. It was that reformers would stop hearing about their own. A reformer who believes his heart is clean has no brake, because he has audited the sin in everyone but the auditor.

G. K. Chesterton had made the point more lightly a generation earlier. In *Orthodoxy* (1908) he observed that "Certain new theologians dispute original sin, which is the only part of Christian theology which can really be proved." He meant it as a joke and as a warning. You didn't need a revelation to believe in human sin. You needed a newspaper.

Then, in 1973, the warning came from an unexpected quarter. Karl Menninger, the Kansas psychiatrist whose clinic in Topeka was one of the most famous in the country, published *Whatever Became of Sin?* Menninger was no preacher. He had watched American culture, the churches included, retire the word, turning sin into crime or into sickness, into something assigned to causes outside the person and treated without a verdict. His worry was not prudish. It was that a society that can no longer say "I have sinned" has lost the one category that takes human responsibility seriously, and with it the possibility of repentance, which is the only road back that doesn't run through excuse. He was writing to a whole culture. He could have been writing to a congregation like mine.

## The sins of the good

Here's what the older church knew that both modern wings forgot. Its vocabulary of sin was not mainly a vocabulary for bad people. It was a vocabulary for the good.

In the deserts of Egypt at the end of the fourth century, the monk Evagrius Ponticus wrote a short manual for people trying to live wholly for God. In the *Praktikos* he named eight kinds of destructive thoughts that assail the soul, from gluttony and lust at one end to vainglory and pride at the other, and he noticed something about the last two. They come for the ones who have made progress. When a monk has overcome his appetites, vainglory arrives to congratulate him on it; when he has overcome vainglory, pride arrives to tell him he did it himself. Two centuries later Gregory the Great, in his vast commentary on Job, reworked the list into what the West would remember as the seven deadly sins and placed pride at the root of them all. These men were not writing about the obviously wicked. They were writing about the dangers that grow best in the soil of real virtue.

That's the part we lost, and we lost it in a way that happens to flatter each side. The conservative church kept a list of sins that mostly belonged to other people: the sexually disordered, the drunk, the secular. The progressive church built a different list that also mostly belongs to other people: the racist, the colonizer, the fundamentalist, the powerful. The lists differ. The grammar is the same. Neither list has much room for pride, or contempt, or the pleasure of being right about someone else, which are the sins that tend to afflict people who make lists.

Cornelius Plantinga, in *Not the Way It's Supposed to Be* (1995), gave the church a definition that closes the escape hatch. Sin, he wrote, is "culpable shalom-breaking": the blameworthy vandalism of the peace and flourishing God intended for his world. Hold both halves. *Shalom-breaking* is as wide as the world; it reaches every fractured system and every economy that grinds the poor, and it licenses all the structural analysis the prophets ever demanded. But *culpable* keeps a will in the sentence. It won't let the breaking float free of a breaker. The modern church, on the left especially, has grown fluent in the breaking and quiet about the culpability. It can describe the wreckage at length without locating a hand on the hammer, least of all its own.

Here is the mechanism, and I'm describing my own heart as much as anyone's. When sin is only structural, it is never quite mine. A structure has no soul to examine at midnight; a system can't kneel. If the real locus of sin is the institution or the economy or the inherited arrangement of power, then I'm no longer a sinner. I'm at worst a beneficiary and at best an ally, and either way the verb has slid off me onto something I can study from a clean distance. I get to be against sin without ever being a sinner. It's among the most comfortable positions in the history of moral thought.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw the social form of this in *Life Together* (1939), written after he had led an underground seminary for the Confessing Church. In the chapter on confession he observed that a pious fellowship permits no one to be a sinner, and so everyone hides his sin from the others and from himself, and the community of the righteous becomes a community of the lonely. Bonhoeffer was writing for young pastors of the Confessing Church. The description fits the modern progressive congregation, where sins can be named endlessly in the abstract but no one can safely be a sinner, because being found on the wrong side of the line is social death. It fits the conservative congregation just as well, where the pious fellowship hid its sinners for decades, sometimes predators among them, rather than let the fellowship look less pious. Both are rooms in which confession has become too dangerous to risk. Both have kept the word "sin" and lost the sinner.

## The Pharisee changed parties

Scripture doesn't let anyone off this lightly, and the place it pins us is a parable most of us have preached without flinching.

Luke says plainly whom it was for: "He also told this parable to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and treated others with contempt" (Luke 18:9, ESV). Two men go up to the temple to pray. The Pharisee isn't a cartoon hypocrite. He really does fast twice a week and tithe everything he gets. By every public standard of his day he stands on the right side. His sin isn't in his analysis; it's in his pronoun. "God, I thank you that I am not like other men, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even like this tax collector" (Luke 18:11, ESV). The other man keeps no list. He can't see past himself far enough to compile one. "But the tax collector, standing far off, would not even lift up his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast, saying, 'God, be merciful to me, a sinner!'" (Luke 18:13, ESV). Jesus says it was that man, and not the religiously serious one, who went home justified.

The modern progressive heart can compose the Pharisee's prayer word for word with the list updated: I thank you that I'm not like the bigots, the nationalists, the ones still asleep in the pews I walked out of. The modern conservative heart composes it just as fluently with a different list: I thank you that I'm not like the secularists, the libertines, the ones who have abandoned the truth. Notice what indicts both. It isn't that the Pharisee faked his virtues. He had them. Real virtue became the ground he stood on to look down, and he went home unforgiven for the one sin that hides perfectly inside being right.

Paul springs the same trap on purpose in Romans. He spends the end of his first chapter on the obvious sins of the pagan world, and the religious reader nods along until the door shuts: "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things" (Romans 2:1, ESV). A chapter later the conclusion is total: "For there is no distinction: for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:22–23, ESV). No distinction between the ones who see and the ones who don't, the early adopter and the holdout, the progressive and the traditionalist.

And there is a scene in Isaiah that shows the right order of things. The prophet sees the Lord high and lifted up, and his first words are these: "And I said: 'Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!'" (Isaiah 6:5, ESV). Both registers are in one sentence. There is a people of unclean lips, a corporate and structural sin, and Isaiah names it. But he names himself first. He doesn't say "I dwell among a people of unclean lips" and stop, which is the progressive temptation. He doesn't say "I am a man of unclean lips" and leave the people out, which is the conservative one. He puts himself inside the indictment and then widens it, and he does it because he has seen God. The order isn't accidental. A person who has actually stood before holiness doesn't begin with the sins of the nation.

## Where confession becomes safe

Why did we stop saying it? I've come to think the deepest reason is practical rather than intellectual: we had nowhere to take it.

A movement can analyze. It can advocate. It can build a moral vocabulary of real sophistication and aim it at the powerful with courage. What it cannot do is forgive. And a community that cannot forgive cannot afford to have sinners inside it, so it exports its sin, locating it always in someone outside the circle. That's not hypocrisy in the ordinary sense. It's a survival mechanism. If there's no mercy on offer, the only safe place to be is among the accusers. The first word of Jesus' public preaching was not an analysis: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ESV). Repentance isn't regret, and it isn't analysis. It's the will turned around, and it's only possible where there's something to turn toward.

That's why the confession of sin is good news and not bad. John writes to a church that already considered itself saved: "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:8–9, ESV). The first sentence is the diagnosis. The second makes it bearable. A church that believes the second sentence can afford the first. A church that has quietly stopped believing in a God who forgives, or never quite believed that forgiveness could reach people like us, will always find the first sentence too expensive, and will spend its moral energy making sure the sin belongs to someone else.

For centuries English-speaking Protestants said a confession on ordinary mornings that held everything this essay has been reaching for. Thomas Cranmer's *Book of Common Prayer* of 1552 gave the congregation a general confession to say together, and one line of it runs: "We have left undone those things which we ought to have done, and we have done those things which we ought not to have done, and there is no health in us." Look at what that sentence contains. It confesses sins of commission, the things I did. It confesses sins of omission, the things I didn't do, which is where most of our complicity in structural wrong actually lives: the injustice I benefited from and didn't oppose, the neighbor I didn't see. And it's said in the first person plural by every first person singular in the room. The prayer book never had to choose between the personal and the structural. It simply asked everyone to say "we" and mean "I."

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn learned the same thing in the Soviet camps and wrote it into *The Gulag Archipelago* (1973). If only there were evil people somewhere, he reflected, who could be separated from the rest of us and destroyed; but the line dividing good and evil runs not between states or classes or political parties, but through every human heart. He reached that conclusion as a prisoner of a regime that had located all evil in a class enemy, and he turned it on himself before he turned it on anyone else.

## The ditch on the other side

There is another ditch, and people dive into it with relief. There's a way of recovering personal sin that quietly retires the social kind, that says the only real sin is in the individual heart, so stop talking about systems, stop talking about race, stop talking about the poor, just get people saved. That isn't the gospel either. It's the gospel with its arms cut off, and it's the version many conservative churches have preached for a century while accusing the progressives of losing the plot. Isaiah's confession includes the people. The prayer book includes what we left undone. Amos is still in the canon. The slaveholder and the abolitionist who despised him both needed the cross, and they needed it equally, though not for the same sins.

So the recovery I'm arguing for isn't smaller than the one the progressive church made. It's larger. It asks for a doctrine of sin wide enough to indict the system and sharp enough to indict the one analyzing it: me, at this desk, writing these sentences with the quiet satisfaction of someone who has figured something out about everyone else.

Because the trap closes again right here. The temptation in writing an essay about the sin we stopped naming is to name it in everyone but the author, to make the forgetting itself the new thing I'm grateful not to be guilty of. *God, I thank you that I am not like the progressives who forgot to repent, or the conservatives who never looked at the system.* There's no exit from that hall of mirrors that doesn't run through the first person. I have been fluent about sin and silent about mine. Not "we," comfortably. Not "they," eloquently. I.

That's the sin we stopped naming. Not the abstract one, which we name constantly, with footnotes. The one with our own name on it. We built whole theologies, sincere and intelligent and partly true, that let us avoid saying it aloud.

The tax collector said seven words and went home justified. We have written libraries and gone home certain. The God who keeps the only record that holds was never impressed by the libraries, and he has not stopped listening for the seven words. What's left, when the essay is finished and the analysis is done, is whether we can say them without adding anyone else's name to the sentence, and whether we believe, as John promised, that the one who hears them is faithful and just to forgive.
