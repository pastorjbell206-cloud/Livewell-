### A promise made to people who need it

Most people meet the prosperity gospel late at night. It comes through a screen at the hour when the bills are on the kitchen table and the test results are still in their envelope, and it says what nobody else is saying: God has not forgotten you, God wants you well, and there is something you can do tonight. Sow a seed. Speak the promise aloud. Refuse to confess the sickness. For someone with no margin left, that is not a small promise. It may be the only one in the room that sounds as if it was addressed to her.

The historian Kate Bowler spent years inside the movement's churches before writing *Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel* (2013), and she organizes its teaching around four themes: faith, wealth, health and victory. Faith here is less trust in a person than a force set in motion by speaking, and wealth and health are the evidence that it works. She also distinguishes two registers. Hard prosperity is the explicit formula of seed and harvest, confession and possession, preached from the healing platform and the television studio. Soft prosperity is gentler and more therapeutic. It never mentions a hundredfold return, but it still assures its hearers that God's plan for them is promotion, favor and a better life now. The first is easy to spot. The second sounds like a great deal of ordinary American preaching.

Nor is the movement only American. In 2006 the Pew Forum on Religion & Public Life published *Spirit and Power*, a survey of Pentecostals in ten countries across the Americas, Africa and Asia, and found that in most of them a majority of Pentecostals agreed that God grants material prosperity to believers who have enough faith, a belief that also ran well beyond Pentecostal churches. The prosperity gospel has become one of the most widely held accounts of what God is like anywhere on earth, and much of its growth has come among the poor.

The platform I write for names prosperity teaching as an error it will not teach as true, and this essay will say why from Scripture. But its believers are not fools, many are brothers and sisters in Christ, and some of the hardest things here are aimed at churches like mine. I pastor a Baptist church. I have never preached the prosperity gospel. I have watched its softer cousin, the quiet assumption that God rewards the faithful with a comfortable life, come through the door of every church I have known, and it has come through mine.

The argument turns on one distinction. Scripture is full of blessing, and God is generous past all accounting. The question is not whether God gives. It is whether God owes. A gift comes from a Father who knows what his children need, on his terms and in his time, and it may arrive as bread or as a cross. A return comes from a mechanism: the right input yields the right output, and if the output fails, the input must have been wrong. The prosperity gospel takes the language of gift and runs it through the machinery of return, and nearly everything that follows (the theology, the money, the blame) comes from that one substitution.

### From New Thought to the television studio

The prosperity gospel did not grow out of the main stream of Christian theology, and its family tree explains much of what it became. One root is the nineteenth-century New Thought movement, whose teachers, beginning with the New England mesmerist Phineas Quimby, held that disease and want were at bottom wrong beliefs that right thinking could cure. A second is the Holiness faith-cure movement of the same decades, which taught that bodily healing was provided in the atonement and could be received by faith. A third is older and more American than either: the conviction, preached from Baptist pulpits as readily as any others, that godliness and getting ahead belong together. Russell Conwell, the Baptist pastor who founded Temple University, delivered his lecture "Acres of Diamonds" thousands of times around the turn of the twentieth century, telling audiences that getting rich was a Christian duty and that poverty nearly always traced back to someone's shortcomings. A Baptist can't read him without hearing his own tradition's accent.

The man who braided the first two strands into a theology was E. W. Kenyon (1867–1948), an evangelist and educator who studied for a time at the Emerson College of Oratory in Boston, a school steeped in New Thought. Kenyon taught that the believer's spoken confession of God's promises brings what is confessed into possession, and that faith works by spiritual laws as reliable as physical ones. How much he owed to New Thought is still argued. D. R. McConnell, writing from within the charismatic world in *A Different Gospel* (1988), pressed the case that the metaphysical debt was decisive; Bowler braids the metaphysical, the Pentecostal and the American faith in self-improvement together. What McConnell documented, and few now dispute, is what happened next. Kenneth Hagin, usually called the father of the Word of Faith movement, reproduced long passages of Kenyon's writing nearly word for word under his own name.

Hagin (1917–2003) traced his ministry to a healing he said he received as a bedridden Texas teenager in the 1930s while meditating on Jesus' words: "whatever you ask in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours" (Mark 11:24, ESV). From that text he built the practice of positive confession: speak the promise as already accomplished, never confess the symptom, and the reality will follow. His Rhema Bible Training Center, opened in Oklahoma in 1974, trained a generation of ministers in the method, and several of them carried it onto national television.

The other main stream came through the healing revival that followed the Second World War. Oral Roberts (1918–2009), a Pentecostal Holiness preacher from Oklahoma, launched his tent ministry in 1947, shortly after, by his own account, he opened his Bible to 3 John 2 and found in the King James rendering a wish that the reader would prosper and be in health. Over the next two decades he made that verse a doctrine he called seed faith, usually summarized in three principles: God is your source, give so that it may be given to you, and expect a miracle. In January 1987 Roberts told his broadcast audience that God would call him home if he did not raise eight million dollars by the spring.

That same year the enterprise came into full public view. The PTL network of Jim and Tammy Faye Bakker, which had sold "lifetime partnerships" in its Christian resort in South Carolina, collapsed in scandal, and in 1989 Jim Bakker was convicted of fraud. Less remembered is what he wrote after prison. In *I Was Wrong* (1996), Bakker said that years of studying the words of Jesus in a cell had convinced him that the prosperity message he had preached was false. Few people have had more reason to want the teaching to be true. He concluded that it was not.

None of this stayed home. The broadcasts, books and conferences that carried the message across America carried it overseas, where it met Pentecostal revival and poverty far deeper than anything in Tulsa, in West Africa, Brazil and the Philippines. A teaching about the blessing of God had become, by every measurable sign, an industry.

### What it gets right

Before any verdict, the prosperity gospel deserves to be stated the way its most thoughtful defenders would state it. Much of what it says is true, and a church that dismisses it without hearing it usually has a quieter error of its own.

The God of the Bible cares about bodies and bread. He made a material world, called it good, and told Israel plainly where its wealth came from: "You shall remember the LORD your God, for it is he who gives you power to get wealth, that he may confirm his covenant that he swore to your fathers, as it is this day" (Deuteronomy 8:18, ESV). The Hebrew vision of shalom is not a mood. It is a table with enough on it, a roof that holds, work that bears fruit, and children who outlive their parents. Jesus healed the sick without first asking whether their sickness had a lesson in it, and taught his disciples to ask for daily bread. A Christianity that talks only of souls and heaven, and treats poverty and sickness as occasions for patient endurance and nothing else, has lost something the prosperity preachers remembered.

They remembered it in places the older churches had often neglected. Pentecostalism grew among the poor, and in Latin America and Africa it gave poor people a God who acted in the world of wages and fevers, not only in the world to come. Sociologists who studied that growth, David Martin foremost among them in *Tongues of Fire* (1990), described how conversion could reorder a household: men gave up drinking, money that had gone to the bar came home, and a discipline of work and family took hold. A preacher who tells the poor that God wants them to flourish is telling them something true that the world has contradicted all their lives. Bowler's history carries that respect; the people she met in prosperity churches were hopeful and hardworking, nobody's dupes, and they wanted a faith that promised something they could see. And it is easy to disdain the prosperity gospel from inside a comfortable life. Those of us with health insurance and a retirement account can afford a theology that is serene about poverty. A mother whose child has no medicine can't, and when she prays for money she is praying the way the Psalms pray.

The steelman, then, runs like this: God is good, God is generous, God cares about the whole person, God answers prayer, and faith expects him to act. Every clause is biblical. The question is what the prosperity gospel does with it, and the sharpest answer came from inside the Pentecostal world. Gordon Fee, a New Testament scholar ordained in the Assemblies of God, wrote *The Disease of the Health and Wealth Gospels* (1985) as a warning to his own movement. He did not deny that God heals or provides. He argued that the prosperity teachers had taken a true expectation of God's goodness and turned it into a right the believer could claim, a guarantee enforced by the correct words. That is exactly the difference between a gift and a return. The prosperity gospel gets the object right and the grammar wrong. Scripture speaks of God's goodness in the grammar of a father's promise, and the prosperity gospel speaks of it in the grammar of a contract.

### What Scripture says about gain

The Bible's oldest sustained answer to the prosperity gospel is Job, and the argument begins before Job suffers anything. When the LORD commends his servant, the accuser asks the question the whole book is written to answer: "Does Job fear God for no reason?" (Job 1:9, ESV). The charge is that Job's piety is a transaction. The prosperity gospel, without meaning to, answers the accuser's question the accuser's way. It tells believers there is indeed a reason to fear God, and the reason is the return.

Then Job loses everything, and his friends arrive with the prosperity gospel's theology run in reverse. Eliphaz speaks first: "Remember: who that was innocent ever perished? Or where were the upright cut off? As I have seen, those who plow iniquity and sow trouble reap the same" (Job 4:7–8, ESV). Sowing and reaping, input and output: if Job is ruined, Job must have sown ruin. Bildad tells a man whose children lie dead, "If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into the hand of their transgression" (Job 8:4, ESV). The friends are not villains. They are devout and sincere, and they defend God's justice with a formula that makes every sufferer guilty. At the end the verdict falls on them: "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7, ESV). The book does close with restoration, but it comes after the formula has been condemned, as a free gift, and only after Job had prayed for the friends who wounded him (Job 42:10). It is grace, and it was never a payout.

Paul met the same teaching and named it. Writing to Timothy in Ephesus, he warns against anyone who "teaches a different doctrine and does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ," and he puts his finger on the root: such teachers are "imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (1 Timothy 6:3, 5, ESV). The word translated "sound" is the ordinary Greek word for being healthy, and the false teacher, Paul says, is sick about controversies (the ESV's "unhealthy craving"). The teaching that promises health is the diseased one. Paul's alternative follows at once:

> But godliness with contentment is great gain, for we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world. But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content. But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils. (1 Timothy 6:6–10, ESV)

Food and clothing are Paul's measure of enough, and he tells the rich a few verses later "to be generous and ready to share" (1 Timothy 6:18, ESV). He condemns the desire to be rich, and above all a religion that recruits that desire as a motive for godliness. That describes the hard prosperity gospel precisely, and more of the soft version than we would like.

The same apostle wrote the verse most often painted on the walls of gyms and locker rooms, and its setting undoes that use. "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13, ESV) concludes a thank-you note for a gift the Philippians had sent him in prison, and he thanks them without pretending he needed it: "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need" (Philippians 4:11–12, ESV). The "all things" include going hungry. The strength of Christ is not the power to escape need but the freedom to stay faithful inside it.

Seed faith leans on Paul's words to Corinth about sowing and reaping bountifully (2 Corinthians 9:6). But Paul is gathering a collection for poor believers in Jerusalem, and his model is Christ, who "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). He states the purpose outright: "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness" (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV). And the harvest he promises is not a private fortune: "You will be enriched in every way to be generous in every way" (2 Corinthians 9:11, ESV). In Paul's economy the seed flows down, from those who have to those who don't, and the harvest is more generosity. In the prosperity economy the seed flows up, from the congregation to the ministry, and the harvest is promised to the sower. The metaphor is the same, and the direction of the money is reversed.

Even 3 John 2, where Roberts began, turns out to be a greeting. Fee pointed out that it is the conventional health-wish that opened ancient letters, and the ESV renders it so: "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul" (3 John 2, ESV). A courtesy is not a covenant. And a few chapters after the mountain-moving promise where Hagin began, the one who taught it knelt in Gethsemane and prayed, "Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36, ESV). No one has ever prayed with more faith than Jesus prayed that night. The cup was not removed, and its not being removed saved the world.

### What it does to the poor

Most critiques of the prosperity gospel stop at what it gets wrong about God. Those errors are real, but they are not the gravest. The gravest is what the teaching does to the people it courts most eagerly.

Follow the logic to its end. If faith and giving reliably produce health and wealth, then the absence of health and wealth is evidence of weak faith or thin giving. The machine that promises deliverance also issues a verdict. The widow who gives what she cannot spare and receives no hundredfold is left without her money and with an account of her poverty that places the failure inside her. The man whose cancer does not yield to confession is told, by the logic if not always in words, that he did not believe enough. It is the theology of Job's friends with a mailing address for donations. The disciples once put the friends' question to Jesus about a man born blind, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" and Jesus refused the premise: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:2–3, ESV). The prosperity gospel keeps asking the question Jesus refused.

The damage doesn't stop at the bank balance. A believer taught that God's love is measured in outcomes will read his own outcomes as a verdict, and when the breakthrough does not come, either God failed him or he failed God. Both roads can end in unbelief. The prosperity gospel manufactures skeptics in the name of the God it misrepresents, and to the unbeliever watching from outside, the preacher's jet is the whole argument against Christianity.

The teaching also moves money in a particular direction, and the direction is where the injustice lives. It recruits where need is sharpest, and the money it gathers flows from the pews toward the platform, where the preacher's wealth is displayed as proof that the method works. Jesus had words for religious authority that draws resources upward from the vulnerable. In the temple during his last week, he warned against the scribes "who devour widows' houses and for a pretense make long prayers. They will receive the greater condemnation" (Mark 12:40, ESV). In the very next scene he watched a poor widow put two small coins into the offering and said that she "out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on" (Mark 12:44, ESV). Her devotion is real, and Jesus honors it. But Mark places her right after the warning about devoured widows, and the next thing Jesus says is that the temple she has just given her living to will be torn down (Mark 13:2). Many readers have heard lament in the scene as well as praise, and no reader can find in it a promise that her two coins will come back a hundredfold.

The most searching Christian response has come from the places where the teaching spread fastest. The Lausanne Theology Working Group met in Akropong, Ghana, in 2008 and again in 2009 to examine the prosperity gospel, with African theologians and church leaders prominent in the discussion, and its statement was published in 2010. It affirms the generosity of God, his care for the bodily and material life of his people, and his power to heal. It then rejects the idea that spiritual health can be measured by material wealth, the manipulation of giving as a means of gain, and the extravagant lives of preachers funded by poor congregations, and it judges the teaching false, a grave distortion of the Bible, and pastorally damaging. That judgment came, in large part, not from comfortable Western critics but from pastors who had sat with the consequences.

No teaching about faith requires a sick person to refuse medical care, and no one should give away money they need for rent, food or medicine on the promise of a return. This essay is not medical or financial advice. Anyone pressed to give beyond their means, or to stop treatment as proof of faith, should talk it through with a doctor and a pastor who has nothing to gain from the answer.

### The comfortable version, and the shape of blessing

It would be easy to end there, with the television preachers on one side and the rest of us safely on the other. The prosperity gospel makes an ideal opponent for a middle-class church, because its excesses are loud and ours are quiet. But Bowler's category of soft prosperity won't let churches like mine off so lightly.

We would never tell anyone to sow a seed, but listen to how we talk. We call the new house a blessing and the layoff a trial. We give testimonies about the job that came through and the scan that came back clear, and almost never about the prayer that was refused and the faith that held anyway. We keep a careful distance from the member whose business failed, and the distance says what our theology never would: something must have gone wrong with him. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton found in *Soul Searching* (2005) that the working religion of many American teenagers raised in churches was what they called moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be good and nice, whose central wish is that they be happy and feel good about themselves, and who is needed mainly when there is a problem to fix. They learned it from us. It is the soft prosperity gospel without the private jet, and Baptists who once filled halls to hear Russell Conwell have no standing to look down on anyone.

The answer to both versions is not a church serene about other people's poverty. It is a different shape of blessing, and Jesus drew it on a hillside. "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted" (Matthew 5:3–4, ESV). Luke's version is blunter, and woes follow the blessings: "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God," and then, "But woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation" (Luke 6:20, 24, ESV). The word blessed in these sayings is not a reward for technique but a declaration, spoken over people who look anything but fortunate, that the kingdom of God has come near them. That blessing is real, and in the end it is bodily. But it comes through the shape of the cross, not around it. Luke's woes end with a warning to those of whom everyone speaks well (Luke 6:26), and a message that guarantees comfort has read the list upside down.

Hebrews 11 is often preached as a gallery of faith's victories, and for most of the chapter it is one: kingdoms conquered, lions' mouths stopped, the dead received back. Then the writer turns without changing his subject. "Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life. Others suffered mocking and flogging, and even chains and imprisonment. They were stoned, they were sawn in two, they were killed with the sword" (Hebrews 11:35–37, ESV). They went about "destitute, afflicted, mistreated," people "of whom the world was not worthy" (Hebrews 11:37–38, ESV). Then comes the sentence the prosperity gospel has no room for: "And all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised" (Hebrews 11:39, ESV). The faith that stopped the lions' mouths was the faith of those who were sawn in two. The writer never suggests the second group believed less; he says the world was not worthy of them.

The prosperity gospel's historian learned this at a cost. Diagnosed with stage four colon cancer in 2015, at thirty-five, Kate Bowler wrote in *Everything Happens for a Reason: And Other Lies I've Loved* (2018) that the formula she had studied at arm's length had a quieter home in her own assumptions, and that what held when it broke was not a better formula but the love of God and of the people around her.

The prosperity gospel offers a Christianity without Good Friday. The real gospel runs through a garden where the prayer was refused and a hill where the Son of God died poor, his clothes divided among the soldiers who crucified him. What came out of the tomb on the third day was the crucified one, still carrying the wounds. The resurrection answers both the prosperity gospel and the poverty that makes it believable: there will be a table, and the hungry will be satisfied, and no one will have bought a place at it.

For those of us in comfortable churches, repentance starts closer to home than the television. It means telling the truth in our testimonies about the prayers God did not answer as we asked, sitting with the member whose business failed without assuming a cause, and giving the way Paul gave, downward toward need rather than toward whatever might reward us. And if you gave money you needed and waited for a harvest that never came, or prayed with all the faith you had beside a bed that emptied anyway, the failure was not yours. The formula was false. God was not withholding a payout, because he was never running a machine. Whether you can trust him again may take years to answer. The one who asks for that trust is the one whose own prayer in the garden was refused, who went to the cross anyway, and who has never measured his love in anything a person can deposit or withdraw.

The accuser's question still hangs over every church in America, mine first: does anyone fear God for no reason? The prosperity gospel answers it one way and the cross answers it another, and we will show which answer we believe in what we give, in what we pray, and in what we say to the family whose prayers were not answered, long before we say it from a pulpit.
