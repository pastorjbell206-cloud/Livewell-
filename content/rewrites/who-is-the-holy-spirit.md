When I first began paying attention to the Christian claim about God, as an atheist who assumed the whole apparatus was a comfort people had invented, two of the three persons came into focus fairly quickly. The Father I could picture as a source, the one Jesus prayed to. The Son was easier still, because he had a face and a body that got hungry and tired in Galilee. The third was a blur: a dove at a river, a wind in an upper room, a flame over someone's head, a presence people described with their eyes closed. Where the Father and the Son had edges, the Spirit had weather.

Two people stand inside that blur. The skeptic suspects that "the Spirit" is what religious people call their emotions when they want those emotions to carry more authority than emotions usually get. The Christian hears the phrase *filled with the Spirit*, pictures something overwhelming he has never had, and quietly wonders whether he missed the part that was supposed to happen to him. The church has given both of them reasons. The pneumatology we actually practice, as opposed to the one printed in our doctrinal statements, often treats the Spirit as the part of Christianity that feels good.

**The Holy Spirit is not a force or a feeling. He is a person, fully God, confessed with the Father and the Son since 381, and his central work is not to produce experiences in us but to join us to Christ and make Christ known. Experiences of the Spirit are real, and some are strong, but they are the fruit of that union and never its foundation.** That distinction, between the Spirit experienced and the Spirit who unites, settles more of the hard questions than any other, including what it means to be filled with him and what to make of the weeks when you feel nothing at all.

## A person, not a power

Scripture introduces the Spirit before light exists. "The earth was without form and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters" (Genesis 1:2, ESV). The Hebrew *ruach* also means breath and wind, and the Old Testament keeps the three meanings braided on purpose. Ezekiel's valley of dry bones plays on all three in one chapter. The bones are "the whole house of Israel" in exile, a people saying, "Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost" (Ezekiel 37:11), and God answers them with a promise: "And I will put my Spirit within you, and you shall live" (Ezekiel 37:14). The Spirit is as real as wind and as unseen, as intimate as breath and as necessary. None of that yet shows he is a person, only that he is God's own life moving toward what is dead.

The personal language arrives on the night before the crucifixion. "And I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Helper, to be with you forever, even the Spirit of truth" (John 14:16–17). *Helper* renders *paraklētos*, the one called alongside, the advocate who stands next to the accused. And *another* matters: a second advocate of the same kind as the first, and the first was Jesus. Two chapters later Jesus says, "it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Helper will not come to you" (John 16:7). No one says that about losing a person and gaining a power.

Honesty requires a concession. A popular argument holds that John proves the Spirit's personhood by using a masculine pronoun for him though the Greek word for spirit is neuter, but in those verses the pronoun can simply reach back to the masculine noun *Paraclete*. The stronger case rests on what the Spirit does. He teaches (John 14:26). He can be lied to, and Peter treats lying to him as lying to God: "why has Satan filled your heart to lie to the Holy Spirit? . . . You have not lied to man but to God" (Acts 5:3–4). He can be grieved (Ephesians 4:30), which is the language of love wounded. He reaches judgments: "it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (Acts 15:28). And the risen Jesus sends his church to baptize "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19), one name, the Spirit in the same breath as the other two. Nobody says gravity grieves or electricity reaches a verdict.

Why, then, does he feel like weather? Jesus answers that too. "He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you" (John 16:14). J. I. Packer, in *Keep in Step with the Spirit* (1984), likened this ministry to floodlighting, where the lamp is set out of sight so that the building can be seen. The Spirit is not dim. He is deferential. The vagueness we feel about him is a clue to his character: he is the member of the Trinity whose whole passion is to make another glorious.

## How the church learned to confess him

For three centuries the church's great arguments were about the Son. The creed of Nicaea in 325, which confessed the Son to be of one being with the Father, said of the Spirit only this: "And in the Holy Spirit." By the 360s and 370s a party had formed that accepted the Son's deity but ranked the Spirit below, as a created power. Their opponents called them the Pneumatomachians, the "Spirit-fighters."

Basil of Caesarea answered them in *On the Holy Spirit*, written around 375. The occasion was a doxology. Basil had led his churches in giving glory to the Father *with* the Son *together with* the Holy Spirit, and critics preferred the older form, glory to the Father *through* the Son *in* the Spirit, which could be heard as making the Spirit the medium of worship rather than its object. Basil did not reject the older wording, but he insisted the newer one was also true. He presses hardest on baptism: if Christians are baptized into the one name of Father, Son, and Spirit, then ranking the Spirit among creatures undoes the baptism by which they became Christians. Basil was notably reserved about calling the Spirit "God" outright in public, a reticence his friend Gregory of Nazianzus defended as pastoral prudence. Gregory was bolder. His *Theological Orations* (380) answered the charge of innovation by arguing that revelation came in stages: the Old Testament proclaimed the Father, the New revealed the Son, and now the Spirit dwells among us and makes himself known.

Basil did not live to see the result. In 381 the council at Constantinople expanded the creed, and its lines on the Spirit are, in the common English rendering, the confession Christians still say: "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son is worshiped and glorified, who has spoken through the prophets." The council confessed his deity by confessing him as the object of worship *with* the Father and the Son, which is Basil's preposition turned into liturgy. The church settled who the Spirit is by asking whom it prays to.

The same creed became the site of one of the longest quarrels in Christian history. The text of 381 says the Spirit "proceeds from the Father," echoing John 15:26. The Latin West added that he proceeds from the Father *and the Son*, *filioque*. The addition is tied above all to the Third Council of Toledo in 589, when the Visigothic king Reccared and his church renounced Arianism, and the addition was meant to guard the Son's full equality with the Father. It drew on Augustine's *The Trinity*, which describes the Spirit as the mutual love of Father and Son, proceeding principally from the Father and also from the Son. The words spread through Spain, Gaul, and Charlemagne's court, but Rome itself was slow to take them up; the creed was first sung with the addition at a papal Mass in 1014, when Pope Benedict VIII crowned Henry II emperor.

The Greek East objected on two grounds, both serious. The first is procedural: the creed was the common confession of the whole church, and one half altered it without the other. The second is theological: the East confesses the Father as the single source within the Godhead and fears that a second source either blurs the persons or leaves the Spirit as the one person who only ever receives. The Western answer at its strongest is that the New Testament calls him "the Spirit of Christ" (Romans 8:9) and has the Son sending him (John 16:7), and that the eternal life of God must match the way God has shown himself. When the papal legates and the patriarch of Constantinople exchanged excommunications in 1054, the filioque stood among the grievances, alongside the deeper dispute over papal authority; the excommunications were lifted in 1965, and the division remains. The question sits above the creedal floor. Both sides confess the Spirit's full deity and personhood, and a Christian on either side can say everything Constantinople said and mean every word.

## What the Spirit does across the canon

In Israel's history the Spirit came upon particular people for particular tasks: judges, kings, prophets, craftsmen. Moses wished it were otherwise: "Would that all the LORD's people were prophets, that the LORD would put his Spirit on them!" (Numbers 11:29). The prophets turned the wish into promise. To exiles whose hearts had failed, God said through Ezekiel, "And I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes and be careful to obey my rules" (Ezekiel 36:27). The promise is first a new heart that can finally keep faith. And through Joel: "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy" (Joel 2:28). The Spirit who rested on a few would be poured on all.

At Pentecost Peter said that this was that: "And in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (Acts 2:17). He changed Joel's "afterward" to "in the last days," and the change is the whole claim: the age to come has broken into this one. When the crowd asked what to do, he did not tell them to seek an experience. "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38).

Gordon Fee's *God's Empowering Presence* (1994), a patient exegesis of every passage in Paul that mentions the Spirit, argues that for Paul the Spirit is God's own personal presence returned to his people, the temple promise fulfilled in a community and in bodies, and that the Spirit is the first installment of the age to come. Romans 8 is the height of it. The Spirit makes us children who cry, "Abba! Father!" and "bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God" (Romans 8:15–16). Yet the chapter is unsentimental. We "who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23), and when prayer collapses, "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). He groans inside the groaning of an unhealed world with us, and he is the pledge that it will end, for the God who raised Jesus "will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you" (Romans 8:11).

What does that presence produce? Paul's best-known list is almost entirely character: "love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control" (Galatians 5:22–23). These are durable dispositions, not moods, and they are what Ezekiel's new heart looks like when it grows.

Then there are the gifts, treated in 1 Corinthians 12 through 14, written to the most gifted and most divided church Paul knew. He never doubts that their gifts are real; what troubles him is the use they make of them. "To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good" (1 Corinthians 12:7). Between his chapters on gifts he sets a chapter on love that strips spectacle of any worth on its own: "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1). The most gifted church in the New Testament was the one that most needed to hear that the Spirit is known by love.

## Filled, baptized, or both

"And do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery, but be filled with the Spirit" (Ephesians 5:18). The contrast is about influence: be governed by the Spirit, not by wine. The grammar matters. The verb is a command, addressed to every Christian and not only the emotionally expressive. It is passive, so God does the filling. It is present tense, closer to *go on being filled*. And it is plural, followed by participles that describe a church rather than a private experience, people "addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs" and "submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:19, 21). In Acts the apostles filled at Pentecost are filled again two chapters later, after a prayer meeting, "and continued to speak the word of God with boldness" (Acts 4:31). Filling is repeatable.

Beneath that verse sits a question that has divided faithful Christians for two centuries: is there a decisive work of the Spirit that comes *after* conversion? Three answers deserve to be heard as their own defenders would put them.

The Wesleyan-Holiness answer begins with John Wesley, who wrote in his journal for 24 May 1738, "I felt my heart strangely warmed," and taught that Christians should expect more than a lifelong stalemate with sin. *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection* (1766) describes perfection as love filling the heart and ruling the life, not freedom from ignorance or mistakes. The American Holiness movement sharpened this into a "second blessing": Phoebe Palmer's *The Way of Holiness* (1843) taught that a believer could lay everything on the altar, which is Christ, and receive entire sanctification by faith at once, a moment the movement increasingly described in the language of Pentecost. At its strongest the view is a protest against low expectations. Paul prayed, "Now may the God of peace himself sanctify you completely" (1 Thessalonians 5:23), and many believers testify to a later surrender that changed them more than their first profession did.

The Pentecostal answer grew from that soil. In January 1901, at Charles Parham's Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, students began speaking in tongues, and Parham taught that tongues are the initial evidence of baptism in the Spirit. In April 1906 William J. Seymour, a Black holiness preacher who had sat under Parham's teaching in Houston that winter, made by segregation rules to listen from outside the classroom, led a revival at 312 Azusa Street in Los Angeles. Black, white, and Latino worshipers prayed together there in the Jim Crow era, and the movement spread from that room across the world. Its case is exegetical before it is experiential. Believers in Samaria had "received the word of God" and been baptized, yet the Spirit "had not yet fallen" on them until the apostles came (Acts 8:14–16). At Ephesus Paul asked, "Did you receive the Holy Spirit when you believed?" and heard, "No, we have not even heard that there is a Holy Spirit" (Acts 19:2). Roger Stronstad's *The Charismatic Theology of St. Luke* (1984) argued that Luke presents the Spirit chiefly as prophetic empowerment for witness, "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you" (Acts 1:8), and that reading Luke only through Paul silences him.

The Reformed and Baptist answer holds that baptism in the Spirit happens at conversion and belongs to every believer. Its anchor is Paul: "For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body . . . and all were made to drink of one Spirit" (1 Corinthians 12:13), written to a church he considered immature, and "Anyone who does not have the Spirit of Christ does not belong to him" (Romans 8:9). James D. G. Dunn's *Baptism in the Holy Spirit* (1970) argued that receiving the Spirit is the heart of becoming a Christian, and John Stott's *The Baptism and Fullness of the Holy Spirit* (1964) gave the view the shape most evangelicals know: one baptism at the beginning, many fillings afterward. On this reading the delays in Acts are hinges in the gospel's movement along the map of Acts 1:8, from Jerusalem to Samaria to the Gentiles in Caesarea (Acts 10:44–48). Richard Gaffin's *Perspectives on Pentecost* (1979) and Sinclair Ferguson's *The Holy Spirit* (1996) press further: Pentecost belongs with the cross and the empty tomb in the once-for-all history of redemption, and it is no more a pattern each believer must repeat than Easter is.

This is a second-order question. Each tradition confesses the Spirit as God and Christ as Lord, and each teaches that the Spirit is given to those who believe; they divide over the sequence and shape of the gift. The conclusion this essay reaches is that every believer is indwelt by the Spirit from the moment he belongs to Christ, a settled fact and not a matter of degree; that Pentecost was a founding hinge, one Pentecost as there is one first Easter, and not a template each Christian must reproduce; and that being filled names the measure of the Spirit's governance over a life he already inhabits. That places it closest to the Reformed and Baptist reading. He lives in every Christian and rules the same Christian differently on different days. The strongest objection has not been fully answered. Acts 8 and 19 read, on their face, like believers who lacked something the apostles expected them to have, and the explanation from redemptive history, however plausible, is one Luke never states. The practical objection lands closer to home: a doctrine that says you already have everything can become a reason never to ask for anything. Jesus left no room for that. "If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!" (Luke 11:13).

A related second-order question, which this essay will not settle, is whether prophecy, tongues, and healing continue today. B. B. Warfield's *Counterfeit Miracles* (1918) gave cessationism its classic form, arguing that the miraculous gifts were the apostles' credentials and ended with them, and Gaffin argued that revelatory gifts belonged to a church "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets" (Ephesians 2:20), and a foundation is laid once. Continuationists answer that the partial gifts pass away "when the perfect comes" (1 Corinthians 13:10), which the next verses tie to seeing "face to face" at Christ's return, and that "do not forbid speaking in tongues" (1 Corinthians 14:39) has not been rescinded. Orthodox Christians stand on both sides. A church can be right about the gifts and still be a clanging cymbal.

## The Spirit and feeling

How did the Spirit come to mean a feeling? The history did not begin in error. Philipp Jakob Spener's *Pia Desideria* (1675) called Lutherans from a dry, argumentative orthodoxy toward heartfelt faith, and the Pietists were right that assent alone is not New Testament faith. In the 1740s the Great Awakening filled colonial meetinghouses with weeping and outcry, and critics such as Charles Chauncy dismissed much of it as enthusiasm, the old word for mistaking one's passions for the voice of God.

Jonathan Edwards stood between the enthusiasts and the despisers, and *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* (1746) remains the wisest American book on the subject. It opens with a concession to the revival's friends: "True religion, in great part, consists in holy affections." But by *affections* Edwards meant more than passing emotions; he meant the vigorous, felt exercises of the inclination and will, the whole soul leaning toward what it loves or away from what it hates. Then he did something neither party wanted. He listed signs that prove nothing either way: that feelings are intense, that they affect the body, that a person talks fluently about religion, that verses of Scripture come suddenly to mind, that a person feels great confidence in his own state. None of them shows the Spirit is present or absent. The true signs are of another kind: love for God's beauty for its own sake rather than for what it brings us, deepening humility, a softened spirit, and chief of all, a life of Christian practice that holds over time. His verdict, from a man who loved the revival and watched it produce counterfeits, is one the church keeps relearning: the Spirit is known by his fruit, and the strength of a feeling proves nothing either way.

The modern world went further than Edwards would have. Friedrich Schleiermacher, trying to win back educated Germans who had given up on Christianity, argued in *On Religion* (1799) that religion's true home is feeling and intuition of the infinite rather than doctrine or morals. By 1902 William James could define religion in *The Varieties of Religious Experience* as "the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine." Something very like that definition now quietly governs how many Westerners think about religion. The skeptic who suspects that Spirit-talk is emotion with a halo has simply taken our own theory of religion and handed it back to us.

The cost to ordinary Christians is plain. If the Spirit's presence is confirmed by a feeling, then the absence of the feeling suggests his absence, and faith becomes a chase for the next experience. C. S. Lewis named the trap in *The Screwtape Letters* (1942): human feeling rises and falls in what the senior devil calls the law of undulation, with little relation to a person's standing before God, and the devils' hope is that a Christian in a trough will take his dryness for God's absence. I have led worship services that stirred a room deeply and changed almost no one in it. Most pastors have. Most have also sat in hospital rooms and jail cells where no one was moved to tears and something steady and unmanufactured was plainly present.

The charismatic tradition is guarding something real here. A Christianity that never crosses into felt encounter with the living God has become less than the New Testament describes. "God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us" (Romans 5:5), and a love poured into a heart is not a proposition. The question is whether experience is the measure of the Spirit. When it becomes the measure, we have made him answerable to our emotional needs rather than to his own purpose, which Jesus named: to convict the world "concerning sin and righteousness and judgment" (John 16:8) and to glorify the Son.

## The one who joins us to Christ

The whole subject turns on one distinction. There is the Spirit as *experienced*: the warmth, the assurance, the conviction that arrives uninvited. And there is the Spirit as the one who *unites*: the person who joins a sinner to Christ, so that everything Christ is and has done becomes hers. The first is real and varies by day, by temperament, by season of life. The second is a fact, and it does not vary.

John Calvin opened the third book of his *Institutes* (1559) with this point. As long as Christ remains outside us, Calvin argued, all he suffered and did for our salvation is of no use to us; the Holy Spirit is the bond by which Christ joins us to himself. Sinclair Ferguson's *The Holy Spirit* (1996) builds on the same conviction. The Spirit attended Jesus at every stage of his life, forming him in Mary's womb, anointing him at the Jordan, driving him into the wilderness, raising him from the dead, and the same Spirit now joins believers to that Jesus, so that his work in us is to make us share what is already true in Christ. He gives us Christ himself, which is why "no one can say 'Jesus is Lord' except in the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 12:3). The bare confession of a believer who feels nothing at all is itself his work.

That changes the anxious question. The Christian lying awake wondering whether he has ever been filled is asking the wrong thing. The New Testament asks whom you are trusting, and it says the trust itself is the Spirit's doing. The best evidence of his work is the size of Christ in your sight: whether, across years, you love him more, trust him further, and are slowly becoming someone who forgives what you used to nurse and confesses what you used to hide.

It changes the skeptic's question too, though it does not answer it. Nothing here proves the Spirit exists, and I won't pretend it does. But what Christians claim is something other than superior feelings. We claim there is a person whose entire work is to point away from himself toward a crucified and risen man, and whose presence is known less by what happens in a crowded room than by what happens, over decades, in a stubborn human character. That claim can be tested against us, and we have often failed it. We have chased the Spirit as a feeling and measured our health by the intensity in the room, and we have shrunk him into a doctrine affirmed on paper and expected to do nothing. Neither failure treats him as a person to be known, obeyed, grieved, and asked.

Ezekiel's vision ends where the church always has to begin again. God asked the prophet in the valley, "Son of man, can these bones live?" and he answered the only honest way: "O Lord GOD, you know" (Ezekiel 37:3). He prophesied, and the bones came together, and sinews and flesh and skin covered them. "But there was no breath in them" (Ezekiel 37:8). A church can be sound and moving and still lie there without breath. The prophet was told to prophesy to the breath: "Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live" (Ezekiel 37:9). On the night before he died, Jesus promised people who were about to fail him, "I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you" (John 14:18). Those of us who first knew fatherhood mostly by its absence hear that sentence differently, and he said it in the middle of promising the Spirit. The Spirit who raised Jesus has not promised to make you feel alive this week. He has promised to join you to the one who is, and to raise your mortal body on the last day. Between now and then come weeks of warmth and weeks of dry bones, and in both the task is the same: to keep asking the Father for what he is glad to give, and to keep in step with a person you cannot see, who is closer than your own breath and has never been the same thing as a feeling.
