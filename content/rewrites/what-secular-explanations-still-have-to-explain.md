## The world that runs without God

We built a world that runs without God, and it runs. An honest believer has to say that to an honest atheist before saying anything else, and most of us won't. The lights come on, the antibiotics work, and the bridge holds because an engineer who prays to no one calculated the load correctly. For more than three centuries the most powerful method human beings have devised for finding out what is true has gone forward on the working assumption that it need not consult heaven, and it has been right, humiliatingly right, about almost everything it has touched. If you are an unbeliever because of this, you are not being lazy. You are noticing something real. I came to faith from atheism, and I noticed it too.

The strong case has a history, and much of it is the church's embarrassment. When Isaac Newton published the *Principia* in 1687, he gave the world one mathematics for the falling apple and the orbiting moon, and he believed the whole system displayed the wisdom of its Maker. But in the queries he added to later editions of his *Opticks*, Newton suggested that the small irregularities of the planets would accumulate until God had to set the system right again. Leibniz, no atheist himself, mocked the idea in the letters he began exchanging with Newton's disciple Samuel Clarke in 1715: a God who has to wind and mend his clock, he said, is a poor clockmaker. Within a century Pierre-Simon Laplace had argued by mathematics that the solar system corrects its own irregularities, and there is a famous story that when Napoleon asked where God was in his system, Laplace answered that he had no need of that hypothesis. The story comes to us secondhand, and its wording is of doubtful provenance, but it stuck, because it named the pattern. Then came 1859, and Darwin, and the design that had seemed to demand a designer turned out to have a mechanism.

Every time we didn't know how something worked, someone said God, and every time the mechanism arrived, God was not found inside it. He had not been disproven. He had become unnecessary to the account. Christians saw the danger before skeptics made sport of it. In a letter from a Nazi prison dated 29 May 1944, Dietrich Bonhoeffer told his friend Eberhard Bethge that it is wrong to use God as a stopgap for the incompleteness of our knowledge, because as the frontiers of knowledge are pushed back, God is pushed back with them and is always in retreat. We are to find God, Bonhoeffer insisted, in what we know, not in what we don't. The god of the gaps has been evicted so many times that a thoughtful person is right to stop renting to him, and this essay won't try to rent him a room.

Charles Taylor describes where this left us in *A Secular Age* (2007). The modern West lives within what he calls the immanent frame: a natural, social and moral order understood as self-sufficient, explicable in its own terms, which can be lived as open to transcendence or closed to it, but in which belief in God is one option among others and no longer the air we breathe. Taylor rejects the subtraction story, the tale that modernity is simply what remains when superstition is peeled away, but he doesn't treat the frame as a mistake to be undone. It is an achievement. We can give a full account of the middle of the world without leaving the world. Believers live inside that frame too, and a believer who claims to have stepped outside it hasn't noticed where he's standing.

## What a mechanism explains, and what it doesn't

The naturalist deserves his best spokesman, and few are better than the physicist Sean Carroll. In *The Big Picture* (2016) Carroll argues that the laws underlying everyday life are now completely understood. He calls them the Core Theory: quantum field theory, the particles and forces of the Standard Model, and gravity at ordinary strengths. Nothing in your kitchen, your bloodstream or your brain escapes those laws. Tables, persons, choices and values are real, but real as ways of talking about the same underlying stuff at higher levels of description. He calls this poetic naturalism, and it isn't crude; it gives the novel, the conscience and the marriage their place while denying that anything beyond nature holds them up. Daniel Dennett, who died in 2024, gave the conviction its sharpest image in *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* (1995): the history of explanation is cranes replacing skyhooks. A skyhook lifts from nowhere, and a crane is built from the ground up. Every appeal to God is a skyhook, and every advance of science a crane that made one unnecessary.

The naturalist's most important sentence is also his most reasonable. "We don't know yet" is not the same as "God did it." A believer who hears the first and writes down the second has done something dishonest, and the record is against him. I grant that fully.

But there's a distinction the argument usually slides past, and this essay turns on it. Explaining a mechanism is not the same as explaining a thing's existence, or why it is intelligible at all. A mechanism tells you how one state of the world gives rise to another under given laws. It can be complete, and the mechanisms of physics are astonishingly close to complete, and still leave untouched the questions it presupposes: why there are laws rather than none, why these laws, why they can be written in mathematics, and why there is anything for them to govern. Leibniz asked the oldest form of the question in his *Principles of Nature and Grace* (1714): why is there something rather than nothing?

That is why classical Christian theology never thought of God as a gap. Thomas Aquinas, writing the *Summa Theologiae* between 1265 and 1274, distinguished the primary cause, God, who gives being to everything at every moment, from secondary causes, the web of created things acting on one another according to their natures. The two don't compete. A complete account of secondary causes, which is what science at its best offers, no more settles the question of the primary cause than a complete grammar of *Hamlet* settles whether Shakespeare existed. David Bentley Hart presses the point in *The Experience of God* (2013): God is not one being among others, a very large item in the inventory of the universe, but the source of being itself, and to search for him among the mechanisms is to mistake the kind of question you're asking. A God who could be found inside the Core Theory would be a piece of the furniture.

Carroll has an honest answer. At some point explanation stops, and the universe or its laws may simply be a brute fact. The believer should concede that every account of reality ends in something not explained by anything further. The real question is not whether you stop but where, and whether the place you stop can carry the weight of what you find when you look around. So what follows is not a list of gaps. It is a list of things that don't shrink when science advances. They were the same size before we knew anything, and they will be the same size when we know everything a physical account could deliver.

## The mind that asks the question

Begin with the remainder the materialist keeps circling back to: not intelligence or behavior, but the plain fact that there is something it is like to be you. In 1974 the atheist philosopher Thomas Nagel published "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" We could know everything about a bat's brain, he argued, and still not know what it is like, from the inside, to be the bat. David Chalmers gave the difficulty its working name in a 1995 paper and in *The Conscious Mind* (1996). The easy problems of consciousness (how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information and reports its states) are hard enough to occupy neuroscience for a century, but for each of them we know what a solution would look like. The hard problem is why any of that processing is accompanied by experience at all, and there we don't. The wavelength we label red is also, from within, the redness of red, and no description of the wavelength contains it.

The naturalist replies come at two strengths. Carroll's is modest: consciousness is a higher-level way of talking about what brains do, and we haven't finished the science. Dennett's was bolder. In *Consciousness Explained* (1991) he argued that the private inner qualities we take ourselves to have are not what they seem, and once Chalmers had named the hard problem, Dennett spent the rest of his career arguing that it rests on a mistake: when the easy problems are solved, nothing will be left over. These are serious positions, and a Christian who laughs at them hasn't read them. But notice who refuses to be satisfied. Chalmers isn't a theist; his own proposals treat consciousness as a basic feature of nature, alongside mass and charge. Nagel, who admitted in *The Last Word* (1997) that he doesn't merely disbelieve in God but hopes there isn't one, argued in *Mind and Cosmos* (2012) that the materialist neo-Darwinian conception of nature is, in the words of his subtitle, "almost certainly false," because it can't account for three things: consciousness, our capacity to reason our way to truth, and the reality of value. He reached for a natural purpose in the cosmos rather than for God, and many colleagues received the book harshly. Still, an atheist of the first rank, with every motive to find the standard story sufficient, looked at the remainders this essay names and said the account wasn't closed.

Nagel's second item is the instrument we use to examine all the others. Science assumes in advance that reality is written in a language the human mind can read. In 1960 the physicist Eugene Wigner published "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences," observing that concepts mathematicians develop for their elegance, with no thought of physics, turn out, sometimes long afterward, to be the ones the world obeys. His closing sentence reads: "The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve." The naturalist reply is strong: of course the world is comprehensible to us, because minds that couldn't track reality got eaten. Grant it. Natural selection can explain why a creature can count the lions and judge the distance to the river. It has no obvious purchase on why the same organ could let Urbain Le Verrier predict, in 1846, from calculations on paper, a planet no one had seen, and have Neptune turn up where he said.

Darwin felt the sharper form of the worry himself. In a letter to William Graham dated 3 July 1881 he wrote: "With me the horrid doubt always arises whether the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy." C. S. Lewis built an argument on that nerve in *Miracles* (1947): if every thought is wholly the product of nonrational causes, the reasoning by which we conclude that naturalism is true seems to lose its claim on us. In February 1948 the philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe, herself a Christian, criticized the argument at the Oxford Socratic Club for failing to separate the causes of a belief from its grounds, and Lewis rewrote the chapter for the 1960 edition. Alvin Plantinga gave the argument its modern shape in *Warrant and Proper Function* (1993) and *Where the Conflict Really Lies* (2011). Selection rewards adaptive behavior, not true belief as such, so if naturalism and evolution are both true, the probability that our faculties are reliable is low or impossible to estimate, and the person who accepts both has reason to doubt the faculties that produced his belief in both. Plantinga's target is not evolution. It is the pairing of evolution with naturalism; a God who meant to make creatures in his image could use evolution to do it.

Critics answer that true belief is generally adaptive and that the argument leans on probabilities no one can assess, and the professional debate is unresolved. What I'd ask the naturalist to see is narrower. Your confidence in reason is not something your worldview hands you. You bring it with you. The Christian has a reason for that trust, because the mind was made by Mind, for truth: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1, ESV). John chose *Logos*, a word his Greek readers heard as the reason and order of the world, and told them that this reason was personal and was God. If mind lies at the bottom of things, minds at the top are a homecoming. The naturalist has to hope that a process indifferent to truth produced truth-trackers reliable enough to discover that it was indifferent to truth. That may be so. It's more of a bet than the confident voice admits.

## A universe balanced for life

Fine-tuning is the easiest remainder to cheapen, so it has to be put carefully. The laws of physics contain constants whose values the laws themselves don't fix: the strengths of the forces, the masses of particles, the energy of empty space. Physicists who study the question have found that, as far as current physics can tell, relatively small changes to several of these values would yield a universe without stable atoms, long-lived stars or complex chemistry, and so without life of any kind we know how to describe. The claim is about the life-permitting range as our present theories describe it, not a demonstration that no other kind of life is possible. In 1953 Fred Hoyle, no friend of religion at the time, reasoned from the amount of carbon in the universe that the carbon nucleus must have an energy level near a particular value, or the stars could never have forged as much of it as there is, and experimenters found the level close to where he said it would be. He later drew the life-permitting lesson himself. Martin Rees, Britain's Astronomer Royal, set out the case in *Just Six Numbers* (1999). One of his six is the fraction of mass turned into energy when hydrogen fuses into helium, about 0.007. At 0.006, he explains, the nuclear bonds that begin the chain wouldn't hold and the universe would be little but hydrogen; at 0.008, the hydrogen would have been consumed early, leaving no long-lived stars and no water. Luke Barnes and Geraint Lewis, two working astrophysicists, concluded in *A Fortunate Universe* (2016) that fine-tuning is a real feature of current physics, not an apologist's invention, while openly disagreeing with each other about what it means.

The naturalist replies are strong, and a believer who hasn't met them shouldn't use the argument. The first is the anthropic principle, named by Brandon Carter in 1973: we could only find ourselves in a universe that permits observers, so our surprise has to be corrected for the fact that we're here. The second is the multiverse. Several lines of current physics, cosmic inflation among them, suggest our universe may be one region of a vast ensemble with differing constants, and if enough tickets are drawn, a winner is no marvel. Rees himself inclines this way. The third is Carroll's: we don't know the full range of conditions under which life could arise, a deeper theory may fix the constants, and fine-tuning isn't what theism predicts anyway, since an all-powerful God could sustain life in any universe he pleased.

Each reply has something true in it, and each has a limit. The anthropic principle explains why we don't observe a hostile universe, not why a hospitable one exists. John Leslie made the point in *Universes* (1989) with a man facing a firing squad of fifty trained marksmen who all miss. He shouldn't be surprised that he is alive to notice he survived. He should be very surprised that he survived, and right to ask whether the misses were arranged. The multiverse may be true, and a Christian has no stake in denying it; God can make as many worlds as he likes. But a mechanism that generates universes with varying constants is itself a law-governed, mathematically structured system, and the question of why there is such a system, and why it is intelligible, comes back one level up. The multiverse relocates the question. It doesn't dissolve it.

Carroll's point is the strongest, and this essay hasn't fully answered it. The Christian reply is that God didn't need a finely tuned universe but chose one, that a Creator who gives his world its own integrity might prefer an order that holds by law rather than by constant repair, which is what Leibniz told Clarke. That is an answer from inside the faith, not a proof from outside it. Fine-tuning proves nothing. It is evidence to be weighed, and it weighs something. "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork" (Psalm 19:1, ESV). The psalmist didn't need the cosmological constant to say that. But the more we learn about the sky, the less it sounds like noise.

## The ought we cannot unsay

The moral remainder begins with a concession. The question is not whether unbelievers are good. They often are, often better than believers. Paul himself says that Gentiles without the law sometimes do what the law requires, and that "they show that the work of the law is written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15, ESV), though his point in context is that this leaves every one of us accountable. Nor is it whether morality has a natural history. Cooperation is fit, empathy is selected, and the apparatus by which a social animal comes to feel that cruelty is wrong can be told as a story about genes, and told well. The remainder is not moral behavior but moral obligation. We don't experience morality as a feeling we happen to have. We experience it as a claim made on us from outside, standing over our feelings and judging them. When I say that a man who tortures a child for pleasure is wrong, I don't mean I dislike it or my tribe evolved to punish it. I mean he is wrong, and would be wrong if every human being alive had evolved to applaud him.

The most honest naturalists see the difficulty. Sharon Street argued in "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006) that if evolution shaped our moral attitudes, the realist must say either that those attitudes bear no relation to independent moral truths, in which case any match between our moral beliefs and the moral truth would be a fluke, or that they do, in which case he owes a scientific story no better than the simpler one in which selection shaped us without any moral truths at all. Street concludes that value is constructed by valuing creatures, not discovered, and Carroll lands in a similar place. J. L. Mackie, who thought objective values would be too strange to exist, conceded in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982) that if they did exist they would make God's existence more probable.

Other atheists refuse the denial. Derek Parfit in *On What Matters* (2011) and Erik Wielenberg in *Robust Ethics* (2014) defend moral facts that are real, objective and binding without God, built into the nature of things as necessary truths. That is the steelman at full strength, and it deserves respect. But notice what it costs. These facts are not physical, not part of the Core Theory, not the product of any mechanism. On their defenders' own account they are real, non-natural and basic, needing no further explanation, and whatever that view is, it is no longer the pure naturalism the rest of the argument depends on. It keeps the moral law and dismisses the Lawgiver, which is coherent and costly.

So the naturalist chooses between Street and Parfit: give up the bindingness, or give up the pure naturalism. Try living in the first. Watch what happens to the sentence "the torturer is really wrong." It becomes "the torturer is something I was built to hate." I know that sentence, because I once held the worldview that produces it, and I know that nobody, the atheist I was included, actually lives there. We go on treating certain things as really wrong with a weight no story about our ancestors can hold up. We can't get the obligation out of our mouths. We only learn to stop asking where it comes from. The historian Tom Holland traced in *Dominion* (2019) the instincts by which secular people judge cruelty and defend the victim back to roots Christianity planted in a Roman world that didn't share them. That's a historian's claim, not a proof, but it sharpens the question of whose ought we are living by.

## A crucified man and an ache that will not close

Two remainders are left, and both concern what human beings believe and want rather than what the cosmos is made of.

The first is the rise of the church, and here the naturalist's case is better than most Christians realize. Rodney Stark estimated in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) that the movement grew about forty percent a decade, from roughly a thousand believers around the year 40 to something like six million by 300, a rate he compared with the growth of the Latter-day Saints in the twentieth century. He explained it by ordinary mechanisms: networks of family and friendship, higher birth rates among people who refused infanticide, a community that treated women better than its neighbors did, and the Christians' care for the sick in the great epidemics of the second and third centuries, when many pagans fled and many Christians stayed. That is a good account of how the church grew, and a Christian can accept it.

But it explains the mechanism of growth, not the thing that grew. Crucifixion was Rome's most shameful death, and a crucified Messiah was an offense to everyone: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). Yet within a few years, in the city of the execution, Jewish monotheists who confessed daily that the Lord is one were proclaiming this man raised bodily from the dead and praying to him. Paul preserves, untranslated in a letter to Greek speakers, the Aramaic prayer *Maranatha*, "Our Lord, come!" (1 Corinthians 16:22, ESV). Larry Hurtado showed in *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) that devotion to Jesus as divine appears in the earliest sources, not as a late borrowing from pagan cults, and around 112 Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, reported to the emperor Trajan that Christians met before dawn to sing hymns to Christ as to a god. N. T. Wright argued in *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) that no Jewish expectation of the period imagined one man raised in the middle of history ahead of everyone else. The best skeptical reply is Bart Ehrman's in *How Jesus Became God* (2014): the disciples sincerely believed they saw Jesus, and visions of the kind bereaved people report explain the belief. That concedes a great deal, and it leaves the oddity where it was. Grieving first-century Jews did not ordinarily conclude from visions that the resurrection of the last day had begun in one man, and then, some of them, die rather than deny it. "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). Paul wrote that when, as he says a few lines earlier, most of the witnesses were still alive to be asked (1 Corinthians 15:6).

The second is the longing Taylor calls fullness. Near the start of *A Secular Age*, he observes that believers and unbelievers alike know moments when life seems fuller, more what it ought to be: at the birth of a child, before a mountain, inside a piece of music, in an act of costly love. The believer locates the source of that fullness in God, the unbeliever in human powers, and modern people live cross-pressured, inside the closed frame while something keeps knocking from beyond it. Freud answered in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927) that religious belief is wish-fulfillment. That's a fair warning; a desire doesn't prove its object. But the argument cuts both ways, since the hope that there is no God, the kind Nagel confessed, is a wish too. And it leaves the strangest part untouched. Hunger has food and thirst has water, but this appetite fits nothing the world contains. Ecclesiastes names it without resolving it: "he has put eternity into man's heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end" (Ecclesiastes 3:11, ESV). The Preacher doesn't call the ache a proof. He says it is there, and too large for the world under the sun.

## What the remainder asks of us

None of this amounts to six proofs. Each remainder has an intelligent naturalist reply that might be right, and I've tried to give those replies in the form their defenders would sign. A God assembled from these six would be the tenant of the shrinking house, and he deserves his eviction. The case is cumulative and modest. Consciousness, the reliability of reason, moral obligation, a hospitable cosmos, a crucified man worshiped by monotheists, and a longing the world can't fill are not gaps in a mechanism. Each is the size it has always been. Together they lean one way, toward a reality whose ground is not less than mind, reason, goodness and love, rather than an equation that happened to produce them and cannot say why.

The believer has a confession to make, and it comes before any claim on the skeptic. Romans opens with an indictment of those who see God's "eternal power and divine nature" in "the things that have been made" and suppress what they see (Romans 1:18-20, ESV). Christians love that chapter because it lets us judge from a safe distance. Then Paul turns: "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself" (Romans 2:1, ESV). We have done this. The church has waved away evidence it didn't like as eagerly as any skeptic, rented rooms to the god of the gaps because arguments are easier than faithfulness, and made belief look stupid by defending it stupidly. Psalm 19 begins with the heavens declaring the glory of God, but it doesn't stay in the sky. It moves to the law of the Lord and ends in the psalmist's own heart: "Who can discern his errors? Declare me innocent from hidden faults" (Psalm 19:12, ESV). The argument from the stars is finished only when it arrives at confession.

To the reader who doesn't believe: you don't have to believe anything to admit the account isn't closed. The remainder won't make you a Christian, and it shouldn't try. Faith doesn't finally rest on it. It rests on a man raised from the dead, and on whether that happened. But the remainder can keep you from doing what I used to do. When these questions came up, I waved them away, because I already knew there was nothing there. I never checked. The waving was the faith. What is left is the light that is on inside you, the reason you trust without being able to ground it, the wrong you cannot unsay, and the ache no finished physics will close. You did not make any of them, and every one of them is still waiting to be accounted for.
