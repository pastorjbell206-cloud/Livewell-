Most people who type "what is repentance" into a search bar already have a working answer, and it's usually some version of feeling bad. You did something wrong, you feel the weight of it, you say you're sorry, and if the sorrow is sincere enough you've repented. Some of that is true. Scripture has a great deal to say about grief over sin. But the working answer leaves out almost everything the Bible means by the word. It leaves out the direction of a life, the neighbor who was wronged, the good works we were proud of, the nation we belong to, and the difference between a grief that heals and a grief that kills. And it can't tell repentance from the shame many of us were raised to confuse it with.

I came to faith from atheism, and I remember what the word sounded like from outside. It sounded like groveling, an institution asking people to feel small so it could feel large. But the thing itself, as the prophets and apostles describe it, is nearly the reverse. It is not a posture of self-contempt. It is a turn toward home, and the whole person has to make it: the mind, the will, the hands, the wallet, and the memory.

## What the words mean, and what they can't tell you

The New Testament word is *metanoia*, with its verb *metanoeō*. Built from *meta* (after, or change) and *nous* (mind), it is often explained in sermons as "a change of mind," and that is a fair place to start. In ordinary Greek the word could mean an afterthought, a reconsidering, even a regret. But a word's parts don't settle its meaning. James Barr's *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961) took apart a whole generation of biblical scholarship for doing exactly this, treating the etymology of a Hebrew or Greek word as though it carried the theology of every sentence the word appeared in. The New Testament writers poured into *metanoia* far more than a revised opinion.

The Gospels themselves make the point. When Judas saw that Jesus had been condemned, Matthew says "he changed his mind" (Matthew 27:3), and he returned the thirty pieces of silver. The Greek verb there is a different one, *metamelomai*, but the English is honest: Judas did reconsider, and then he went out and hanged himself. If repentance were nothing more than a change of mind, Judas repented. The New Testament does not say he did, and the difference between his regret and Peter's weeping is the difference this essay is about.

What fills the Greek word is Hebrew. The Old Testament's great word for repentance is *shuv*, to turn, to turn back, to return, and it runs through the prophets like a refrain. It is a word about direction rather than feeling. Israel has walked away from the covenant God, and the call is to come back. "For I have no pleasure in the death of anyone, declares the Lord GOD; so turn, and live" (Ezekiel 18:32). The Greek translators of the Old Testament usually rendered *shuv* not with *metanoeō* but with *epistrephō*, to turn around, and the apostles sometimes used the two verbs side by side as if to make sure nothing was lost. Peter tells the crowd at Solomon's Portico, "Repent therefore, and turn back, that your sins may be blotted out" (Acts 3:19). Paul summarizes his preaching before Agrippa as a call "that they should repent and turn to God, performing deeds in keeping with their repentance" (Acts 26:20). Mind, direction and deeds, in one sentence.

That is the freight the word carries when Jesus opens his public ministry in Galilee. "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15). Notice what comes first. The kingdom is not offered as a reward for repenting. It has arrived, and repentance is the fitting response to news that is already true. You turn because the King has come near. Repentance in the Gospels is not a precondition you meet in order to be noticed. It is what it looks like to believe the news.

John the Baptist, a little earlier, had given the same call a very concrete edge. When the crowds came to the Jordan, he refused to let them rest on their pedigree. "Bear fruits in keeping with repentance. And do not begin to say to yourselves, 'We have Abraham as our father'" (Luke 3:8). And when they asked what, exactly, they should do, he didn't answer with a feeling. To the crowd: "Whoever has two tunics is to share with him who has none, and whoever has food is to do likewise" (Luke 3:11). To the tax collectors, whose trade ran on skimming: "Collect no more than you are authorized to do" (Luke 3:13). To the soldiers, who carried the power to squeeze civilians: "Do not extort money from anyone by threats or by false accusation, and be content with your wages" (Luke 3:14). Each answer finds the place where that person's power had gone crooked. Repentance, for John, was not a generic spiritual mood. It had an address, a job and a pay stub.

The etymology, then, is a door and not a house. *Metanoia* begins in the mind, because you can't turn from what you refuse to see, but it ends in the return of the whole person to God, with the neighbor feeling the difference.

## The word that split the church

For a thousand years the Western church read the New Testament in Latin, and in Jerome's Vulgate, finished around the turn of the fifth century, the Baptist's call and Jesus's opening words were rendered *poenitentiam agite*. Jerome's Latin could simply mean "repent," but the phrase could also be heard as "do penance," and over the medieval centuries that was increasingly how it was heard. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 required every Christian who had reached the age of discretion to confess to a priest at least once a year. Penance came to be taught as a sacrament with three acts on the penitent's part: contrition of heart, confession of mouth, and satisfaction in deed.

In 1516 the Dutch humanist Desiderius Erasmus published a Greek New Testament, the *Novum Instrumentum*, printed alongside his own fresh Latin translation and a body of annotations. Erasmus was no revolutionary and never left the Roman church. But his translation set aside *poenitentiam agite* for the Greek *metanoeite* and rendered it *poeniteat vos*, be penitent, and his second edition of 1519 went further with *resipiscite*, come to your senses, be wise again. The idea is close to what Jesus says of the younger son in the far country, who "came to himself" (Luke 15:17) among the pigs. A scholar's footnote about one verb put a question to an entire penitential economy: had the church been telling people to perform something that Jesus had told them to become?

A year later, on October 31, 1517, an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg posted ninety-five theses for academic debate. Most readers remember them as an attack on the sale of indulgences, and they were. But Martin Luther began somewhere deeper. His first thesis, in the translation of the American edition of *Luther's Works* (volume 31), reads: "When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said, 'Repent' [Matt. 4:17], he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance." His second thesis added that Jesus's word could not be taken to mean the sacrament of penance, the confession and satisfaction administered by the clergy. The first thesis was a claim about what a Christian life is: repentance is not an occasional transaction for the moments you sin badly but the permanent shape of a life lived before God. In a letter of May 1518 to his mentor Johann von Staupitz, set at the front of his *Explanations* of the theses, Luther recalled that learning the Greek word, *metanoia*, from *meta* and *nous*, had changed how he heard it.

The Reformation's heirs built on that thesis in their confessions. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 taught that repentance properly has two parts, contrition (the terror of a conscience that knows its sin) and faith (the trust that sins are forgiven for Christ's sake), and that good works follow as its fruit. The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 went further and made joy half of repentance. Genuine conversion, it teaches, involves two things: the dying-away of the old self, which it describes as heartfelt sorrow over sin and a growing hatred of it, and the coming-to-life of the new self, which it describes as a wholehearted joy in God through Christ and a delight in doing his will. A sixteenth-century Reformed catechism, not a modern self-help book, insists that a repentance with no joy in it is only half a repentance.

Fairness requires hearing the Catholic answer in its own voice, because the Protestant retelling is often a caricature. The Council of Trent, in its fourteenth session in 1551, defined contrition as sorrow of soul and hatred of the sin committed, joined to the resolve not to sin again. Satisfaction, in Catholic teaching, does not purchase the forgiveness of sin, which comes from Christ alone; Trent taught that the satisfaction a penitent offers is made through Christ and draws its worth from him. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1992) says that Jesus's call to conversion aims first not at outward works but at the conversion of the heart, and that without interior conversion outward penances remain sterile and false. It also insists that because many sins wrong a neighbor, the penitent must do what is possible to repair the harm, returning stolen goods and restoring the reputation of someone slandered. A thoughtful Catholic would say the sacrament exists to keep repentance from staying in the head: it makes you say the sin aloud to another human being, it gives you a spoken word of pardon you can hold onto, and it attaches repair to confession. Those are real goods. Luther himself prized private confession and absolution, and the Augsburg Confession asked that it be retained.

Sort the question before settling it. That God forgives sinners who turn to him through Christ is first-order; the Nicene Creed confesses "one baptism for the forgiveness of sins," and every tradition that stands on the creed confesses a God who pardons the penitent. Whether penance is a sacrament, what role a priest's absolution plays, and how satisfaction relates to grace are second-order questions that have divided faithful churches for five centuries without dividing them from the faith. This essay stands with Luther's first thesis, that repentance is the whole life and not only the confessional, and it leaves the sacramental question to the reader's own conscience and tradition. The strongest objection a Catholic might raise deserves to be left standing: a repentance that is "the whole life" can become so diffuse that it never lands anywhere, never gets said aloud, never costs a thing. Protestants have often proved that objection correct.

## Two kinds of grief

Paul wrote his second letter to Corinth after a visit that had gone badly and a letter that had cost him tears. He had rebuked them, and they had grieved, and now he writes to sort their grief into two kinds. "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Both are real sorrow. The difference is where the sorrow goes.

Worldly grief is sorrow that circles the self. It grieves the exposure, the lost reputation, the consequences, the damaged image. It is often intense, and it can look from outside exactly like contrition, but its center of gravity is the one who sinned. Because it never leaves the self, it has nowhere to go but down, and Paul's verdict on it is blunt. It produces death. Judas is its purest case in Scripture. His regret was sincere, his restitution was actual (he brought the money back), and his sorrow ended at the end of a rope, because it was sorrow about Judas and never became a turn toward the one he had betrayed.

Godly grief is sorrow that turns. It is, in Paul's phrase, grief "according to God," grief that looks at the sin from where God stands and so sees first not the damaged self but the wronged God and the wronged neighbor. Peter denied Jesus three times in a courtyard, and Luke records that "the Lord turned and looked at Peter" (Luke 22:61), and "he went out and wept bitterly" (Luke 22:62). His sorrow was as bitter as Judas's. But it was grief in the presence of a look, and the look was not finished with him. Godly grief goes somewhere. It moves toward the one who was wronged, and it ends, as Paul says, without regret, which may be the strangest promise in the passage. The sorrow that repents is the only sorrow you will never wish you hadn't felt.

The canonical portrait of godly grief is Psalm 51, and its superscription is part of the text, not an editor's decoration: "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet went to him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba." The heading forces the psalm to be read against 2 Samuel 11 and 12, which is to say against a king who took another man's wife, arranged the husband's death in battle, married the widow, and went on governing for months as though nothing had happened. Nathan told David about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, and the king, who could see injustice perfectly well at a distance, pronounced that the thief deserved to die and "restore the lamb fourfold" (2 Samuel 12:6). Then Nathan turned the story around, and David, to his lasting credit, did not reach for a defense. "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Samuel 12:13).

Psalm 51 is what that sentence sounds like when it is given room. "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me" (Psalm 51:3). The next verse has troubled readers for centuries: "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight, so that you may be justified in your words and blameless in your judgment" (Psalm 51:4). David had sinned against Bathsheba and against Uriah, whose blood was on his hands, and he surely knew it. The line is not a denial that his neighbors were wronged. It is a confession that the deepest wrong is also the widest: every injury to a person made in God's image is finally an offense against the God who made them. Then comes the request that shows godly grief's direction. "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10). David does not ask for help managing his image or softening the consequences. The consequences in 2 Samuel are severe, and the psalm does not bargain them away. He asks for something he cannot make, a new heart, and he knows what God will receive: "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Psalm 51:17).

Søren Kierkegaard wrote his long discourse *Purity of Heart Is to Will One Thing* in 1847 as a spiritual preparation for confession, and its sharpest pages anatomize the ways a person can will the good while secretly willing something else. He calls this double-mindedness. A man may turn from his sin because he fears the punishment, or because he wants the reward, or because he wants the good to win by his own hand, and in each case the turning is really about something other than the good itself. He treats this not as crude hypocrisy but as the ordinary condition of the human heart. Its trouble isn't that it feels too much. Its trouble is that it wills two things at once: to be forgiven, and to remain at the center.

## Fruit a neighbor can count

John asked for fruit, and the most vivid fruit in the Gospels grows in Jericho. Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector, a man grown rich on what he could skim from his neighbors. When Jesus invited himself to his house, the crowd grumbled, and Zacchaeus answered the grumbling with his ledger. "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8). The Law required a man who confessed a wrong to "make full restitution for his wrong, adding a fifth to it" (Numbers 5:7); fourfold was the penalty for a stolen sheep (Exodus 22:1), and it was the sentence David had pronounced on the rich man in Nathan's parable without realizing he was pronouncing it on himself. Zacchaeus sentenced himself to a measure far beyond what the Law asked of a man who confessed, and paid it gladly. Jesus gives the verdict: "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9).

Notice the order, because it guards against a misreading. Zacchaeus did not earn Jesus's visit by repaying his victims. The visit came first, uninvited and unearned, and the restitution was his response to having been found. Repentance is not the price of grace. It is what grace looks like when it reaches a man's accounts. But notice also that the repair was not optional decoration on an inward change. A Zacchaeus who felt deeply sorry and kept the money would not have repented in any sense Luke recognizes.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer named the alternative in *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937), written while he trained pastors for the Confessing Church. Cheap grace, he argued, is forgiveness preached without repentance, grace that justifies the sin rather than the sinner and leaves everything as it was. His diagnosis reaches past the state church he was addressing. Any of us can preach cheap grace to ourselves: a pardon claimed with nothing named and nothing returned, the release of a debt we never admitted we owed.

Repair is where repentance becomes visible to the person who was wronged, and it is where most of us stall. It means naming the specific thing without the softening clauses. It means giving back what can be given back: the money, the credit, the reputation we damaged in a conversation they never heard. It means accepting consequences we didn't choose, and not demanding that the injured party hurry their trust to match our remorse. The site's essay on [forgiveness and trust in marriage](/writing/forgiveness-in-marriage) follows this into the hardest room most people will ever repent in, and I won't repeat it here. The principle is the one John gave the soldiers: repentance has to reach the place where your power went crooked, or it hasn't reached anything.

## Repenting of our righteousness

Jesus's parable of the two sons ends with the wrong son outside. The younger one, who wasted his inheritance, "came to himself" and went home, and the father ran to meet him. The elder one, who never left and never disobeyed, heard the music and "was angry and refused to go in" (Luke 15:28). His complaint is a résumé: "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command" (Luke 15:29). Timothy Keller's *The Prodigal God* (2008) argued that the parable is aimed chiefly at him, at the Pharisees and scribes who had grumbled that Jesus ate with sinners, and that both sons wanted the father's goods more than the father. One tried to get them by leaving, the other by obeying. Keller's conclusion is one religious people rarely hear: the gospel calls us to repent not only of our sins but of our righteousness, of the self-salvation project that turned our good deeds into a claim on God.

That last sentence is the hardest part of repentance for those of us who grew up in church or who preach in one. The older brother's sin doesn't look like sin. It looks like faithfulness, and it is defended as principle. I have led in ways that needed repentance, with a pride I didn't see and a defensiveness I dressed up as conviction, and the dressing was convincing for a long time, mostly to me. I have defended things that deserved to fall and called my refusal a matter of principle. Kierkegaard's double-mindedness lives most comfortably here, in the heart that obeys in order to be owed.

Religious readers tend to hear the call to repentance as addressed to the younger son, which is why we can preach it without flinching. The parable leaves the older son standing in the yard with the father pleading with him. How the Spirit reshapes a life over years is the subject of the site's essay on [how Christians change](/writing/how-do-you-actually-change); the point here is narrower. You cannot turn from a direction you refuse to believe you are facing, and the righteous rarely believe it.

## When a people repents

Scripture also knows a repentance that is spoken in the plural. In the first year of Darius, Daniel read in Jeremiah that the exile would last seventy years, and he responded not with a strategy but with confession. "We have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled, turning aside from your commandments and rules" (Daniel 9:5). Daniel is the Bible's model of a faithful exile, and yet he says "we." Later he describes himself as "confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel" (Daniel 9:20), and he refuses to plead on the basis of Israel's record: "For we do not present our pleas before you because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy" (Daniel 9:18). Nearly a century later the returned exiles gathered in Jerusalem, and the Levites led them through the whole history of Israel's rebellion in Nehemiah 9, ending in a confession that justifies God rather than the people: "Yet you have been righteous in all that has come upon us, for you have dealt faithfully and we have acted wickedly" (Nehemiah 9:33).

Corporate repentance of this kind is real and sometimes necessary. In June 1995, at its 150th anniversary, the Southern Baptist Convention adopted a resolution on racial reconciliation that acknowledged the convention's origins in its relation to slavery and repented of racism, including its own, and asked forgiveness of African Americans. Whatever one judges of how fully it has been lived out, it had the shape of Daniel 9. It said "we," and it named a specific history.

The misuse runs in two directions, and both should be named with the same instrument. One is to confess the nation's sins while quietly meaning someone else's. Second Chronicles 7:14 ("if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways") is quoted on banners and at rallies as though "my people" meant a country and "wicked ways" meant the other party's agenda. On the right the call to national repentance has often meant repentance for secularism, abortion and sexual revolution; on the left it has often meant repentance for racism, nationalism and greed, sins the speaker's own tribe is presumed to have outgrown. Some of each list names real sin. But the promise in that verse is addressed to "my people," covenant Israel at the dedication of Solomon's temple, and it asks them to turn from their own wicked ways; Daniel counts himself among the guilty. The other misuse is repentance as performance: the institutional apology crafted to end a news cycle, the public statement that confesses in the passive voice and changes nothing. Both treat repentance as a tool. Daniel treats it as the truth told before God, with himself inside the indictment.

## Repentance is not shame

The first sinners in Scripture did not repent. They hid. "I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself" (Genesis 3:10). Then came the blaming, the man pointing at the woman and at the God who gave her. Hiding and blaming are shame's native reflexes, and they are the opposite of repentance, which comes out of the trees and says what happened.

Psychology arrived at a similar distinction by its own road. Helen Block Lewis, in *Shame and Guilt in Neurosis* (1971), distinguished guilt, which is focused on a thing done, from shame, which is focused on the self who did it: not "I did a bad thing" but "I am bad." June Tangney and Ronda Dearing's *Shame and Guilt* (2002) summarized years of research suggesting that guilt of that specific kind tends to move people toward confession and repair, while shame tends to move them toward hiding, denial and defensive anger. Psychology describes the two roads well. Scripture says where they end: guilt named before a merciful God becomes confession, and confession ends in pardon. Shame left to itself is worldly grief, sorrow circling a self it cannot fix.

Many Christians were taught to confuse the two. They were told that feeling worthless was humility, that the proof of repentance was how long you could stay miserable, that a sin confessed must still be relived before every new authority. That is not repentance but a counterfeit of it, and it often came from people who were protecting themselves. If that is your story, the site's essay on [religious trauma](/writing/religious-trauma-is-real) was written for you, and a wider account of what sin is and is not lives in [what went wrong with the world](/writing/what-went-wrong). This is a pastor's essay, not psychological advice, and a counselor can help with what it can't. If self-condemnation has turned toward thoughts of ending your life, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, tonight.

Shame says you must hide because of what you are. Repentance says you may come out because of who God is. "God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance" (Romans 2:4). The one who hung on the cross "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2) and took the nakedness of the garden onto himself, so that sinners need not hide any longer. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). Repentance is safe only because of that.

Possidius, who knew Augustine for nearly forty years and wrote his life, records how the old bishop died in 430, with the Vandals besieging his city. He had the few psalms of David that concern repentance copied out and set against the wall where he could read them from his bed, and he read them in his last days and wept. Augustine had written the *Confessions*, preached for decades, and defended grace against those who thought it unnecessary, and he spent his final days where Luther would later say every believer lives, turning again. He wept, and he kept reading.

Repentance, then, is not a feeling to be manufactured or a record to be relived. It is a direction taken again tomorrow, toward the person you wronged, and away from the righteousness you are proudest of. The father is still standing in the yard, pleading with the elder son. The parable never tells us whether he went in, and it leaves that door open for us.
