We carry a picture of how marriages end, and it nearly always involves a discovery. A message on the wrong phone, a confession at the kitchen table, a door closing at one in the morning that splits a life into before and after. Those endings are real, and they deserve every ounce of care the church can give. But they aren't how most marriages end. The more common ending has no scene in it at all. Two people raise the children, pay the mortgage, sit through the ball games and the funerals, and somewhere in the middle of all that competence they become roommates who share a last name. When someone finally asks what happened, the answer usually comes with a shrug: we just grew apart.

Couples who reach that place tend to describe it in the same lopsided way. One spouse has been living through a long, slow emergency, reaching for years and learning that the reaching goes nowhere, until the reaching stopped and a private conclusion quietly took its place. The other experienced nothing unusual. The house ran, and there were no fights worth remembering, and the absence of conflict was read as health when it was really the silence of someone who had stopped expecting anything. So when the sentence finally arrives, *I'm not sure I love you anymore*, one of them hears an announcement and the other has been drafting it for years.

We usually explain this as a problem of time. We're busy, the children take everything, work never lets up, and the marriage gets whatever is left, which is nothing. Some of that is true, and the calendar isn't innocent. But it's only part of the story. If drift were only a scheduling problem, a standing date night would cure it, and anyone who has tried to warm a cold marriage with a restaurant reservation knows better. You can sit across a candle from someone for two hours and never once arrive.

The argument of this essay is that drift is a condition of the heart before it is a condition of the calendar. The church has an old name for it, far older than the modern idea that marriage exists to make us happy: acedia. It describes a sorrow that settles over the good right in front of you, a weariness with the very place and person you've been given, and a restlessness that would rather be almost anywhere else. It is a disorder of love, and a disorder of love can't be scheduled away. It has to be healed, and Scripture heals disordered loves the way it always has: by attention, by repentance, and by a faithfulness older than ours.

### The noonday demon

In the late fourth century Evagrius of Pontus, a monk in the Egyptian desert, wrote down with unnerving precision the thoughts that assailed the men who had gone out there to pray. In his *Praktikos* he names eight of them, and he calls acedia the heaviest. He gives it a strange title, the noonday demon, drawn from the Greek version of Psalm 91:6, which the ESV renders as the promise of protection from "the destruction that wastes at noonday." The demon, Evagrius says, comes in the late morning and stays through the middle of the afternoon. It makes the sun seem to stand still and the day seem fifty hours long. It sends the monk to the window again and again to see whether anyone is coming. It fills him with distaste for his cell and his work, convinces him that no one around him really cares for him, and sets his mind wandering toward some other place where he could serve God more easily and be happier doing it.

Read that with a marriage in mind and it stops sounding ancient. The monk isn't in a crisis and hasn't renounced his vows. He has simply grown weary of the good he chose, and the weariness feels to him like insight. He's sure the problem is the cell.

John Cassian carried the desert teaching into the Latin West around 420, writing the *Institutes* for new monasteries in southern Gaul, and the tenth book is devoted to acedia. Cassian adds a detail married people will recognize. The monk gripped by acedia praises communities far away, imagines how he would flourish among other brothers, and finds the ones next to him careless and dull. The remedy Cassian prescribes, built on Paul's instructions to the Thessalonians about quiet labor, is almost comically plain: stay where you are and work with your hands. Acedia always wants elsewhere. Its cure is always here.

The word traveled a long way and shrank as it went. Gregory the Great, in the late sixth century, folded it into sadness in his list of capital sins, and by the late Middle Ages it had largely become sloth, which to modern ears means laziness. But when Thomas Aquinas treated it in the *Summa Theologiae* around 1271 (II-II, question 35), he wouldn't let it collapse into idleness. Acedia, he argued, is a sorrow in the face of spiritual good, and it is grave because it sets itself against the joy that charity takes in God. Following Gregory, he counts among its offspring a restless wandering of the mind toward what is not in front of it. The acedic person is not someone who does too little. It's someone for whom the good itself has become heavy.

Josef Pieper pressed that insight into the modern world in *Leisure: The Basis of Culture* (1948). The opposite of acedia, he argued, is not hard work, and a ceaseless, driven busyness can be one of its disguises, a way of refusing to rest in the good one has actually been given. The drifting spouse is almost never idle. He works late, coaches the team, answers the email at ten at night, and feels like the most responsible person in the house. The betrayer at least knows he has done something. The drifter feels like a hard worker. Pieper helps us see why he would rather be exhausted by the office than present in the quiet kitchen: the kitchen asks for something the office never does, the willingness to receive another person with no scoreboard running.

One distinction has to be drawn with care. Kathleen Norris, in *Acedia & Me* (2008), read her own long marriage alongside the desert fathers and argued that the old word names something our vocabulary had lost, while insisting that acedia and clinical depression are not the same thing. Some withdrawal in marriage is illness, not sin, and a spouse who has gone flat and silent may need a physician before a sermon. If that describes you or someone you love, talk to a doctor; if the flatness has turned toward despair, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Acedia is sorrow toward a good it can still see; depression can take the seeing itself. To confuse the two is to moralize an illness or medicate a sin.

### How marriage came to carry everything and be tended by no one

Acedia is ancient, but its American form has a history, and the history explains why drift now so often ends in divorce rather than in the grey endurance of earlier generations.

For most of human history marriage was an institution before it was a relationship. It organized property, labor and lineage, and on a farm or in a shop husband and wife worked the same ground, so that affection grew out of shared work rather than carrying the whole weight of the bond. Industrialization pulled work out of the home, and in 1945 the sociologists Ernest Burgess and Harvey Locke gave the result its textbook title: *The Family: From Institution to Companionship*. Marriage would now be held together chiefly by companionship and affection. Andrew Cherlin, in his 2004 article "The Deinstitutionalization of American Marriage," traced the next turn, beginning in the 1960s, toward what he called the individualized marriage, measured by personal growth and self-expression. As marriage's practical necessity fell, its symbolic prestige rose, and it became what Cherlin calls a capstone, the crowning achievement set in place once the rest of a life is in order, rather than a cornerstone laid first so that a life could be built on it.

In *The Marriage-Go-Round* (2009), Cherlin argued that Americans hold two ideals with unusual intensity at the same time: marriage as the gold standard of adult life, and personal fulfillment as the self's first obligation. Their collision, he showed, produces more marrying, more divorcing and more cycling between partners than in comparable Western nations. Eli Finkel's *The All-or-Nothing Marriage* (2017) added the sharpest diagnosis. Since roughly 1965, he argues, we have asked marriage to help us become our best selves, a far heavier demand than asking it to feed us or keep us company, while giving it less of the time and attention that demand requires. The best marriages, on his account, are better than they have ever been. The average marriage is starving.

The law followed the culture rather than leading it. In 1969 Governor Ronald Reagan signed California's Family Law Act, which took effect on 1 January 1970 and made California the first state to allow divorce without proof of fault. Every other state followed, New York last in 2010. No-fault divorce didn't invent the idea that a marriage ends when its feelings do. It ratified what Robert Bellah and his colleagues found everywhere in *Habits of the Heart* (1985): a therapeutic attitude that treats love as an exchange between two selves seeking fulfillment, a commitment that lasts as long as it serves.

Brad Wilcox gives the resulting picture a name in *Get Married* (2024): the soulmate model, in which marriage exists above all for romance and personal fulfillment and succeeds or fails on the strength of the fit. If the love lives in the fit, then when the feeling thins, the natural conclusion is that the fit was wrong. Drift stops being a condition to be healed and becomes information to act on.

The sociologists got the symptom right. We did load marriage with more than it could carry, and we did give it less than it needed. But the secular account can't see the whole thing, because it shares the anthropology it describes. It assumes the self is the thing to be fulfilled and love is the feeling that reports whether the fulfilling is happening. Scripture assumes something else: that love is covenant loyalty, the steadfast love the Old Testament calls *hesed*, practiced before it is felt, and that the self is found by being given away. Brought under that light, the modern marriage looks less like a failed experiment in happiness than like the desert cell: a good was given, it grew heavy, and we began looking out the window.

The church is not outside this indictment, and I'm not either. Conservative churches absorbed the soulmate language into wedding sermons about the one person God made just for you, and then, when the feeling faded, reached for permanence as a club without asking whether anyone had been tending what they were ordering people to keep. Churches that made peace with the therapeutic often did the opposite, baptizing fulfillment and quietly treating a cold marriage as a verdict. Underneath, the two share a grammar: both treat a marriage as something that either works or doesn't, and neither asks what the spouses have actually been loving.

### The case that you simply married the wrong person

There's a serious objection here, and it deserves the form its own defenders would sign. People change. The twenty-four-year-olds who married each other are not the forty-six-year-olds who now share the house, and sometimes the change carries them in genuinely different directions, with different convictions and different hopes. What the church calls drift is often just the honest registration of incompatibility discovered late. To relabel it acedia, a sin, is to moralize a mismatch, to heap guilt on people who did nothing wrong except grow up, and to keep them in loveless marriages by persuading them the lovelessness is their fault. Many married young under pressure from church cultures that treated early marriage as an achievement. And the spouse who finally names the distance is usually the one who has been paying attention all along; telling her to try harder blames the only person who was trying.

Much of that is true, and a Christian account of marriage that can't admit it has made an idol of the institution. Some marriages were made badly, and the church has at times been complicit in making them. And the spouse who has been reaching alone for years is not the one who needs a lecture on turning toward. If that's you, nothing here is meant to add to a load you've carried alone.

But the objection rests on a confusion between two different things: difference and distance. Incompatibility is a claim about who two people are. Drift is a claim about what two people have been doing. Stanley Hauerwas made the point with deliberate provocation in a 1978 essay in *The Christian Century*, arguing that we always marry the wrong person, because none of us knows who we are marrying and marriage itself changes us into someone else. The question is never whether your spouse turned out to be a stranger. Every spouse does. The question is whether you kept looking long enough to know the stranger.

Iris Murdoch gives the best picture I know of what that looking does. In *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970) she describes a mother-in-law who finds her son's wife vulgar and tiresomely juvenile, and who, without any change in outward behavior, sets herself to look again, justly and lovingly, until she sees a young woman who is not vulgar but refreshingly simple, not undignified but spontaneous. Nothing about the daughter-in-law has changed. The mother-in-law's attention has. Murdoch borrowed the word *attention* from Simone Weil, who argued in a 1942 essay on school studies that the love of neighbor, in its fullness, comes down to being able to ask another person what they are going through, and then to wait, emptied of our own agenda, for the answer.

Set against Murdoch and Weil, a good deal of what we call discovered incompatibility turns out to be a stranger we made by not looking. We stopped asking and started assuming. I don't say that to deny that some marriages end, or that one spouse can hold a marriage together alone; one can't, and sometimes the other has already gone. I say it because the incompatibility story, however true in a given case, is also the story acedia most wants to tell. It locates the problem in the fit and asks nothing of the eyes.

### Love is trained before it is felt

Augustine gave the church its most durable account of why this is so. In *The City of God*, in book fifteen, he defines virtue briefly and truly as the right ordering of love, the *ordo amoris*. We are creatures whose loves are always being ordered or disordered, and whose lives follow what we love most. That is why drift is so hard to see from the inside. It doesn't happen by decision. It happens by the slow reordering of what the heart reaches for first.

James K. A. Smith updated Augustine for the age of the smartphone in *You Are What You Love* (2016). His argument is that our loves are trained less by our ideas than by our habits, the repeated bodily practices he calls liturgies, and that the culture runs liturgies of its own whether we notice them or not. The mall forms desire, and so does the feed, and so does the evening routine in which two people sit in the same room, each lit by a separate screen, night after night for a decade. No one intends that routine to teach anything, but it trains two hearts to reach first for something other than each other, and after enough years the training feels like nature.

The psychologist John Gottman, with Joan DeClaire in *The Relationship Cure* (2001), describes the same mechanism in the language of research. Marriages are sustained by bids: small, constant offers of connection, a remark about the weather, a hand on a shoulder, a sigh that means *ask me*. The partner either turns toward the bid, turns away from it, or turns against it. Couples who last have a habit of turning toward; couples who drift have a habit of turning away, seldom in anger and almost always out of habit. Gottman's work is the empirical shadow of Augustine and Smith. Love is a habit before it is a feeling, and the feeling mostly follows the habit.

One line in the Song of Songs indicts me. The young woman explains why she's sunburned: "My mother's sons were angry with me; they made me keeper of the vineyards, but my own vineyard I have not kept!" (Song of Songs 1:6). I'm married to Susanna. We have five sons. I've been the pastor of First Baptist Church of Fenton for twelve years, and a pastor's work never runs short of good reasons, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. I know from the inside how easy it is to keep a great many vineyards and neglect the one that is mine. I was raised without a father, so I have no excuse for not knowing what absence costs, and I still find in myself the pull toward the urgent over the present, the email over the face across the room. Absence with a clear conscience is the most dangerous kind, because nothing in it feels like leaving.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer understood the mechanism from a prison cell. In May 1943, from Tegel, he wrote a wedding sermon for his friend Eberhard Bethge and Bethge's bride, Renate, and in it he turned the soulmate model inside out. It is not their love, he told the couple, that sustains the marriage; from their wedding day on, it is the marriage that sustains their love. The soulmate model assumes the feeling carries the covenant, so when the feeling thins the covenant must be failing. Bonhoeffer assumes the reverse. The covenant, kept, is what carries the love through the seasons when the feeling can't carry itself. But a covenant can only sustain a love that is being practiced inside it. Covenant without attention is not faithfulness. It's possession.

### What the prophet heard at the altar

Scripture's sharpest word on this comes from Malachi, writing to Judah in the century after the return from exile, when the temple had been rebuilt and the enthusiasm that rebuilt it had gone stale. The book addresses a people who kept the forms of worship while their hearts drifted, and in the second chapter it ties that drift to their homes. The men of Judah were weeping at the altar because God no longer accepted their offerings, and Malachi tells them why:

> But you say, "Why does he not?" Because the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant. (Malachi 2:14)

The Hebrew of the next verse is notoriously difficult, but the charge is clear. The word translated *faithless* means to act treacherously, and it's the same word the book uses for Judah's betrayal of God. The men were putting away the wives of their youth, and Malachi won't let them keep their worship and their treachery in separate rooms. Then comes the instruction, repeated twice so no one misses it: "So guard yourselves in your spirit, and do not be faithless" (Malachi 2:16). The guarding happens in the spirit. Long before a man signs anything, the treachery has begun in what he stopped guarding inside. And he calls her a companion, not only a wife by covenant; it was the companionship that had been abandoned while the legal status stayed on the books.

The Song of Songs gives the other side of the same truth, not a prophet's warning but a lover's longing. "O my dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the crannies of the cliff, let me see your face, let me hear your voice, for your voice is sweet, and your face is lovely" (Song of Songs 2:14). That is attention as desire: *let me see, let me hear*. And the very next verse turns to guarding: "Catch the foxes for us, the little foxes that spoil the vineyards, for our vineyards are in blossom" (Song of Songs 2:15). Nobody ignores a lion in the vineyard. People ignore little foxes. The Song knows that love in blossom is love at its most vulnerable, and that what ruins it is the small uncorrected thing, left alone because it's small.

Paul gathers all of this into Christ. In Ephesians 5, after calling the whole church to submit "to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21), he tells husbands to love their wives "as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25), and then he chooses two verbs that are about maintenance rather than heroics: "For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church" (Ephesians 5:29). Nourishing and cherishing are what you do every day to something alive. And Paul lifts the whole thing into the heavens: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). A marriage is meant to be a small, visible picture of a love that never takes its eyes off its beloved.

Here I have to say plainly what covenant does not mean. Nothing in Malachi, the Song or Ephesians requires anyone to remain in danger. A spouse who abuses has already broken the covenant, and no teaching on faithfulness, submission or staying ever obliges a person to endure violence or cruelty. If you are unsafe, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, and tell someone you trust. Everything in this essay is written for marriages that have cooled, not for homes that have become dangerous.

There's one more text, and it stings because of where it was sent. Decades after Paul's letter, the risen Christ addressed the church in Ephesus, the city whose name that letter traditionally bears, and he commended it: "I know your works, your toil and your patient endurance" (Revelation 2:2). They were orthodox, busy and tireless. Then: "But I have this against you, that you have abandoned the love you had at first. Remember therefore from where you have fallen; repent, and do the works you did at first" (Revelation 2:4–5). Christ's diagnosis of that church reads like a description of marital drift. Its cure is three verbs, and none of them is *feel*. Remember. Repent. Do.

### The God who did not drift

The deepest reason drift has to be named as a sin, and not merely a mismatch, is that the whole Bible is the story of a God who refused to drift from a people who drifted from him. Hosea, whose own marriage became a parable, hears God grieving: "What shall I do with you, O Ephraim? What shall I do with you, O Judah? Your love is like a morning cloud, like the dew that goes early away" (Hosea 6:4). That is acedia seen from heaven's side, a love that evaporates by midmorning without anyone deciding it should. The astonishing thing is not God's anger, though it is real, but his pursuit. "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (Hosea 2:14). "And I will betroth you to me forever. I will betroth you to me in righteousness and in justice, in steadfast love and in mercy" (Hosea 2:19).

At the cross that pursuit reached its end. God's attention did not waver even when ours was turned entirely away, and the Son who loved the church and gave himself up for her did it while the church was asleep in the garden, unable to watch with him one hour. That is where drift has to be brought, to the one place where a drifting heart can be forgiven and taught to love again. Repentance for drift is strange, because you're confessing ten thousand small turnings away, most of which you can't remember. But the God of Hosea knows them all, and he isn't waiting at a distance for you to fix them and come back presentable. "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" (Lamentations 3:22–23). New every morning, which is to say, as daily and as undramatic as the neglect was.

That is also why healing will disappoint anyone who wants it to match the size of the loss. The years weren't built of large acts, and they won't be rebuilt by them. Drift is undone the way it was done, one ordinary day at a time: a question asked and the whole answer waited for, a phone set face down, a hand reached for in the dark. None of it will feel significant on the day, and the spouse who stopped expecting may not notice for a long while. If the distance is wide, a wise pastor and a good counselor are not a sign of failure but of seriousness. And if only one of you is willing, that is a grief to be carried honestly before God, not a failure you owe anyone an apology for.

But the plan isn't the heart of it. The heart of it is that acedia is always at the window, and the noonday hour comes to every marriage: the long middle, when the children are loud or gone, the work is heavy, and the person you promised yourself to has become the most familiar face in your life and therefore the easiest one not to see. The desert fathers said the demon's great lie is that the good life is somewhere else. It is the same lie in every kitchen in America. The good you were given is in the next room, and it's harder to love than the one you imagine through the glass, because it's real, and because it's yours, and because loving it asks you to die a little to the self that wanted to be anywhere else. Christ went further than that, all the way to a cross, and rose, and keeps turning toward a church that keeps turning away. Somewhere in your house tonight there's a face you haven't really looked at in a long time. The question the desert left us, and the one Christ asked in Ephesus, is whether you'll keep staring out the window or turn around.
