## A cause the church got right

When the American church decided that human trafficking was its business, it acted with a conviction and coherence it rarely manages. Conferences filled. Youth groups raised money for rescue organizations. Much of that energy was good, because the buying and selling of human beings is about as direct an assault on the image of God as there is, and a church that stayed quiet about it would deserve every accusation the skeptic has ever made.

The energy also produced law. In the late 1990s an unusual coalition formed in Washington: evangelical and Catholic organizations, Jewish advocates, secular feminists, and members of Congress who agreed on almost nothing else. The political scientist Allen Hertzke tells the story in *Freeing God's Children* (2004), arguing that religious conservatives, newly turned toward international human rights, supplied much of the coalition's grassroots force. On October 28, 2000, President Clinton signed the Victims of Trafficking and Violence Protection Act, which gave the United States its first comprehensive federal framework against trafficking, protected victims who had been treated as criminals, and created the State Department's annual report grading how governments around the world fight the crime. Christians were an honest part of that achievement, and nothing here takes it away.

But a cause can be right and still be held selectively. We also have to ask which trafficking we oppose, whose exploitation we name from the pulpit, and whose we walk past on the way to the rescue conference. The honest answer, for much of the American church, is that our outrage has concentrated on the form of the crime we can condemn from outside and gone quiet about the form we are inside of. And in the summer of 2020, a good deal of Christian concern for trafficked children was captured by rumors that made helping real children harder.

I have to put myself inside that. I've been moved by anti-trafficking campaigns, given to rescue organizations, and preached the horror of sex trafficking with real anger. And I haven't asked, with anything like the same rigor, who picked the tomatoes at the bottom of my grocery bag, or whether the price that pleased me was possible because someone bound by a debt couldn't leave. That is not a small inconsistency. It's a calibration, and this essay tries to recalibrate it against the whole of Scripture rather than the part that flatters us.

One practical word first, because this subject can reach a reader who is living it. If you or someone near you is in immediate danger, call 911. The National Human Trafficking Hotline takes calls at 1-888-373-7888 and texts at 233733, at any hour, for victims, survivors, and anyone who suspects something is wrong.

## What trafficking is, and what it isn't

Precision is the first form of care here, because a word that covers everything protects no one. The international definition comes from the Palermo Protocol, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in November 2000. It builds trafficking out of three elements: an act (recruiting, transporting, transferring, harboring, or receiving a person), a means (threat, force or other coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, the abuse of power or of a position of vulnerability, or payments to someone who controls the victim), and a purpose, which is exploitation, including sexual exploitation, forced labor, slavery and servitude, and the removal of organs. Where any of those means has been used, the victim's apparent consent is irrelevant. Where the victim is under eighteen, no means has to be shown at all.

American law, passed the same autumn, says much the same thing. The 2000 Act defines the severe forms of trafficking as sex trafficking induced by force, fraud, or coercion, or involving anyone under eighteen, and obtaining a person's labor or services through force, fraud, or coercion for the purpose of involuntary servitude, peonage, debt bondage, or slavery. Notice what neither definition requires. Nobody has to cross a border or be kidnapped. Trafficking is defined by control and exploitation, not by movement, and the means the law lists are dominated by fraud, deception, debt, and the manipulation of someone already vulnerable. The picture most of us carry, a stranger snatching a child from a parking lot, is not the crime's typical shape. The typical shape is a promise: a job, a visa, a relationship, a loan, and then a trap closing slowly around it.

The best global estimate we have is the joint report of the International Labour Organization, Walk Free, and the International Organization for Migration, *Global Estimates of Modern Slavery* (2022). It estimated that on any given day in 2021 about 49.6 million people were living in modern slavery, a category that joins forced labor and forced marriage. Forced labor accounted for 27.6 million of them and forced marriage for about 22 million. Of those 27.6 million, about 17.3 million were in forced labor in the private economy, about 6.3 million were in forced commercial sexual exploitation, and nearly 4 million were in forced labor imposed by state authorities. These are modeled estimates, and should be stated no more strongly than that. But the proportion is hard to miss. Fewer than a quarter of the people in forced labor are in the commercial sex trade. Most are in fields, kitchens, factories, fishing boats, construction sites, and private homes.

Two distinctions keep the argument honest. Trafficking is not every hard or underpaid job, and it isn't all child labor. Wage theft is a sin and a crime (the subject of [a separate essay on fair wages](/writing/theology-fair-wages)), but a worker who is shorted and can quit is not in the same condition as one who can't leave. Trafficking sits at the far end of a continuum of exploitation, and what marks the far end is that exit has been taken away. The second distinction runs the other direction. Forced labor is not a lesser crime because it is only labor. The law treats a person held in a brothel and a person held on a fishing boat as victims of the same offense, and so should the church.

Kevin Bales, whose *Disposable People* (1999) did much to bring the subject back into public view, argued that the new slavery differs from the old in ways that make it harder to see. The old slavery claimed legal ownership, and because enslaved people were expensive, slaveholders had a cruel economic reason to keep them alive. The new slavery rarely claims ownership and treats people as cheap and replaceable, used for a season and discarded. A slavery with no paperwork hides inside ordinary commerce. It doesn't look like a crime. It looks like a supply.

## What Scripture names, and what it refuses to sort

The Bible's first trafficking story is a family story. Joseph's brothers pull him out of a pit and sell him to traders bound for Egypt for twenty shekels of silver (Genesis 37:28). It is done by the people who knew him best, with a story ready to cover it. Scripture opens its account of the trade in persons with the pattern the law would later have to learn: the one who sells you is often the one you trusted.

The law answers the crime directly. "Whoever steals a man and sells him, and anyone found in possession of him, shall be put to death" (Exodus 21:16, ESV). Deuteronomy repeats the command for anyone who takes a fellow Israelite and treats him as a slave or sells him (Deuteronomy 24:7). A skeptical reader will rightly point out that the same chapter of Exodus regulates servitude rather than abolishing it, and that Leviticus 25:44–46 permits buying foreigners as permanent slaves. That difficulty is real and deserves more than a sentence; the church's long misuse of those texts is taken up in [the essay on reading the Bible without making it say what you want](/writing/how-to-read-the-bible-without-making-it-say-what-you-want) and [the essay on the church's crimes](/writing/apologetics-hasnt-the-church-done-terrible-things). But the verse still makes seizing and selling a human being a capital crime, and it extends guilt past the kidnapper to the one "found in possession." The buyer is not innocent because someone else did the stealing. The Atlantic trade ran on buyers who believed otherwise.

The prophets widened the charge from persons to nations. Amos opens his book with oracles against Israel's neighbors, and the charge against Gaza and against Tyre is that they delivered whole populations into captivity and handed them over to Edom (Amos 1:6, 1:9). Then he turns to Israel itself, and the indictment moves from the battlefield to the market: "they sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6, ESV). Joel, indicting the nations that had carried off Judah's people, names both forms of the trade at once.

"and have cast lots for my people, and have traded a boy for a prostitute, and have sold a girl for wine and have drunk it" (Joel 3:3, ESV).

The children in Joel's indictment were exchanged for a night's pleasure and a jug of wine, and God says he has kept the ledger.

The New Testament keeps the two together in a place almost nobody reads for this purpose, Paul's list of those the law exists to restrain.

"the sexually immoral, men who practice homosexuality, enslavers, liars, perjurers, and whatever else is contrary to sound doctrine," (1 Timothy 1:10, ESV).

The word the ESV translates "enslavers" is *andrapodistēs*, a slave-dealer or kidnapper. Commentators have long noticed that Paul's list runs roughly along the second table of the Ten Commandments: dishonoring parents, murder, sexual sin, then the dealer in persons where the commandment against stealing would fall, then the liars and perjurers where false witness would fall. The church has usually preached one half of 1 Timothy 1:10 with great confidence and the other half not at all. Paul put the sexual sin and the trade in human beings in the same verse, a word apart. He didn't sort them. We did.

The canon's last word comes in Revelation 18, the lament over Babylon, the city that stands for every economy built on luxury and domination. The merchants weep over her not because they loved her but because "no one buys their cargo anymore" (Revelation 18:11, ESV). Then comes the cargo list: gold, silver, jewels, fine linen, ivory, spices, wine, oil, flour, cattle, horses, chariots. It ends with "and slaves, that is, human souls" (Revelation 18:13, ESV). The word for slaves there is *sōmata*, bodies, the ordinary commercial term, and John follows it with *psychas anthrōpōn*, the souls of human beings, as if to correct the invoice. At the bottom of Babylon's manifest, after the luxuries, sit people, and the whole economy is judged by the last line.

Under all of it lies the first chapter of the Bible. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). Trafficking is a theological crime before it is a legal one, because it treats a bearer of God's image as merchandise, whether in a brothel or on a trawler. The mechanism differs. The blasphemy is the same.

## Where our outrage stops

If the image of God is violated equally at both ends of the trade, why has the church's attention been so uneven? Look at where the other end has surfaced. In 2015 the Associated Press published *Seafood from Slaves*, an investigation that traced fish caught by captive men on Southeast Asian boats into the supply chains of American supermarkets and restaurants; it won the Pulitzer Prize for Public Service in 2016 and led to the release of more than two thousand men. In 2020 NORC at the University of Chicago, in a study funded by the U.S. Department of Labor, estimated that about 1.56 million children were in child labor in cocoa production in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana during the 2018–19 season, nearly two decades after the chocolate industry signed the Harkin-Engel Protocol of 2001. Most of those children work on family farms, and child labor is not the same as trafficking; the honest point is narrower. Coercion hides inside chains of production long enough that nobody at the end of the chain has to know. In December 2021 Congress passed the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, which presumes that goods from China's Xinjiang region are made with forced labor unless an importer proves otherwise.

Labor trafficking touches us in a way sex trafficking mostly doesn't: its products sit on our shelves and in our church kitchens. Our outrage is selective partly because our implication becomes much harder to contain once the frame widens.

The case for focus deserves to be heard at full strength. Organizations that concentrate on sex trafficking argue that a diffuse campaign is a weak one, and that the movement's real victories, in legislation, prosecution, and survivor care, came from narrow, relentless attention. Sex trafficking also carries a distinct horror. The violation is bodily and intimate, it falls disproportionately on women and girls, and a moral tradition that treats sexual violation as uniquely grave is not wrong to do so. Resources are finite, and triage is not hypocrisy. The supply-chain alternative has its own failure mode, too: boycotts launched from comfortable countries have sometimes cost poor workers the only jobs available to them.

All of that should stand, and nothing here asks a rescue ministry to dilute its mission. The problem comes when the narrowness of the campaign becomes the narrowness of the vision. And the narrowing runs in both political directions, which is how you know it is tribal rather than moral.

A conservative Christianity comfortable condemning the brothel can go quiet about the farm and the processing plant, because that conversation touches immigration policy, guestworker visas that bind a worker to a single employer, and the purchasing habits of everyone in the sanctuary. A progressive Christianity fluent in supply chains can go quiet about the commercial sex trade itself, because naming its violence plainly costs standing in circles where it has been redescribed as ordinary work. The policy question underneath is genuinely contested. Since 1999 Sweden has criminalized the purchase of sex but not its sale, on the reasoning that demand drives the trade; in 2015 Amnesty International voted to support the decriminalization of consensual sex work, publishing the full policy in 2016, on the reasoning that criminalization pushes the people selling sex into danger and away from police they might otherwise call. Faithful people land differently on that question, and it is second-order. The reality both sides say they oppose is not. A trade in which so many are coerced, so many enter as children, and so many cannot leave is not made clean by calling it work.

The critique has run the other way as well. The sociologist Elizabeth Bernstein, who spent years at evangelical and secular feminist anti-trafficking gatherings, argued in "The Sexual Politics of the 'New Abolitionism'" (2007) that the alliance behind the 2000 law increasingly understood justice as policing, rescue, and prosecution, and paid far less attention to the economic conditions that make people vulnerable. She called the pattern "carceral feminism." A Christian doesn't have to accept her whole framework to feel the force of the question. A movement that knows exactly whom to arrest and has little to say about wages, migration, or debt has chosen the part of the problem that asks nothing of its own members.

The prophets did not sort this way. Amos moved from the slave-raiding nations to the merchants who sold the needy for sandals, to the courts that took bribes, to the worship that dressed it all in piety, and he never checked which indictment his audience could afford. Isaiah's congregation fasted energetically while the economy around them ground down the poor, and God refused to be impressed: "Behold, you fast only to quarrel and to fight and to hit with a wicked fist" (Isaiah 58:4, ESV). Visible devotion and structural blindness can live in the same sanctuary for a long time. My own outrage has been tuned to the forms of exploitation I can condemn without auditing my own economic life.

## When rescue becomes a rumor

Moral panic about trafficking is older than the internet. In 1885 the London journalist W. T. Stead published "The Maiden Tribute of Modern Babylon" in the *Pall Mall Gazette*, exposing the sale of girls into prostitution. Much of it was true, and the outcry pushed Parliament to raise the age of consent from thirteen to sixteen that year. But the panic over what was called "white slavery" carried a great deal of exaggeration with it, and in the United States it produced the Mann Act of 1910, whose loose language about transporting women for "immoral" purposes was soon used against consensual relationships. In 1913 the heavyweight champion Jack Johnson, a Black man, was convicted under it over his relationship with a white woman, in a prosecution widely understood as racially driven; he was pardoned only in 2018, long after his death. A real evil, badly described, produced a law that was turned against the wrong people.

The summer of 2020 repeated the pattern at the speed of a phone screen. QAnon, the conspiracy theory claiming that a secret cabal of elites was trafficking and abusing children, borrowed the name of a century-old children's charity for its hashtag, #SaveTheChildren, and the slogan spread through ordinary feeds, including many Christian ones, carried by people who had no idea where it came from. On August 7, 2020, Save the Children issued a statement saying it was not affiliated with any of these campaigns. That July, an anonymous post had suggested that expensive storage cabinets listed on the retailer Wayfair were really children being sold, and the claim went viral. Polaris, the nonprofit that operates the national hotline, said publicly that it had received hundreds of Wayfair reports in a single week, none from anyone with a specific connection to a missing child, and that the volume made it harder to serve people who needed help.

The movement's own advocates are not exempt. For years advocates, officials, and churches repeated the claim that major sporting events, the Super Bowl above all, bring huge surges of sex trafficking. The Global Alliance Against Traffic in Women reviewed the evidence from past events in *What's the Cost of a Rumour?* (2011) and found nothing to show that sporting events cause increases in trafficking for prostitution. Congregations that would never have touched QAnon passed that story along every January, with good intentions and no sources.

Misinformation harms real victims in specific ways. It floods the lines built for them. It teaches parents to fear the stranger at the store and miss the adult grooming a child online, or the employer holding a worker's passport. It discredits careful advocates when the rumors collapse. And it turns trafficked children into props in someone else's political drama, which is a way of using them twice.

Scripture treats this as a moral question. "You shall not spread a false report. You shall not join hands with a wicked man to be a malicious witness" (Exodus 23:1, ESV). A false report becomes ours when we pass it on, and passing it on in defense of children doesn't make it true. (The broader failure of truthfulness in the church, right and left, is the subject of [the essay on truth in a post-truth age](/writing/truth-in-post-truth-world).) Love for the vulnerable includes the discipline of checking before sharing. A rumor about trafficking is not a small sin because it came from a good heart. It is a large one because it came dressed as love.

## The church at its best

The church has something better to offer than panic, and its own history shows what that looks like. William Wilberforce, a young member of Parliament, was converted in 1785. On October 28, 1787, he wrote in his diary: "God Almighty has set before me two great objects, the suppression of the Slave Trade and the Reformation of Manners." Notice the pairing. The man most associated with the end of the British slave trade did not treat personal morality and economic justice as rival agendas. He and the circle later called the Clapham Sect, among them Henry Thornton, Zachary Macaulay, Hannah More, and Granville Sharp, held both for decades.

Their method is instructive. They gathered evidence, much of it collected by Thomas Clarkson at the docks of Bristol and Liverpool. They made the image of God visible: Josiah Wedgwood's medallion of 1787 showed a kneeling, chained African beneath the words "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?" And they asked ordinary households to change what they bought. In 1791, after Parliament defeated Wilberforce's first abolition bill, William Fox's pamphlet urged the British public to stop buying slave-grown sugar, and a great many families did, in one of the first mass consumer campaigns of modern history. Parliament abolished the British slave trade in 1807 and slavery across most of the empire in 1833, in the summer Wilberforce died.

The Clapham circle had its own selectivity. Wilberforce spoke for the Combination Act of 1799, which outlawed workers' associations, and the radical journalist William Cobbett, most pointedly in an open letter in his *Political Register* in 1823, accused him of pleading for enslaved Africans while doing nothing for the laborers of England. The accusation was not entirely fair, and not entirely wrong. Even the church's best witnesses drew the boundary of their outrage near the edge of their own comfort. They were right about what they saw, and they saw more than most.

The modern heir of that tradition is less romantic and more useful. Gary Haugen, a lawyer who had directed the United Nations investigation into the Rwandan genocide in 1994, founded International Justice Mission in 1997, a Christian organization that works with local police, prosecutors, and courts in poor countries to free people from violence, including forced labor and sex trafficking, and to see perpetrators convicted under local law. In *The Locust Effect* (2014), written with the federal prosecutor Victor Boutros, Haugen argued that the global poor lack not only food and medicine and schooling but protection. Where the police can be bought and the courts don't function, everyday violence, enslavement included, devours every other gain the way locusts devour a harvest. The argument moves attention from the rescue scene, which photographs well, to the unglamorous work of making justice systems serve people who have never been able to count on them.

That shift, from the raid to the courtroom, is what the church at its best has done. The Clapham Sect changed the law of an empire and the buying habits of a nation. IJM's stated aim is local systems that go on protecting people after the rescuers leave. Both refuse the fantasy that justice is an event we attend. It's a debt we keep paying.

## Buying without looking away

What does faithfulness ask of an ordinary household? Not paralysis. No one can audit the whole economy, and a conscience that tries to trace every thread will either break or quietly give up. Scripture doesn't ask for omniscience. It asks us to refuse the excuse of arranged ignorance. "Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those who are stumbling to the slaughter" (Proverbs 24:11, ESV). The next verse anticipates our defense before we make it.

"If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this,' does not he who weighs the heart perceive it? Does not he who keeps watch over your soul know it, and will he not repay man according to his work?" (Proverbs 24:12, ESV).

The proverb doesn't condemn the person who couldn't have known. It condemns the person who arranged not to (the difference is taken up in [the essay on individual and systemic sin](/writing/individual-sin-and-systemic-sin-explained)).

In practice that means a few things, done steadily rather than heroically. Learn which goods carry the heaviest documented risk, seafood, cocoa, cotton and garments among them, and let that change some of what you buy, even at a cost. Ask the same question of the institutions you have a voice in, including your church, which buys coffee and chocolate and T-shirts in quantity. Support the slow work of prosecution, survivor housing, and legal aid, not only the most dramatic videos. Learn the ordinary signs: someone who doesn't hold his own identity documents, who owes an impossible debt to an employer, who is never allowed to speak for herself. And when something seems wrong, don't play detective and don't confront anyone. Call the National Human Trafficking Hotline at 1-888-373-7888, or text 233733, and in immediate danger call 911. This is moral counsel, not legal advice; the hotline can connect people to legal help. If you are a survivor reading this, the shame of what was done to you belongs to the people who did it, not to you, and the same hotline exists for you as well.

None of this saves anyone or makes us clean. It is the ordinary obedience of people who have been told the truth about the trade and have decided not to unknow it.

Return, finally, to the bottom of Babylon's manifest, where the human souls are listed after the horses. The Gospels contain one more sale. Judas went to the chief priests and asked what they would give him to hand Jesus over, "And they paid him thirty pieces of silver" (Matthew 26:15, ESV). Matthew later ties the sum to the prophets (Matthew 27:9–10), and behind it stands Zechariah 11, where thirty shekels is the contemptuous wage paid to a rejected shepherd. Behind that stands the law, which set thirty shekels as the compensation owed to a master when an ox gored his slave (Exodus 21:32). The Son of God was sold for the price of a slave's life. He entered the trade in persons as its merchandise, and he went through it to the cross.

That is why Peter can write of a purchase that turns the whole trade inside out. Christians, he says, were ransomed "not with perishable things such as silver or gold" (1 Peter 1:18, ESV), "but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:19, ESV). Every other purchase of a human being takes a life to make a profit. This one spent a life to set the purchased free. A church bought at that price cannot treat anyone as cargo, not the girl in the hotel room and not the man on the fishing boat, not the one it is comfortable to grieve and not the one whose labor is sitting in its own pantry.

Somewhere tonight a person is being held by a debt, a threat, or a lie, and the goods of that captivity are moving toward shelves where people like us will find them unremarkable. God has kept the whole ledger. The sorting was always ours, never his.
