Most of us know the moment. Someone we love is certain about something that is going to hurt them, a decision already made or a story about themselves already settled, and they have come to us not for counsel but for a blessing. The culture has already told us what love requires of us in that moment. To love a person is to confirm them. To hesitate, to question, to say the hard thing, is treated as a small act of violence, and whoever does it gets filed with the people who hurt them before. I've felt the pull of that verdict more often than I'd like to admit. The easy road is paved with the other person's relief and the hard road with their anger, and most of us, most evenings, would rather be thanked than resented.

The question underneath is old, and it deserves a better answer than either side of our culture usually gives it. Is affirmation the same thing as love? This essay argues that it is not, and that the difference matters most exactly where the two look identical. Affirmation wills the comfort of the moment. Love wills the good of the other person. On most days they wear the same face, which is why hardly anyone can tell them apart until years later, when the bill arrives. The progressive church did not invent the confusion. It imported it from the wider culture, baptized it, and began preaching it back to people as grace. But the conservative church has its own counterfeit, older and louder, and an honest argument has to put both on the table before it digs beneath them to the love the church confessed long before either counterfeit existed.

## What the case for affirmation gets right

Start with the strongest form of the case, in words its best defenders would sign. The Christian who says that love means affirmation is rarely saying that anything goes. She is usually saying something closer to this. The church has spent centuries loving people by demolition. It told the depressed they lacked faith, the doubting that they were rebels, the divorced that they were second class, and it spoke about gay and lesbian teenagers with a contempt it never once aimed at the greed in its own pews. People were called worthless and the calling was named preaching. Against all of that, she wants a church whose first word to a wounded person is welcome, and she believes that is the gospel's own order.

She can show her work. The most common complaint the religious leaders brought against Jesus was not that he taught false doctrine but that he kept the wrong company: "This man receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2, ESV). When he passed through Jericho he looked up into a sycamore tree at a collaborator with the occupying power and said, before Zacchaeus had repented of anything, "Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for I must stay at your house today" (Luke 19:5, ESV). The welcome came first. The repentance came afterward, at the table, as its fruit. Paul makes the order explicit when he asks whether we "presume on the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience, not knowing that God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance" (Romans 2:4, ESV). Kindness is the road to change, not its reward.

The psychologists she reads say something similar. Carl Rogers, whose client-centered therapy shaped a whole generation of pastoral counselors, gave the name "unconditional positive regard" to the counselor's steady respect for the person in the chair, a regard that does not rise and fall with the client's performance. In *On Becoming a Person* (1961) Rogers reported, in effect, that people seem able to change only once they stop having to defend themselves. Anyone who has watched a frightened person soften in the presence of someone who is not afraid of them knows he saw something true. It sits closer to grace than much of what the church has offered.

In its sharpest form today, the question presses on debates about sexuality and identity, and there the serious case made by affirming theologians is an argument about what particular texts mean. That argument deserves to be met on its own ground, which this essay does not attempt. The question here is prior, and both sides of that debate have to answer it: what is love, and is it ever allowed to say no?

All of that must be granted, and the conservative reader should sit with it before going on, because the conservative church has too often inverted the gospel's order and demanded change as the price of welcome. But notice what happened at Zacchaeus's table. Nobody in that house thought Jesus had come to confirm Zacchaeus in the tax business. The welcome produced a man standing up in the middle of dinner to say, "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8, ESV). Jesus explained the visit in one line: "For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10, ESV). That is the welcome of a rescuer. It accepts the person completely, and precisely for that reason it cannot be neutral about the road he is on. The error was never in honoring the person. It came later and slowly, when honoring the person hardened into never contradicting the person, and the dignity of the self quietly became the sovereignty of the self. Once the self is sovereign, agreement is the only loving move left.

## How feeling all right replaced being made right

The sociologist Philip Rieff saw that shift coming before most of the church felt it. In *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966) he described a civilization moving its highest good from salvation to well-being, from being made righteous to feeling all right. He predicted that the figure of the priest, who tells you the truth about yourself under a sacred order that makes demands of you, would give way to the figure of the therapist, whose office is to reconcile you to the self you already have. He called the new type "psychological man," a person who asks of every belief and every relationship whether it makes him feel better. Rieff was not writing against therapy, and neither am I. He was warning about what happens when a tool of healing becomes a creed. A culture organized around feeling all right will not argue with the call to repentance. It will simply stop understanding what repentance is for.

Watch what happened to Rogers's insight on its way out of the counseling room. Inside the room, the counselor's regard served a client who had come because he wanted to change. Outside it, regard for the person turned into a rule that the person's own account of himself must never be questioned. Through the 1970s and 1980s the self-esteem movement carried that rule into schools, parenting manuals and a good many pulpits. California set up a state task force on self-esteem in 1986, on the theory that raising it might protect young people against everything from crime to failure in school. When Roy Baumeister and his colleagues reviewed the research in 2003, they found that high self-esteem predicted far less than it had been sold to deliver, and that pumping it up did not reliably produce better performance or better behavior. The creed outlived its evidence, the way creeds do.

Two scholars gave the deeper current its names. In *Habits of the Heart* (1985), Robert Bellah and his colleagues described "expressive individualism," the conviction that each person has a unique core of feeling that must be expressed if he is to be fully himself. Charles Taylor, in his Massey Lectures published as *The Malaise of Modernity* (1991), refused the easy sneer at that ideal. He thought the call to be true to oneself carried real moral force. But he argued that it degrades the moment it is cut off from what he called "horizons of significance," some good outside the self that the self is answerable to. Without such a horizon, the self has no measure but itself, and to question it is to attack it. Inside that frame, affirmation is the only form of love that still makes sense.

The church did not stand outside this current. It swam in it, on both banks. When Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton published the National Study of Youth and Religion as *Soul Searching* (2005), they named the working faith of American teenagers "moralistic therapeutic deism": God wants people to be good and nice, the central goal of life is to be happy and to feel good about oneself, and God is mainly there to help when there is a problem. They found it across traditions, in mainline and Catholic youth groups and in conservative evangelical ones too. Nobody taught it from a pulpit as doctrine. It was simply the air. The progressive church gave that air a theology; the conservative church gave it a worship band and never noticed it was there. A church that has lost the power to say no to the people it loves has not become more loving. It has become more afraid, and learned to call the fear kindness.

## The oldest map of love

The church once had a far more precise account of love than any of this, and it is worth recovering, because it answers the progressive and the conservative counterfeit at the same time.

Augustine, finishing *The City of God* in the 420s, offered what he called a brief and true definition of virtue: the order of love, *ordo amoris* (15.22). The moral life, for Augustine, is not chiefly a question of whether we love but of whether our loves are rightly ordered, God above all and every other good beneath him and loved on his account. Sin, on this map, is seldom the love of something evil. It is usually a real good loved out of its place, a creature asked to carry the weight of the Creator. The self is one of those goods. It is meant to be loved, and loved well, but beneath God and not in his place. Expressive individualism did exactly what Augustine warned against. It took a real good and moved it to the top of the order, where it was never built to stand. A love that affirms a disordered love does not heal it. It blesses the disorder and hands the idol back to the worshiper.

Thomas Aquinas sharpened the definition in the thirteenth century. Following Aristotle, he wrote that to love is to will good to someone (*Summa Theologiae* I-II, q. 26, a. 4). Love therefore has two objects, the person and the good that is willed for the person, and it cannot keep one by giving up the other. This is the distinction the whole question turns on. Affirmation quietly collapses the second object into the first: it wills that the person feel good about where they already are. Feeling good is a real good, but it is one good among many and nowhere near the highest. Love that wills only that good will sooner or later sacrifice every other good the person has, their health, their marriage, their soul, to keep the room warm.

C. S. Lewis pressed the same point from another side in *The Four Loves* (1960). He distinguished the natural loves, affection and friendship and romantic love, from charity, the love that wills the other's good and is willing to be unwelcome in the willing. He borrowed from Denis de Rougemont the warning that a natural love begins to turn demonic at the moment it begins to be treated as a god. Affirmation offered as love is exactly that: natural affection crowned. It wants the warmth of agreement so badly that it will trade away the good of the person to keep it, and it will call the trade tenderness.

Scripture assumes all of this because it assumes something we would rather not believe about ourselves. "There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death" (Proverbs 14:12, ESV). That sentence offends our age in every clause, because it says the inner certainty I am surest of can be the very thing killing me, and that I am not equipped to know the difference from the inside. None of us is a reliable narrator of his own life. The man who has had too much to drink is sure he can drive. The bitter man is sure he is owed. I am sure, most mornings, that my motives are cleaner than they are. A love that only ever ratifies my account of myself has agreed to lie to me in the hour I am most deceived, which is the very hour I most need it not to.

## Watch how Jesus loved

Jesus is the standard, and his way of loving refuses both of our counterfeits.

A wealthy man ran up to him, knelt, and asked what he must do to inherit eternal life. Mark includes a line that none of the other Gospels do: "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me'" (Mark 10:21, ESV). The love and the hard word are one act, in one sentence. Then comes the part we skip: "Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:22, ESV). Jesus let him go. He did not run after him with a softer version. A love that will not let you walk away is control, and a love that will only let you stay if it never challenges you is flattery. Jesus practiced neither.

The story of the woman brought to him in the temple courts belongs here too, with an honest note: the oldest manuscripts of John's Gospel do not contain it, though the church has long received it as true to him. Her accusers wanted a verdict that would destroy her. The crowd today mostly wants a verdict that would leave her alone. Jesus gives neither. "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:11, ESV). The affirming reading keeps the first clause and drops the second. The harsh reading keeps the second and forgets the first. Jesus said them in one breath, and the order matters: the release comes first, and the release is what makes the new life possible.

Then John takes us to a graveside. When Lazarus has been dead four days, Jesus comes to the tomb, and "Jesus wept" (John 11:35, ESV). He does not stand at a clinical distance and counsel the family toward acceptance. He enters the grief and feels its full weight. And then, with the tears still on his face, he does what acceptance would never have done. "Lazarus, come out" (John 11:43, ESV). The compassion did not stop at comfort. It refused to leave the man where everyone had already agreed he now belonged.

That gives us the distinction in its plainest form. Acceptance of a person is not approval of the path he is on. Jesus offered the first without reserve and withheld the second without apology, and he never once treated them as competing loves. Affirmation meets you in the tomb and brings flowers. Love meets you there too, weeps with you, and calls you out.

## The counterfeit on the other side of the room

I have to turn now to my own side of the room, because the conservative church has its own forgery, and in some places it has done more damage than the one I have been describing.

There is a Christianity that confuses truth-telling with cruelty. It wields the hard word the way a man uses a hammer he has come to enjoy, and it takes the wreckage it leaves as proof of its faithfulness, because somebody got hurt and hurt feels like courage. Nothing in this essay is a permit for that. Paul put it beyond appeal: "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1, ESV). Truth without love is not more truthful. It is noise. And in the same chapter Paul writes the sentence that refuses both counterfeits at once: love "does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6, ESV).

The conservative church also affirms, only it chooses different people to affirm. It has affirmed its donors and its famous men. In 2022 an independent investigation by Guidepost Solutions reported how leaders of the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee had handled allegations of sexual abuse for years, with survivors too often treated as threats to the institution rather than as people it owed protection. That is affirmation of the tribe, the refusal to tell the powerful men who are ours the truth they did not want to hear. The same church has often affirmed its nation and its party with a warmth it never offered the stranger. The progressive counterfeit affirms the self. The conservative counterfeit affirms the tribe. Both have made one decision: never to tell a hard truth to the people whose approval we need. The instrument that convicts one convicts the other.

Scripture keeps refusing to let us pull truth and love apart. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend; profuse are the kisses of an enemy" (Proverbs 27:6, ESV). The proverb calls a wound faithful and a kiss suspect, and it locates the difference not in how the moment feels but in what the moment wills. Hebrews says the same of God himself: "For the Lord disciplines the one he loves, and chastises every son whom he receives" (Hebrews 12:6, ESV), and then, with great tenderness about how it feels, "For the moment all discipline seems painful rather than pleasant, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it" (Hebrews 12:11, ESV). The writer is not describing a father who enjoys correcting. He is describing a father who will not abandon his children to their own worst certainties. None of this is a dispute about tone. A church that cannot say no to the people it loves will not find the strength, when the hour comes, to say no to the people it fears.

## Speaking the truth in love

The way forward is not a new balance between two values. It is an older form of love the church once practiced and has nearly forgotten.

For centuries Christians counted among the spiritual works of mercy a short list that ran: instruct the ignorant, counsel the doubtful, admonish the sinner, bear wrongs patiently, forgive offenses willingly, comfort the afflicted, pray for the living and the dead. Notice where admonishing the sinner sits. It is listed as mercy, next to comforting the afflicted and forgiving the one who wronged you. The old church did not think of the hard word as the opposite of compassion. It thought of it as one of compassion's works, and it placed it beside patience and forgiveness so that no one could practice it without them. Paul gives the posture in a single verse: "Brothers, if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness. Keep watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted" (Galatians 6:1, ESV). Restore, not expose. Gentleness, not force. And the one speaking is reminded, in the same breath, that he is no safer than the one he speaks to.

Bonhoeffer wrote *Life Together* (1939) out of the underground seminary he led at Finkenwalde, and it is the best short account I know of how this works among ordinary believers. He argued that nothing is crueler than a leniency that leaves another person in their sin, and nothing more compassionate than a rebuke that calls them back. But before he says a word about rebuke, he describes the ministries that must come first: holding one's tongue, listening, practical help, bearing one another's burdens. The right to speak the hard word is earned by years of the softer ones. A church that wants to recover truth-telling without first recovering listening will produce only more of the cruelty the progressive critique rightly condemned.

John describes the incarnate Son as "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14, ESV). He does not say balanced between them, or alternating between them. Full of both, in one person, at the same time. That is why the famous phrase from Ephesians cannot be split. Paul wants the church, no longer tossed about by every wind, to be "speaking the truth in love," so that "we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ" (Ephesians 4:15, ESV). The goal is maturity, a people growing up. I do not get to keep either word without the other. I cannot be the truth-teller who skips the love; that is the literalist's lie, and it has emptied churches. I cannot be the lover who skips the truth; that is the gentler lie, the one that smiles, and it is mine. I am fluent in warmth, and I have used the fluency to excuse my silence, telling myself I was being gentle when I was being afraid. From the outside the two look identical.

The deepest answer to the question of this essay is the cross. If affirmation were love, if the most loving thing God could do were to confirm us as we are, then the cross would be a gratuitous brutality, a Father giving up his Son for people who needed only to be celebrated. The cross makes sense only if something in us could not be affirmed and could only be redeemed. And yet the cross is also the most complete welcome ever offered: "but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). Not after we changed. While we were still sinners. The progressive instinct is right that the welcome comes first, before any repentance, with no conditions attached. The conservative instinct is right that the welcome is a rescue and not a confirmation. They were never two truths to be balanced. They were one act, performed once, on a hill outside Jerusalem, by a love that saw the whole of us and would neither flatter us nor leave us where we were.

So we are left with the harder love and our reluctance to practice it. Sometime this week someone you love will be certain of something that is going to hurt them, and you will feel the old pull toward the easy yes. The question to carry into that room is not whether you will be kind. It is whose good your kindness is willing. Love that tells the truth may be resented tonight and believed only years from now, and it may never be thanked at all. That love has a shape. It hung on a cross, it knew the worst about every one of us, and it still refused the easy yes that would have left us in the grave.
