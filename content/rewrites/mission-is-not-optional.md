Most of us learned to think about mission as a department. There's a committee that meets on a weeknight, a line in the budget, a table in the lobby with a map and a jar, and one Sunday a year when a visiting couple shows slides from somewhere far away. Mission, in that picture, is something the church has, the way it has a nursery, and it belongs to the people who are into that sort of thing.

People outside the church hear the word differently, and they deserve a hearing. To a skeptic, "mission" can sound like recruitment at best and conquest at worst: a religion that can't leave anyone alone, with a long history of arriving on the same ships as the soldiers. Many doubting Christians hear it as a quiet accusation, one more thing they're failing at. Each picture holds something true. But all three treat mission as something the church decides to do, or fails to do. The older claim is that mission is first something God does. The church doesn't own it and doesn't opt into it. The church exists because of it.

The argument turns on a distinction missiologists have drawn for decades, between *missions*, plural, and *mission*, singular. Missions are the programs, trips and budget lines a particular church runs. Mission is the sending of God himself: the Father sending the Son, the Father and the Son sending the Spirit, and the Spirit sending the church into the world. Any single program may be optional. Mission can't be, because a church that isn't being sent has stopped being the church and become a club with religious vocabulary whose first instinct is to preserve itself.

## Sent the way the Son was sent

Begin in a room with the doors locked. On the evening of the first Easter, John tells us, the disciples had shut themselves in for fear of the authorities who had just crucified their teacher (John 20:19). They weren't strategizing. They were hiding. Jesus came and stood among them, showed them his hands and his side, and said the words that would define them:

> "Jesus said to them again, 'Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you.'" (John 20:21, ESV)

Then he breathed on them and gave them the Holy Spirit (John 20:22). The sending comes after the wounds are shown and the peace is given. It's a commission handed to frightened people, and it takes its shape from the sending of the Son. Jesus had prayed the same thing the night before he died: "As you sent me into the world, so I have sent them into the world" (John 17:18, ESV). The Son was sent into flesh, into rejection and finally into death. The church is sent on the same terms.

For most of the modern era Western Christians spoke differently. William Carey's *Enquiry* (1792) had to argue, against an objection common among his fellow Particular Baptists, that Christ's command to disciple the nations hadn't expired with the apostles, and the century that followed was the great age of Protestant missionary societies. By the World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910, mission meant an enterprise of the Western churches, organized, funded and sent outward from Christendom to everyone else.

Then the confidence broke. Two world wars were fought between the supposedly Christian nations that had been sending the missionaries, and after the Communist victory of 1949 China, the largest mission field on earth, expelled them within a few years. When the International Missionary Council met at Willingen in West Germany in July 1952, the question pressing on it was whether mission had been a Western project all along. The direction the meeting took was that mission had never belonged to the churches in the first place. It belonged to God, and it had its source in the sending life of the Trinity. The German missiologist Karl Hartenstein, writing his report on the meeting, summed up that direction under a Latin phrase he had used before, *missio Dei*, the mission of God. The phrase isn't in the conference's own statements, but it came out of Willingen attached to it, and it stuck.

David Bosch, a South African missiologist, traced the shifting understandings of mission across Christian history in *Transforming Mission* (1991) and described what the phrase came to mean. The classical doctrine of the Trinity, in which the Father sends the Son and the Father and the Son send the Spirit, was extended to include one more movement, the triune God sending the church into the world. On this account mission is not first an activity of the church but an attribute of God, and the church is caught up in a movement already under way. Christopher Wright turned the usual sentence around in *The Mission of God* (2006): it is less that God has a mission for his church than that God has a church for his mission.

The idea carries a danger, and it was realized within a generation. If God is at work everywhere, the church can start to look optional to its own mission. In parts of the ecumenical movement in the 1960s, influenced especially by the Dutch missiologist Johannes Hoekendijk, *missio Dei* was stretched until God seemed to work chiefly through the movements of history, with the church at best one instrument among many. A World Council of Churches study published in 1967, *The Church for Others*, spoke as though the world should set the church's agenda. The Anglican bishop and historian Stephen Neill had already warned where that broadening led, in *Creative Tension* (1959): if everything is mission, nothing is.

So the recovery of *missio Dei* cuts two ways. It rescues mission from being a Western enterprise with a religious budget, and it can dissolve mission into everything good that happens anywhere. The way through is to keep the phrase tied to its source. God's mission has a name and a face. It is the sending of the Son, crucified and raised, and of the Spirit who bears witness to him. A mission that no longer needs to say his name isn't God's mission enlarged. It's something else wearing its clothes.

## The Great Commission read where it stands

The best-known mission text in the Bible is usually quoted as a command to go somewhere else and do something impressive. Read in place, it says something harder and kinder. The scene is a mountain in Galilee, and Matthew records the disciples' response to the risen Christ in a sentence no church marketing department would have allowed:

> "And when they saw him they worshiped him, but some doubted." (Matthew 28:17, ESV)

The commission isn't given to a team of the certain. It's given to worshipers, some of whom were still unsure what they were looking at, and Jesus didn't wait for the doubts to resolve before he sent them. That should matter to anyone who has sat in a pew feeling like a fraud because the questions haven't gone away. He begins with authority, not with the task:

> "And Jesus came and said to them, 'All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me.'" (Matthew 28:18, ESV)

Everything that follows rests on who already holds the world. Then the command:

> "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," (Matthew 28:19, ESV)

In Matthew's Greek the one main verb is "make disciples." Going, baptizing and teaching are participles gathered around it. That point can be pressed too far, since a participle attached to a command shares its force, but the emphasis is real. The goal isn't travel, or a decision recorded on a card. It's disciples, people baptized into the name of the triune God and taught to live as Jesus taught. The last verse ends on the promise that holds the whole Gospel together:

> "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." (Matthew 28:20, ESV)

Matthew opened with a child named Immanuel, God with us (Matthew 1:23), and he closes with the same promise in the mouth of the risen Lord. Christ doesn't send his people away from himself. "All nations" reaches back further still, to the call of Abraham: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3, ESV). The prophets insisted that the Lord's servant would be a light to the nations, not only a restorer of Israel's fortunes (Isaiah 49:6). Paul says Scripture "preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham" (Galatians 3:8, ESV). The Great Commission is the promise to Abraham coming due.

Luke's version sharpens it. Just before the ascension the disciples ask whether Jesus will now restore the kingdom to Israel (Acts 1:6). He declines the timetable and gives them a commission:

> "But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." (Acts 1:8, ESV)

The circles move outward from where they already stand, through Samaria, the neighbors they'd been raised to despise, to everyone else. It's easy to love the end of the earth, which is far away and asks little of us beyond money and prayer. Samaria is harder, because Samaria lives across the street and votes the other way. A church that funds a well on another continent while its members can't name the family three doors down has reversed the arrow. The end of the earth is where the movement arrives, not a substitute for where it starts.

## Word and deed: the argument since Lausanne

If the church is sent, what exactly is it sent to do? For half a century that question has divided Christians who agree on nearly everything else, and both sides deserve a hearing at full strength, because each guards something the other can lose.

The modern argument has a date and a place. In July 1974 several thousand evangelical leaders from well over a hundred countries met in Lausanne, Switzerland, at the International Congress on World Evangelization, convened by Billy Graham. Two Latin American theologians, René Padilla of Ecuador and Samuel Escobar of Peru, told the gathering that North American evangelicalism had exported a gospel shorn of its demands for justice, one that called individuals to Christ while leaving the structures of their poverty untouched and sometimes blessed. Padilla later developed the case in *Mission Between the Times* (1985) as *misión integral*, integral mission: word and deed as one witness to one kingdom.

The Lausanne Covenant, drafted by a committee chaired by the English pastor John Stott, held both concerns together. Its fifth article affirmed that evangelism and social and political involvement are both part of Christian duty, and it expressed penitence for the times evangelicals had treated them as mutually exclusive. Its sixth said that within the church's mission of sacrificial service, evangelism is primary. The next year, in *Christian Mission in the Modern World* (1975), Stott argued that John 20:21 is the most crucial and most challenging form of the Great Commission, because "as the Father has sent me" makes the Son's mission the pattern of ours, and the Son came to serve as well as to preach. In June 1982 a consultation in Grand Rapids, across the state from the church I serve in Fenton, called social action a consequence of evangelism, a bridge to it and a partner alongside it, while keeping evangelism's priority.

Christopher Wright pressed the integral case to the scale of the whole Bible. In *The Mission of God* he argues that God's purpose from Genesis to Revelation is to redeem a people from every nation and renew the whole creation, and he takes the exodus as the model, a redemption at once political, economic, social and spiritual. He would rather call evangelism the ultimate part of mission than the first, since the church doesn't always begin with words but must never stop short of them. His case, in the form he'd sign, is that the God of the Bible never saves only souls, and a church that defines its mission as words alone ends up bearing witness to a smaller God than the one who brought Israel out of Egypt and will raise the dead in their bodies.

Kevin DeYoung and Greg Gilbert answered in a book whose title asks this essay's question, *What Is the Mission of the Church?* (2011), and their case should be heard in the form they'd sign. They don't say Christians may ignore the poor; they insist Christians must love their neighbors and do good to everyone. What they deny is that every good thing a Christian does is the mission of the church. They define it as making disciples by proclaiming the gospel and gathering those disciples into churches. They read John 20:21 with verse 23, where the sending is tied to the forgiveness of sins, and argue that Stott asks the word "as" to carry more than it can. Their deepest worry is practical. When mission stretches to cover every worthy cause, proclamation is the task most likely to drop quietly out, because it costs the most social credit. It is Neill's warning in a new form.

It helps to name the order of the question. Nothing in the creeds settles it, and faithful churches confessing the same Christ land on different sides. It's a second-order question about how to name and rank duties both sides accept: Wright believes in preaching, and DeYoung and Gilbert believe in feeding the hungry. An older distinction, one DeYoung and Gilbert's case leans on, helps here: what the church does as a gathered, organized body, with its preaching, sacraments and elders, is not the same as what its members do scattered through a city's kitchens, offices and hospitals all week. The pulpit must preach Christ crucified, and no food pantry can do that for it. The nurse and the teacher who never preach a sermon are no less sent.

This essay doesn't choose between them, and its argument doesn't need to. What it holds is what both positions already hold. The church is sent as the Son was sent, and no Christian is exempt from the sending. The sending reaches the neighbor and the city as surely as the nations, and it seeks the good of the whole community it's sent into. And it never outgrows the need to name Christ: Wright calls evangelism the ultimate part of mission, and DeYoung and Gilbert make proclaiming the gospel the heart of the mission itself. How to name and rank the church's duties within that is a question on which faithful Christians can disagree and remain brothers and sisters. Each side presses an objection the other hasn't fully answered. Churches that affirm word and deed together in principle tend to drift toward deeds alone in practice, because deeds are admired and proclamation resented, and a slogan about integration is no protection against the drift. Churches that define mission narrowly can drift the other way, into a congregation doctrinally precise and invisible to the poor on its own block, and a careful definition is no protection against that. The failure both books warn against is the same: a church that has stopped being sent at all.

## Seek the welfare of the city

The Old Testament has its own word for the sent life, and it comes from a letter to people who didn't want to be where they were. In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar carried King Jehoiachin, the royal household, the officials and the skilled workers of Jerusalem off to Babylon (Jeremiah 29:1–2). Back home a prophet named Hananiah promised the exile would be over within two years (Jeremiah 28:3). Jeremiah wrote to tell them the opposite.

> "Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce." (Jeremiah 29:5, ESV)

They were to marry, raise children and see those children married, because the stay would last seventy years (Jeremiah 29:10), long enough that most of the adults reading the letter would be buried in Babylonian ground. Then came the sentence that has become the charter for Christian presence in a city that doesn't share its faith:

> "But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare." (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV)

The word behind "welfare" is *shalom*, which is fuller than the English: not only the absence of war but the flourishing of a whole community, just dealing, bread on the table, peace in the streets. The exiles were told to seek the shalom of the empire that had besieged their city and marched them away, and to pray for it. They weren't told to assimilate, and they weren't told to withdraw and wait out the wicked city. They were told to bind their good to the good of their captors' city. Look at the verb. God doesn't say "the city where you've ended up." He says "the city where I have sent you." Even exile was a sending.

The fuller reading of the chapter, and of whether American Christians can honestly call themselves exiles, is in [Are Christians Exiles? Jeremiah 29 and the Welfare of the City](/writing/exile-is-not-the-end). For mission the point is simpler. Most of us didn't choose our towns for missionary reasons. We went where the job was, where the rent was manageable, where family lived. We were sent anyway.

Lesslie Newbigin, a missionary and bishop in India from 1936, came home to England in 1974 and found it harder ground than the one he'd left. In *Foolishness to the Greeks* (1986) he told the Western church to see its own neighborhood with a missionary's eyes. What that means for ordinary conversation is taken up in [How to Talk About Your Faith Without Being Weird or Pushy](/writing/how-to-talk-about-faith).

I have to stand inside this. It's possible to know more about unreached peoples across the world than about the people in the shadow of your own steeple, and I've been that pastor. Distant mission lets us love in the abstract, and that isn't worthless, but it can insulate us from neighbors who are complicated and may not want what we're offering. A church that parachutes into a neighborhood it doesn't live in, carrying solutions devised by people who've never lived there, can do real harm, and some congregations keep to their own lane out of humility rather than indifference. That's true, and it isn't enough. Jeremiah didn't ask the exiles to fix Babylon. He asked them to live there as though its good were theirs, which begins with knowing a place the way you know a person, slowly, by staying, and by learning names before offering answers.

## The church that is mostly somewhere else

Many of us point a second arrow the wrong way. We picture the global church as somewhere else, the subject of a slideshow and a line in the giving, with ourselves as the senders and them as the sent-to.

That picture is more than half a century out of date. The historian Andrew Walls showed in *The Missionary Movement in Christian History* (1996) that Christianity's heartland has always moved, and in the twentieth century it moved decisively south. Philip Jenkins documented the shift in *The Next Christendom* (2002). By the century's end most of the world's Christians lived in Africa, Latin America and Asia, and Jenkins suggested that anyone wanting to picture a typical Christian today should think of a woman in a Nigerian village or a Brazilian favela, not a suburban American. Lamin Sanneh, the Gambian-born scholar who taught at Yale, explained in *Translating the Message* (1989) why the faith outlived the empires that often carried it: once Scripture is in a people's own language, the gospel is theirs. The harder history of ships, flags and what was done in Christ's name is told in [Were Christian Missions Just Colonialism? An Honest History](/writing/colonialism-and-missions).

This bears on the skeptic's charge. Hearing "mission" as the West imposing its religion describes a real past, but the present has moved out from under it. The fastest-growing churches are led by Africans, Asians and Latin Americans, increasingly sending missionaries of their own, Europe and North America included.

The New Testament won't let us keep the global church at a distance anyway. Paul tells the Corinthians they are one body and draws the consequence:

> "If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together." (1 Corinthians 12:26, ESV)

The body of Christ has no nationality. The believer in a house church where gathering is illegal is a sister, nearer kin in the only family that lasts than many people on our own street. Hebrews presses further: "Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body" (Hebrews 13:3, ESV). The command isn't only to send help. It's to locate ourselves inside their situation, because the body in chains is the body we belong to.

Paul made that unity concrete in the collection he spent years organizing, gifts from the Gentile churches of Greece and Asia Minor for poor believers in Jerusalem (Romans 15:26; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4; 2 Corinthians 8–9). It was proof that the wall between Jew and Gentile had come down (Ephesians 2:14), and Paul refused to describe it as charity flowing one way. The Corinthians' abundance would supply Jerusalem's need, he wrote, "so that their abundance may supply your need" (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV). The churches of the global South are often poor in money and rich in what the Western church has run short of: a theology of suffering that doesn't collapse when prayers go unanswered, a clarity about the cost of discipleship that comes from paying it. I've prayed for the global church the way a person prays for a cause rather than a family, briefly and at a distance, without quite believing their joys were mine or their suffering my own. That's a failure of imagination before it's a failure of generosity. When we treat the global church as the recipient of our resources while staying closed to what it can give us, we cut ourselves off from part of our own inheritance.

## Mission at the size of a household

If mission belongs to God and the whole church is sent, then the mission field most Christians will ever have isn't reached by plane. It's the street, the workplace and the table. That doesn't shrink mission. It means no Christian is exempt and no ordinary week is too small to count.

Start with the neighbors: the widow two doors down who hasn't had a real conversation since the funeral, the family that moved in last spring and still knows no one, the man home from prison who can't get hired because of his record. Loving them begins with learning names, staying long enough to be trusted, and noticing who hasn't been seen lately. Scripture's concern for the people no one speaks for, and why so few of them sit in our pews, is traced in [What Does the Bible Say About Widows, Orphans and Strangers?](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger).

Then the work. Paul told the young church in Thessalonica, a small community under suspicion, what its witness should look like:

> "and to aspire to live quietly, and to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands, as we instructed you," (1 Thessalonians 4:11, ESV)

> "so that you may walk properly before outsiders and be dependent on no one." (1 Thessalonians 4:12, ESV)

Paul assumed the outsiders were watching, and that honest labor, paid debts and a quiet life were part of what they'd see. The nurse who treats the difficult patient as carefully as the grateful one isn't waiting for the real mission to start after work. The longer case for work as calling is in [What Is Vocation? A Christian View of Work and Calling](/writing/what-is-vocation-work-as-calling).

Then the table. The New Testament word for hospitality, *philoxenia*, means love of the stranger, not the entertaining of friends. Paul told the Romans to "seek to show hospitality" (Romans 12:13, ESV), and Hebrews puts a command and a wonder in one sentence: "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" (Hebrews 13:2, ESV). A household that opens its table to people who aren't already its friends is obeying a command given to every believer, not to a committee, and a lonely culture notices. It takes an extra chair and a willingness to be interrupted, not a large house.

None of this lasts on effort alone. Mission cut off from prayer and Scripture turns into activism, and activism without roots burns out or becomes one more good cause. Jesus gave the image on the night he was betrayed:

> "Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in me." (John 15:4, ESV)

The branch doesn't bear fruit by straining but by staying attached, which is why formation and mission aren't rivals. Luke shows them as one movement in the first Christian community: "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42, ESV). A few verses on, "the Lord added to their number day by day those who were being saved" (Acts 2:47, ESV). A church that has to choose between forming its people and sending them has misunderstood both.

Yet the drift runs almost always inward. The people already inside give, serve and tell us what they need, and a church that wants to keep them happy keeps aiming at them. None of it is wicked, but a congregation organized entirely around the people already in the room has reversed its reason for existing, and the numbers can look healthy while it happens. I've pastored toward that drift without meaning to. "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake and the gospel's will save it" (Mark 8:35, ESV). Jesus said it to individuals. It fits congregations too. A church can hoard its life and die with the lights still on.

So go back to the room where it started. The doors were locked, and the reason was fear. Nobody inside had a strategy, and even later, on the mountain in Galilee, some still doubted. Jesus didn't wait for them to open the door. He stood among them anyway and showed them his wounds before he said a word about sending. Whatever mission is, it begins there: not with our competence or courage but with a crucified man who is alive, who had every reason to be finished with friends who ran from him, and who breathed on them instead and sent them out carrying the peace he'd just given them. That breath hasn't been taken back. Most of us keep our doors shut for smaller fears than theirs: awkwardness, busyness, the dread of seeming strange, the comfort of a life arranged around people who already agree with us. Locked doors didn't stop him then. Whether the people behind them will let themselves be sent to the street outside is the question every household carries out of church on Sunday and into Monday, and it isn't answered once.
