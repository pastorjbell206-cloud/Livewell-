A great many people who take apart the faith they were raised in don't end up with nothing. That's the part the warnings in evangelical churches tend to miss. They picture deconstruction as a slope with atheism at the bottom, and some people do slide all the way down. But a larger number stop partway, catch their breath, and rebuild. They find a church with a different sign out front, or a podcast and a reading list and a few friends who left the same way, and they call what they've built progressive Christianity. They'll tell you, and they mean it, that they didn't lose their faith. They reconstructed it.

I want to take that claim seriously, because it deserves it. The people who make it are usually not trying to leave Jesus. They're trying to keep him from the people who hurt them in his name. I also want to ask the question it raises, which is a harder one than either the alarmed pastor or the relieved podcaster tends to ask. When a faith is taken apart and rebuilt, what does the rebuilding stand on? Everyone who deconstructs ends up reconstructing something, because nobody lives in a field. The real question is what the new house is built from: from what was received, or from what the builder already believed was good.

That distinction is the whole of this essay. A faith can be rebuilt around an ethic or around a confession. The first keeps the moral passions of the Christian story (love, welcome, justice for the poor) and treats the doctrines that once held them up as scaffolding that can come down once the building stands. The second keeps the passions too, but insists they were never free-standing; they rest on things that happened, a God who acts, a Christ who was raised, a gospel that was handed down before any of us arrived. The church has tried the first kind of reconstruction before, on a large scale, for about a century and a half, and we have its record. (For the broader story of what deconstruction is and where the word came from, see [What Is Faith Deconstruction, and What Does Exvangelical Mean?](/writing/deconstruction-is-not-destruction). This essay asks the narrower question of where so much of it lands.)

## What the progressive Christian is right about

Start with the case in the form its best defenders would sign, because the version worth answering is the strong one.

The progressive Christian says something like this. I was handed a faith that was fused to one political party, that treated questions as threats and women as a problem to be managed, that covered for powerful men and interrogated their victims. I read the Gospels and found a Jesus who ate with the people my church kept at the door, who saved his hardest words for the religious leaders, who told a parable in which the hero was the despised foreigner. I found prophets who cared more about the widow and the day laborer than about anyone's worship style. When I took the faith apart, I wasn't rejecting that Jesus. I was rescuing him. I let go of what I couldn't honestly believe (the certainty, the fear, the political captivity, some of the doctrine) so that I could keep what I could. And unlike many of my friends, I stayed. I still pray. I still go to church. I still call myself a Christian, and I've paid for that on both sides.

Much of that is true, and a pastor who can't say so has disqualified himself from answering it. The wound was real. The churches that produced it are real, and no pastor gets to assume his own is not among them. Anyone who pastors in America has preached, at some point, a faith that bundled the gospel with a nation, a party, or a comfortable middle-class life and called the bundle Christianity. When the bundle failed people, some of them concluded that Christ had failed. That's on us before it's on them. The progressive Christian is also right that the Gospels are full of the poor, that the church has often been on the wrong side of the powerless, and that certainty is not the same thing as faith.

The most honest voice of that generation made the case with more grace than her critics usually managed. Rachel Held Evans, who grew up in evangelical Tennessee and spent years wrestling in public with the faith she was given, published *Searching for Sunday* in 2015, four years before her death at thirty-seven. It is a book about leaving church and coming back, and what's striking about it is where she came back to. She organized it around the sacraments (baptism, confession, communion and the rest) and found her way home through an Episcopal congregation, through liturgy, bread and water, words older than anyone in the room. Whatever one makes of her conclusions on contested questions, the shape of her return points somewhere. The deepest progressive testimonies are often testimonies of being found by something old.

So the question isn't whether progressive Christians are sincere, or whether they were wronged. It's whether a faith rebuilt around its ethic, with its confession treated as negotiable, can hold. That's a question history can help answer, because it has been asked before.

## The first time the church tried it

The progressive church of our moment is not new. It is the second generation of an experiment that began in Berlin at the end of the eighteenth century.

In 1799 a young Reformed preacher named Friedrich Schleiermacher published *On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers*. His audience was his own circle, the brilliant young Romantics of Berlin who found the church's doctrines unbelievable and its sermons dull. Schleiermacher didn't argue with them about doctrine. He tried to go under it. Religion, he told them, isn't at its core a set of propositions or a moral code; it is a sense and a feeling for the infinite, and the doctrines are secondary attempts to put that feeling into words. It was a brilliant apologetic move and a sincere one. He wanted to win people the church was losing. But it moved the center of the faith from what God had said and done to what the religious person feels, and everything that came after him had to decide whether the doctrines were the house or only the paint.

A century later the most learned church historian in Europe gave the answer that would govern liberal Protestantism for a generation. In the winter of 1899 to 1900, Adolf von Harnack lectured to hundreds of students at the University of Berlin, and the lectures appeared in 1900 as *What Is Christianity?* His image was the kernel and the husk. Christianity, he said, had come wrapped in the husk of its first-century world: miracles, apocalyptic expectation, Greek metaphysics, the creeds of the councils. Peel the husk away and you find the kernel, which he summarized as the fatherhood of God, the infinite value of the human soul, and the commandment of love. It sounds generous. It sounds like the faith with the embarrassing parts removed.

Then two scholars noticed something about the kernel. In 1906 Albert Schweitzer published the book known in English as *The Quest of the Historical Jesus*, a survey of every major liberal life of Jesus written over the previous century. His verdict was devastating and simple: each scholar had peeled away the husk and found, underneath, a Jesus who shared the scholar's own ideals. The Jesus Schweitzer found in the sources was stranger than any of them, an apocalyptic prophet who did not fit the nineteenth century at all. Three years later the Catholic modernist George Tyrrell, himself no conservative, put the point in an image that has never been bettered. In *Christianity at the Crossroads* (1909) he wrote, in effect, that Harnack had looked down the long well of Christian history and seen at the bottom the reflection of a liberal Protestant face.

That is the mechanism, and it's worth stating plainly because it is still running. When you strip a faith down to what you find believable and good, what remains will be what you already found believable and good. The kernel is always the peeler's own face. This doesn't mean the kernel is false; the fatherhood of God and the commandment of love are gloriously true. It means that a faith reconstructed this way can't correct you, because everything in it that might have corrected you was classified as husk.

The American version of the argument came to a head on May 21, 1922, when Harry Emerson Fosdick, a Baptist preaching in the pulpit of First Presbyterian Church in New York, delivered a sermon called "Shall the Fundamentalists Win?" Fosdick's case deserves its due. He pleaded for room in the church for people who could not affirm the virgin birth, the inerrancy of Scripture, or a literal second coming, and he asked whether the fundamentalists meant to drive them out. He was a generous man trying to keep thoughtful young people in the church, and on the question of tolerance he had a point.

The following year J. Gresham Machen, a New Testament scholar at Princeton, answered with *Christianity and Liberalism* (1923), and his answer was not a plea for more toleration or less. His claim was that the argument wasn't between two kinds of Christianity at all. Liberalism, he argued, had become a different religion that used the same vocabulary: a faith in human goodness and moral progress in which Jesus was the great example rather than the crucified and risen Savior, and in which the gospel's news of what God had done was replaced by an exhortation about what we should do. You could disagree with Machen about many things, and many did. But the question he put to Fosdick is the one this essay puts to the progressive Christian: when the confession is treated as husk, is what remains still the same faith, or a different one wearing its clothes?

## A house swept and put in order

Jesus once told a short, strange story about a house. An unclean spirit leaves a man and wanders through dry places looking for rest. Finding none, it returns, and "when it comes, it finds the house empty, swept, and put in order" (Matthew 12:44, ESV). So it goes and brings seven other spirits worse than itself, and the last state of the man is worse than the first. The story is about the generation that watched Jesus and would not receive him, not about modern theology. But its shape is exactly the shape of the problem. A house cleaned of what was wrong and left empty does not stay empty. Something moves in, and it rarely announces itself as worse.

What moved into the liberal Protestant house through the twentieth century was not wickedness in any cartoon sense. It was the moral consensus of the educated class the churches most wanted to keep. When that consensus was patriotic, the mainline was patriotic; when it was confident in science and progress, the pulpits preached progress; when it turned toward therapy, the sermons turned therapeutic. In 1963 John A. T. Robinson, the Anglican bishop of Woolwich, published *Honest to God*, which told a huge readership that the image of God "up there" or "out there" had to go and drew on Paul Tillich's language of God as the ground of our being. Three years later, on April 8, 1966, *Time* put three words on a black cover: "Is God Dead?" The mainline denominations, whose membership had peaked in the middle of the 1960s, began a decline that has continued in every decade since.

In 1972 Dean Kelley, a staff member of the National Council of Churches and no fundamentalist, published *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing*. His argument was sociological rather than doctrinal, and it stung because it came from inside. Churches that ask little, he found, give people little reason to stay; churches that make serious claims about the meaning of life and ask serious things of their members hold them. A faith that has handed its hard edges back to the culture has nothing left that the culture doesn't already offer, usually with better parking.

By 1998 the reconstruction had reached its logical end. John Shelby Spong, the Episcopal bishop of Newark, published *Why Christianity Must Change or Die* and, the same year, twelve theses that he framed as the call for a new reformation. Spong argued that theism as a way of speaking about God was finished, that the virgin birth and the physical resurrection could not be believed, and that the idea of the cross as an atonement for sin should be dismissed. He was sincere, and he believed he was saving the faith for thinking people, exactly as Schleiermacher had two centuries before. But at the end of that road there was very little left to save. The house had been swept all the way to the studs, and what occupied it was the bishop's own humane and educated conscience. It was a decent tenant. It was not the risen Lord.

Here the conservative reader will want to nod, and needs to stop. The evangelical wing of American Protestantism ran its own deconstruction, and it began earlier. The historian Nathan Hatch showed in *The Democratization of American Christianity* (1989) how the revivalists and frontier preachers of the early republic taught Americans to distrust creeds, clergy and tradition, to read the Bible by themselves and trust their own experience of conversion; "no creed but the Bible" became a proud slogan. They swept out the councils, the catechisms and the liturgy as surely as Harnack swept out the miracles, and they filled the house with a different tenant: the moral and political consensus of their own communities. The young woman who leaves an evangelical church today and finds a progressive one has usually never been told that the faith is older than 1950. We handed her a house with nothing in it older than her grandparents, and then we were shocked when she redecorated.

## Same jewels, a different portrait

Around the year 180, a bishop in Gaul named Irenaeus wrote a long work against the teachers who were reshaping the faith in his day, and in the first book of *Against Heresies* he gave an image that fits our situation with uncomfortable precision. Imagine, he said, that a skilled artist has made a beautiful portrait of a king out of precious stones. Someone comes along, pries the stones loose, and rearranges them into the likeness of a dog or a fox, and then tells simple people that this is the king, because look, the jewels are all genuine. Irenaeus's point was that his opponents used real words from Scripture. Every stone was real. The portrait was not.

That image cuts in every direction, and it has to, or it's just a weapon. The progressive reconstruction keeps real jewels. Love of neighbor is a jewel. Welcome to the outsider is a jewel. Justice for the poor is a jewel from the prophets' own crown. But when the stones are arranged by the reconstructor's sense of what matters, the face that appears tends to be a face we already knew: a Jesus who agrees with the sensibilities of a thoughtful, compassionate, educated modern person, who confirms what our circle already believes and challenges mainly the people outside it. The conservative reconstruction does the same thing with a different set of stones. It takes real jewels (the authority of Scripture, the sanctity of life, the goodness of family and nation) and arranges them into a face that looks remarkably like a patriotic, prosperous, middle-class American. Both portraits use genuine stones. Both look suspiciously like the people who assembled them. Tyrrell's well has two sides.

The test Irenaeus offered was not whether the stones were real but who drew the original portrait. The church had a shape of the faith handed down from the apostles, which he called the rule of truth, and it was the pattern that told you how the stones went together. Paul had given the same pattern in its earliest form a century and more before: "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3, ESV), that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day. Notice the verbs. Paul received it and delivered it. He didn't reconstruct it. The gospel arrives as something handed to us, and its first-order claims are not scaffolding around an ethic; they are the ethic's ground. "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). Paul didn't treat the resurrection as a husk around the commandment of love. He treated it as the reason love is not a beautiful doomed gesture.

This is the precise place where a reconstruction around an ethic breaks. Take the resurrection out and the commandment of love is still true, but it becomes advice, and advice is only as strong as the one giving it. A crucified teacher who stayed dead can inspire you. He can't forgive you, and he can't stop you from reshaping him into your own image, because he isn't there to object. The confession is what keeps the portrait from becoming a mirror.

## Found in the house of the Lord

Reconstruction in Scripture has a particular shape, and it is not the shape of a builder working from his own plans.

In the eighteenth year of King Josiah, around 622 BC, workmen were repairing the temple in Jerusalem, which had been neglected and defiled through generations of kings who worshiped whatever the surrounding nations worshiped. In the middle of the renovation, the high priest came out with something in his hands. "I have found the Book of the Law in the house of the LORD" (2 Kings 22:8, ESV). When it was read to the king, he tore his clothes. The reform that followed, the most thorough in Judah's history, was not Josiah's program for a better religion. It was what happened when a king let an old book, found under the rubble of his own house, tell him what to tear down and what to rebuild. The repairs uncovered something the repairers hadn't put there.

That's the difference between reconstruction and redecoration. Redecoration begins with our taste and asks which parts of the old faith still suit it. Reconstruction begins with what was found and lets it rearrange our taste. Jeremiah, who began to prophesy as a young man in those same years of Josiah's reign, put it in a single sentence: "Thus says the LORD: 'Stand by the roads, and look, and ask for the ancient paths, where the good way is; and walk in it, and find rest for your souls'" (Jeremiah 6:16, ESV). The next line in the verse is the sad one, and it's the one every generation is tempted to say, progressive and conservative alike: "But they said, 'We will not walk in it.'" The ancient path is not the road of the 1950s or of the 1990s. It's older than every American version of the faith, and it will offend some part of every one of them.

What would it mean, concretely, for someone who has taken their faith apart to rebuild on what was received rather than on what they already believe? It would mean saying the Apostles' Creed or the Nicene Creed aloud, slowly, and letting its claims stand as claims rather than poetry: that God made the world, that the Son took flesh and suffered under a named Roman governor, that he rose, that he will judge the living and the dead. It would mean reading the whole Bible, including the parts that trouble the progressive conscience and the parts that trouble the conservative one, and letting both kinds of trouble stand until you understand them. It would mean finding a church where the Lord's Table is set and the confession of sin is said, where the faith is older than the congregation. And it would mean keeping every true thing the deconstruction taught you: the care for the victim, the suspicion of power dressed as piety, the refusal to let Jesus be conscripted by a party. None of that has to be surrendered. It has to be re-rooted, so that it grows from the gospel instead of standing in for it.

## The face at the bottom of the well

I came to faith from atheism, which means I did my demolition from the outside. I had taken the whole thing apart before I ever walked in. What stands at the center of the faith the apostles handed down is not what anyone would design. A religion constructed out of our own ideals would not put a cross at the center of it, or a resurrection, or a judgment, or a grace that asks for everything. That it does not look like us is one of the better reasons to think we did not make it up.

That's the test I'd offer anyone standing in the dust of a faith they've taken apart, and I have to offer it to myself first, because pastors are among the most skilled portrait-rearrangers alive. Look at the Jesus you've rebuilt. Does he ever disagree with you? Does he ever tell your side something it doesn't want to hear? Does he have any claims on you that your circle would find embarrassing? If the answer is no, you haven't found the Jesus of the Gospels. You've found the bottom of Tyrrell's well, and the face there is yours. The conservative who has never been rebuked by his Christ has made the same discovery with different lighting.

The Christ the apostles delivered is not so tame. He will side with you against the people who wounded you in his name, and he will also, in the same breath, tell you to forgive them. He will confirm your hunger for justice and then tell you that you, too, need mercy. He will not let you keep him as the patron of your best self, because he died for your worst one. That's not a portrait anyone would draw of themselves. It's the reason it can be trusted.

Keep tearing down what is false. There is a great deal of it, and much of it was built by people like me. But decide, while the hammer is still in your hand, what you mean to build on. There is one wall in this house that has held every weight put on it for twenty centuries: a man crucified under Pontius Pilate who did not stay dead. Every generation has tried to reshape him, and every generation's portrait has faded. He keeps walking out of the frame. The question left for the one standing in the rubble, and for the one who never left the pew, is the same: whether you want a faith you built, or the Lord who was raised, and who is not going to look like you.
