Most of us can recite the American story about school. Education is the ladder out of poverty, the ladder stands in every neighborhood, and anyone willing to climb can climb. Some of that is true. Public schooling has carried millions of children past the circumstances they were born into. But the story holds only if we agree not to look too closely at the ladders themselves: who pays for them, and why the rungs sit so much closer together in one ZIP code than in the one a few miles down the road.

Those of us who can afford to choose do look closely, at least at our own. We read the school ratings before we read the inspection report on the house. We know which district line adds to a home's price, and we pay it, because love for our own children is real and a parent who ignores it fails a duty Scripture gives him. The trouble isn't that we care about our children's schools. The trouble is what all that caring does in aggregate, across a county and across a century, when every household acts sensibly inside an arrangement none of us would have designed and then calls the result merit.

The church has mostly filed this under policy and left it there. The filing is wrong, though not because the church has a policy to hand down. It has something older and harder to dodge. For most of its history the church was the institution that taught poor children to read, and for a stretch of its American history some of its congregations were the institution that kept Black children out of white classrooms. Both records are ours. And beneath every school question Christians argue about runs a distinction that decides how we answer them: whether children are the future, an investment a nation makes for returns it expects later, or neighbors now, persons already standing among us whom Christ has set in the middle of the room.

## What Scripture Says About Children and Teaching

The central text of Israel's faith is a text about teaching. Moses gives the Shema, the confession that the LORD is one and must be loved with all the heart, soul and might, and then turns at once to the question of how it will outlive the people who first heard it: "And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart" (Deuteronomy 6:6, ESV). The next sentence moves the words from the heart into the house: "You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:7, ESV). Read in its setting, this is covenant law rather than advice to devout families, spoken to a whole people on the edge of a land full of cities they didn't build and cisterns they didn't dig (Deuteronomy 6:10–11), and it assumes that forgetting is the default. Parents stand first in line for the work, but not alone. Later in the same book, the law is to be read aloud every seventh year to the whole assembly, and Moses names the little ones and the sojourner in the towns among those who must hear it (Deuteronomy 31:12). Teaching the young belonged to the household and the people together, and it reached the outsider's children too.

Proverbs is the book of that teaching, and its best-known verse is usually read as a promise: "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6, ESV). Scholars still argue whether "the way he should go" means the path suited to this child or the one road of wisdom, and Proverbs deals in observations about how life tends to go rather than guarantees. Either way the verse assumes someone is doing the training, for years. It also sits four verses below a sentence that should keep any reader from treating formation as a private asset: "The rich and the poor meet together; the LORD is the Maker of them all" (Proverbs 22:2, ESV). The book that tells parents to train their children also tells them that the poor man's child and the rich man's child have the same Maker.

The Gospels go further, and they go there by way of an argument among grown men. On the road to Capernaum the disciples had been debating which of them was greatest, and Jesus answered with a gesture before he answered with a word: "And he took a child and put him in the midst of them, and taking him in his arms, he said to them," (Mark 9:36, ESV). Then the word itself: "Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me, and whoever receives me, receives not me but him who sent me" (Mark 9:37, ESV). O. M. Bakke's study of childhood in the early church, *When Children Became People* (2005), shows how startling this was in a Greco-Roman world where a child was a person under construction, legally owned by the father, and where exposing an unwanted newborn was lawful and common. Bakke traces how early Christian writers refused exposure and abortion and came to speak of children as full members of the household of faith. The church slowly learned to see what Jesus had done.

Matthew tells the same scene and carries it further. There the disciples ask outright who is greatest in the kingdom, and with the child still standing among them Jesus gives the most severe warning in the Gospels: "but whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea" (Matthew 18:6, ESV). The verb behind "causes to sin" means to trip, to set a snare or an obstacle in someone's path. Notice who is being warned. Not the Romans or the Pharisees. The disciples. The people most likely to hinder children, in Matthew's telling, are the ones closest to Jesus, busy with the important business of the kingdom and annoyed at the interruption. A chapter later they prove it. When parents bring their children for a blessing and the disciples rebuke them, Jesus overrules his own men: "but Jesus said, 'Let the little children come to me and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven'" (Matthew 19:14, ESV).

This is where the distinction does its work. Much American talk about schools treats children as the future. The 1983 federal report *A Nation at Risk* framed the weakness of American schooling as a threat to the country's economic standing, and politicians of both parties have talked that way since: children as tomorrow's workforce, education as an investment with returns. Scripture does think in generations. The psalmist resolves not to hide God's deeds from the children but to "tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD" (Psalm 78:4, ESV). But the biblical generational frame is covenant faithfulness, handing on what was received, and it never turns the child into a means. The future frame, left to itself, does exactly that. It values the child for what she will become and lets us postpone what she is owed until the payoff looks worth it. The child Jesus took in his arms was a neighbor, whatever he would grow up to be, and he was owed welcome that afternoon.

Augustine remembered the other side of that frame. In the first book of his *Confessions* (written around 400) he recalls praying as a small boy not to be beaten at school while the adults, his parents among them, laughed at the beatings he dreaded. Adults find it easy to call a child's present suffering a phase on the way to something else. Jesus didn't.

## How the Church Built the Schoolhouse

The history of Western schooling is, for long stretches, the history of the church teaching children nobody else would teach. The Rule of Benedict, written in the sixth century, built reading into the daily life of the monastery and handed each monk a book to read through during Lent, and monasteries became the places where literacy survived the collapse of Roman civic education. In 789 Charlemagne's *Admonitio generalis* directed that monasteries and cathedrals keep schools where boys could learn to read, along with the psalms, chant and the reckoning of the church calendar. These schools served the church's own needs first, clergy who could read the liturgy and copy the Scriptures, and it would be dishonest to dress them up as universal education. But they carried a conviction the church never fully lost: a people formed by a book must be taught to read it.

The Reformation turned that conviction outward. In 1524 Martin Luther wrote an open letter to the councilmen of the German cities urging them to found and fund Christian schools. His argument was blunt and civic as much as it was spiritual. A city will spend freely on walls, roads, bridges and weapons, he said, and ought to spend far more on its young, because a city's real strength lies less in its walls and buildings than in citizens who are able, learned, wise and well brought up. Parents, he admitted, often lacked the time, the ability or the will to teach their children themselves, and so the community had to take up the charge. And he wanted schools for girls as well as boys, which in the Europe of 1524 was not a small thing. His schools were meant to form evangelical Christians, but the principle outlived its purpose. Where the Reformation went, schools and literacy tended to follow, because a faith that puts the Bible in every household needs every household able to read it.

In 1780 a newspaper publisher in Gloucester, England, named Robert Raikes began paying women to teach the children of the local poor on Sundays. Many of those children worked in factories the other six days, and Sunday was the only day they could be reached. The first Sunday schools were not the age-graded Bible classes most American Christians picture now. They were literacy schools for working children, teaching reading from the Bible because the Bible was the book at hand and the one Raikes most wanted them to read. The movement spread quickly across Britain and into America. It met the child in front of it and changed the shape of a community at the same time.

The American chapter of this history belongs above all to Black Christians. Before the Civil War most slaveholding states had made it a crime to teach an enslaved person to read, and the laws said plainly what the lawmakers feared. Frederick Douglass wrote in his *Narrative* (1845) of the day his master forbade his lessons on the ground that learning would spoil a slave and leave him unfit for slavery, and of realizing in that moment that reading was the road out. When emancipation came, the freedpeople acted on what Douglass had learned. James D. Anderson's *The Education of Blacks in the South, 1860-1935* (1988) argues that former slaves were the first Southerners to campaign for universal, state-supported public schooling, and that they built and paid for schools of their own before any government helped them. Black churches were often the first schoolhouses. The Freedmen's Bureau, founded in 1865, and Northern missionary societies like the American Missionary Association supplied teachers and funds, but the buildings, the students and much of the will came from Black congregations. Morehouse College began in 1867 in the basement of Springfield Baptist Church in Augusta, Georgia; Spelman began in 1881 in the basement of Friendship Baptist Church in Atlanta. A people who had been forbidden the alphabet under threat of the whip met in church basements to learn it, and they read the Scriptures that had been read at them for two centuries.

That record deserves to be told whole. The common schools Horace Mann championed after becoming secretary of the Massachusetts board of education in 1837 were Protestant in character, with the King James Bible read daily, and Catholic immigrants experienced them as institutions built to make their children Protestant. Charles Glenn's *The Myth of the Common School* (1988) traces how the common school was conceived partly as a project of forming a unified national character. Catholics answered by building their own, and at the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore in 1884 the bishops directed every parish to have a school. Some of the church's schoolhouses were also instruments of one group of Christians forming another group's children in its own image. That tension never went away, and it still sits beneath the school-choice debate.

## How the Church Also Built the Wall

On May 17, 1954, a unanimous Supreme Court ruled in *Brown v. Board of Education* that segregated public schools were inherently unequal and violated the Fourteenth Amendment. The next year the Court told the South to desegregate "with all deliberate speed," and much of the South heard the deliberate and ignored the speed. Some resisted in the open. Prince Edward County, Virginia, closed its entire public school system in 1959 rather than integrate it, and kept it closed for five years while white children attended a private academy and many Black children went without formal schooling at all. Elsewhere the resistance was quieter. When the courts finally lost patience at the end of the 1960s and ordered desegregation without further delay, private schools opened across the South almost overnight. Historians call them segregation academies.

This is where honesty costs something. Many of those academies were church-affiliated. They met in church buildings, were sponsored by congregations or took a church's name, and others described themselves as Christian schools without any single church behind them. Some white congregations supplied the land, the classrooms and the respectability. Kevin Kruse's *White Flight* (2005), a study of Atlanta, shows how resistance to integration moved from the language of open segregation to the language of private choice and freedom of association, as white families withdrew from public parks, pools, neighborhoods and schools they no longer wished to share. The courts eventually intervened. In *Green v. Connally* (1971) a federal court barred the IRS from granting tax exemptions to racially discriminatory private schools in Mississippi, and in *Bob Jones University v. United States* (1983) the Supreme Court ruled eight to one that a religious school practicing racial discrimination could lose its exemption.

Fairness requires two qualifications, and both are true. Not every Christian school founded in those years was a segregation academy. Many were started for reasons that had nothing to do with race: the Supreme Court's school-prayer decisions of 1962 and 1963, disputes over curriculum, and a conviction, which Scripture supports, that parents answer to God for how their children are formed. Many Christian schools today enroll children of every race, and many Black families in American cities have sought out school-choice programs for their children. The second qualification cuts the other way. The racial division of American schooling was never only a Southern project or only a church project. Northern cities produced it through housing, and they did it without academies.

The mechanism there was the map. Beginning in the 1930s, federal mortgage underwriting marked Black and immigrant neighborhoods as hazardous for lending, and restrictive covenants kept Black families out of white neighborhoods until the Supreme Court declared them unenforceable in *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948). Because American public schools have long been funded in large part by local property taxes, segregated housing produced unequal schools automatically, year after year, without anyone having to intend it again. In 1973 the Supreme Court heard that argument from Mexican American parents in a poor San Antonio district, and in *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez* it ruled five to four that education is not a fundamental right under the federal Constitution and that property-tax funding did not violate equal protection. Many states have since reformed their formulas under their own constitutions, but the basic architecture remained. Jonathan Kozol's *Savage Inequalities* (1991) walked readers through the schools of East St. Louis, Camden and Chicago and then drove them a few miles to the suburbs, where spending on each child ran far higher. The contrasts he recorded were the design working as built.

I have to be careful here, because it's easy to read that history as an indictment of people long dead and harder to read it as an account of where many of us live. A great many suburban congregations in this country, including many in Michigan, sit in communities that grew as white families left the cities, and whose schools are strong partly because the tax base went with them. Like a lot of parents, I have chosen where to live with the school ratings open, and my sons have benefited from rungs other children in this county don't have. That is how structural sin usually works. Not through villains. Through decent people doing the reasonable thing inside an unreasonable arrangement, and then calling the sum of it merit. The prophets had a word for societies that grind the poor down while the comfortable call it order. Amos spoke of those who "trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth" (Amos 2:7, ESV), and he said it to people who were religious, prosperous and sure of God's favor.

## The School-to-Prison Pipeline, and Its Critics

Inequality in schooling also shows up in who gets removed from the room. The phrase "school-to-prison pipeline" names a pattern critics began describing around the turn of the century: exclusionary discipline, meaning suspension, expulsion and referral to police, falling hardest on poor children, Black children and children with disabilities, and pushing them out of school and toward the juvenile courts.

The history is recent and specific. The federal Gun-Free Schools Act of 1994 required states receiving federal education money to mandate at least a one-year expulsion for any student who brought a firearm to school. That was a defensible response to a real danger. What followed was an expansion few had voted for. Across the late 1990s, and especially after the Columbine shooting in 1999, many districts extended zero-tolerance rules well beyond weapons, to fights, defiance, disruption and dress codes, and school resource officers became a fixture in many buildings. Conduct that an earlier generation of principals would have handled with a phone call home began to produce suspensions and, in some places, arrests.

The disparities are documented by the federal government's own collection. In 2014 the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights, reporting data from the 2011 to 2012 school year, found that Black students were suspended and expelled at three times the rate of white students. A 2011 study by the Council of State Governments Justice Center, following Texas seventh graders through their school records, found that most removals were at the discretion of school officials rather than required by state law, that Black students were more likely than otherwise similar white students to receive discretionary discipline, and that students suspended or expelled were more likely to have contact with the juvenile justice system the following year.

The critics of the pipeline framing deserve to be heard at full strength, because they are not defending cruelty. They make two arguments. The first concerns causation. A child suspended at twelve and arrested at fifteen may have been set on that road by the suspension, or both events may flow from the same troubles: an unsafe home, an absent father, untreated trauma. Correlation does not by itself show cause, and some researchers have argued that much of the racial gap in discipline narrows once prior behavior is accounted for. The second argument concerns the other children. The student who disrupts a classroom day after day is not the only image-bearer in it. The other twenty-five are usually poor too, and their one real chance at learning to read is being spent on someone else's crisis. Wealthy families buy their way out of chaotic schools; poor families can't. When federal guidance in 2014 warned districts that racially disparate discipline could violate civil-rights law, critics argued that some districts cut suspensions on paper while leaving teachers without the support to keep classrooms safe, and the guidance was rescinded in 2018. A teacher holding a difficult room together deserves the church's honor, not its lecture.

Much of that is true, and it should chasten anyone tempted to preach this as a simple morality tale. But the critics' case doesn't dissolve the problem of discretion. Where the rules leave room for judgment, judgment falls unevenly, and a thirteen-year-old Black boy's defiance is too often read as menace where a white boy's is read as a bad day. And a school that has no way of responding to a disruptive child except to send him away has not achieved order. It has moved the disorder somewhere the rest of us don't have to see it. The millstone warning in Matthew 18 was spoken to disciples about obstacles laid in a child's path. It gives no Christian permission to romanticize disruption, and it gives none to shrug at a system that treats the most wounded children as problems to be removed rather than persons to be formed.

The guardrail here fires in both directions. On the right, the failure has often been a carceral reflex dressed as moral seriousness, reading a nine-year-old's trauma as defiance and calling a police referral accountability. On the left, it has often been reform imposed from district offices, suspension targets pursued without the counselors that would make them safe, and a sentimentality about disruption that parents who depend on those schools can't afford. Both failures land on the same children. The poor child who is removed and the poor child whose classroom is ruined belong to the same neighborhood and the same Lord.

## Money, Choice and Reading: An Argument to Have Well

Christians disagree about remedies, and they should be able to disagree well. The case for public school reform begins with the fact that most American children, including most poor children, attend public schools and will for the foreseeable future. It holds that a shared institution serving every child is a real common good and that funding still matters, and it has had to defend the second claim for sixty years. James Coleman's federal report *Equality of Educational Opportunity* (1966) found that family background explained more of the differences in achievement than school resources did, and economists such as Eric Hanushek have long argued that how money is spent matters more than how much. But a 2016 study in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* by C. Kirabo Jackson, Rucker Johnson and Claudia Persico, following children who grew up through court-ordered school finance reforms, found that sustained increases in spending improved educational attainment and adult earnings, with the largest gains for children from low-income families. Coleman never concluded that neglect was harmless. A finding that disadvantage weighs heaviest at home is an argument for more attention to the children carrying it.

The case for school choice, including Christian schools and homeschooling, is just as serious. It holds that parents, not the state, bear first responsibility before God for forming their children, which is the plain sense of Deuteronomy 6. The Supreme Court agreed in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters* (1925), striking down an Oregon law that would have forced every child into public schools and declaring that "the child is not the mere creature of the State." Choice advocates note that the common school was never neutral, that wealthy families already choose by buying houses, and that letting funds follow a poor child out of a failing school gives her family the exit the affluent take for granted. Homeschooling became legal in every state by 1993, and many families chose it for reasons of conscience that deserve respect.

These are prudential questions, second-order matters on which faithful Christians disagree with integrity. What is first-order is the conviction that every child bears God's image and is owed real formation, and that a society which forms some children for flourishing and others for failure has done something Scripture names as injustice. The same instrument has to measure both sides, and the question to put to every remedy is what it does for the child whose parents can't work the system. Choice fails that child when it serves the family organized enough to leave and forgets the one still in the building. The defense of the public school fails her when it protects the institution, and the adults it employs, ahead of the children it is failing. Literacy shows how deep this goes. For decades, influential parts of the education establishment taught young children to guess at words from pictures and context rather than to decode them, long after the National Reading Panel reported in 2000 that systematic phonics instruction works. The children whose parents could compensate at home survived. Poor children paid. That failure came from the progressive center of the profession, and no amount of funding would have cured it. The segregation academy, by contrast, came from white Christians who called it freedom, and the carceral reflex has found its warmest welcome on the right. A church that can name only one of these has chosen a tribe.

## Neighbors Now

What can a congregation do? Nothing that settles the structural question, and a good deal that matters anyway. Churches can learn the schools near them as well as they know their own budgets. They can offer tutoring and mentoring, sustained over years, in the forms a principal actually asks for. They can honor the teachers in their pews and hold the choice debate with charity, knowing the union teacher and the homeschooling mother may both be obeying conscience. None of this is heroic. It is what Raikes's teachers did, and what the freedpeople did in church basements, and it is how a people of the book, at their best, have treated a child who can't yet read it.

But the deeper change is in how we see. As long as children are the future, their needs can wait for the next budget, the next election, the next study, and we can tell ourselves our own children's advantages are simply prudence. If children are neighbors now, the eight-year-old two districts over who can't yet read her schoolbook is the child Jesus put in the middle of the room while his disciples argued about who mattered most. The disciples were us. We still argue about greatness, and we still find the interruption inconvenient.

The millstone warning was addressed to the people closest to Jesus, and it still is. The Lord who spoke it went to a cross for people who had hindered him in every way they knew, and he rose, and the mercy he offers reaches even those of us who built our comfort on someone else's missing rungs. That mercy doesn't excuse what we did. It makes repentance possible, and repentance in this case has a shape: a seat at a table beside a child with a book, and a willingness to ask what our comfort has cost her. She is not waiting to become someone. She is already here, and she is already his.
