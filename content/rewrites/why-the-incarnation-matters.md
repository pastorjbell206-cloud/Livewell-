Most of us met the incarnation first as a scene: a stable, a young mother, animals, a star, shepherds kneeling in straw, small enough to print on a card. There's nothing wrong with the scene. The trouble is that for most people it has quietly become the doctrine, and the scene alone says nothing that would trouble a first-century philosopher or stop a modern skeptic. A baby is born, the world is sweetened for a week, and life goes back to normal.

Say the claim plainly and it sounds like something else. The Christian church confesses that the eternal Son of God, through whom the universe was made, became a human being: conceived in a young woman's womb, born during a Roman registration, taught a trade, and executed by the ordinary machinery of an empire. He did not merely appear in human form, the way the gods of Greek myth borrowed bodies for an afternoon. He did not stop being God. And according to the creeds, he has not stopped being human either.

When I was an atheist, this was the part of Christianity I was least prepared for. I had rejected gods who sat at the top of the universe demanding that we climb toward them. I had no category for a God whose whole movement was downward. Most people who reject the incarnation, I've come to think, have rejected the greeting card and never met the claim.

**The incarnation is not the preface to salvation, a disguise God wore long enough to reach the cross and then set aside. In the church's oldest reading it is the shape of salvation itself: God healing human nature by taking all of it, body and mind, birth and death, and never giving it back. The distinction that makes the claim thinkable is one the skeptic rarely hears, that Jesus is fully human without being merely human. And its consequences reach well past Christmas, into what a body is, what ordinary work is worth, and what we owe the flesh of the people next to us.**

## What the New Testament Actually Claims

The Gospel of John opens by echoing Genesis: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). For a Greek reader the *logos* was the rational order of the cosmos; for a Jewish reader it was the creating speech of God (Psalm 33:6). John claims both, "All things were made through him" (John 1:3), and then says what neither audience could accept: "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).

Two words carry the weight. *Flesh*, *sarx*, names the perishable stuff of a creature, the part that gets hungry and bleeds and decays. And *dwelt* translates a verb built on the word for a tent, reaching back to the tabernacle where the glory of the LORD filled a tent pitched among a wandering people (Exodus 40:34). The glory that once rested over a tent of skins now lives in a body of skin. "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (John 1:18). If you want to know what God is like, you are pointed to a particular man.

Paul says the same thing in the middle of a church quarrel (Philippians 4:2), and his remedy is a portrait of God. "Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross" (Philippians 2:5–8). Many scholars read these lines as a hymn the church was already singing before Paul wrote, which would make them one of the earliest Christian confessions we have.

Read the grammar closely. Christ "emptied himself," and the next phrase says how: "by taking the form of a servant." The emptying is an addition, not a subtraction. The Son does not stop being "in the form of God"; the one who owns everything chose to own nothing. Then the hymn turns upward, "so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Philippians 2:10), borrowing from Isaiah 45, where the LORD swears that every knee will bow to him and to no other. A Jewish monotheist gave Jesus the worship Isaiah reserved for the God of Israel.

Colossians widens the frame to the cosmos: "He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15), and "in him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:17). The one who keeps the atoms in their places took a body made of them.

Hebrews supplies the necessity. "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Hebrews 2:14–15). "Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect" (Hebrews 2:17). And then, pastorally: "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). That last clause treats sin not as part of what makes us human but as the wound in our humanity. A sinless man is not less human than we are. He is the one fully human life the world has seen.

## Is God Becoming Man Even Coherent? The Skeptic's Case

The strongest objection to the incarnation is not that it is too miraculous. It is that it seems to make no sense.

Baruch Spinoza put it bluntly in a letter to Henry Oldenburg in 1675. When certain churches say that God took on human nature, he wrote, he did not know what they meant; the claim seemed to him about as absurd as saying a circle had taken on the nature of a square. The logic behind the jab is serious. God, as classical theology itself defines him, is infinite, unchanging and all-knowing. A human being is finite, begins to exist, learns and dies. Luke says the boy Jesus "increased in wisdom and in stature" (Luke 2:52), and Jesus says of the last day, "no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32). If Jesus simply is God, then by the most basic rule of identity the same person is both all-knowing and ignorant of the day, both unchanging and growing. That looks less like mystery than like contradiction with incense around it.

Three centuries later John Hick and the contributors to *The Myth of God Incarnate* (1977) pressed the case from inside the church, and without contempt. Hick argued that orthodoxy had never given the formula a literal meaning that could be stated, and that it was better read as poetry, a way of saying that in Jesus people met God. He also saw a moral cost: if God became man only once, in one province of one empire, every other faith stands outside the truth by definition.

Some of this has to be conceded. Christians have often said "mystery" exactly where the argument should begin, which is a refusal wearing a robe.

The answer begins with a distinction the objection usually misses. The creeds never say that divinity and humanity became one nature. That would be a circle becoming a square, and the church condemned it as heresy. They say that one person, one "who," has two natures, two "whats." A contradiction requires affirming and denying the same thing of the same subject in the same respect, and the Fathers answered that the Son is all-knowing according to his divine nature and grows in wisdom according to his human nature. The skeptic can call that a verbal trick. The philosopher Thomas V. Morris, in *The Logic of God Incarnate* (1986), argued that it isn't, by distinguishing being *fully* human from being *merely* human. Every human we have met is created, began to exist and knows only a little, but properties common to humans are not thereby essential to being human. A person can have everything it takes to be human without every limitation the rest of us share. Morris added a model in which Christ has a divine mind and a genuinely human mind that grows and learns within it, one philosopher's proposal rather than the church's dogma, and enough to show that the charge of plain contradiction is not as simple as Spinoza assumed. Philippians already pointed this way: God taking a nature on is not a circle turning into a square.

Søren Kierkegaard, in *Philosophical Fragments* (1844), called God in human form "the absolute paradox," the place where reason meets something it could never have produced. But paradox, for him, was not nonsense, and he told a parable to show why God would do something so strange. A king loves a humble young woman and realizes that if he comes to her in royal robes, he will never know whether she loves him or his crown. To be loved as an equal, he has to come down to where she is. The incarnation, on this reading, is what love does when it refuses to win by overwhelming.

None of this makes the incarnation easy to picture. Hard to picture is not the same as impossible to mean. The skeptic may still judge the claim false, but the people who hold it did not fail to notice the problem. The church noticed it in the fourth century and argued about it for well over a century.

## Four Ways to Get Jesus Wrong, and the Councils That Said No

The early church reached its doctrine of Christ by refusing, one after another, the explanations that made Jesus easier to understand. Each heresy was a serious attempt to solve the skeptic's problem by giving up something the gospel cannot survive without.

The first came from Arius, a respected priest in Alexandria who around 318 began teaching that the Son was the first and highest of God's creatures, divine in rank but not in being: there was a time, his party said, when he was not. Arianism was reasonable, and that was its appeal. It protected the oneness of God without asking anyone to believe that the eternal God had been born. The Council of Nicaea in 325 answered that the Son is *homoousios*, "of one substance" with the Father. A creature, however exalted, cannot give us God. If the one on the cross is only the best thing God ever made, then God stayed safe and sent someone else.

The second attempt came from the other side. Apollinarius, bishop of Laodicea and a fierce opponent of Arius, proposed that the divine Word took the place of a human mind in Jesus: a human body, with God doing the thinking. The Council of Constantinople in 381 condemned the view, and Gregory of Nazianzus, writing to a priest named Cledonius around 382, gave the principle the church has leaned on ever since. In the line usually rendered from his Greek, what was not assumed was not healed. If Christ left out the human mind, the human mind was left out of salvation, and the mind is where our sin is most at home.

The third error divided Christ in two. Nestorius, made patriarch of Constantinople in 428, objected to calling Mary *Theotokos*, the one who bore God, and kept a careful distance between the eternal Word and the man Jesus, as if the two were partners sharing a life. Cyril of Alexandria saw the danger: if there are two subjects in Christ, the one crucified under Pilate is not the Son of God but a man the Son was closely associated with. The Council of Ephesus in 431 affirmed *Theotokos*, less to honor Mary than to say who her son is. The one she held was God.

The fourth error overcorrected. Eutyches, an aged monk in Constantinople, taught that after the union there was only one nature, the humanity absorbed into the divinity. Leo of Rome answered in his *Tome* of 449, arguing that each nature does what is proper to it in communion with the other. At Chalcedon in 451 the council confessed one and the same Christ "in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." The first two phrases guard against Eutyches and the blending of God and man into a third thing; the last two guard against Nestorius and the splitting of Christ into two.

Those four phrases are not an explanation. They are a fence, telling you what you may not say and leaving the mystery standing inside the boundary. Honesty requires adding that the definition itself divided the church, since the Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian and Syriac Orthodox churches rejected its wording, and modern dialogues between these churches and the Chalcedonian ones have largely concluded that much of that quarrel was over words rather than faith. The fence was drawn by fallible men in a violent century. It has held because nothing better has been found.

The heresies are not someone else's problem; they live on in us under other names. Adolf von Harnack's *What Is Christianity?* (1900) argued that the gospel Jesus preached concerned the Father and the infinite worth of the soul, not Jesus himself, and much progressive Christianity since has honored a Jesus who is the finest of teachers and not quite God. That is Arius with better manners. Meanwhile much devout piety has a Jesus who never really felt fear or had to learn anything, whose humanity is a costume. That is the old error that he only seemed human, at home in conservative churches that would never use its name. The same instrument cuts both ways. A merely human Jesus cannot save us, and a Jesus who only looks human cannot reach us.

## Why Did God Become Man? Athanasius and Anselm

Two books eight centuries apart give the classic answers, and each sees what the other underplays.

Athanasius attended Nicaea as a young deacon of Alexandria and spent his long life, including five exiles, defending what it confessed. *On the Incarnation*, traditionally dated around 318 though many scholars now place it in the 330s, begins with Genesis. Human beings were made from nothing and given a share in God's life, and when they turned from God they began sliding back toward nothing. Athanasius treats sin as a corruption running through human nature, which is why repentance alone could not reverse it: repentance can stop a person sinning, but it cannot undo what sin has done to him. God, in his telling, faced something like a dilemma. It would be unworthy of his goodness to let creatures made in his image perish, and unworthy of his truthfulness to pretend that death, the promised consequence of disobedience, did not matter. So the Word took a body capable of death, offered it on behalf of all, and by uniting it to his own life clothed it in incorruption.

He compares humanity to a portrait so stained that the likeness is lost, which can be restored only if the subject himself comes and sits again for the painter. And near the end he writes the line for which he is best remembered: the Word became human so that we might be made divine. The Eastern church has always read that as participation, the creature sharing by grace in the life of God, never the creature turning into the Creator, and it echoes 2 Peter 1:4, which speaks of becoming "partakers of the divine nature."

Anselm of Canterbury finished *Cur Deus Homo*, "Why God Became Man," in 1098, as a dialogue with a monk named Boso, who presses the objections unbelievers were raising: couldn't an all-powerful God simply forgive? Anselm answers through honor and debt. Sin robs God of the honor his creatures owe him, and the debt is one humanity owes and cannot pay, since everything we could offer is already owed. Only God could pay it, and only a man ought to, so the one who pays must be both. When Boso suggests a small sin might be overlooked, Anselm gives the sentence that has outlived his framework: *nondum considerasti quanti ponderis sit peccatum*, usually rendered, "you have not yet considered how great the weight of sin is."

Some criticism of Anselm is fair. His argument breathes a feudal air, and read carelessly it makes God sound like an offended nobleman rather than a Father. But the core survives. Forgiveness is never free for the one who forgives, and Anselm saw that the cost of forgiving humanity had to be borne by someone standing on both sides of the breach.

Put the books together and the logic comes into focus. Athanasius sees sin mainly as a disease, and the incarnation as God entering it to heal from inside. Anselm sees sin mainly as a debt, and the incarnation as God becoming the kind of being who could pay it. Hebrews 2 holds both in one breath: the Son took flesh to destroy the power of death and "to make propitiation for the sins of the people" (Hebrews 2:14, 17). Only God can save, and only a human being can be saved from the inside. The incarnation is where the two facts meet.

That is why it can't be treated as a preface. Gregory's principle means the whole of Christ's human life belongs to the rescue: the birth, the wilderness, the tears at a friend's grave (John 11:35), the terror in Gethsemane. Irenaeus, writing around 180, called it recapitulation, Christ walking obediently back over the ground Adam lost. The cross is the center, but the incarnation is the whole body the center belongs to.

## The Manger Without the Tinsel

With the doctrine in view, the Christmas narratives read less comfortably.

Luke begins with power. "In those days a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be registered" (Luke 2:1). The most powerful man on earth is counting what his subjects owe him, and the Son of God enters history as a line in that ledger, laid "in a manger, because there was no place for them in the inn" (Luke 2:7). A manger is a feeding trough. Then the angel tells shepherds on night watch, "For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Savior, who is Christ the Lord" (Luke 2:11). Those titles weren't neutral. A calendar inscription set up at Priene in Asia Minor around 9 BC hailed Augustus as a savior and his birthday as the beginning of good news for the world. Luke's angel gives the emperor's titles to a baby in a trough. The first Christmas announcement was a quiet act of treason.

Luke doesn't let the joy stand unshadowed. In the temple the old man Simeon takes the child in his arms and tells Mary, "Behold, this child is appointed for the fall and rising of many in Israel, and for a sign that is opposed (and a sword will pierce through your own soul also), so that thoughts from many hearts may be revealed" (Luke 2:34–35). Even the blessing carries a sword.

Matthew is harder still. His genealogy names four women besides Mary, each carrying a story a tidy mind would have left out: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth the Moabite, and one Matthew calls only "the wife of Uriah" (Matthew 1:6), a standing reminder of David's adultery and murder. Then Herod, outwitted by the magi, "sent and killed all the male children in Bethlehem and in all that region who were two years old or under" (Matthew 2:16), and Matthew answers with Jeremiah: "A voice was heard in Ramah, weeping and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted, because they are no more" (Matthew 2:18). The holy family flees to Egypt as refugees. The Christmas story, read as written, contains a massacre of children, and the text does not explain it away.

That is the point. God entered a world where emperors count the poor and kings kill children to protect a throne, and he entered it at the bottom. The sentimental Christmas keeps the baby and loses the world he was born into, and so it loses the reason he came. It loses the history too. C. S. Lewis, who also came to faith out of unbelief, argued in "Myth Became Fact" (1944) that in Jesus the old stories of a dying and rising god came down into history. Myths happen once upon a time. This happened in the reign of Augustus, in a village with a name.

## The Body, the Workbench and the Ordinary Day

The incarnation offended the ancient world for a reason. Educated opinion assumed that spirit is high and matter low, and by the second century the systems we call Gnostic taught that the material world was the work of a lesser, ignorant power and that salvation meant escaping it. Some who called themselves Christians tried to spare Christ the indignity by teaching that he only seemed to have a body. The New Testament had already drawn the line: "every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (1 John 4:2). Ignatius of Antioch, writing to the church at Tralles around 110 on his way to die in Rome, insisted that Jesus was truly born, truly ate and drank, truly suffered under Pontius Pilate and was truly raised. If he only seemed to suffer, Ignatius asked, why was Ignatius in chains? Irenaeus, answering the Gnostic teachers of his day, wrote that *gloria Dei vivens homo*, the glory of God is a living human being. The incarnation is God's second signature under the verdict of Genesis: "And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31).

It also sanctifies the part of life nobody records. "Jesus, when he began his ministry, was about thirty years of age" (Luke 3:23). Of the years before, the Gospels give us almost nothing: that he went home to Nazareth with his parents "and was submissive to them" (Luke 2:51), that he grew, and that his neighbors knew him as "the carpenter, the son of Mary" (Mark 6:3), a builder who worked with his hands. The eternal Word spent most of his earthly life in the unremarked labor that fills every life, and Irenaeus taught that Christ passed through each age of human life to make it holy. If God spent those years at a workbench, ordinary years are not a delay before real life starts. The meal cooked again, the child taught to walk, the shift nobody thanks you for: God did not find these beneath him.

And it gives bodies a claim on us. "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:19–20). Jesus spent his ministry touching lepers and feeding crowds, and he said, "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40). The strongest objection comes from inside the church: bodies pass and souls endure, so with limited time the soul must come first. There's truth in that, since the gospel is more than bread. But the incarnation won't honor the clean separation the argument depends on. I have preached the incarnation for years and lived as if it were mostly about the soul's eternal destination, moving quickly past bodily suffering because I hadn't reckoned with what it means that God took flesh. The same instrument cuts the culture around us. A church that treats the body as a waiting room for the soul and an economy that prices bodies by their output are making the same Gnostic mistake from opposite ends. What we believe about the body of Jesus determines what we owe the body of a neighbor.

## The Flesh He Kept

The incarnation did not end at Easter. The risen Jesus insists on his body: "See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me, and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have" (Luke 24:39). He tells Thomas, "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side" (John 20:27). The risen body still bore the wounds. Years later Paul writes in the present tense, "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). The Son did not take off our nature once the work was done. There is now human flesh at the center of God's life, and it has scars.

That is where the doctrine becomes most personal. Christianity has never offered a tidy explanation for suffering; the book of Job spends most of its length refusing one. What it offers is stranger and, in the dark, more durable: a God who is not watching pain from a window. He has been hungry, exhausted, betrayed by a friend, bereaved, and afraid of a death he could see coming. Pastors spend a great deal of time in rooms where nothing useful can be said about why. What can still be said there is that the one people are angry at is not untouched by it. He came down into exactly this.

The Bible ends not with souls escaping bodies but with a renewed earth, where God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more" (Revelation 21:4). Tears are bodily. The final comfort of God is a touch.

So go back to the card on the mantel and refuse, for one moment, to find it cozy. That is God with limits, the source of all words unable yet to speak one, a body that will be nailed to wood, will walk out of a tomb, and will not be set aside. The smallness was never sentiment. It was the rescue. And if God has joined himself to flesh and will not let go of it, then every tired, aging, hungry, disabled or inconvenient body you pass this week, your own included, is the kind of thing God himself chose to become. The doctrine was settled at Chalcedon. Whether we believe it is decided somewhere much closer, at the door of the next person whose body is asking something of us.
