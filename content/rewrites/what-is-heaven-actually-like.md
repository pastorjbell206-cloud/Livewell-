## The heaven most of us were handed

Ask people what heaven is like and most will describe the same picture, whether they believe in it or not. Clouds. White robes. Harps. Souls without bodies drifting somewhere far above the world, singing in a service that never ends. It's on the sympathy cards and in the funeral homilies, and it's what we tell children when a grandparent dies. Press a believer gently and many will admit, with some embarrassment, that the prospect sounds less like joy than like a very long afternoon in a waiting room. The skeptic hears the same description and draws the obvious conclusion: this is a sedative for people who are afraid of the dark, a pale reward promised to make the present easier to endure.

Some of that is fair. If that picture were the Christian hope, the skeptic's charge would land cleanly, and the believer's quiet dread would be the honest response. But the picture is a composite, assembled over many centuries from Greek philosophy, medieval painting, Victorian sentiment and a few biblical images pulled loose from their setting. Honesty requires admitting that some of its pieces really are in the Bible. The harps are there, in the worship scenes of Revelation. So are the white robes. The clouds come from Paul's language about believers meeting the Lord in the air. What isn't in the Bible is the thing the pieces were used to build: an eternity in which the body is left behind, the earth is discarded, and the saved spend forever somewhere that is emphatically not here.

The argument of this essay turns on one distinction. There are two ways to picture the end of the story. In the first, heaven is escape from the world: the soul slips out of the body at death, rises to a better realm, and never looks back at the creation it has outgrown. In the second, heaven is the renewal of the world: God raises the dead in bodies and makes his home with them in a creation healed of everything that has spoiled it. The first is the picture most of us were handed. The second is the one the Bible actually tells, from Isaiah's prophecy of new heavens and a new earth to the last two chapters of Revelation, where the holy city comes down rather than the saints going up.

That distinction doesn't settle every question, and a good deal of this subject has been ruined by people who claimed to know more than anyone can. Scripture is generous about the shape of the future and sparing about its furniture. It also speaks of a real state between death and resurrection, and of a vision of God that the church has treasured for centuries and that no talk of renewed earth should crowd out. Those need careful handling. So do judgment and hell, which any honest account of heaven has to face. But the shape is clear, and it isn't clouds. The Christian hope is not that we'll finally get away from here. It's that here will finally be made right, and we'll be raised to live in it.

## Where the clouds came from

The escape picture is older than Christianity, and it has a respectable pedigree. In the *Phaedo*, Plato's account of the last day of Socrates, who was executed at Athens in 399 BC, the philosopher faces death calmly because he believes the soul is the true self and the body its prison. Philosophy, Socrates argues there, is a lifelong rehearsal for dying, a slow training of the soul to detach from the appetites and distractions of the flesh so that it can finally be free of them. It's a noble vision. It made courage in the face of death reasonable, and it taught generations to prize the invisible over the merely comfortable. It also treats the material world as a lower order of reality, something to be outgrown.

The early church knew exactly how attractive that vision was, and it fought it. In the second century a family of movements usually grouped as Gnosticism taught that the material world was the botched work of a lesser god and that salvation meant the spirit's release from it. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, answered them around 180 in *Against Heresies*, and the fifth book of that work is a sustained argument that the God who made the world intends to redeem it: the flesh that was formed from the earth will be raised, and the creation itself will be restored rather than abandoned. The church wrote its answer into the baptismal confession that became the Apostles' Creed. The creed does not confess the immortality of the soul. It confesses "the resurrection of the body." That line was a boundary marker. It was drawn precisely against the idea that the body is a shell the real person sheds.

Yet the Platonic instinct never fully left. Augustine, whose *City of God* (completed around 426) ends with a long meditation on the resurrection body and the vision of God, was firmly on the creed's side of that line, but centuries of devotion leaned toward the soul's ascent. Medieval art and preaching fixed attention on the moment of death and the soul's destination, so that the resurrection became the distant epilogue rather than the climax. Colleen McDannell and Bernhard Lang, in *Heaven: A History* (1988), trace a further shift in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when heaven in Protestant imagination became increasingly human-centered: a domestic reunion of families and friends, less about God than about the people we had lost. The Swedish visionary Emanuel Swedenborg, whose *Heaven and Hell* appeared in 1758, fed that stream directly. In his system every angel had once been a human being, which is one root of the sentimental conviction, now nearly universal, that the dead become angels.

They don't. Scripture keeps the two orders of creatures distinct. When Jesus answered the Sadducees' trick question about a woman married seven times, he said that in the resurrection people are like the angels in not marrying (Matthew 22:30). He was speaking about marriage, and he was describing the resurrection, which the Sadducees denied, not a transformation of human beings into another kind of being. Hebrews calls angels ministering spirits sent to serve those who will inherit salvation, and Paul tells the Corinthians that believers will one day judge angels. The destiny of a human being is to be raised as a human being, more fully human than now.

Randy Alcorn, in *Heaven* (2004), gave the whole drift a name, Christoplatonism: the habit of reading Scripture through a Platonic assumption that spirit is good and matter is suspect, so that every biblical promise about land, bodies, food and cities gets quietly converted into a symbol for something immaterial. The name is useful because it implicates the church rather than the culture. We did this. Pastors did it at gravesides, and I have heard myself do it, reaching for the thinner comfort because it was what the room expected. We painted those clouds ourselves.

## Between death and the resurrection

None of this means that nothing happens when a Christian dies. The New Testament speaks plainly of a real state between death and resurrection, and any account that skips it has overcorrected. To the thief dying beside him, Jesus said, "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). Paul, writing from custody and unsure whether he would be executed, weighed his options and confessed, "My desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better" (Philippians 1:23). Elsewhere he says he would rather be away from the body and at home with the Lord (2 Corinthians 5:8). Whatever else is true of the dead in Christ, they are with him, and Paul can call that far better without any embarrassment.

N. T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* (2008) is the most widely read modern attempt to set this in proportion. Wright doesn't deny the intermediate state; he insists on it. His argument is that the New Testament treats it as a resting place rather than a destination. "Paradise," in the Jewish usage of Jesus' day, suggested a garden of rest and refreshment, not the final end of all things. Going to be with Christ at death is real and good and far better, but it is the first stage of a two-stage hope. The second stage, the one the New Testament keeps pointing at, is the resurrection of the body in a renewed creation. The trouble with the cloud picture is not that it describes the intermediate state too warmly. It's that it mistakes the waiting room for the house.

The tradition has always sensed the incompleteness of that middle state. Thomas Aquinas, commenting on 1 Corinthians 15, argued that a soul separated from its body is not a complete human being, so that even a soul at rest in God's presence is not yet the whole person God made and means to save. Christians have argued about the details. Some, reading Paul's habit of calling the dead "those who have fallen asleep," concluded that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection; John Calvin wrote his first theological treatise, the *Psychopannychia*, drafted in 1534, against that view. The sleep language deserves a fair hearing, because it tells the truth that death is temporary for those in Christ. But "far better" is hard to square with oblivion, and most of the church has read sleep as a description of how death looks from this side rather than of what the dead experience. In 1336 Pope Benedict XII's constitution *Benedictus Deus* defined for the Roman Church that the souls of the saints see God immediately after death, before the resurrection. Protestants who reject the authority behind that decree have largely shared its conclusion about the saints' conscious rest.

Into the reticence of Scripture on this middle state, our own generation poured a flood of books. Don Piper's *90 Minutes in Heaven* (2004) and *Heaven Is for Real* (2010), Todd Burpo's account of his young son Colton's experience during emergency surgery, sold in the millions. *The Boy Who Came Back from Heaven* (2010), credited to Kevin Malarkey and his son Alex, told of the boy's visit to heaven after a car accident. In January 2015 Alex publicly retracted it, writing that he had not died or gone to heaven and had made the story up, and urging readers to trust the Bible as sufficient. His publisher, Tyndale House, withdrew the book. That retraction doesn't prove that every such account is invented, and people who report these experiences deserve neither mockery nor credulity. What it does show is how badly we wanted a map the Bible declined to draw. Paul, caught up into paradise, said only that he heard things that cannot be told (2 Corinthians 12:4). And in Jesus' parable of the rich man and Lazarus, Abraham warns that people who will not hear Moses and the Prophets will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead. Notice, too, what most of these books share. They describe a vivid heaven the soul visits and returns from, and they rarely mention the resurrection at all. They are the escape picture in its modern, bestselling form.

## A new heaven and a new earth

The final hope has a long prehistory in the prophets. In words addressed to a people who knew exile and a ruined city, Isaiah heard God say, "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind" (Isaiah 65:17). What follows in that chapter is a description of land. People build houses and live in them. They plant vineyards and eat the fruit. Their labor isn't wasted, their children aren't born for calamity, and the wolf and the lamb graze together on God's holy mountain where nothing hurts or destroys. The oracle still speaks of death, since someone who dies at a hundred is called young, and that is one reason readers have argued over how literally to take its details. Isaiah is using the best life he could imagine in a restored Jerusalem to point past it. But he couldn't picture God's future without picturing earth, and neither can the rest of the canon.

Revelation takes up Isaiah's promise and carries it past the last enemy. John sees a new heaven and a new earth, and then the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God. The direction of travel is the whole point. The voice from the throne announces, "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God" (Revelation 21:3). The word for dwelling recalls the tabernacle, the tent God pitched among Israel in the wilderness. The end of the Bible is God moving in. Death is gone, mourning is gone, and God himself wipes the tears from his people's faces. In the final chapter the tree of life, lost at the gate of Eden, grows again beside a river, its leaves given for the healing of the nations, and the curse pronounced on the ground in Genesis 3 is lifted. John even sees the kings of the earth bringing their glory into the city, and the glory and honor of the nations carried in with them. The story runs from a garden to a garden city, and it ends here, on a healed earth.

The one on the throne says, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5). Notice the grammar: all things new, not all new things. Some interpreters lean hard on the Greek word for "new" here, arguing that it means renewed in quality rather than brand-new in time. A word study alone can't bear that much weight, but the pattern of the whole New Testament can. Paul says that "the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God" (Romans 8:21). He pictures the created order groaning like a woman in labor, and believers groaning with it as they wait for the redemption of their bodies. Labor pains, for all their agony, end in a birth.

J. Richard Middleton's *A New Heaven and a New Earth* (2014) traces this hope from Genesis to Revelation and argues that the New Testament never once presents heaven as the final home of the redeemed. Among his more striking observations is a textual one. The older English translations of 2 Peter 3:10 said the earth would be "burned up," a reading that seemed to settle the question in favor of annihilation. But the earliest and best Greek manuscripts read that the earth and its works will be found, or laid bare, which is why the ESV says they "will be exposed." The fire in that passage refines and discloses. It doesn't simply destroy. Wright makes a parallel point about the verse that launched a thousand rapture charts. When Paul says believers will meet the Lord in the air (1 Thessalonians 4:17), he uses a word for the civic custom of going out to greet an arriving dignitary and escorting him back into the city. The saints don't meet their Lord in the clouds in order to leave with him. They meet him to welcome him home.

Alcorn's *Heaven* presses the practical conclusion. If God meant to scrap the world, it would be strange that he called it very good in the beginning and strange that the Bible's last vision is a city descending to it rather than souls evacuating from it. The creation isn't a rough draft.

## Raised bodies and the face of God

At the center of the renewed world stands a body. Writing to Corinthian Christians who found bodily resurrection embarrassing, Paul reached for the image of a seed that goes into the ground bare and comes up as something fuller, continuous with what was sown and yet transformed. Then he summed it up: "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44). The word "spiritual" there has misled generations of readers into picturing something vaporous. Paul's contrast isn't between material and immaterial. It's between a body animated by ordinary mortal life and a body animated and governed by the Spirit of God. Wright spent much of his longer study *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) showing that in the Jewish and pagan world of the first century, "resurrection" never meant the soul's survival. It meant a body, alive again after death.

The risen Jesus is the prototype, which is why Paul calls him the firstfruits, the first sheaf of a harvest still to come. The Gospels describe him eating broiled fish, inviting Thomas to touch his wounds, and keeping his scars. They also describe him appearing in a locked room and going unrecognized at first by people who knew him well. He is neither a ghost nor a resuscitated corpse. His body is more solid than before, not less, and it carries a life that can't die again. Whatever we will be, we'll be like that. This is the creed's "resurrection of the body" in flesh and bone, and it quietly dismantles the boredom the cloud picture produces. We imagine heaven as dull because we imagine it as less: less body, less world, less to do. The Bible describes more.

But more of what, finally? Here the older tradition supplies something the new-creation emphasis can forget. For most of Christian history the highest hope was not a place at all but a sight: the beatific vision, the sight of God that is itself the soul's final happiness. Jesus promised that the pure in heart will see God (Matthew 5:8). Moses was told that no one could see God's face and live, yet Revelation says of the redeemed that they will see his face. John holds the whole hope in one sentence: "Beloved, we are God's children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* (written between 1265 and 1274), argued that human beings are made with a desire to know that nothing created can finally satisfy, so that our last and perfect happiness can only be the vision of God's own being. Augustine closed *The City of God* on the same note: the endless sabbath of the saints would be rest, sight, love and praise, without end. Gregory of Nyssa, in his *Life of Moses* (around 390), added that this vision is never exhausted. Because God is infinite, the soul goes on stretching toward him forever, always receiving more. That is the tradition's answer to boredom, and it's a better one than ours.

Hans Boersma's *Seeing God* (2018) recovers that tradition, and he writes partly as a critic of the new-creation writers. His worry is that some recent Protestant eschatology, neo-Calvinist theology in particular, risks making the renewed earth the goal and God the backdrop, a heaven of gardens and cities in which the vision of God becomes one pleasure among many. He reads created things sacramentally, as signs that point past themselves to the God who gives them, and he insists that we will see God in the face of Christ. The criticism deserves to be heard, because the renewal emphasis can drift into a Christianized paradise of good things with the Giver politely in the background.

The two emphases are best held together, each guarding the other. The new creation tells us where and how: in bodies, on a healed earth, among neighbors, with work and culture carried into the city. The beatific vision tells us what for. The point of the renewed world is that God dwells in it and is seen, and every good thing there will be transparent to him, the way a window is for the light. A bodiless vision forgets the resurrection. A new earth without the vision forgets why anyone would want to live forever.

## Judgment, and the choice Lewis imagined

An honest account of heaven can't skip the rest of what Scripture says about the end, and the renewal picture makes that harder, not easier. A world with no more tears is a world in which evil has been dealt with, and in the Bible it is dealt with by judgment. The creed binds every Christian to the confession that Christ will come to judge the living and the dead, and no one in the New Testament speaks of judgment more often or more soberly than Jesus. Revelation's city has gates that are never shut, yet John also says that some remain outside it. Miroslav Volf, in *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996), argued from the experience of the Balkan wars that a belief in God's final judgment is not the enemy of peace but one of its conditions: people who have been wronged can lay down vengeance only if they trust that someone will one day set things right. What the victims of history ask is whether anyone will ever answer for what was done to them.

What judgment finally involves is a harder question, and faithful Christians have answered it differently. The view with the greatest weight across the history of the church, defended at length by Augustine in the twenty-first book of *The City of God*, holds that the punishment of the impenitent is conscious and without end. A minority view, conditional immortality, holds that immortality is God's gift rather than the soul's native possession, so that those who refuse him finally perish; John Stott, in *Essentials* (1988), tentatively said he found it more persuasive, while insisting the question be held with humility. A third view, universalism, holds that God's love will finally reconcile everyone, so that judgment is real but remedial. It is usually traced to Origen in the third century, and its modern form is often held only as a hope. It deserves to be stated fairly, but it should not be mistaken for a creedal option: the church rejected it in the sixth-century condemnations of Origenism, and it has stood outside the historic mainstream ever since. The judgment itself is first-order, confessed in the creed. Whether the punishment of the lost is endless or ends in their perishing divides careful believers within that mainstream, and this essay leaves that question where honest readers of Scripture have had to leave it: open, and grave.

C. S. Lewis offered something other than a doctrine. *The Great Divorce* (1945) is a dream, and Lewis says plainly in his preface that he is not guessing at the actual conditions after death. A bus carries the narrator from a grey, sprawling, endlessly quarrelsome town to the edge of heaven, where the passengers turn out to be ghosts, thin and half-transparent. The grass there is so real it hurts their feet. Bright figures come out to meet them, often people who knew them in life, and invite them to stay and go on into the mountains. Nearly every ghost goes back to the bus, because staying would mean surrendering the grievance, the pride, the respectability or the smothering love they've made into an identity. Lewis's guide sums it up: in the end there are those who say to God that his will be done, and those to whom God finally says that their own will be done. The terrible thing in the book isn't that heaven is thin. It's that heaven is too solid for people who have made themselves thin.

That image cuts us before it cuts anyone else. The escape picture, for all its piety, is also a way of refusing the solid world: its demands, its neighbors, its bodies, its injustices, all of it left behind as though none of it would last. The renewal picture takes all of that back. If the earth is going to be healed rather than discarded, then the body you're living in, the work of your hands and the injustice you shrugged at all have a future in God's judgment and God's mercy. Paul ends his long argument for the resurrection by telling the Corinthians that their labor in the Lord is not in vain. He could say so only because the Lord had been raised.

The way into that world runs through a grave. God did not renew creation by skipping death; he entered it, in the body of his Son, and came out the other side with the scars still visible. That is why Christians stand at a graveside and say something stranger than "they're in a better place." The body in that casket is the body God intends to raise. The earth it's buried in is the earth God intends to heal. Those we've buried are with Christ, and they are waiting, as we are, for a morning that hasn't come yet. Until it does, we carry the ache every good thing leaves behind, and the harder knowledge that we ourselves will have to be made solid enough to stand on that grass.
