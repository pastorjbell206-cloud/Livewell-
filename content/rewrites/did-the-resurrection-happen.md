## The one claim Christianity can't soften

Most people who ask whether the resurrection happened have already answered it without quite asking. Some absorbed the answer in a pew, sung every spring until it stopped sounding like a claim about a corpse. Others absorbed the opposite from the air of modern life. Both answers feel like conclusions. Very often they are inheritances. And the church has made the confusion worse, because we preach the resurrection on one Sunday a year and then live the other fifty-one as though death had the final word after all. I say that as someone who preaches it.

Christianity is unusual in refusing to let its central event drift into metaphor. Writing to the church at Corinth around the year 54, Paul stated the stakes with a bluntness that still startles: "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). A few lines later he went further: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, ESV). He didn't say the ethics would survive. He said the whole thing stands or falls on whether a particular man, executed under Pontius Pilate, was dead on a Friday and alive on a Sunday. The Nicene Creed, in the form confirmed at Constantinople in 381 and still confessed across the churches, holds Paul to it: "on the third day he rose again in accordance with the Scriptures." This belongs to the confessional floor. It isn't a second-order question like baptism or the millennium, where faithful churches disagree and remain brothers. It is the hinge on which the rest turns.

Two distinctions keep the question honest. The first is between resurrection and resuscitation. Lazarus came back to the life he had left, and he died again later. The claim about Jesus is different: "We know that Christ, being raised from the dead, will never die again; death no longer has dominion over him" (Romans 6:9, ESV). It isn't that a heart restarted. It is that a body passed through death into a life death can't touch, the first piece of the world to come arriving ahead of schedule.

The second distinction is between an event and a meaning. Marcus Borg, debating N. T. Wright in *The Meaning of Jesus: Two Visions* (1999), argued that Easter's truth doesn't depend on anything having happened to Jesus' corpse; what matters is that his followers went on experiencing him as a living presence and that God vindicated his way. Borg was a serious and generous man, trying to keep faith open to people who could no longer believe in miracles. But his proposal gives away the one thing Paul refused to give away. If nothing happened to the body, the dead do stay dead, and the church can offer only an inspiring memory. Conservatives have their own way of emptying the claim, which is to shout it so tribally that it becomes a password rather than a report. Neither the metaphor nor the slogan is what the first witnesses said.

So the question deserves to be asked as a historical one, the way one asks whether Caesar crossed the Rubicon. The claim of this essay is narrow. The resurrection isn't provable the way a theorem is. But it is the best available explanation of a set of facts that most historians of the period, including many skeptics, already grant. The rival explanations are real, and each one, pressed hard, explains part of the evidence and has to borrow from the resurrection to explain the rest.

## The oldest evidence is older than the Gospels

The popular skeptical picture, which I held for years before I believed any of this, runs roughly as follows. The Gospels were written decades after Jesus died, in communities that loved him, and somewhere in those forty or sixty years a teacher's memory swelled into a risen Lord. It's a reasonable picture with one serious problem. The earliest statement of the resurrection isn't in a Gospel at all.

In the same letter, Paul reminds the Corinthians of what he taught them on his first visit, around the year 50, in the vocabulary of formal transmission:

> For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep. Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me. (1 Corinthians 15:3–8, ESV)

"Delivered" and "received" are the terms Jewish teachers used for handing on a fixed tradition, and the passage has the marks of something memorized: the balanced clauses, the repeated "that," the Aramaic name Cephas for Peter, the stock phrase "the twelve" when by then there were eleven. Paul is quoting. When did he receive it? He tells the Galatians that about three years after his conversion he went up to Jerusalem "to visit Cephas and remained with him fifteen days," and that he also saw "James the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:18–19, ESV). His conversion is usually dated within a few years of the crucifixion, which most historians place in 30 or 33. That puts his contact with the named witnesses, and very likely the creed itself, within about five years of the events it reports. Gary Habermas and Michael Licona, gathering the scholarly judgments in *The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus* (2004), show how far across the spectrum the early date runs. Gerd Lüdemann, a German critic who didn't believe Jesus' body was raised, placed the formation of this tradition within the first few years after the crucifixion.

Legend needs time and distance, and here there is almost none. The claim was circulating in fixed form while Peter and James were alive to be asked.

The creed says Jesus "was buried," and the Gospels fill that in: Joseph of Arimathea, a member of the council, asked Pilate for the body and laid it in a rock-cut tomb (Mark 15:43–46). Skeptics fairly ask whether Rome would release the body of a man executed for sedition, since crucified bodies were often left exposed. But practice in Judea could differ. The Torah commanded that an executed man's body "shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God" (Deuteronomy 21:23, ESV), and Josephus observes that the Jews of his day took down even crucified criminals and buried them before sunset (*Jewish War* 4.317). In 1968 an ossuary found in Jerusalem at Giv'at ha-Mivtar held the bones of a man named Yehohanan with a nail still lodged in his heel bone: a crucified man of Jesus' century who received a family burial. And a council member of the very body that condemned Jesus is an odd hero for his followers to invent.

Then the women. All four Gospels agree that the first people to find the tomb empty were women, Mary Magdalene prominent among them. In the first-century world a woman's testimony counted for little in public; Josephus, in the *Jewish Antiquities* (c. 94), reports that his people's law didn't accept women as witnesses (4.219), and the pagan critic Celsus, around 175, mocked the story as resting on a hysterical woman (Origen, *Against Celsus* 2.55). Luke admits the apostles thought the women's report "an idle tale" (Luke 24:11, ESV). Richard Bauckham, in *Gospel Women* (2002) and *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006), argues that the Gospels name these women so carefully because they were the known sources of the tradition, living guarantors whose names the first hearers would recognize. A community inventing its founding proof wouldn't choose witnesses its own world discounted.

Mark, the earliest Gospel, ends (in the form most scholars take to be original) with the women fleeing: "for trembling and astonishment had seized them, and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid" (Mark 16:8, ESV). That isn't how propaganda is written. And the earliest counter-explanation on record grants the empty tomb. Matthew reports the story the authorities spread: "His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep" (Matthew 28:13, ESV). You don't accuse someone of stealing a body that is still in its grave. The argument was never about whether the tomb was empty. It was about why.

The appearances are the other half, told with a strange mix of solidity and unfamiliarity, and without triumph. The travelers to Emmaus speak of Jesus to the stranger beside them in grief: "But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel" (Luke 24:21, ESV). Matthew records at the final mountain that "when they saw him they worshiped him, but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17, ESV). Legends polish. These accounts keep the doubt in the text.

## Why nobody was expecting this

Judaism in the Second Temple period, before the Romans destroyed the temple in 70, held a range of views about death. The Sadducees denied any resurrection. The Pharisees and many others held a hope that had grown out of the prophets and sharpened under persecution: "And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2, ESV). But in every version, resurrection was something God would do for the righteous together, at the end of the age. Martha speaks the ordinary faith of her people: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day" (John 11:24, ESV). No one expected one man to be raised alone, in the middle of history.

N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) spends more than eight hundred pages on this background. He finds that "resurrection," pagan and Jewish, always referred to a body; the soul's survival had other names. Then he shows how early Christian belief changed the Jewish hope in ways no one had anticipated. Resurrection moved from the edge of belief to the center of preaching. It split into two stages, one man now and everyone later. The raised body was transformed, physical but no longer perishable. And the one raised was the Messiah, whom no Jewish text expected to die at all. Where, Wright asks, did such a belief come from, held so early and so uniformly by people who had every reason to expect nothing of the kind?

A crucified messiah wasn't a paradox waiting to be solved. It was a contradiction in terms. The Messiah was supposed to defeat Israel's enemies and rule; a man executed by the occupying power had plainly failed, and the Torah pronounced such a death cursed (Deuteronomy 21:23). Paul knew how the message sounded: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). The period gives us a control group. Judas the Galilean led a revolt around AD 6 and, as Acts 5:37 puts it, perished. Theudas gathered followers at the Jordan in the 40s, and the Roman governor had him beheaded. Simon bar Kokhba, hailed as messiah by Rabbi Akiva, died when Rome crushed his revolt in 135. Their followers scattered or found new leaders. None claimed their man had been raised. When a would-be messiah was killed, the verdict was public and final.

Wright gives the logic its sharpest form: neither an empty tomb alone nor appearances alone would have produced the belief. An empty tomb without meetings would have been read as grave robbery. Meetings without an empty tomb would have been read as visions of the dead, which that world knew well and didn't confuse with resurrection. When the disciples first saw him, Luke says, they "thought they saw a spirit" (Luke 24:37, ESV); when Peter turned up at a prayer meeting after his release from prison, the believers said, "It is his angel!" (Acts 12:15, ESV). It took the tomb and the encounters together, Wright argues, to make first-century Jews say "resurrection" about a crucified man.

Then there are the people. The disciples scattered at the arrest, and Peter denied knowing Jesus. Within weeks, according to Acts, the same men were preaching in the city that had executed him. Two conversions are harder still to explain from inside the movement. James, the brother of Jesus, hadn't believed: "For not even his brothers believed in him" (John 7:5, ESV), and Mark records that his family once went out to seize him, saying he was out of his mind (Mark 3:21). Yet the creed lists an appearance to James, he came to lead the Jerusalem church, and Josephus reports that around 62 the high priest Ananus had him stoned (*Jewish Antiquities* 20.200). Most scholars accept that brief passage as genuine, unlike the fuller reference to Jesus in book 18 (18.63–64), which was at least reworked by later Christian copyists. Skeptical brothers don't usually die for worshiping their siblings. And Paul wasn't grieving Jesus at all. He was hunting his followers: "you have heard of my former life in Judaism, how I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13, ESV).

A distinction belongs here, because Christians often use this point badly. It's said that the apostles died for their testimony, though the evidence is good for Peter, Paul and James and rests on later tradition for most of the others. More to the point, a martyr's death proves sincerity. It doesn't prove truth. What the change in Peter, James and Paul does establish is that fraud is the wrong explanation. They weren't lying. They were convinced. That moves the argument to the only place it can honestly go: what convinced them?

## The skeptic's case at full strength

Christians lose this argument most often when they win it too easily. The alternatives have a long history, and they deserve to be stated in the form their own defenders would sign.

The history of doubt begins inside the New Testament, with the charge of theft. In 1778 Gotthold Lessing published a fragment by the late Hermann Samuel Reimarus that revived it: the disappointed disciples took the body and invented a spiritual messiah. The rationalist H. E. G. Paulus, in 1828, proposed instead that Jesus had been taken down unconscious and revived in the tomb. A fellow skeptic ended that theory's respectability. David Friedrich Strauss, whose *Life of Jesus* (1835) had already treated the Gospel miracles as myth, pointed out in *A New Life of Jesus* (1864) that a half-dead man creeping from a tomb, badly in need of care, could have awakened his friends' pity but never the conviction that he had conquered death. Strauss thought the belief arose from visions, and that suggestion is where serious skepticism has lived ever since.

Its most thorough modern form is Gerd Lüdemann's *The Resurrection of Jesus: History, Experience, Theology* (1994). Lüdemann accepted that Peter and Paul really had experiences of seeing Jesus and argued that both can be explained psychologically. Peter, shattered by grief and the guilt of his denial, saw the one he had failed, and the vision released him. Paul, inwardly divided over the very faith he was persecuting, broke under the strain into a vision of its Lord. Others followed Peter, since visions spread through communities charged with expectation and loss. The body, Lüdemann concluded, decayed in the ground.

Bart Ehrman sets out a related case in *How Jesus Became God* (2014). He grants that Jesus was crucified and that some followers, Peter and Paul among them, sincerely believed they saw him alive afterward; indeed he treats that belief as the engine of everything that followed. But he doubts the burial. Romans usually left crucified bodies on display as a deterrent, he argues, and a member of the condemning council is unlikely to have given Jesus an honorable tomb. Without a known tomb, the empty-tomb stories are later legend, grown through decades of retelling. Ehrman also points to research on bereavement: many people who lose someone close report seeing or sensing that person afterward. And a historian, he argues, can't affirm a miracle in any case, because history deals in what probably happened, and a miracle is the least probable of events. That point has an older father. David Hume argued in *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) that since the uniform experience of mankind is that the dead stay dead, no testimony can establish a resurrection unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the event. Witnesses err constantly, so the scale never tips.

The most searching treatment comes from a scholar who isn't a skeptic. Dale Allison, a New Testament historian and a Christian, gave two large books to the question, *Resurrecting Jesus* (2005) and *The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History* (2021), and did much of the skeptics' homework better than they had. He gathered the modern literature on apparitions of the dead and showed that such experiences are common and are sometimes reported by several people at once. That wounds a favorite apologetic line, that hallucinations are always private and never happen to groups. As a flat statement it claims more than the evidence allows, and Christians who repeat it should stop. Critics add that the accounts grow more physical over time, from Paul's bare list to Luke's Jesus eating fish and John's Thomas invited to touch the wounds, while Paul himself calls the risen body "a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44, ESV).

At full strength, then, the case runs this way. Some followers had powerful experiences of Jesus after his death, of a kind well documented among the bereaved. Devout Jews primed by apocalyptic hope interpreted them in the only vocabulary large enough to hold them, and the empty tomb came later, as the story acquired a body to match the belief. And the historian must prefer any natural account to a miracle. This isn't a straw man. It's the view of a large part of the academy, and a thoughtful reader can hold it without dishonesty.

## Weighing the explanations

Start with the common ground, which is larger than either side usually admits. E. P. Sanders, a leading historian of Jesus and no apologist, near the close of *The Historical Figure of Jesus* (1993) judges it a fact that Jesus' followers, and later Paul, had resurrection experiences, and says plainly that he does not know what reality gave rise to them. That is the honest shape of the question. The experiences are granted. The dispute is over their cause.

The grief-vision account explains a real part of the evidence, and Allison was right to make Christians face it. But it struggles in three places. The first is the interpretation. Visions of the dead meant then what they mean in the bereavement literature now: the dead person is at peace, or near. That is the category Luke's disciples and the prayer meeting in Acts reached for. Nobody concluded from such experiences that a dead man had been bodily raised and the end of the age had broken into the middle of history. Something pushed the first Christians past the available category into one that didn't yet exist. The second is the people. Grief can account for Peter and Mary. It can't easily account for James, who hadn't believed, or for Paul, who wasn't mourning Jesus but trying to destroy his movement. Lüdemann's answer, that Paul was unconsciously drawn to what he persecuted, is possible, but it reads a hidden conflict into a man who remembered his former self as confident: "as to zeal, a persecutor of the church; as to righteousness under the law, blameless" (Philippians 3:6, ESV). The third is the body. A vision leaves the corpse where it was, and the resurrection was preached first in Jerusalem, within walking distance of the grave.

That is why Ehrman is right to press the burial. The evidence for it stands in the creed Paul received within a few years of the crucifixion, where "buried" followed by "raised" is itself a claim about a grave, and it fits what Josephus and the Yehohanan ossuary tell us about Judean practice. Allison, for all his concessions to the skeptics, didn't think the burial and tomb traditions could be waved away as late inventions.

The legend account runs into the calendar. Five years isn't enough for a myth to overwrite the memory of people alive and leading the community, and the physicality critics find growing in the later Gospels is already in Paul, read in his own world. When Paul contrasts a "natural body" with a "spiritual body," the Greek adjectives describe what animates the body, not what it is made of: ordinary human life in the one case, the Spirit of God in the other. Paul's image is a seed sown and the same thing raised transformed, which is why Wright argues that "spiritual body" never meant a ghost.

The resurrection hypothesis has one great advantage over its rivals. It explains the whole set at once: the tomb, the appearances, the doubting brother and the persecutor, an unexpected belief held early and uniformly, and a crucified messiah whose movement grew instead of dying. The rivals each rescue a corner. Theft explains the tomb and not the sincerity; visions explain some experiences and not the tomb or the category; legend explains later texture and not the early creed. To cover the field a skeptic has to stack theories, and each addition makes the stack less likely.

So the weight finally rests where Hume put it, on the scale before the evidence arrives. Wolfhart Pannenberg, the German theologian who insisted that the resurrection be treated as a historical claim, argued in *Jesus: God and Man* (1968) against Ernst Troeltsch's principle of analogy, set out in 1898, which ruled out in advance any event without parallel in ordinary experience. Such a rule, Pannenberg objected, decides in advance what history is allowed to contain. His deeper point was about context: Jesus had claimed God's own authority among a people who hoped for the resurrection of the dead, and the Easter event, on Pannenberg's reading, was God's confirmation of that claim. Put in terms of probability, a resurrection is wildly improbable as a random event in a closed universe. It isn't improbable in the same way if there is a God, and if the man in question had made that claim among a people whose Scriptures expected God to raise the dead. Then the resurrection isn't a freak. It's the vindication of a claim already on the table.

I held Hume's scale as an axiom for years before I saw what it was doing. If there's no God, the resurrection is the least likely explanation, and any strained natural account ought to win. But whether there is a God is precisely what's in question, and the scale answers it quietly before the investigation begins. That's a decision presented as a finding. The honest skeptic can still say the prior is too heavy for him to move. He can't honestly say the evidence moved him. And Christians are guilty of the mirror image. Plenty of us decided the answer in childhood and have never looked at the evidence, and a faith that has never looked is more fragile than it knows. Both of us owe the question more than an inheritance.

## If it happened

When Paul stood before King Agrippa, he said he could speak boldly because the king already knew about these things, "for this has not been done in a corner" (Acts 26:26, ESV). That is the posture of the whole early Christian claim: a report about a known man, executed in a known city under a known governor, offered to anyone willing to check. The faith was born making itself checkable, and it has never been able to withdraw the offer.

So it is fair to end with the other half of Paul's conditional. Having spelled out what follows if Christ has not been raised, he turned: "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV). Firstfruits is a harvest word, the first sheaf that promises the rest of the field. If it happened, it isn't a private miracle for one man. It is the first piece of the new creation, and it tells us what God intends for everything else he has made.

That has consequences no reader can keep at a safe distance. If it happened, the verdict passed on Jesus by the council and by Rome was overturned by a higher court. Every judgment power passes on the weak, the executed and the forgotten is therefore provisional. If it happened, the body matters, because God didn't discard Jesus' body and won't discard ours; the embodied life of a Tuesday, the work, the marriage, the care for a dying parent, isn't a waiting room for a purely spiritual afterlife but the material God means to raise. Paul draws exactly that conclusion: "Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58, ESV).

And if it happened, it is also a judgment. In Athens Paul told the philosophers that God "has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed; and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead" (Acts 17:31, ESV). The empty tomb means the one who taught the Sermon on the Mount has been confirmed as the standard we'll be measured by, and none of us, the preacher very much included, meets it. That's why the resurrection comes with forgiveness in its hands. The risen Jesus met Peter, who had denied him three times, not with a rebuke but by asking three times whether he loved him, and then giving him work to do (John 21:15–17). The event that exposes us is the one that makes restoration possible.

None of this settles the question for the reader who isn't persuaded. You can weigh everything here and still conclude it didn't happen, and that's a respectable place to stand. But you can't honestly conclude that the question is trivial, or that it was settled long ago by people who looked and found nothing. The ones who looked hardest, believers and skeptics alike, found a set of facts that won't lie down.

For those of us who confess it, the harder question may be the one we ask ourselves on Monday. We say the creed. We sing it every spring. And then most of us, most weeks, live as though the dead stay dead, as though what we build and lose and bury is simply gone, as though power's verdict were final. If the tomb is empty, that way of living is the thing that isn't true. The matter was supposed to be closed when the stone was rolled across the entrance. It wouldn't stay closed, and it still hasn't.
