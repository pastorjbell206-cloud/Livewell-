Most Christians who have lived through depression or disabling anxiety can name the moment someone at church told them to pray more. It is rarely said with cruelty, and usually by someone who means every word. But it lands as a verdict: you are still sick because you have not believed hard enough. Some of them stop telling anyone. Some leave the church. Some come close to leaving more than that.

So before anything else, a plain word to anyone reading this from inside the dark. This essay is not medical or mental-health advice, and it is written by a pastor, not a physician. If you are thinking about ending your life, or you are afraid you might, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, at any hour, and a trained person will answer. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. If the heaviness has been with you for weeks, if it is taking your sleep, your appetite, your work or your ability to love the people near you, tell a doctor, and ask about seeing a therapist or a psychiatrist. That is not a failure of faith. Scripture, as we will see, gives an exhausted prophet bread and sleep before it gives him a sermon.

The real question is not whether Christians should care about mental health. It is what the church believes mental suffering is. Beneath the counsel to "just pray about it" sits a theology, usually unexamined, that treats the mind as the seat of the spirit and therefore treats a sick mind as a sick soul. This essay turns on one distinction: between reading a Christian's anguish as sin or weak faith to be corrected, and reading it as suffering in a fallen body, which the church is called to carry with the person who bears it. As a general account of depression and anxiety, the first reading is wrong, and it has done real harm.

## Why "Just Pray More" Fails the People Who Hear It

The failure is not a rumor. In 2014 Lifeway Research, in a study co-sponsored by Focus on the Family, surveyed Protestant pastors alongside people living with acute mental illness and members of their families. The broad picture was sobering: most pastors said they seldom spoke to their congregations about mental illness, while sufferers and their families said they wanted the church to talk about it openly, so that it would no longer be a taboo. Silence teaches. A congregation that hears about cancer, grief and divorce from the pulpit and never hears the word depression learns what can be said in church and what has to be hidden.

Matthew Stanford, a Christian psychologist and neuroscientist, describes in *Grace for the Afflicted* (2008) a pattern anyone inside the church will recognize. Believers who brought serious mental illness to their congregations were told it was really sin, or weak faith, or the work of a demon, and some were counseled to set aside the treatment they had been prescribed. Stanford treats serious mental illness as real disease while granting that sin and spiritual struggle may be present too, and he refuses both reductions, the clinical one that sees only chemistry and the churchly one that sees only sin.

The psychiatrist Curt Thompson argues in *The Soul of Shame* (2015) that shame works by isolation: it drives us into hiding from the very community that could heal us. The believer who is told her depression is a faith deficiency now carries two afflictions, the illness and the fresh evidence that she is spiritually defective. The second demands concealment. She stops reporting the darkness, because every report has been received as unbelief, and the concealment cuts her off from the people who might hold her, which feeds the illness. No one inside that machine means harm, which is exactly why it is so hard to shut off.

There is a hierarchy at work, too. When a church member is diagnosed with cancer, meals appear, the prayer list carries her name, and no one asks what sin opened the door. When a church member is diagnosed with severe anxiety, she gets advice. A church that would never tell a diabetic to stop taking insulin and pray instead has often told the depressed exactly that. The difference is not doctrine. It is which suffering makes us uncomfortable.

The arithmetic behind "just pray more" is older than any of us. Walking past a man born blind, Jesus's own disciples asked, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2, ESV). They had two columns, suffering and somebody's sin. Jesus refused the ledger: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3, ESV). The church that turns every downcast soul back into a charge sheet is running the disciples' arithmetic over Jesus's objection. Some version of that counsel has come out of my own mouth. I was handed the wall between faith and medicine already built, and I reinforced it before I understood what it was.

## The Case for Caution, and Where It Breaks

The church's wariness about psychiatry deserves a fair hearing, because it guards something true. Scripture treats guilt, fear and despair as matters between a person and God. For much of the twentieth century the dominant schools of psychology treated guilt as a neurotic symptom rather than a moral signal, and a pastor who sent every troubled member to a therapist was conceding that the gospel had nothing to say to the human heart.

That was the burden behind Jay Adams's *Competent to Counsel* (1970), which launched the nouthetic counseling movement. Adams drew on the psychologist O. Hobart Mowrer, who argued against much of his own field that real guilt is real and cannot be talked away as illness. Adams concluded that much of what was labeled mental illness was better understood as sin and its consequences, though he granted that genuinely organic illness belongs to physicians. His defenders would put it this way: he was trying to give troubled Christians back to their pastors and their Bibles, and to insist that the gospel speaks to the whole heart. His critics, among them Christian psychologists and psychiatrists and, in time, some within the biblical counseling movement he founded, answered that the model read suffering as sin too readily and gave the body too little weight. Philip Rieff, no evangelical, saw the larger shift in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966): the West was trading a self formed by commanding truths for a psychological self whose highest aim was to feel well. And from inside psychiatry, Allen Frances, who chaired the task force behind the DSM-IV, cautioned in *Saving Normal* (2013) against diagnostic inflation, the drift by which ordinary grief gets reclassified as disorder and medicated.

All of that can be granted. Not every dark season is a pathology. Guilt is sometimes guilt, and conviction is sometimes the Holy Spirit at work. A church that outsourced every inner struggle to a prescription would have abandoned its own competence: confession, meaning, community and hope. There is a progressive failure that mirrors the conservative one, a therapeutic Christianity that dissolves sin into trauma and redemption into coping, until a person passes through a whole crisis without anyone asking what God might be doing in it. A framework with no category for a guilty conscience has not become more compassionate than the gospel. It has become smaller than the patient.

But the objection breaks at a specific point. It assumes that because Scripture addresses the inner life, every affliction of the inner life must be a spiritual failure. It has quietly exempted the brain from the fall, as though the one organ we think with could not be diseased, injured or worn down by trauma, so that whatever goes wrong there must have been chosen. The creation "was subjected to futility" (Romans 8:20, ESV), and our bodies are part of that creation.

So the distinction has to be drawn with care. Sin, or a failure of trust, calls for repentance and is answered with confession and forgiveness. Suffering in a fallen body and a fallen world calls for care, patience, treatment and the company of the church. The two can coexist in one life, and telling them apart takes wisdom and often more than one kind of help. But treating the second as the first is its own kind of false witness. It accuses a sufferer of something she has not done.

It also helps to sort the questions by how much hangs on them. What binds every Christian is that a person in anguish is met with care and not with verdicts, that suffering is not in itself evidence of sin, and that the body of Christ carries its weaker members. Where the line between sorrow and sickness runs in a particular life, when medication is wise, and how pastoral and clinical care should divide the labor are real second-order questions; a nouthetic counselor and a Christian psychiatrist can argue them in good faith and remain brothers. What neither may do is leave the person alone. The church that says only "pray harder" and the church that says only "seek help" have both handed the sufferer off, one to shame and the other to a referral list, instead of staying in the room.

## Elijah Under the Broom Tree, and the Prayers the Bible Kept

In 1 Kings 18 Elijah stands on Mount Carmel against the prophets of Baal, calls down fire, and sees the drought broken. In the next chapter a single threat from Jezebel undoes him. He runs as far as Beersheba and on into the wilderness, sits down under a broom tree, and asks to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers" (1 Kings 19:4, ESV). The collapse comes after the summit, and no theology that makes despair the opposite of faith can explain it.

What God does next is the whole lesson. He does not rebuke Elijah or tell him to claim a promise. Elijah sleeps, and an angel wakes him, touches him and says, "Arise and eat" (1 Kings 19:5, ESV). He eats and sleeps again, and the angel comes a second time with food and the tenderest explanation in the chapter: the road ahead is more than Elijah has in him (1 Kings 19:7). God says it without reproach, as a plain fact about a finite creature he made. Before a single word of theology, he tends the body.

Only then, at Horeb, does the word come, and it comes not in the wind or the earthquake or the fire but in "the sound of a low whisper" (1 Kings 19:12, ESV). It comes as a question, asked once in the cave and again after the whisper: "What are you doing here, Elijah?" (1 Kings 19:9, 13, ESV). Elijah answers twice in the distorted arithmetic of despair: I alone am left. God corrects the facts gently, telling him of seven thousand in Israel who have not bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18), and he gives Elijah a companion. Elisha is part of the cure. Sleep, bread, a question, the truth and a friend, in that order. The divine response to a faithful man's collapse was not a lecture. It was rest and a meal.

Elijah is not an exception. Psalms 42 and 43 are widely read as a single poem, bound by a refrain that appears three times (42:5, 42:11, 43:5): "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?" (Psalm 42:5, ESV). The psalmist, whose tears have been his food day and night (Psalm 42:3), argues with his own soul, tells it to hope, and sinks again, and the refrain returns because the argument has to be made more than once. That is often what faith in depression looks like: a stubborn, repeated turning toward God from inside a darkness that has not lifted.

Psalm 88 goes further. It is the darkest lament in the Psalter, and it never turns. The psalmist's soul is full of troubles and his life draws near to Sheol (Psalm 88:3), and the final line is not a vow of praise but a report: "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18, ESV). Yet the psalm opens by addressing "O LORD, God of my salvation" (Psalm 88:1, ESV). The psalmist asks why God casts his soul away and hides his face (Psalm 88:14), and he keeps praying anyway, and the Holy Spirit kept that prayer in the church's hymnbook. That means a church that insists every testimony end in victory is more squeamish than the Bible it preaches.

Jeremiah, beaten and put in the stocks by the priest Pashhur, accuses God of overpowering him and then sings praise to the God who "has delivered the life of the needy from the hand of evildoers" (Jeremiah 20:13, ESV). The very next verse curses his birth: "Cursed be the day on which I was born!" (Jeremiah 20:14, ESV). The chapter ends with his question about why he was born to see toil and sorrow (Jeremiah 20:18). Praise and despair sit side by side, unreconciled, in one of God's most faithful servants, and Scripture does not treat the despair as proof that the praise was false.

None of these texts diagnoses anyone, and we should not pretend Elijah or the psalmist had a condition from a clinical manual. The point is simpler. The Bible records anguish of mind in its prophets and puts it in the mouths of its worshipers, and it shows God meeting it with provision rather than rebuke.

## Gethsemane, Paul in Asia, and What Philippians 4 Actually Says

The Gospels give us a Savior in a garden. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus told Peter, James and John, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me" (Matthew 26:38, ESV). He fell on his face and asked the Father whether the cup might pass. Hebrews, looking back, says that "in the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death" (Hebrews 5:7, ESV). The sinless one went into anguish, prayed through it, and did not sin in it. The presence of anguish in a human soul proves nothing about the absence of faith.

Paul is just as honest about himself. He tells the Corinthians what happened to him in Asia: "For we were so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself" (2 Corinthians 1:8, ESV). The man who had seen the risen Christ was crushed past his capacity, and he does not explain it away. He says it taught him to rely not on himself "but on God who raises the dead" (2 Corinthians 1:9, ESV). The hope he reaches for is resurrection, not a technique, and it comes through the despair rather than around it.

That brings us to the verse most often used as a weapon against anxious Christians. "Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God" (Philippians 4:6, ESV). Read alone, it can sound like a command to stop feeling a feeling. Read in its setting, it sounds very different. Paul is in chains (Philippians 1:13), uncertain whether he will live or die (Philippians 1:20), writing to a church under pressure from outside and troubled by a quarrel inside (Philippians 4:2). The verse just before says, "The Lord is at hand" (Philippians 4:5, ESV). This is a prisoner telling a strained congregation where to take the weight, not a comfortable man scolding the fearful.

What the sentence asks is that in everything, you bring it. What Paul sets against anxiety is prayer, rather than a calm you manufacture and present to God as proof of faith: naming the need, asking, giving thanks, handing it over while it is still a mess. And the promise is not that the feeling will vanish on schedule but that "the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:7, ESV). Guarding is a military word. A garrison guards a city that is still under threat.

Paul's own vocabulary confirms it. The verb he uses for anxiety in 4:6 is the one he uses earlier, with approval, for Timothy, who will be "genuinely concerned for your welfare" (Philippians 2:20, ESV). Elsewhere he speaks of "the daily pressure on me of my anxiety for all the churches" (2 Corinthians 11:28, ESV), with no hint of confession. For Paul, anxious care in itself is not sin; what he calls the church away from is carrying it alone.

Read this way, the verse is an invitation, and it says nothing against medicine, any more than "give us this day our daily bread" forbids going to work. A Christian with an anxiety disorder who prays and also takes the medication her psychiatrist prescribes is not hedging her bets. She is receiving her daily bread from more than one of the Father's hands.

## The Church Has Been in the Dark Before

The stigma can look ancient. The history is more merciful than our recent habits. For most of Christian history the care of the soul and the care of the mind were not split into separate professions, and the best physicians of souls assumed the soul had a body.

The desert monks of the fourth century knew a heaviness they called acedia, the noonday demon, which Evagrius of Pontus catalogued in his *Praktikos* as the most oppressive of the afflicting thoughts. Acedia is not simply depression, but the tradition's response is instructive. John Cassian, in his *Institutes* early in the fifth century, treated acedia as a vice to be fought, but the remedy he prescribed for the afflicted monk was patience, prayer and steady manual work, after the example of the apostle Paul, who worked with his own hands. The remedy assumed that what a person did with his body had something to do with the state of his soul.

Martin Luther knew the dark from the inside. He called his worst seasons *Anfechtungen*, assaults or trials: the terror of standing before God without assurance, the sense of being abandoned and accused. These were spiritual trials in his own understanding, and we should not diagnose him across five centuries, but they were bodily ordeals too, and in the summer of 1527 one brought him near collapse. In letters of counsel, including his counsel in 1530 to Jerome Weller, the melancholy young man who lived in his house as tutor to his children (collected in Theodore Tappert's edition, *Luther: Letters of Spiritual Counsel*, 1955), he urged the melancholic not to be alone, to seek company and good food, and not to argue endlessly with their gloomy thoughts. He did not tell them the trial proved they lacked faith. In 1539 he wrote that three things make a theologian, prayer, meditation and *tentatio*, which he glossed as *Anfechtung*. For Luther the dark could be a school of faith.

William Cowper, among the finest English poets of the eighteenth century, was tormented by depression for most of his life. After a breakdown in 1763 that brought him near death, he came to faith in 1764 under the care of Nathaniel Cotton, an evangelical physician at St Albans, and later settled in Olney near its curate, John Newton. Newton did not rebuke his friend's darkness. When Cowper collapsed again in 1773, Newton and his wife, Mary, took him into the vicarage and cared for him there for more than a year. Together they produced the *Olney Hymns* (1779), which include Cowper's "God Moves in a Mysterious Way." Cowper spent much of his remaining life convinced he was shut out from grace. Newton never stopped believing otherwise, and never stopped being his friend.

Charles Spurgeon, the most famous preacher of the next century, said publicly what the church often whispers. He carried depression through much of his ministry, sharpened by the disaster at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall on 19 October 1856, when a false cry of fire in his packed congregation set off a crush that killed seven people. He was twenty-two. In *Lectures to My Students* (1875) he gave a whole lecture, "The Minister's Fainting Fits," to preparing young pastors for depression, telling them that such seasons come to most who labor in the gospel, that the causes are often bodily, and that the darkness is no proof of unbelief. No one who reads Spurgeon could accuse him of a thin view of sin. He simply refused to confuse sin with sickness.

Then the paths diverged. With the rise of modern medicine and the asylum in the nineteenth century, a division of labor hardened into a wall. The brain went to the doctors and the soul to the clergy, and each grew suspicious of the other. Freud's generation often treated religion as a symptom, and much of American evangelicalism answered in kind, treating psychology as a rival gospel and antidepressants as a crutch for the faithless. We built that wall. Luther, Newton and Spurgeon would not have recognized it.

## One Person, Body and Soul: Medicine as Common Grace

The wall was never biblical. Scripture does not describe a soul temporarily housed in a machine. It describes a creature. "Then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature" (Genesis 2:7, ESV). The Hebrew word there, *nephesh*, often translated "soul," names the whole living person. Christians still debate the metaphysics of body and soul, but the creeds settle what matters here: we confess the resurrection of the body, not the soul's escape from it. Tertullian, early in the third century, called the flesh the hinge of salvation. A faith that confesses the incarnation and the empty tomb cannot treat the body as spiritually irrelevant, and the brain is part of the body.

So the mind can suffer in all the ways the body can. It can be injured, inflamed, starved of sleep, altered by chemistry, shaped by trauma. God "knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14, ESV). Paul told Timothy, "No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments" (1 Timothy 5:23, ESV). He did not tell him to pray his stomach well. He gave him the remedy he had, and he traveled with "Luke the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14, ESV).

The Reformed tradition calls gifts like these common grace: God's goodness to all people through the ordinary gifts of creation, including knowledge and skill. John Calvin argued in the *Institutes* (1559) that wherever truth appears, even in pagan writers, it comes from the Spirit of God, that to despise it is to dishonor the Spirit, and he counted medicine among those gifts. On that account a well-tested antidepressant, a skilled psychiatrist, a trained therapist and a night of real sleep are not rivals to grace. They are among the ways grace arrives, through hands God has trained.

Kathryn Greene-McCreight, an Episcopal priest and theologian, wrote *Darkness Is My Only Companion* (2006) out of her own life with bipolar disorder, and the book refuses both easy answers. She has known seasons when she could not pray at all, and she describes being carried then by the prayers of the church and of friends. She treats psychiatric care as a gift rather than a defeat, and faith as something that continues in the dark rather than something the dark disproves. Her title borrows the last line of Psalm 88 as the Book of Common Prayer renders it.

There is a justice question here too. Serious distress tends to concentrate where poverty, unstable housing and chronic powerlessness concentrate, and the care runs the other way. The Community Mental Health Act of 1963 promised local care to replace the old state hospitals, and the hospitals emptied while the promised network was never fully built or funded. The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008 required many health plans that cover mental health care to do so on terms comparable to physical health care, and the gap between that law and many people's experience remains wide. Christians may disagree about how public care should be funded. They may not pretend the question belongs to someone else. The prophets never treated the plight of the needy as someone else's business.

## What the Church Is For in the Dark

If medicine is a gift, what is the church for? John Swinton, who worked for years as a psychiatric nurse before becoming a practical theologian at Aberdeen, answers in *Resurrecting the Person* (2000) with a word the church already owns: friendship. People with serious mental illness, he argues, are too often lost behind their diagnoses, known as cases rather than persons, and what the church can give, which no clinic can prescribe, is the kind of friendship Jesus offered to those everyone else kept at a distance. That is Elisha sent to Elijah. A therapist sees someone for an hour a week. A congregation can walk beside her for years.

In practice this means naming depression and anxiety from the pulpit as afflictions, not scandals. It means testimonies that include the therapist and the prescription, and meals for the family whose son is in a psychiatric ward, just as for the family whose son is in surgery. It means pastors who know which counselors and psychiatrists in town can be trusted and who say plainly that referral is care. "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2, ESV) was never limited to the burdens that make us comfortable.

None of this promises a cure on demand. Some who pray faithfully and take their medication will carry the dark for years. Paul says that "we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23, ESV). Such groaning is what faith sounds like in a body not yet raised. On the cross Christ took the opening cry of Psalm 22 as his own, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46, ESV). If forsakenness passed through the soul of the sinless one, then darkness in the soul of a believer is not proof that God has gone. And the tomb he left empty is the promise that the body, brain included, will be raised whole.

If you are reading this from under the broom tree, telling someone is not a failure of faith. It is Elijah eating. Call or text 988, tell a doctor, and tell one person who will sit with you. For the rest of us, who have handed out verses where bread was needed, the call is simpler and harder than any program. Repent of the wall, and go sit under the tree.
