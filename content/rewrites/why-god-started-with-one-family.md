There is a question that bothers honest readers of the Bible, and it surfaces at the twelfth chapter of Genesis. The first eleven chapters are about everything: the heavens and the earth, the first man and woman, the first murder, a flood that undoes creation, a tower whose builders are scattered across the earth. Then the camera stops sweeping and settles on one man. Abram is seventy-five, his wife is barren, and the text gives no reason for the attention it suddenly pays him, no virtue, no achievement, no sign that he was looking for God at all. From there on, the story belongs to his descendants: one household, then twelve tribes, then a small nation pressed between the empires of the Nile and the Euphrates.

If God meant to rescue the whole world, why did he start with one family? On its face the narrowing looks like favoritism, a God who loves everyone in principle and one clan in practice. Many readers have closed the book at exactly this point, and not all of them were looking for an excuse. Some had watched the idea of a chosen people do real damage in history and wanted no part of a God who picks sides.

They deserve a real answer, and it turns on a distinction the Bible draws more carefully than its critics usually notice. **God did not choose one family instead of the world. He chose one family for the sake of the world. Favoritism is grounded in the worth of the one favored and ends with him; election, as Scripture tells it, is grounded in the love of the one who chooses and is aimed past the chosen toward everyone else. And the family God chose was no model household. It was a house of rivalry, deceit and favoritism, so the promise to bless all the families of the earth began its work inside a family that needed the blessing as badly as anyone.**

The Bible's great rescue opens in a tent, with a marriage under strain, a couple who can't have children, and an argument about who will inherit the house.

## Babel and the Eleven Chapters Before Abraham

Genesis doesn't let the call of Abraham be read on its own. Chapters 1 through 11 trace a widening wound. Adam and Eve reach for what was forbidden; Cain kills Abel; by Genesis 6 the earth is "filled with violence" (Genesis 6:11). Even after the flood, Noah lies drunk in his tent and a curse falls on his grandson. The Hebrew verb for cursing, *'arar*, falls five times in these chapters: on the serpent (3:14), the ground (3:17), Cain (4:11), the ground again at Noah's naming (5:29), and Canaan (9:25).

Gerhard von Rad, whose Genesis commentary appeared in German between 1949 and 1953 and in English in 1961, noticed a pattern here. Each sin brings judgment, and each judgment carries a quiet token of grace. God clothes the man and woman he expels (3:21), marks Cain so no one will kill him (4:15), and binds himself never again to destroy the earth by flood (9:11). Then comes Babel, and the pattern breaks. The builders are scattered, and no mitigating grace follows. Von Rad argued that the primeval history ends on this unresolved note deliberately, leaving a question hanging over chapter 11: what is God's relation now to the nations he has scattered? The answer is the call of Abraham.

Babel is the counter-story. The builders say, "Come, let us build ourselves a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, and let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be dispersed over the face of the whole earth" (Genesis 11:4). They build upward, gather inward, and want a name they've made themselves. God's word to Abram reverses each move. He is sent outward, and the name he'll carry is one God gives, not one he seizes. And the last thing Genesis says before the call is a dead end: "Now Sarai was barren; she had no child" (Genesis 11:30). Human greatness at Babel and human impossibility in Sarai's tent stand side by side. God begins with the second.

> Now the LORD said to Abram, "Go from your country and your kindred and your father's house to the land that I will show you. And I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and him who dishonors you I will curse, and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed." (Genesis 12:1–3)

Count the words for blessing in those verses and there are five, answering the five curses before them. The blessing is plural and the curse singular: "those who bless you," but "him who dishonors you." And the last clause, the one we skip, is the reason for all the others. The word translated "families" is the word Genesis 10 uses for the "clans" of the nations spreading across the earth after the flood. The table of nations turns out to be a list of addresses, every one a place the promise is meant to arrive.

Christopher J. H. Wright built *The Mission of God* (2006) around this hinge. Genesis 1 through 11 lays out the problem, a ruined world and divided nations; Genesis 12 launches God's answer, which is the election of one people not away from the nations but for them. In Wright's formulation, paraphrased, it's less that God has a mission for his people than that God has a people for his mission.

## What God Promised Abraham in Genesis 12, 15 and 17

The first word of the call costs more than a modern reader feels. In the ancient Near East the household wasn't a private refuge from the world; it was the world. The Hebrew phrase is *bet 'ab*, the father's house, and the archaeologist Lawrence Stager showed in a 1985 article how thoroughly it shaped village life in early Israel: land, labor, protection, name and inheritance all ran through it. Joshua reminds Israel that Terah, Abraham's father, "served other gods" beyond the Euphrates (Joshua 24:2), and two generations later Rachel steals her father's household gods when she flees (Genesis 31:19). To leave the father's house was to leave everything that told an ancient person who he was. God takes that structure apart and promises to build a household on a different foundation: not inheritance, but promise.

Years later, in Genesis 15, Abram complains: "O Lord GOD, what will you give me, for I continue childless, and the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus?" (Genesis 15:2). God takes him outside to count the stars, "So shall your offspring be," and then comes the verse Paul would build on: "And he believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness" (15:5–6).

What follows is stranger, and it's the heart of the covenant. Abram cuts animals in half and lays the pieces opposite one another. This was an ancient form of binding oath; Jeremiah 34:18 describes men who cut a calf in two and passed between its parts, a self-curse that said, may this be done to me if I break my word. But Abram doesn't walk between the pieces. A deep sleep falls on him, and after dark "a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces" (15:17). God alone walks the path of the self-curse while the one who received the promise sleeps. The covenant's security never rested on Abraham.

Genesis 17 comes when Abram is ninety-nine, thirteen years after Ishmael's birth. God renames him, "for I have made you the father of a multitude of nations" (17:5), and gives the covenant its sign. Circumcision is for every male in the house, "whether born in your house or bought with your money from any foreigner who is not of your offspring" (17:12). From its first day the covenant's mark was cut into bodies that weren't Abraham's by blood. Ishmael, about to be set aside as the line of promise, is circumcised that same day (17:26), and God says of him, "I have blessed him and will make him fruitful and multiply him greatly" (17:20). The boundary was porous from the start, and the one not chosen for the line was not abandoned.

The plainest statement of why God chose Abraham comes on the road to Sodom. The LORD says that "all the nations of the earth shall be blessed in him," and gives the reason: "For I have chosen him, that he may command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice, so that the LORD may bring to Abraham what he has promised him" (Genesis 18:18–19). Wright reads this as a compressed map of the Old Testament: election, then a household that practices righteousness and justice, then the blessing of the nations. The middle term is a family. And the first thing Abraham does after hearing why he was chosen is plead for a city of strangers, asking, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (18:25).

## The Strongest Objection: Why One Family, Why One People?

The objection deserves its full strength first. In 1730 the English deist Matthew Tindal published *Christianity as Old as the Creation*. A perfectly good and just God, he reasoned, would give every human being equal access to what salvation requires, so true religion must be universal, available to reason in every age and nation. A revelation given to one people in one corner of the world, reaching most of humanity late or never, can't be the work of a God who loves all his creatures equally. On this view particularity is not a mystery. It's evidence against the claim.

A sharper moral version came later. In *The Curse of Cain* (1997), the literary scholar Regina Schwartz argued that the biblical logic of one God and one chosen people forms identity by exclusion and rests on an assumption of scarcity: there is only so much blessing, and someone must be left out. Esau's cry after Jacob has stolen their father's blessing puts the logic she describes in a single line: "Have you but one blessing, my father? Bless me, even me also, O my father" (Genesis 27:38). If blessing is scarce, the chosen will fight to keep it and the unchosen to take it.

History gives her plenty of evidence, and an honest Christian won't pretend otherwise. Afrikaner nationalism made a sacred memory of the vow sworn before the Battle of Blood River in 1838 and read its history as a covenant with God. And the church, having claimed Israel's election as its inheritance, spent centuries treating the Jewish people with contempt, which reached one of its ugliest points when Martin Luther published *On the Jews and Their Lies* in 1543. The idea of a chosen people has been used as a weapon, and those of us who believe in it have handled it that way.

The prophets said much of this first. Moses tells Israel, "It was not because you were more in number than any other people that the LORD set his love on you and chose you, for you were the fewest of all peoples, but it is because the LORD loves you and is keeping the oath that he swore to your fathers" (Deuteronomy 7:7–8). At Sinai Israel is called "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation," and the reason given is that "all the earth is mine" (Exodus 19:5–6); a priest exists for others. Amos takes the phrase from Genesis 12 and turns it into a threat: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2). Election, in Israel's own Scripture, means more judgment, not less.

The most searching answer to Tindal came from Lesslie Newbigin, the missionary bishop who served in India for most of the years between 1936 and 1974. In *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) he gave a chapter to the logic of election, and his argument, in paraphrase, runs this way. Tindal's alternative assumes salvation is a private transaction between each soul and God, something that could be delivered to every person directly and at once. But the Bible's salvation is the healing of a human race whose bonds with God and one another are broken. A salvation like that has to be handed from person to person, received from someone else, carried across the distance between neighbors. Someone has to go first, and the one who goes first is chosen not for privilege but for responsibility. The elect, Newbigin insisted, hold in trust what is meant for others.

That is the distinction the question turns on: the means are particular, and the end is universal. Tindal and Schwartz both read the means as though they were the end, as though God chose Abraham in order to have Abraham. Schwartz's scarcity reading, the strongest part of her case, also runs into the fact that the story of Jacob and Esau doesn't end with Esau's cry. It ends twenty years later, when Esau runs to meet the brother who robbed him and says, "I have enough, my brother; keep what you have for yourself" (Genesis 33:9).

A remainder is left over, and it should be named. Why did the rescue move so slowly? Why did most of the families of the earth live and die for centuries without hearing the promise that named them, and what becomes of those who never heard? Faithful Christians disagree on that last question. The Bible's answer to "why one family?" is strong. Its answer to "why so long?" is left largely with God, and Abraham's own question is the right place to leave it: the Judge of all the earth will do what is just.

## Sarah and Hagar, Jacob and Esau, Joseph and His Brothers

Genesis refuses to idealize Abraham's family. Erich Auerbach, a Jewish philologist driven from his chair at Marburg, wrote *Mimesis* (1946) in wartime exile in Istanbul and opened it by setting the binding of Isaac in Genesis 22 beside a scene from Homer's *Odyssey*. Homer's heroes, he observed, are evenly lit and do not change; the biblical figures are "fraught with background" and altered by a history that runs across decades. The patriarchs are formed, slowly and at great cost.

The formation starts badly. Almost immediately after the promise, famine drives Abram to Egypt, and he has Sarai say she is his sister so the Egyptians won't kill him to take her. The man meant to bless all families brings plagues on the first foreign household he meets, and Pharaoh is the one with moral clarity: "What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she was your wife?" (Genesis 12:18).

Then comes Hagar, and the honest reader should slow down. Sarai, still barren, gives her Egyptian servant to Abram so the household can have a child through her. The arrangement was ordinary in its world; the laws of Hammurabi of Babylon, from the eighteenth century before Christ, contemplate a wife who gives a slave woman to her husband, and even anticipate the servant who then claims equality with her mistress. Genesis tells the ordinary solution and shows its cost. Hagar conceives and looks on her mistress with contempt; Abram says, "Behold, your servant is in your power; do to her as you please"; and "Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled from her" (Genesis 16:6). Phyllis Trible placed Hagar first in *Texts of Terror* (1984), her study of women used and discarded within Israel's own Scripture, and her reading should be heard before anyone hurries to a lesson.

Yet something happens in the wilderness the family didn't intend. The angel of the LORD, appearing for the first time in the Bible, finds Hagar by a spring. He tells her to call her son Ishmael, "because the LORD has listened to your affliction" (16:11), and Hagar does something rare in Scripture: she gives God a name. "You are a God of seeing," she says, and explains, "Truly here I have seen him who looks after me" (16:13). Years later, sent away for good with bread and a skin of water, she lays the boy under a bush when the water is gone and sits a bowshot off: "Let me not look on the death of the child" (21:16). "And God heard the voice of the boy" (21:17). The blessing meant for the nations reaches one of them first through the family's failure.

The next generation repeats the pattern, and it's a pattern of parents. "Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob" (Genesis 25:28). A divided house produces a son who deceives his blind father and a brother who plans to kill him. Jacob flees, is deceived in turn by Laban, and returns twenty years later, lamed from a night of wrestling and terrified of Esau. Then Esau "ran to meet him and embraced him and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept" (33:4), and Jacob says, "for I have seen your face, which is like seeing the face of God, and you have accepted me" (33:10).

And Jacob does to his sons what his parents did to him. "Now Israel loved Joseph more than any other of his sons, because he was the son of his old age. And he made him a robe of many colors" (Genesis 37:3). The brothers sell Joseph into slavery. The Jewish scholar Jon Levenson, in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993), traced how often Genesis passes over the firstborn for a younger, beloved son who is brought nearly to death and then raised: Isaac on the mountain, Jacob in exile, Joseph in the pit. The ancient household ran on the rights of the firstborn, and God's choosing keeps cutting across them.

This settles the favoritism question from inside the text. Genesis is full of favoritism, and the narrator paints every instance of it as ruinous. A writer who thought divine election was favoritism writ large would not have taught his readers to see favoritism as a sin that wrecks families. Human favoritism closes the circle around the preferred child. God's election sends the blessing outward through the one chosen.

The end of Genesis makes that visible. Judah, who proposed selling Joseph, offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place: "please let your servant remain instead of the boy" (44:33). "All the earth came to Egypt to Joseph to buy grain" (41:57), and old Jacob, who once stole a blessing, stands before Pharaoh and blesses him (47:7). Genesis ends with Abraham's family feeding the nations through a brother the family sold: "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today" (50:20). The promise has been kept in miniature, through a family's sin turned by grace.

I was raised without a father, and I have five sons, so I can't read these chapters as a spectator. What steadies me isn't that the patriarchs got fatherhood right. Mostly they didn't. It's that God kept his promise through their households anyway, and that the story shows those households changing: Jacob limping toward Esau, Judah offering himself for his father's other favorite, Joseph weeping over the brothers who sold him. The family of promise is not where grace was rewarded. It's where grace went to work.

## The Church as the Family of Abraham

The New Testament opens with Abraham's name: "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1). The line that follows runs through Tamar, Rahab, Ruth the Moabite and "the wife of Uriah," a family carried through foreigners and scandal like the one Genesis described.

Paul wrote about Abraham in the middle of the church's first great argument. Gentiles were coming to faith, and some Jewish believers insisted they be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to belong to God's people; the question reached Jerusalem around the year 49, in the meeting Acts 15 records. Galatians fights the same battle, and its central argument is a reading of Genesis: "Know then that it is those of faith who are the sons of Abraham. And the Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, 'In you shall all the nations be blessed.' So then, those who are of faith are blessed along with Abraham, the man of faith" (Galatians 3:7–9). The inclusion of the nations was the plan's first sentence.

"Now the promises were made to Abraham and to his offspring. It does not say, 'And to offsprings,' referring to many, but referring to one, 'And to your offspring,' who is Christ" (Galatians 3:16). The promise narrows from the world to one family, from one family to one people, from one people to one descendant, and in him it widens to everyone: "And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (3:29). N. T. Wright argued in *The Climax of the Covenant* (1991) that Paul read the covenant with Abraham as God's answer to the sin of Adam, the one purpose through Israel for the world, reaching its climax in Israel's Messiah. The narrowing was the route the rescue took.

In Romans 4 Paul presses the order of Genesis: Abraham was counted righteous in chapter 15 and circumcised in chapter 17, years later. "He received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised. The purpose was to make him the father of all who believe without being circumcised" (Romans 4:11). He is "the father of us all" (4:16), and his faith was faith in the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17). The barren womb in Sarah's tent and the empty tomb outside Jerusalem are the same kind of act by the same God.

Ephesians gives the promise its household shape. Gentiles were once "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12), but Christ "has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" (2:14). "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (2:19). The Gentiles are "fellow heirs, members of the same body, and partakers of the promise in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (3:6). The father's house Abraham left is answered by the household of God, open to every family Genesis 10 named.

Here the Gentile church has to hear Israel's warning, because we've failed it the same way. Paul asks whether God has rejected his people and answers, "By no means!" (Romans 11:1). Gentile believers are wild branches grafted into Israel's olive tree: "do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you" (11:18). Levenson shows in *Inheriting Abraham* (2012) that Abraham isn't a neutral figure the three "Abrahamic" faiths share on equal terms; each tradition reads him through its own commitments. How the church and the Jewish people now stand in God's purpose is a question faithful Christians answer differently, and it doesn't touch the creeds. But a church that treats its adoption into Abraham's family as grounds for contempt toward Abraham's children has turned election back into favoritism.

## What the Chosen Carry

Every group that has believed itself chosen by God has been tempted to read the choosing as a verdict on its worth instead of a summons into someone else's rescue. The church has done it for two thousand years, building walls where God meant doors. I've done it in miniature more times than I'd like to count, enjoying the warmth of belonging to God's people and forgetting that belonging is a sending. The hardest part of grace is not receiving it. It's remembering that it was never given to be kept.

The same temptation lives in the Christian home. A household can make its own children the center of its universe, treat its safety as the whole of its calling, and call the arrangement faith. Genesis 18 shows another shape. Abraham sits at his tent door in the heat of the day, sees three strangers, runs to meet them and feeds them, a scene Hebrews remembers: "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" (Hebrews 13:2). Then God names the household's purpose, righteousness and justice taught to children, and Abraham prays for Sodom. The chosen home in Genesis feeds strangers, teaches its children justice and intercedes for the city. It's a forgiven family, turned outward, and it was never a model one.

The story's deepest turn comes on a mountain. God tells Abraham to offer Isaac, and on the way up the boy asks where the lamb is. "God will provide for himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son" (Genesis 22:8). The knife is stopped. Paul heard that chapter when he wrote that God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). What God spared Abraham, he did not spare himself. And the curse that hung over the world from Genesis 3 was not waved away: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13), "so that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles" (3:14). God passed between the pieces in the dark of Genesis 15 and took the self-curse on himself. On the cross he paid it.

So the skeptic's question comes back to those of us inside Abraham's family in another form. If God chose Abraham for the sake of the nations, and chose us in Abraham's offspring for the same reason, our belonging is a debt still being paid out. Near every household that bears Christ's name there is a Hagar the family hasn't seen, an Esau it hasn't embraced, a brother it sold for something small, a stranger at the tent door in the heat of the day. The promise named all the families of the earth, and it still does. Whether they find the blessing through us, or only the wall, isn't settled in an essay. It gets settled at the door.
