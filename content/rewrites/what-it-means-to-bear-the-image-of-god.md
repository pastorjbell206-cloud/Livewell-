Almost everyone in the West believes that a human being carries a worth that nobody granted and nobody can take away. We say so in courtrooms and hospital ethics committees, on protest signs held up on opposite sides of the same street, and in the preamble of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), which opens by recognizing an inherent dignity belonging equally to every member of the human family. The conviction is so common that it feels like the air we breathe. Ask where it came from, though, and most of us go quiet. We know that people matter. We're much less sure why, and less sure still what we would say if someone pressed the question hard.

## A Conviction Living on Borrowed Ground

The skeptic presses it, and he deserves to be heard at full strength. Human beings are the product of a long and unguided process. We are clever animals with a remarkable gift for language and cooperation, but nothing in the physics of the universe marks one arrangement of matter as sacred and another as disposable. Worth, on this account, is a human invention, a useful agreement we make with one another so that we can live together without tearing each other apart. Friedrich Nietzsche saw further than most unbelievers into what follows. In *Twilight of the Idols* (1889) he mocked the English moralists who gave up Christian belief while assuming they could keep Christian ethics, arguing that the ethics rested on the faith and would fall when it fell. He didn't consider that a sad conclusion. He considered it an honest one.

What the skeptic sees is real. Human dignity is not self-evident in the way the hardness of a stone is self-evident. The ancient world did not assume it. Aristotle taught in the *Politics* that some people are slaves by nature, and the great empires were organized around the conviction that some lives weigh far more than others. Jürgen Habermas, a philosopher with no stake in defending the church, granted in an interview collected in *Time of Transitions* (2006) that the egalitarian universalism behind modern ideals of freedom, conscience, human rights and democracy is the direct heir of the Jewish ethic of justice and the Christian ethic of love, and that there is still no alternative to that inheritance. On this point the skeptic and the Christian agree more than either usually admits. Our dignity has a root. The question is whether the root is real.

That question sends us back to the first page of the Bible, to a sentence about being made in the image of God. Most Christians can recite it. Far fewer could say what it means, and the church itself has argued about its meaning for eighteen centuries. The argument matters because the answer decides who gets counted. A doctrine of the image that ties human worth to something a person can do will sooner or later meet people who cannot do it. A doctrine that ties worth to what God has done will not.

We should admit at the start that the church has not earned the right to lecture anyone about human dignity. The tradition that taught the image of God also produced sermons defending slavery, segregation and eugenics. That history doesn't refute the doctrine. It does mean that anyone who teaches it, beginning with the one writing these words, stands under it before he stands behind it.

## What Genesis Meant by an Image

Genesis 1 was not written into a vacuum. The peoples around Israel knew exactly what the image of a god was. It was a statue, carved and gilded and installed in a temple through elaborate rites, and once installed it was treated as the place where the deity's presence and power became available. That world knew one other kind of divine image: the king. Egyptian and Mesopotamian royal texts speak of the ruler as the image or likeness of a god, the one through whom heaven's authority reached the ground. Gerhard von Rad, in his commentary on Genesis (revised English edition, 1972), compared the idea to the habit of great kings who set up statues of themselves in far provinces they could not visit, so that the statue announced whose realm this was.

Against that background, the opening chapter of the Bible is close to shocking. God says, "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness," and at once attaches a task: dominion over the creatures of sea, sky and land (Genesis 1:26). Then comes the verse the whole tradition circles.

> "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

J. Richard Middleton's *The Liberating Image* (2005) reads this as a deliberate reply to the royal ideology of Babylon. Mesopotamian myths pictured humans as laborers made to relieve the gods of their toil, and they reserved the divine image for the king. Genesis gives royal dignity and royal responsibility to humanity as such, male and female together, and it pictures a God who shares his rule generously instead of hoarding it. John Walton adds a temple dimension in *The Lost World of Genesis One* (2009). He reads the seven days as God ordering the cosmos to be his dwelling, and just as an ancient temple was finished by installing the image of its god, the cosmic temple receives its image in the human creature. The two readings fit together. Humanity stands in God's world as his living likeness, both his representative and a sign of his presence.

Two details keep us from reading this sentimentally. The first comes in Genesis 5:3, where the same pair of Hebrew words, *tselem* (image) and *demut* (likeness), describe Adam fathering Seth in his own likeness and after his image. That is the language of sonship. To be made in God's image is to be made something like his child, with a family resemblance meant to be recognized. The second detail is that the image does not vanish when things go wrong. After the fall, after Cain, after a flood sent because the earth was full of violence, God renews his covenant with Noah and grounds the prohibition of murder in the image itself.

> "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image." (Genesis 9:6)

The reason a human life may not be taken is not the victim's goodness, usefulness or innocence. It is what God made him. Sin has corrupted the world by this point in the story, yet the image still marks every person, and to strike the image is to strike at the One it represents.

Psalm 8 sings the same truth from underneath. The psalmist looks up at the night sky and wonders why God would take notice of creatures so small, and then he answers his own wonder in royal language.

> "Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honor." (Psalm 8:5)

Crowned is a king's word. The psalm goes on to speak of dominion over the works of God's hands, and the letter to the Hebrews later reads it as fulfilled in Jesus, the true human who wears the crown the rest of us were made for (Hebrews 2:5–9). The image in Genesis is a status and a calling given to the whole human creature by a God who speaks and appoints. It is something he does before it is anything we do.

## Three Readings, Each at Its Strongest

The church has never agreed on exactly what the image is. Its three main answers each deserve a hearing in the form their own defenders would sign, because each sees something true.

The oldest and longest-lived is the substantive reading, which locates the image in something human beings possess: reason, will, the soul's capacity to know and love God. Irenaeus of Lyons, writing *Against Heresies* around 180, drew a distinction between image and likeness (book 5, chapters 6 and 16). In his account the image belongs to the human creature as God formed it, while the likeness is the communion with God that the Spirit gives, lost in Adam and restored when the Word became flesh. Thomas Aquinas refined the picture in the *Summa Theologiae* (First Part, question 93). He placed the image in the intellectual nature by which humans can know and love God and described it at three levels: a natural aptitude for knowing and loving God, which every human being shares; the image of grace, in those who actually know and love him, however imperfectly; and the image of glory, completed in the blessed. The strength of this reading is considerable. It takes seriously what Scripture assumes on every page, that human beings are addressed by God, commanded, called to answer, capable of prayer and conscience and worship in a way no other creature is. And Aquinas was careful. His first level belongs to human nature itself, not to how well a given person exercises it.

The weaknesses show up at the edges. Most modern Old Testament scholars read *tselem* and *demut* in Genesis 1:26 as parallel terms rather than two different things (Genesis 5:3 uses them in reverse order), so Irenaeus's division is hard to find in the text, and Calvin had already set it aside (*Institutes* I.15.3). More seriously, once the image is identified with a capacity, someone will ask about people in whom the capacity is absent, undeveloped or destroyed.

The second reading is relational. Karl Barth, in *Church Dogmatics* III/1 (1945), noticed that Genesis 1:27 never defines the image by a faculty. It moves directly from God creating humanity in his image to God creating them male and female. For Barth the image consists in the human being as a creature made for encounter, an I addressed by the divine Thou and living alongside a human Thou. The God who says "Let us," who within himself is the fellowship of Father, Son and Spirit, creates a creature whose very existence is existence-with-another. Barth called this an analogy of relation, a phrase Dietrich Bonhoeffer had already used in the lectures published as *Creation and Fall* (1933), and Barth acknowledged Bonhoeffer's reading of the passage as he developed his own. The strength of this reading is that it matches the Bible's constant interest in covenant, in God speaking and people answering, and it refuses to picture the human being as a sealed individual. Its weakness is that Genesis never quite says the image is the relation between man and woman, and the relational reading can quietly turn relationship into one more capacity to be measured. Then the isolated, the abandoned and the unloved start to look dimmer than everyone else.

The third reading, the royal or functional, has gained ground over the last century on the strength of the ancient Near Eastern evidence. Middleton and Walton, building on von Rad, argue that the image is an office. Humanity is God's appointed representative, charged to rule creation as he would, with care, fruitfulness and justice. This reading stands closest to the text's own world, and it gives the doctrine a moral spine, because to be the image is to have a vocation. Its danger is subtle. If the image is what we do, then those who cannot rule, work or steward anything seem to fall short of it.

## Having an Image and Being One

The three readings share a temptation, and naming it clears the ground. Each can be heard as describing something a person has: a rational soul, a capacity for relationship, a role to perform. Things you have can be had in greater or lesser measure. They can be damaged, lost or never developed at all. If the image is a possession, then the newborn has less of it than the philosopher, the man with advanced dementia has less than he did at fifty, and the child with a profound intellectual disability may seem to have hardly any. Nobody in the great tradition intended that conclusion. But a doctrine framed as possession invites it, and the modern world, which kept the language of dignity after losing its ground, has been quick to accept the invitation.

The distinction that changes the question is between having an image and being one. Genesis does not say that God gave human beings an image to carry around. It says he made them in it, after it, according to it. John Kilner's *Dignity and Destiny* (2015) presses hard on that grammar. He argues that the prepositions point to a pattern outside us, that the New Testament identifies the pattern as Christ, and that being created in God's image is therefore a status God confers and a destiny he intends, rather than a quality we hold inside ourselves at higher or lower strength. On this account sin badly damages how well we reflect God, but it cannot revoke what God has made us to be. That fits Genesis 9, where the murdered man is protected by the image after the fall, and it fits the letter of James, which says that people who are cursed by Christian tongues are still made in God's likeness.

The New Testament completes the picture by naming the original. Paul writes of Christ:

> "He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." (Colossians 1:15)

Adam was made according to the image; the Son is the image. What we were made to reflect, he is. That is why Hebrews can read the crown of Psalm 8 as resting finally on Jesus, and why Paul can speak of believers being transformed into the same image as they behold the Lord's glory (2 Corinthians 3:18). We aren't hunting inside ourselves for a lost jewel. The image is a person, and our humanity is measured against him, which means it is measured against someone who loves us.

Paul's word about renewal belongs in its setting. He is telling ordinary believers in Colossae to stop lying to one another, since they

> "have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator." (Colossians 3:10)

The very next verse says that in this renewed humanity there is no Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave or free, because Christ is all and in all. Renewal into the image turns out to be a shared life rather than private self-improvement, and the first thing it does is loosen the categories we use to rank one another.

Now the hard cases, which are where a doctrine proves itself. John Swinton, a theologian who worked for sixteen years as a nurse in mental health and learning disability care before teaching, argues in *Dementia: Living in the Memories of God* (2012) that a person's identity is not secured by her own ability to remember. We are held in God's memory, and the church is called to remember people on God's behalf when they can no longer remember themselves. If the image is something you have, dementia looks like its slow theft. If it is something you are because God made you and holds you, then the woman who no longer knows her daughter's name is as fully his image as she ever was.

The same logic runs outward. It reaches the child with Down syndrome, whose life is not a diminished version of someone else's. It reaches the unborn child, who has not yet exercised a single capacity and does not need to. It reaches the old man in a care home whose useful years are long behind him, the immigrant whose papers are in question, and the prisoner whose worst act is a matter of public record. None of them earned their dignity by a capacity, and so none of them can lose it by losing one.

## Two Who Refused to Look Away

The doctrine has often been betrayed. It has also, at moments of real cost, been obeyed, and two of those moments show what the image looks like when someone actually believes it.

Around 379, Gregory of Nyssa preached a series of homilies on Ecclesiastes. In the fourth he reached the Preacher's boast that he had bought male and female slaves (Ecclesiastes 2:7). Slavery ran through the whole economy of the late Roman world, and most Christian teachers of Gregory's century accepted it as a fact to be softened rather than a wrong to be named. Gregory named it. He asked his hearers what price could possibly be set on a creature made in the likeness of God, and he argued that since God himself had made human nature free, no human buyer could claim a power over it that God had never claimed. He reminded them that owner and owned are born the same way, suffer the same griefs, die the same death and stand before the same judge. It didn't end slavery. It did show that the logic of Genesis, taken seriously, reached further than the church was ready to go.

More than eleven centuries later the same logic was argued at Valladolid in Spain. In 1550 the emperor Charles V suspended new conquests in the Americas and convened a panel of theologians and jurists to hear a dispute that ran into 1551. Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, a learned humanist, argued from Aristotle that the native peoples of the New World were natural slaves, less rational than Europeans, and could justly be conquered and governed for their own good. Bartolomé de las Casas answered him. He was a Dominican friar who had once held an encomienda, a grant of native labor, and had given it up after coming to see the whole system as unjust. At Valladolid he insisted that the peoples of the Americas were fully rational human beings, with their own governments, arts and virtues, made by God and capable of receiving the faith freely, and that the gospel could never rightly be carried by the sword. The panel never issued a clear verdict. The conquest went on. But Las Casas forced Christendom to hear its own doctrine turned against its own conduct.

Notice what each man was fighting. Sepúlveda's case was a capacity argument: these people reason less well, so they may be ruled like children or owned like tools. Las Casas met him partly on that ground, showing that the Indians reasoned perfectly well. Gregory went deeper. He rested the slave's dignity on who had made him and left the slave's cleverness out of the question. That is the difference between an image you have and an image you are, worked out in history, and it explains why capacity arguments keep returning. They are always available to anyone who needs a reason to rank.

Honesty requires one more detail. Earlier in his career Las Casas had suggested bringing enslaved Africans to the colonies to spare the native peoples, and years later, in his *History of the Indies*, he confessed that the advice had been gravely wrong, because Africans were enslaved as unjustly as the Indians. Even the church's great defender of the image could see it plainly in one face and miss it in another. That is no reason to discount him. It is a warning to every one of us who assumes our own sight is clear. The tradition that produced Gregory and Las Casas produced far more Sepúlvedas, people gifted at finding some qualifying trait that let one group bear the image a little less fully than another, exactly where money or power was at stake. We haven't outgrown that gift. We've only changed which qualifications we find persuasive.

## The Image We Ration

Every political tribe in America appeals to the image of God, and every one of them applies it selectively. That judgment has to be earned, so each side deserves its strongest statement first.

Christians on the right have defended the unborn child with a conviction that comes straight from Genesis. They are correct that a human being does not become an image-bearer by reaching a certain size, a certain stage of development or a certain level of being wanted, and many of them have spent years, money and reputation caring for mothers in crisis because they believe it. Christians on the left have defended the immigrant, the prisoner and the poor with an equally serious conviction. They are correct that legal status, a criminal record or a bank balance cannot touch what God has made, and many of them have given their lives to people no one else would visit.

Yet each side tends to stop where its coalition stops. Some who speak movingly about the unborn describe migrants as an infestation, treat the incarcerated as beyond moral concern, and talk about the poor as though their worth rose and fell with their work. Some who speak movingly about the migrant treat the unborn as tissue and have grown comfortable with laws that make death easier to reach for the old, the ill and the disabled. Canada legalized medical assistance in dying in 2016 and in 2021, through Bill C-7, widened it to people whose natural deaths were not reasonably foreseeable, and the case for it was made largely in the language of dignity and choice. What matters most is the common grammar under these opposite positions. The right is tempted to measure worth by belonging: the citizen, the innocent, the productive. The left is tempted to measure it by autonomy: the person able to choose. Different people fall outside each measure, but both are doctrines of an image you have. Shared roots don't mean equal guilt on every question, and faithful Christians will still weigh particular policies differently. The doctrine doesn't write legislation. It does forbid contempt, and it forbids any account of human worth that a person can fall below.

At this point the argument stops being about them. James, writing to churches, observes the absurdity of one tongue doing two things.

> "With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse people who are made in the likeness of God." (James 3:9)

He isn't describing pagans. He's describing people at worship, people who sing on Sunday and dismiss their neighbors by Monday. John Calvin, in the chapter on self-denial in the *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1559, III.7.6), told readers tempted to withhold kindness from someone unworthy that they should look past the person's deserts to the image of God in him, which is worthy of every love we could give. I've said the words about the image from a pulpit more easily than I've lived them, and I suspect the same is true of anyone reading this. We affirm the doctrine in general and edit it in particular, and our edits track our fears and our tribe with uncomfortable precision.

Jesus closes the last way out. In the judgment scene of Matthew 25 the King identifies himself with the hungry, the stranger, the naked, the sick and the imprisoned, and he says, "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40).

The deepest reason the image can't be earned is found at the cross. The One who is the image of the invisible God was himself treated as the least: stripped, numbered with criminals, executed by people who bore his image and had turned it against him. Rather than revoke it from them, he died to renew it in them, and on the third day God raised him, so that the renewed humanity Paul describes to the Colossians is the risen life of Jesus spreading into people like us. The last word about any human being is therefore neither what they can do nor what we have done to them. It is what God made and what Christ bought.

None of us will leave this page seeing every face rightly. The change comes slower than that, through confessing the sorting we do without noticing, repenting of the contempt we have learned to call realism, and letting the church become the kind of people among whom the old rankings lose their grip. Somewhere near you is a person you've already filed under problem, burden or enemy. The image was there before you noticed them, and it will still be there after you've forgotten them. What remains open is whether you will be found to have seen it.
