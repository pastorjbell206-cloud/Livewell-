The water is the same in both buildings, and that is the strange part. On a Sunday morning in a Baptist church, a grown man walks down the steps of a tank set into the wall behind the pulpit, is lowered backward under the water, and comes up streaming. A few streets away, in a Presbyterian or Lutheran or Catholic church, a mother hands her infant son to a minister, who pours water over the child's head three times, and the boy will never remember it. The same triune name is spoken over both of them. Both congregations believe something real has just been done. And between the two buildings stands a wall that has held since the 1520s and shows no sign of coming down.

Most people meet that wall sideways. A couple from different traditions has a first child, and one side of the family expects a christening while the other expects a dedication. A believer baptized as an infant joins a Baptist church and is told, kindly but firmly, that membership means going into the water again, and he hears in it a verdict on the parents who carried him to the font. A skeptic watching from outside draws the obvious conclusion: if Christians can't agree on what to do with a basin of water, why trust what they say about God? Part of that is true. The disagreement is real, learned and prayerful people have stood on both sides of it for five centuries, and it won't be settled in an essay.

But the skeptic's conclusion, and the anxious believer's, rests on a confusion the quarrel itself tends to hide. There are two questions here, and they aren't the same size. The first is what baptism is: what the New Testament says happens when a person is washed in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. The second is who should receive it and how the water should be applied. On the first question the churches agree far more than their arguments suggest. The Nicene Creed, in the form confirmed at Constantinople in 381 and still confessed by Catholic, Orthodox, and most Protestant churches, says "one baptism for the forgiveness of sins." That much is creedal ground. Who goes into the water, and how deep, is a second-order question. It divides faithful churches without dividing the faith. Naming the order doesn't make the question small. It tells you how hard you are allowed to hit.

*The fight is over who belongs in the grave and how far under they go. Nobody in the fight denies there is a grave.*

## What the New Testament Says Baptism Is

The practice begins with a command from the risen Christ: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19, ESV). Matthew's preposition is *eis*, "into," and the name is singular though it is threefold: a person is handed over into a belonging he didn't author.

At Pentecost, Peter gives the first instruction anyone received about what to do with the news of the resurrection: "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is for you and for your children and for all who are far off, everyone whom the Lord our God calls to himself" (Acts 2:38–39, ESV). Then Luke reports, "So those who received his word were baptized, and there were added that day about three thousand souls" (Acts 2:41, ESV). *Added* is a community word, and the next verse describes the people they were added to, devoted to the apostles' teaching, the fellowship, the breaking of bread, and the prayers. Nowhere in Acts is baptism private. Someone else always does it, in front of others, who then belong to the one who came up out of the water.

Paul gives the deepest account. In Romans 6 he is answering an objection to grace: "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?" (Romans 6:1, ESV). His answer is not a new rule but a reminder of something that has already happened to them: "Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:3–4, ESV). The baptized person has been joined to a death that was not his own and a resurrection he could never have produced. That is why going on in sin is unthinkable for him. It would be living as though the funeral never happened.

The same letters say it from other angles. "For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27, ESV). "For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13, ESV). Baptism is union with Christ and entry into his body, and those are one reality seen from two sides, because no one is joined to the head and left outside the body. Colossians ties the cluster together, in a text that will return when the argument starts: "In him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, by putting off the body of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God, who raised him from the dead" (Colossians 2:11–12, ESV). Burial and resurrection again, held together with faith and laid beside the old sign of circumcision.

Then there is the verse every tradition has to handle carefully. Peter recalls Noah's ark, in which "a few, that is, eight persons, were brought safely through water" (1 Peter 3:20, ESV), and goes on: "Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you, not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 3:21, ESV). (An ESV footnote offers "pledge" as an alternative to "appeal.") Look at the shape of the sentence. Peter says as strongly as the New Testament says anything that baptism saves, and in the same breath refuses to let anyone locate that power in the water. It isn't a bath. The saving power runs "through the resurrection of Jesus Christ." The sacramental traditions are tempted to forget the second half of that verse, and my own tradition has too often been embarrassed by the first. Still, a Baptist and a Presbyterian will preach Romans 6 the same way, and both confess that baptism is the church's claim that something has been done *to* a person, not merely by him.

## How the Early Church Baptized, and Whom

The oldest church manual we have outside the New Testament, the Didache, usually dated to the late first or early second century, gives the earliest instructions for the rite. Its seventh chapter tells the church to baptize in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit in running water, literally "living water"; failing that, in other water, cold or warm; and if there is not enough of either, to pour water on the head three times in the triune name. The mode is flexible and the name is fixed. And because six chapters of moral teaching come before this instruction, the candidate the Didache pictures is someone who has been taught. Credobaptists rightly notice that. Paedobaptists rightly notice that nothing in it excludes a household's children.

Around 200, in Carthage, Tertullian wrote the first surviving treatise devoted entirely to baptism. Near its end he counsels delay, especially for little children and for the unmarried. He asks why the age of innocence should hurry to the forgiveness of sins, and would have children come when they are able to know Christ. Baptists have long cited Tertullian as proof that infant baptism was not settled. Paedobaptists answer that nobody argues against a practice nobody follows, so children were plainly being baptized in Carthage by 200. Both readings are correct. And Tertullian was no Baptist before his time. His worry was serious sin after baptism, not a doctrine of the believers' church.

Half a century later, in the same city, the question came from the opposite direction. A bishop named Fidus asked whether newborns should wait until the eighth day to be baptized, as boys had waited for circumcision. Cyprian of Carthage, writing for a council of North African bishops around 253, answered that grace should be withheld from no one, not even an infant just born. By then infant baptism was ordinary enough that the only question was how soon, and the link to circumcision that Calvin would build on thirteen centuries later was already being drawn.

Yet the fourth century shows how unsettled practice remained even in devout homes. Augustine tells in his *Confessions* (c. 397–400) how his Christian mother, Monica, had him made a catechumen in infancy, and how, dangerously ill as a boy, he begged for baptism. He recovered, and it was put off. Ambrose baptized him in Milan at Easter 387, when he was thirty-two. The usual motive for such delays was fear of sinning after baptism, not a theology of believer's baptism, and honesty requires both sides to admit it.

It was Augustine, ironically, who made infant baptism the settled rule of the West. Against Pelagius, who denied that Adam's sin passed to his descendants, he argued in *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants* (412) that the church baptizes infants for the forgiveness of sins, infants commit none of their own, and so they must bear an inherited sin. A council at Carthage in 418 condemned anyone who denied that newborns should be baptized for the forgiveness of sins. For more than a thousand years afterward, baptizing infants was simply what Western Christians did, though the practice was already there in Cyprian, long before Augustine needed it.

Modern scholarship has fought the same battle with better tools. The Lutheran scholar Joachim Jeremias argued in *Infant Baptism in the First Four Centuries* (1958) that the practice reached back to the apostolic households. Kurt Aland answered in *Did the Early Church Baptize Infants?* (1961) that there is no certain evidence before about 200. The exchange ran on into Jeremias's reply of 1962, and neither man moved the other. Everett Ferguson's *Baptism in the Early Church* (2009) concludes that the immersion of believers was the normal early practice and that infant baptism emerged in the late second century, most likely out of the emergency baptism of sick children in danger of death, and spread from there. The honest summary is modest. That converts were baptized on their profession of faith is plain in the New Testament. Whether their infants were baptized with them is exactly what the texts don't say.

## Zurich, 1525: When the Water Became a Wall

For a thousand years the question slept in the West, and the Reformation woke it. Ulrich Zwingli began preaching in Zurich in 1519 on the principle that Scripture alone governs the church, and some of his most eager young followers, Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz among them, pressed that principle further than he would go. Where, they asked, does the New Testament command that infants be baptized? Zwingli admitted in his 1525 treatise on baptism that he had once had doubts of his own. But he concluded that infant baptism was the covenant sign of a Christian people, and in January 1525, after a public disputation, the city council sided with him and ordered parents to bring their unbaptized infants to the font. On 21 January 1525 Grebel baptized George Blaurock, and Blaurock baptized others. Their enemies called them Anabaptists, "rebaptizers." They insisted they were baptizing for the first time, and most of them did it by pouring.

In March 1526 the council made rebaptism punishable by death. On 5 January 1527 Felix Manz was drowned in the Limmat, the river that runs through the city. He is often called the first Anabaptist martyr at Protestant hands. That February a gathering at Schleitheim under the former monk Michael Sattler, himself executed that May, adopted what became known as the Schleitheim Confession. Its first article gives baptism to those who have learned repentance and amendment of life, who truly believe their sins are taken away by Christ, and who walk in his resurrection and want to be buried with him into death, and it rejects infant baptism in the hardest terms. Behind it stood a church of the regenerate, gathered apart from the civil order that was persecuting them.

The magisterial reformers answered with confessions of their own. The Augsburg Confession (1530), the Lutheran charter drafted by Philip Melanchthon, taught in its ninth article that children are to be baptized and so received into God's grace, and it condemned the Anabaptists by name. The condemnation was not only on paper. Through the rest of the century Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic authorities alike imprisoned, banished, and executed Anabaptists; the Reformers did not treat them as brothers who differed on a secondary point. John Calvin gave the covenant argument its classic form in the fourth book of his *Institutes* (final edition 1559), chiefly at IV.16: the covenant with Abraham and the covenant in Christ are one covenant, differing in administration and not in substance, circumcision and baptism signify the same promise, and so the sign goes where it always went, to believers and their children. The Westminster Confession (1646) set this down for the English-speaking Reformed world, adding that dipping is not necessary, since pouring or sprinkling rightly administers it.

Say plainly what happened in Zurich. Christians who confessed justification by grace alone put other Christians to death over how they applied the water that signifies union with the one who died for his enemies. This essay holds the believer's-baptism conviction, and it has to be careful not to tell this story so that it flatters its own side. The free churches went on to commit smaller sins of their own: refusing the Lord's Table to believers whose faith no one doubted, rebaptizing people as though their parents' prayers had been theater. Nobody drowned for it. But contempt is contempt, and we have been tempted by it more than we like to admit.

The English Baptists came by a separate road. John Smyth, a Separatist exile in Amsterdam, concluded in 1609 that infant baptism was no baptism. He baptized himself, then Thomas Helwys and the rest of his small congregation, and defended the step in *The Character of the Beast* (1609). Helwys returned in 1612 to found the first Baptist church on English soil. Smyth's circle, like the early Swiss Anabaptists, appears to have baptized by pouring. It was the Particular Baptists, Calvinist in theology, who settled the question of mode, adopting immersion around 1641 and 1642. Their First London Confession (1644) described the manner of the ordinance as dipping or plunging the whole body under water, reasoning that a sign must answer to the thing signified, which for them included the believer's share in Christ's death, burial, and resurrection.

The order of that history matters. The Anabaptists settled *who* a century before the Baptists settled *how*, and the two questions came apart in history because they are different questions. The case for believer's baptism doesn't stand or fall on immersion, and immersion by itself doesn't settle who should be baptized. The Orthodox churches, after all, fully immerse their infants, which reminds Baptists that immersion is not their private property.

## Sacrament or Ordinance: What Does the Water Do?

Beneath the debate over who and how lies a third question, older and in some ways deeper: what the water actually does. Augustine gave the classic definition in his *Tractates on the Gospel of John*: "Accedit verbum ad elementum, et fit sacramentum," the word comes to the element, and it becomes a sacrament. A sacrament, on this account, is a visible sign through which God gives what he has promised.

The Catholic Church holds the fullest Western form of this view. The Council of Trent's decree on the sacraments (1547) taught that they confer grace *ex opere operato*, by the act performed, not by the holiness of the minister, to those who place no obstacle in the way. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1992) teaches that God has bound salvation to baptism, while he himself is not bound by his sacraments.

Luther kept the sacrament and moved its center of gravity. In the *Small Catechism* (1529) he says the water doesn't do such great things by itself; the word of God in and with the water does them, together with the faith that trusts that word. In the *Large Catechism* he insists that baptism, though performed by human hands, is God's own work, and he counsels the Christian whose conscience accuses him to answer, "I am baptized," taking hold of a promise that sits outside the weather of his feelings. Because baptism rests on God's word and not on the strength of the recipient's faith, Luther could baptize infants without making their faith its ground.

Calvin called baptism a sign and seal of the covenant, a means the Spirit truly uses, and Westminster added that its effect is not tied to the moment it is administered. Zwingli went furthest the other way, treating the sacrament chiefly as a pledge by which a person shows himself before the church to be Christ's. The irony is that the popular Baptist account of what baptism *means* often sounds closer to Zwingli than to anyone, while the Baptist conviction about *who* comes from the very people Zwingli's Zurich put to death.

Baptists generally prefer the word *ordinance*: an act Christ ordained and commanded, done in obedience, confessing the believer's union with him in his death, burial, and resurrection. The Baptist Faith and Message (2000) describes baptism as an act of obedience symbolizing the believer's faith in a crucified, buried, and risen Savior. But ordinance need not mean "mere." The British Baptist scholar G. R. Beasley-Murray argued in *Baptism in the New Testament* (1962) that the apostles ascribe to baptism, received in faith, the gifts of union with Christ, forgiveness, and the Spirit, and that Baptists who reduced it to an illustration had drifted from their own Bibles in reaction against sacramentalism. That is a Baptist conscience indicting Baptists, and it deserves a hearing in every baptistry.

Karl Barth, the most influential Reformed theologian of the twentieth century, had publicly challenged infant baptism in a 1943 lecture on the church's teaching about baptism. In the last part-volume of the *Church Dogmatics* published in his lifetime, IV/4 (1967), he concluded that infant baptism cannot be defended from the New Testament, and that water baptism is not a sacrament at all but the human answer of obedience and confession to God's own act, baptism with the Holy Spirit. His conclusion pleases Baptists on who and troubles them, along with Lutherans and Catholics, on what. The lines of this argument don't run where the denominational signs say they do.

The sacramental view puts the weight on God's promise attached to the act. The ordinance view puts the weight on Christ's command and the believer's answer. At their best, both locate the saving power in the crucified and risen Christ, exactly where 1 Peter 3:21 puts it. At their worst, the sacramentalist lets the water carry what faith was meant to carry, and the ordinance-holder turns baptism into a monument to the strength of his own decision.

## Infant Baptism and Believer's Baptism at Full Strength

Take the paedobaptist case first, at full height, because the caricature is easy and worthless. The Presbyterian at the font isn't baptizing his daughter out of sentiment or as insurance. He is standing on the one unbroken storyline of the Bible. When God made his covenant with Abraham he commanded, "He who is eight days old among you shall be circumcised" (Genesis 17:12, ESV). For two thousand years the children of the covenant people carried its sign before they could understand it. Did God narrow his promise in the new covenant to exclude the children he had always included? Peter's first sermon holds the old pattern open: "the promise is for you and for your children" (Acts 2:39, ESV). The households press the point. Lydia "was baptized, and her household as well" (Acts 16:15, ESV); the Philippian jailer "was baptized at once, he and all his family" (Acts 16:33, ESV). Paul even tells a believer married to an unbeliever, "Otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is, they are holy" (1 Corinthians 7:14, ESV). Above all, grace comes before our grasp of it, and an infant at the font is the gospel in its plainest form: a person receiving a love he did nothing to earn and could not yet want. Behind this case stand Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin, Wesley, and Edwards, with nearly the whole church for well over a thousand years.

Now give the credobaptist the same refusal to caricature. He isn't stingy with grace; he is reading the New Testament with a plain eye. Every baptism Acts describes follows hearing and believing: "those who received his word were baptized" (Acts 2:41, ESV). Paul and Silas "spoke the word of the Lord to him and to all who were in his house" (Acts 16:32, ESV), and afterward the jailer "rejoiced along with his entire household that he had believed in God" (Acts 16:34, ESV). A household that hears and believes is exactly what a Baptist expects to see baptized. More deeply, the credobaptist argues that the new covenant differs from the old not in size but in kind. Jeremiah promised a covenant in which no one would need to urge his neighbor to know the Lord, "for they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest" (Jeremiah 31:34, ESV), and Hebrews 8 applies that promise to the church. Membership in Israel came by birth; membership in the new covenant comes by new birth. The counterpart of circumcision in Colossians 2 is a circumcision "made without hands" (Colossians 2:11, ESV), the inward work that baptism signifies, so the sign belongs to those in whom that work can be confessed. And Romans 6 speaks of burial, which a damp forehead can hardly picture. The credobaptist's most serious warning is a gentle one: a child told all his life that he was made a Christian by something done before he could say yes may never realize he still needs to say it.

On mode the church has always sat more loosely, and it should. Calvin himself granted in the *Institutes* that the word *baptize* means to immerse and that the ancient church immersed, and still judged the difference unimportant. Defenders of pouring add that Luke uses a form of *baptizo* for an ordinary washing before a meal (Luke 11:38). The picture of burial leans toward immersion, and that remains the strongest argument in the mode debate. But nothing in the resurrection depends on the depth of the water.

Each side carries a question it finds harder than it lets on. The Baptist has to explain where the New Testament revokes the inclusion of children, and why a covenant that grows wider in every other direction, taking in Gentiles, women, and slaves, should grow narrower at the point of children. The paedobaptist has to explain where the New Testament shows a single infant being baptized, and how a church full of the baptized but never converted differs from the nominal Christendom every movement of reform has had to fight.

The churches have tried to talk across the line. The Faith and Order Commission of the World Council of Churches, meeting at Lima, Peru, in 1982, produced *Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry*, which treated infant and believer's baptism as practices within the one baptism. It asked churches that baptize infants to guard against apparently indiscriminate baptism, and churches that baptize only believers to show more visibly that children are placed under the protection of God's grace. It also insisted that baptism is unrepeatable, so that any practice which could be read as rebaptism must be avoided. That last request is the whole dispute in one sentence.

## Where This Essay Lands, and What It Leaves Open

This essay lands on believer's baptism: baptism belongs to those who profess faith in Christ. On mode it leans toward immersion as the fullest form of the sign, because it preaches with the body what Romans 6 preaches in words, and something real was lost when the tank became a bowl; but it will not make the depth of the water a wall. It is the conviction of the tradition I serve in. It is a conviction held, not a verdict handed down, and it is not the only faithful reading. Anyone who has watched a godly Presbyterian or Lutheran pastor pour water on a baby's head has seen faith at work in the act and knows better than to call it anything less.

The strongest objection this position hasn't fully answered is the covenant household. The New Testament's silence about removing children from the people of God is loud, 1 Corinthians 7:14 is hard to account for, and God's dealing with families from Abraham forward is not a Presbyterian invention. There is a practical objection too, and it lands on those of us who hold this view. Churches that baptize only believers promise a membership of the regenerate and don't deliver one. Our baptistries have seen plenty of professions that didn't last, and many of our churches baptize children young enough that the distance from the font is smaller than our rhetoric suggests. Nobody on either bank has achieved a pure church by getting the water right.

So the question stays open, as it has for five centuries, and this essay leaves it open among brothers, which is more than Zurich managed. You can read this, take your child to the font, and remain my brother. You can wait for the day your child can speak for himself, bring him down into the water then, and remain my brother. What none of us may do is treat the water as though it were the cross, or treat the Christian on the other bank as though he had missed Christ. He hasn't. He is standing in the same river, arguing about its depth, and both of you are already wet.

The Limmat still runs through Zurich. It is worth remembering what the men on its bank in January 1527 thought they were defending, because the temptation didn't die with them: to care more about being right about the sign than about the death it signifies. Paul said we were "baptized into his death" (Romans 6:3, ESV), the death of the one who died for his enemies, not the death of our opponents. Every baptism in every mode says the same thing over the person in the water: you have no life of your own left to defend, because you have been buried with Christ, and whatever you have now, you were given. Luther's counsel still holds on the worst nights, when the conscience accuses: answer with "I am baptized." It happened, and it never depended on you.

That leaves a question heavier than the one about infants and tanks, and every baptized reader carries it out the door. If you went under, whether you remember it or not, the water already said the old self is dead. The argument between the two banks can wait for brothers to have it honestly. What can't wait is whether the one who came up out of the water is living as someone who has died.
