Every Sunday morning there is an atheist in my pulpit. He didn't slip in at the back, and he isn't a visitor. He's the man I was for many years, whose reasons I can still give without notes, and he stands inside the man who now preaches. Most conversion stories knock the earlier self flat by the second paragraph. I won't do that to him. He wasn't a fool, and if I write about him as though he were, every reader who stands where he stood will be right to stop trusting me. Some of you stand there now, and you can smell a setup from a long way off.

The facts belong on the table before any argument touches them. I was raised without a father. He wasn't dead, only gone, and the absence was a decision someone kept making every morning by not coming back. I didn't drift out of a childhood faith, because there was no childhood faith to drift out of. I came to belief as a grown man, and against my own preference. I've now spent years as a pastor, I lead First Baptist Church of Fenton, Michigan, and I'm raising five sons on the far side of the word *father*. Everything that follows leans on those facts, and none of them is decoration.

Christians have a verse they like to aim at men like the one I was. "The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1, ESV). In its setting it points somewhere other than the philosophy department. The fool of Hebrew wisdom is short on moral seriousness, not intelligence, and the psalm knows its fools by what they do: they "eat up my people as they eat bread" (Psalm 14:4, ESV), living as though no one will ever call them to account. Paul takes up this psalm in Romans 3 to put everyone under the same verdict, the religious and the irreligious alike. The practical atheism it describes, living as though God does not see, is as much a danger in the pulpit as in the seminar room, and it is the second atheist this essay has in view, though the first gets most of the words.

## The case I held, at its strongest

Start with what I believed, because the atheism in most testimonies is a costume and mine was a conviction. I believed the universe was what it was and owed nobody an explanation beyond its own workings. I believed the mind was what the brain does, the way digestion is what the stomach does, and that when my brain stopped I would stop, as a candle stops. I believed morality was a survival strategy our ancestors had dressed up in the language of commandment, and that the sense of meaning woven into things was the last and most stubborn illusion, a small warm animal projecting itself onto a cold and enormous sky. I didn't hold any of this as a wound. I held it as maturity. There's a real dignity in facing what you take to be the truth without flinching, and I had that dignity, and I don't disown it now.

I found Bertrand Russell at nineteen and read him the way other young men read Scripture. In "A Free Man's Worship" (1903) he argues that human beings are the product of causes that had no foresight of what they were producing, and that our origin, our hopes and fears, our loves and beliefs, are the outcome of "accidental collocations of atoms." Most people hear that as bleak. I heard it as clean. It asked nothing of me and promised nothing to me, and after a childhood built around a promise that wasn't kept, a universe that made no promises felt like the first honest thing anyone had offered me.

The position has had its classic statements, and they deserve to be heard in the form their authors would sign. On 6 March 1927, at Battersea Town Hall in south London, Russell gave the lecture later published as "Why I Am Not a Christian." If everything needs a cause, he said, then so does God, and if something can exist without one, it may as well be the world. Underneath the arguments, he said, religion rests primarily on fear, and the Christian religion as organized in its churches had been, and still was, the principal enemy of moral progress in the world. What gives the lecture its force isn't any single argument. It's the tone of a man refusing to be frightened.

Richard Dawkins gave the same instinct a Darwinian edge in *The God Delusion* (2006). Any mind capable of designing a universe, he argued, would be at least as complex and so at least as improbable as what it designed, so a designer explains nothing and only moves the question back a step. He read religion as a byproduct of mental habits that once served survival, and our moral sense as the work of kin selection and reciprocal kindness, sometimes misfiring in generous ways. The view of morality I described above, a survival strategy dressed in the language of commandment, is this one in plainer words, and it's a serious view. The essay on [whether you can be good without God](/writing/can-you-be-good-without-god) takes it on directly.

The most careful case belongs to J. L. Mackie, whose *The Miracle of Theism* (1982) examined the arguments for God at their strongest, judged that they fail, and pressed the problem of evil once more. In "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955) he had argued that the core claims of theism are flatly inconsistent with the evil we see. Alvin Plantinga's free will defense in *God, Freedom, and Evil* (1974) is widely judged to have answered that strict charge of contradiction, but the objection didn't vanish. It moved from logic to evidence, and in that form it's at least as heavy. The essays on [whether God exists](/writing/does-god-actually-exist) and on [why a good God permits suffering](/writing/if-god-is-good-why-suffering) weigh these arguments in full.

Christopher Hitchens, in *God Is Not Great* (2007), pressed the moral indictment: religion is not only man-made and false but poisonous, sanctifying cruelty and protecting the powerful. I believed then that religion had real crimes on its ledger, and I believe it still. The church has blessed wars and burned women and shielded men who hurt children, and its defenders' reflex of changing the subject always struck me as nearly as damning as the crimes. I won't change the subject now. The church's failures aren't a misunderstanding the skeptic needs corrected. They're facts the believer has to carry. When I finally came to faith, it wasn't because someone explained those facts away, since no one can. It was in spite of them, and a believer who won't say that plainly is asking you to trust a version of history neither of you believes. The essay on [whether the church has done terrible things](/writing/apologetics-hasnt-the-church-done-terrible-things) faces that record.

Then there's the most candid passage in the literature of unbelief. Thomas Nagel, in *The Last Word* (1997), admitted that he wants atheism to be true. It wasn't merely that he didn't believe in God and hoped he was right, he wrote; he hoped there was no God and didn't want the universe to be like that. He called it the cosmic authority problem and suspected it was common. Nagel deserves credit for saying what few will say, and his candor cuts both ways: if desire can sit near the root of unbelief, it can sit near the root of belief too.

## A conclusion and a home

Beneath the arguments there was a smaller, harder fact I couldn't see for years. I was a fatherless boy who had decided very early that the first duty of a mind is to refuse the comforting lie, because somewhere a man had made promises to a woman and a child and hadn't kept them.

I have to be careful here, with the same care I'd want from you. It would be too easy to say I rejected God the Father because I had no father, and it would insult both the arguments and the boy. The arguments were real, and some of them are still the best the position has, and I don't wave them away from this side of the line. But it would also be a lie to leave the absence out of the room. A doctrine that promised a Father who arrives felt, at a level below argument, like cruelty dressed up as comfort. Something in me had settled the question before the philosophy showed up to defend it, and the settlement was simple: fathers don't come.

The psychology of belief has been argued in both directions. Sigmund Freud, in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927), read God as the longing for a protecting father projected onto the sky. Paul Vitz, in *Faith of the Fatherless* (1999), turned the argument around: a dead, absent or failed father can produce unbelief as readily as a longing produces belief. Vitz built that case from the biographies of well-known atheists and believers that he chose himself, not from any controlled study, so it stands as a suggestive hypothesis about motive and no more, and he didn't offer it as proof that atheism is false. Each saw something, and neither can decide whether God exists; the essay on [whether faith is just wishful thinking](/writing/is-faith-just-wishful-thinking) sets them side by side. C. S. Lewis named the underlying mistake in a short essay of 1941 he called "Bulverism": assuming a person is wrong and then explaining how he came to be so wrong, without ever showing that he is. How a man came to hold a view and whether it's true are separate questions, and the rule cuts both ways.

The distinction that matters isn't between reasons and motives, as if one person had only reasons and another only motives. It's between a conclusion and a home. A conclusion is where your reasons take you. A home is where you live, and you live there partly because of what it keeps out. My unbelief was both. It was a conclusion, and it was also a shelter, the one place the father who didn't come couldn't reach me, because in that place there were no fathers anywhere, and a cold that falls on everyone isn't a rejection. I had made the whole sky cold so that my own patch of it would stop meaning what it meant. A home doesn't tell you what to believe. It tells you which questions not to press, and that turned out to matter more.

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), gives this a wider frame. Modern unbelief often tells a subtraction story about itself: strip away the comforting illusions, and what remains is simply the truth. Taylor argues that exclusive humanism isn't a residue. It's a construction with its own moral beauty, and one of its strongest attractions is the figure of the grown-up who has the courage to face an indifferent universe without consolation. I recognize that figure, because I held my unbelief as exactly that kind of maturity. Taylor doesn't say the picture is false because it's attractive, only that it's a stance to be weighed like any other, not a default that needs no defense. Inside what he calls the immanent frame, the world can be lived as open to something beyond it or as closed, and the closed reading, offered as what any clear-eyed person simply sees, is what he calls spin: a reading that hides the fact that it's a reading. He says the same of the open reading when it's offered that way.

The same instrument has to be turned on the other side of the table, including the pulpit I stand in. Belief can be a home too, and the church has often offered it as one. When the pulpit treats doubt as disloyalty, or answers grief with a slogan, or changes the subject when the record of the church comes up, it has built a shelter and called it faith. That is the second atheist in the pulpit, the one who says the creed and lives as though nothing is watching. The atheist I was didn't know for years that he'd built a shelter. The one in the pulpit often doesn't know either.

## Erosion, not lightning

If you're waiting for the argument that got me, there wasn't one. There was an erosion. It took years, and for most of those years I didn't know it was happening. Conversion stories reach for lightning because lightning sells. The truth is slower and stranger, and it doesn't flatter the convert.

The first thing that moved was small, and I filed it away. At the end of good days, with work done well and time spent among people I liked, there was a residue: a sense of something owed that nobody was paying, an appetite with no object. I had a theory for it, and it was a good theory. Evolution built into us a hunger for meaning because the hunger was useful, and a hunger that fires with nothing to feed on is exactly what the atheist account predicts, a vestigial appetite like the craving for sugar in a world that no longer needs us to hoard calories. I still think that's the best answer the position has. But I noticed, over years, that the answer explained the ache without ever making it smaller. A pain that really is a misfire ought to shrink once you can see the wiring. This one didn't. A hunger that survives its own explanation is a strange kind of hunger, and I didn't press on the strangeness, because pressing on it frightened me in a way I couldn't have justified.

The difference is between explaining a thing and explaining it away. Lewis drew the inference in *Mere Christianity* (1952): hunger has food and thirst has water, and if a person finds in himself a desire nothing in this world can satisfy, the likeliest explanation is that he was made for another world. The objection deserves its weight: a desire doesn't guarantee its object, and Lewis offers an inference to the best explanation, not a proof, only as strong as the claim that the longing has no earthly object. But the Christian account had named the datum long before the evolutionary one offered to dissolve it. Augustine, opening his *Confessions* around 397, told God that our hearts are restless until they rest in him. Two accounts look at the same ache. One calls it noise and the other a signal, and the ache itself doesn't settle which. Alvin Plantinga, in *Warrant and Proper Function* (1993), added a warning for the debunker: if our faculties were shaped by unguided evolution for survival alone, we'd have no good reason to trust them on questions like this one, including the reasoning that reduces the ache to wiring. Critics contest the argument, but the warning is fair.

The second thing that moved was a category I met before I believed in its object. Rudolf Otto, in *The Idea of the Holy* (1917), described an experience he called the *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*, the presence before which a person trembles and toward which he's drawn, dread and desire refusing to come apart, a sense of the holy he held to be prior to, and not reducible to, the moral. I read Otto as an unbeliever and recognized the description, and that unsettled me more than any proof could have, because I had the experience and my account of the world said the category was empty. Certain music did it, and certain silence, and moments when the ordinary tilted and something enormous seemed to stand just behind it. I'd been calling those moments aesthetics. Otto had the nerve to call them data, and I couldn't shake the suspicion that he was being more empirical than I was, and that I was the one explaining away evidence to protect a theory.

The third thing was reading the wrong book, which is how it usually happens. In *Surprised by Joy* (1955), Lewis describes the end of his own atheism: in the Trinity Term of 1929, he writes, he gave in, admitted that God was God, and knelt and prayed, perhaps that night "the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England." The first time I read it, I took it for a pose, English understatement performing humility. Then it began happening to me, and I understood it was a report. Lewis pictures himself brought in like a prodigal, kicking and struggling and looking everywhere for a way of escape, and that detail is what makes the account credible. A man who wants to believe and talks himself into it has done something psychological. A man who has arranged his whole intellectual life around not believing, and finds himself believing anyway, against his preference and his pride, has run into something outside himself. Even Lewis's story was slower than his telling: Alister McGrath, in *C. S. Lewis: A Life* (2013), dates the kneeling to 1930 from the letters, and it was a surrender to theism only, with faith in Christ following in 1931. I didn't want Lewis to be right. Wanting had nothing to do with it, and that was his point.

So the arguments didn't save me. They cleared the ground. I lost confidence in the clean cosmology one beam at a time: the ache that wouldn't shrink, the category that wouldn't stay empty, the reluctant witnesses who had stood exactly where I stood and been overtaken. Then a night came when the resistance simply had nothing left to stand on, and what I had spent years calling loneliness turned out to have been a knock. I didn't walk an aisle. There was no music, no stage, no hand on my shoulder, only a man alone at the end of his reasons. The oldest and most embarrassing word in the religious vocabulary is the only accurate one. I was found.

## The father who runs

The text did what the philosophy couldn't. Luke 15 opens with a complaint. The religious professionals are scandalized by the company Jesus keeps, and they say so: "This man receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2, ESV). He answers them with three stories about lost things, a sheep, a coin and a son. The third story had been waiting for me my whole life.

The younger son asks for his inheritance while his father is still alive, which Kenneth Bailey, reading the parables against Middle Eastern village life in *Poet and Peasant* (1976), argued amounted to wishing the father dead. Some scholars think Bailey leaned too hard on modern village custom, but the insult is plain enough on the page. The son burns through the money in a far country, famine comes, and he ends up feeding pigs and envying what they eat. Then he composes a speech, the way desperate men do, and rehearses it on the road. He'll confess that he has sinned and is no longer worthy to be called a son, and he'll ask for work: "Treat me as one of your hired servants" (Luke 15:19, ESV). It's a good speech, and theologically correct. It asks for employment instead of sonship, because he has done the math on what he deserves, and the math is right.

Then comes the line that ended my defenses. "And he arose and came to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20, ESV). I couldn't stop reading it slowly. *While he was still a long way off* means the father had been watching the road. You don't see a figure at that distance unless you're looking for it, and you don't look for it only once. The watching was a habit, the posture of a man who never stopped expecting. *His father saw him and felt compassion* before the speech, before the apology, before a single line of the account was settled. *And ran*, which, Bailey notes, a dignified patriarch in that world would not do. The son begins his rehearsed confession, and if you lay verse 21 beside verse 19 you'll see that he never reaches the clause about hired servants. The father talks over him and calls for the best robe, a ring and shoes (Luke 15:22). The speech that was meant to negotiate the terms of his diminishment is interrupted by a father who won't let the word *servant* be spoken over his son.

The story doesn't end there. Jesus told it to the grumblers of verse 2, and its last scene belongs to them. The older son, the one who never left and never broke a rule, stands outside the feast in a rage, and the father goes out to him too and pleads with him. "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours" (Luke 15:31, ESV). Jesus never tells us whether the older brother goes in. The parable leaves him standing in the yard, near the house and far from the father, and that's where the religious are most tempted to stand. The atheist I was had never been inside the house. The practical atheist in the pulpit never leaves it and can still be a long way off. The father goes out to both.

I had spent a lifetime assembling evidence for a single proposition: fathers don't come. It was the axiom under the arguments, the cold under the sky. And here, at the center of the book I had dismissed without reading, was a story built to scandalize exactly that axiom, a father who doesn't wait to be approached with terms, who has been watching the road the whole time, and who runs. The psalms say it flatly: "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation" (Psalm 68:5, ESV). I didn't read that verse and then believe. I believed, and then I read that verse, and I sat with it a long time, like a man finding his own name in a will.

## What believing cost

This account doesn't end in triumph, because I didn't win. I lost. What I had spent years building and defending was taken from me, and I can still name the losses.

I lost the home. Unbelief had been the place the absence couldn't reach, with no fathers anywhere and a cold that fell on everyone, a grief spread so wide that it stopped singling me out. Faith took the roof off that shelter. It handed me back my own wound with my own name on it and told me the wound had been seen the whole time. That's a comfort, and it's also among the most painful things I've ever been told.

I lost the man who couldn't be fooled. My unbelief carried more of my identity than any doctrine I hold now. I was the one who saw through things, I belonged to the company of the clear-eyed, and belonging isn't nothing to a boy who belonged to no one. Believing meant becoming, in that company's eyes, exactly what I had most feared to be, which was taken in. This is the ethic Taylor describes, and it's a real one, with real courage in it. There are men I respect who regard a conversion like mine as a failure of nerve. I understand them perfectly. I would have said the same, and said it well.

And I lost the simplicity, which no one warns you about. For the atheist, suffering is an argument. For the believer it's a relationship, and years of pastoring, with its hospital rooms and gravesides and marriages coming apart, have made the old objections heavier to me, not lighter, because now they have faces. Mackie's argument lands differently when it's a prayer. I pray the word *Father* with no muscle memory for it, learning the vocabulary from the far side, and I'm raising five sons while I learn it, trying to be for them the evidence I never had. That weight didn't exist when I believed nothing. Unbelief asks less, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

What makes the weight bearable isn't an answer to Mackie. It's where the gospel puts the absence. At the center of the Christian story is a Son crying out to a Father who does not seem to come: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, ESV). Jesus prays the opening line of Psalm 22, a psalm that begins in abandonment and ends in vindication, and he prays it from inside the dark, not from above it. The faith I came to doesn't pretend abandonment is unreal. It puts that cry at the heart of its worship and insists the story didn't end on Friday. The resurrection doesn't explain suffering away. It tells us who has already been through it, and who will have the last word over it.

## For the reader still on the other side

If you stand where I stood, I owe you the same honesty at the end as at the start, so I'll say plainly what I'm not doing. I'm not going to grab your collar. I spent years on the other side of that grip and I hated it, and nobody was ever loved into anything by a checkmate. I don't think you're stupid. Your arguments may well be better than those of the believers around you. Mine were.

The arguments have their place, and the essays on [whether God exists](/writing/does-god-actually-exist), on [whether faith is irrational](/writing/is-faith-irrational), on [why Christianity rather than another faith](/writing/why-christianity), and on [what follows if Christianity is wrong](/writing/what-if-we-are-wrong) state the case against belief as its defenders would and answer it as honestly as we can. Read them looking for the place where the writer flinches.

I'll ask only one thing of you, the thing I refused to do, which costs nothing and risks everything. Press on the strangeness. Find the ache your theory explains but never reduces, and instead of filing it, sit with it long enough to notice whether it behaves like a misfire or like a signal. Audit your certainty the way you audit everyone else's. Ask how much of it is reasoning and how much is the shape of the room you happen to be standing in, and whether the room has a wound in it, as mine did. You don't have to believe anything at the end of that exercise. That isn't mine to ask, and the pressure you're braced for isn't coming.

The same request stands for the believer, and for the preacher first. Faith kept as a shelter from every hard question is the second atheism, and it can recite the creed perfectly. Ask whether your certainty is a conclusion or only a home.

But I'll tell you the last thing I know, the thing I couldn't have known when I was where you are. The approach doesn't feel like an approach. It feels like ordinary life: reading and working and lying awake, an ache you've explained and a strangeness you've filed and a book you keep not quite throwing across the room. The man being approached is the last to know. For years I stood in the clean cold, certain of one thing above all the others, that no one was coming.

Somebody was watching the road.
