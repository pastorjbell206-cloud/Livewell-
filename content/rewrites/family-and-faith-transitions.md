Picture two people, neither of them anyone in particular, typing nearly the same words into a search box late at night from opposite ends of the same sentence. Imagine the first became a Christian eight months ago. She quit drinking the way she used to, started reading a Bible she once mocked, and went home for Thanksgiving to find that her brother had warned everyone she'd joined something. Her mother took her aside in the kitchen and asked, gently and with real fear, what those people had done to her. Imagine the second is a father who raised his son in church and taught a Sunday school class for twenty years. He has just heard, over the phone, that the boy doesn't believe any of it anymore, and that he thinks, with a kind of patient pity, that his father never really thought it through. The two are imagined, but the question each is asking is common enough: how a family survives when the most important thing in one person's life looks, to the people who love him most, like a sign that something has gone wrong.

This essay is written for both of them, and for a third reader who is easy to forget: the mother in the kitchen, frightened for a new believer and unsure whether her fear is fair. Pastors get called into this conversation from both ends, and the first thing worth saying is that a change of faith is never private. Faith sets what a family treats as real, what it says grace over, what it does on Sunday morning and at a deathbed, what it hopes for its children. When one person's faith changes, in either direction, the shared world of the family changes with it, and everyone at the table feels the floor move.

I came to faith from atheism, so the view from the unbelieving side of the table isn't foreign to me. To a great many unbelievers, believers don't look wicked. They look credulous, holding on to something no thoughtful adult could hold. Knowing that view from the inside keeps me from reading a family's alarm as hostility. Much of what a new believer hears as opposition is love that can't find any other words. And the same knowledge makes me honest about the other direction, because the child who leaves the faith often looks at his believing parents exactly the way unbelievers so often look at believers, and he isn't always wrong about what he sees in the church, even when he's wrong about God.

The argument here turns on one distinction. Three different things feel almost identical from the inside, and a great deal of family damage comes from confusing them: persecution, which is suffering for Christ; ordinary friction, which is the cost any family pays when one member's world changes; and the convert's own zeal, which is suffering the believer has brought on himself and then baptized. Only the first carries the promise of blessing. The place to begin is a fact the Gospels don't hide: Jesus's own family thought he had lost his mind.

## "He is out of his mind"

Mark tells it without softening. Jesus had just chosen the twelve, and the crowds were pressing in. "Then he went home, and the crowd gathered again, so that they could not even eat. And when his family heard it, they went out to seize him, for they were saying, 'He is out of his mind'" (Mark 3:20–21, ESV). The phrase the ESV renders "his family" is literally "those from him," his own people, and the verb for what they meant to do is the one Mark later uses for the arrest in Gethsemane. They were coming to take him home by force.

See it from their side. The eldest son of a Nazareth household had left the family trade, gathered a band of fishermen and a tax collector, and was now drawing crowds so large he wasn't eating. And the authorities had noticed: "And the scribes who came down from Jerusalem were saying, 'He is possessed by Beelzebul,' and 'by the prince of demons he casts out the demons'" (Mark 3:22, ESV). A village family in an honor culture knew exactly what it meant when men from Jerusalem came asking about your son. Their verdict, that he was beside himself, was almost certainly a protective one. Better to bring him home as unwell than to watch him be condemned as dangerous. There is love in that, and fear, and shame, the same mixture in any family that has watched one of its own become someone they can't explain.

Mark then folds the scribes' accusation inside the family's visit, so that the two verdicts lie side by side. The scribes say he is possessed; his family says he is mad. Neither can make room for the possibility that he is exactly who he says he is. His family sends word in from outside. "And he answered them, 'Who are my mother and my brothers?' And looking about at those who sat around him, he said, 'Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother'" (Mark 3:33–35, ESV).

Those words are hard, and they're meant to be. Jesus is saying that a new household has formed around him, bound not by blood but by doing the will of God, and that its claim now runs deeper than the claim of the family at the door. But he didn't disown his mother, and the rest of the story proves it. John says flatly, "For not even his brothers believed in him" (John 7:5, ESV). Yet from the cross, in the last hours of his life, Jesus arranged for his mother's care: "Woman, behold, your son!" and to the beloved disciple, "Behold, your mother!" (John 19:26–27, ESV). He redefined family around the will of God without abandoning the family he was born into.

And the family didn't stay at the door. Paul lists among the witnesses of the risen Christ a name we would not have predicted: "Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles" (1 Corinthians 15:7, ESV). James, the brother who had not believed, became a pillar of the Jerusalem church. Luke's account of the days after the ascension places the whole group in one room: "All these with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers" (Acts 1:14, ESV). The family that set out to seize him ended up praying in his name. Notice what brought them there. It wasn't a better argument at the door. It was the resurrection.

That is the first word for the new believer whose family has said the same about you. You're in company you couldn't be ashamed of. But you aren't more faithful than Mary was, and the story doesn't end with the family outside.

## The case the family would make

A family that says you've lost your mind is usually making an argument, even when it comes out as tears, and the argument deserves to be heard in its strongest form before anyone answers it. We have watched people we love get pulled into groups that cost them everything. We remember Jonestown, where in November 1978 more than nine hundred people died in the Guyana jungle at the command of a preacher who had begun by promising community and racial justice. We have seen people find religion at the lowest point of their lives, in the wreck of a divorce or an addiction, and we have seen some of them lose it again within a year. You changed quickly. You have new friends who seem to matter more than we do. You say things you would have laughed at two years ago, and when we push back you seem to hear it as proof that you're right. From where we stand, that doesn't look like wisdom. It looks like capture.

The argument is old. Around the 170s a pagan philosopher named Celsus wrote an attack on Christianity, *The True Word*, which survives because Origen of Alexandria quoted it at length in his reply around 248. In the third book of Origen's reply, Celsus makes what may be the first family-values case against the church. He describes wool-workers, cobblers and laundry-workers, the least educated people in the city, who get hold of children and foolish women in private houses and tell them not to listen to their fathers or their schoolteachers, who only talk nonsense, but to come away to the workshop and learn what is truly good. To Celsus, Christianity was a movement that pried children loose from the authority of their homes. He was not entirely wrong about what he saw. The early church did grow house by house, and it did call sons and daughters to a loyalty their fathers didn't share.

Modern sociology has confirmed the pattern Celsus resented. In 1965 John Lofland and Rodney Stark published a study of a small millenarian group in the San Francisco Bay Area, an early American branch of what became the Unification Church. They found that people rarely joined because the doctrine persuaded them first. They joined because they had formed close attachments to members, and because their ties to people outside the group were weak or had been strained. Stark later made this finding the backbone of *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), arguing that the early church spread chiefly along existing networks of family and friendship, so that conversion was less a matter of being argued into a creed than of bringing one's faith into line with the people one had come to love.

Read with the family's eyes, that research sounds like an accusation, and in part it is one. But it also cuts against the charge of brainwashing. Eileen Barker studied the movement that had become the public symbol of it, and in *The Making of a Moonie: Choice or Brainwashing?* (1984) she found that the great majority of people who attended its recruitment workshops never joined, and most who did join eventually left. People were choosing, and most chose no.

William James saw the deeper problem more than a century ago. In *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), he criticized what he called medical materialism, the habit of dismissing a person's religious experience by pointing to its psychological or physical origins, as if calling Paul's vision on the Damascus road an epileptic seizure settled the question of what he saw. James argued that a state of mind should be judged by its fruits rather than by its roots, which is close to what Jesus said long before: "You will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16, ESV). He described conversion as the process by which a divided self, conscious of being wrong and unhappy, becomes unified around religious realities that had been at the edge of a person's life and now stand at its center. From the inside, that feels like coming to one's senses. From the outside, it looks like a personality change. Both descriptions can be sincere.

So the family's argument has a true center, and the new believer should grant it without resentment. Some groups do capture people, and a loving family has every right to name the signs: a group that demands you cut off your family, that takes control of your money, that keeps secrets from new members, or that treats its leader as beyond question. If those are present, the family's alarm is a gift, not an attack. But if they aren't, then "you've lost your mind" is doing the work James warned against. It explains a conviction away by where it came from, and it never gets around to asking whether it's true. The fair answer to a frightened family isn't to prove you're sane. It is to let them watch the fruit for as long as they need to.

## What the sword in Matthew 10 divides

The verse most often quoted to a new believer in a hard family is also the one most often misread. "Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth. I have not come to bring peace, but a sword. For I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. And a person's enemies will be those of his own household" (Matthew 10:34–36, ESV). Some believers hear a license in it: if my family is angry, I must be doing something right. Outsiders hear the home-wrecking faith Celsus described. Both readings lift the words out of their setting.

The setting is a commissioning. Jesus is sending the twelve out, and the whole chapter is a sober briefing on what it will cost them. He warns that "brother will deliver brother over to death, and the father his child, and children will rise against parents and have them put to death" (10:21, ESV), and he ties their treatment to his own: "If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more will they malign those of his household" (10:25, ESV). The sword in verse 34 is not something the disciples carry. Luke's parallel names it plainly as "division" (Luke 12:51, ESV), and in Matthew's chapter every blow falls on the disciples, not from them. The sword is what happens to a household when one of its members answers to a Lord the others do not.

Jesus is quoting the prophet Micah, and the source matters. Micah 7 is a lament over a society coming apart. "The godly has perished from the earth" (Micah 7:2, ESV), the prophet mourns; officials take bribes, neighbors can't be trusted, "for the son treats the father with contempt, the daughter rises up against her mother, the daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law; a man's enemies are the men of his own house" (Micah 7:6, ESV). In Micah the broken household is a symptom of judgment, and the contempt of the son for the father is part of the sin being mourned. Then the prophet turns: "But as for me, I will look to the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation; my God will hear me" (Micah 7:7, ESV). Jesus borrows the prophet's picture of divided homes. He doesn't borrow the son's contempt and commend it. The disciple is placed in the prophet's posture, waiting on God in a divided house, not in the posture of the son who despises his father.

The next verses make the point sharper. "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me, and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me. And whoever does not take his cross and follow me is not worthy of me. Whoever finds his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matthew 10:37–39, ESV). Luke's version speaks of hating father and mother (Luke 14:26), and Matthew's wording shows how that Semitic idiom was heard: it's about rank, not feeling. Jesus doesn't ask anyone to love a parent less than before. He asks to be loved more than anyone, which is a different thing, and it only makes sense if he is who he claims. And the image that follows is a cross, not a sword. The disciple who is divided from family is told to carry his own suffering, not to inflict it.

So the verdict on this text is simple enough to carry. Jesus warned that loyalty to him would divide households. He never made the division the proof of faithfulness.

## Persecution, friction or your own zeal

Persecution deserves its name, and naming it honestly keeps the word from being cheapened. In Carthage in 203, a young woman named Vibia Perpetua, about twenty-two, well born and nursing an infant son, was arrested as a catechumen preparing for baptism. Part of the account that survives, *The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity*, is her own prison diary, and its most painful pages are about her father, the one member of her family, she wrote, who would not rejoice in her suffering. He came to her again and again. Once she pointed to a water jar and asked whether it could be called anything other than what it was; when he said no, she told him that neither could she be called anything but what she was, a Christian. He threw himself at her in anger and then went away. Later he came back broken, begging her to pity his gray hairs, to think of her mother and brothers and her baby who couldn't live without her, kissing her hands and calling her no longer daughter but lady. At her hearing the presiding official, Hilarianus, told her to spare her father's old age and her son's infancy and offer the sacrifice for the emperors. She refused. When her father kept pressing her, Hilarianus had him thrown down and beaten with a rod, and Perpetua wrote that she felt it as if she herself had been struck, and grieved for his wretched old age.

Look at what she held together. She would not give up Christ to spare her father, and she never once despised him for asking. Her refusal was a single sentence; her grief for him fills the page. The fear her father felt was not foolish. He was right that she would die, and right that her son would grow up without her. That's persecution: suffering that comes because you belong to Christ and won't stop belonging to him. It still happens. In parts of the world today a convert can lose an inheritance, a marriage and sometimes her life at her own family's hands. If you are threatened or hurt by a family member over your faith, that is abuse, not a spiritual trial to be endured in silence. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. If the weight of it has pushed you toward despair, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

Most new believers in America are facing something else, and calling it persecution insults Perpetua and wounds the family. Peter describes it exactly: "With respect to this they are surprised when you do not join them in the same flood of debauchery, and they malign you" (1 Peter 4:4, ESV). They are surprised. The brother who loses his drinking partner, the mother who worries you've traded your family for a church, the father who jokes about your Bible at dinner: this is ordinary friction, the price every family pays when one member's world changes. It hurts, and it isn't persecution. It's grief that hasn't yet learned what it's grieving.

And then there is the suffering we bring on ourselves. Peter anticipated that too: "But let none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or as a meddler. Yet if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed, but let him glorify God in that name" (1 Peter 4:15–16, ESV). He puts the meddler in the same list as the thief. The new believer who turns every holiday into a debate, leaves pamphlets on the guest-room pillow and tells a grieving aunt where her unbelieving husband has gone will meet resistance, and it will be deserved. Those of us who came to faith from outside the church are especially prone to this, because the relief of finally seeing makes us impatient with everyone who doesn't. The honest test is humbling: would they still be angry with me if I believed exactly the same things and carried them more gently? If the answer is no, the offense is mine and not the gospel's.

## Honoring the parents who think you're wrong

The fifth commandment doesn't expire at eighteen, and it doesn't wait for agreement. "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12, ESV). The Hebrew verb for honor comes from the word for weight. To honor your parents is to give what they say and feel real weight, even when you can't do what they ask. Paul draws a line that matters here: "Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. 'Honor your father and mother' (this is the first commandment with a promise), 'that it may go well with you and that you may live long in the land'" (Ephesians 6:1–3, ESV). Obedience belongs to children under their parents' roof, and even there it is "in the Lord." Honor belongs to every season of life.

Jesus was sharp with anyone who used religion to get around it. Some were declaring their money Corban, given to God, so that it couldn't be spent on their aging parents, and he said they were "making void the word of God by your tradition that you have handed down" (Mark 7:13, ESV). A faith that gives you reasons to dishonor your mother is not the faith of Jesus. The limit of honor is real, but it is narrow. When the council in Jerusalem ordered the apostles to stop preaching, "Peter and the apostles answered, 'We must obey God rather than men'" (Acts 5:29, ESV). You may not deny Christ, or do what he forbids, to keep the peace at home. That's the line Perpetua held. Short of it, honor can nearly always still be given.

In practice that looks less dramatic than a new believer expects. At your parents' table, keep their customs; at yours, say grace without turning it into a sermon aimed at the guests. Stop trying to fix each other: the parent who keeps slipping devotional books into care packages and the grown child who keeps forwarding articles that debunk religion are sending the same message, which is that they can't accept the other as he is. Peter's instruction was written for exactly this kind of household: "but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect, having a good conscience, so that, when you are slandered, those who revile your good behavior in Christ may be put to shame" (1 Peter 3:15–16, ESV). The defense is given to someone who asks. The manner is part of the command. And the life is what the slander finally can't be made to fit.

Sometimes a boundary is needed, and a boundary isn't a wall. A boundary says, "I love you, and I'm not going anywhere, but I won't argue about this every time we're together." A wall ends the relationship. Walls are sometimes necessary, when disagreement has become abuse or contempt has become control, but they should be the last resort, never the first. And time is often kinder than the first months suggest.

## When it's your child who has changed

The most famous parent of a wandering child in Christian history nearly shut the door. When the young Augustine joined the Manichees, a sect his mother Monica considered blasphemous, she wept for him, he later wrote in the *Confessions*, more than most mothers weep over a child's death, and for a time she refused to live with him or let him eat at her table. Then she dreamed that she stood on a wooden rule, grieving, and a radiant young man told her to look, because where she was, there her son was also, and she saw Augustine standing beside her. When he tried to read it as a promise that she would come around to his view, she answered at once that it said he would be where she was, not the other way round. After that she let him back to the table. It took nearly nine more years. He slipped away from her to Rome by night, and she followed him to Italy anyway. He came to faith in a garden in Milan in 386 and was baptized the following Easter, and at Ostia, shortly before she died, she told him that the one thing she had wanted to live for was to see him a Christian, and God had given her more than that.

Her tears weren't the lesson. Her open table was. The parent of a new believer and the parent of a child who has left the faith are in the same position, and the temptation for both is the one James called medical materialism: to explain the change away. He's just been hurt. She's just in a crisis. They've got her. Sometimes that's partly true, but it never tells you whether what your child now believes is true, and your child can feel the difference between being understood and being diagnosed. Many who leave the faith are carrying questions the church refused to hear, or wounds the church caused, and those deserve a hearing before anything else does.

The child who has left owes something too. Language borrowed loosely from James Fowler's *Stages of Faith* (1981) is easy to use as a ladder: I've moved on to a reflective faith, and my parents are stuck at the conventional one. Fowler's own scheme doesn't allow that, since his stage beyond critical reflection learns to receive again, with a second innocence, much of what criticism set aside. A parent's faith that has carried them through a miscarriage, a bankruptcy and a burial isn't a problem to be solved. It's a life that has been lived. Disagree with it if you must, but don't look down on it.

Here is what neither side can do: argue the other into the kingdom. Jesus's brothers weren't won at the door. They were won by an empty tomb. Monica lived to see her son baptized. Perpetua's father, as far as her Passion tells us, never believed, and she went to her death not knowing whether he ever would. Both women were faithful, and faithfulness looked the same in each of them: refusing to deny Christ, and refusing to despise the one who wouldn't follow. You don't get to choose which of their stories becomes yours. If your family thinks you've lost your mind, you're carrying a small share of what Jesus carried from his own, and you aren't permitted to carry it as a sword. The one who asked "Who are my mother and my brothers?" spent some of his last breath on the cross providing for his mother. That pattern is heavier than winning any argument at the table. Keep the table open, keep the fruit where they can see it, and leave the ending with the one who walked out of the grave, because it was never yours to write.
