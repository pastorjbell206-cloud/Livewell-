Ask anyone who has seriously tried to become a better person, and sooner or later they'll tell you the same thing. Resolve carries you for a while. The new habit holds for three weeks, maybe six, and then a hard stretch comes, the old pattern resumes, and you discover it had only been waiting. Desire for a different life turns out to be cheap; the different life is not. The church has its own monuments to the fact, like the accountability group that became a place to report the same failure every Thursday.

Many people assume Christianity is a religious edition of the same project. On that view the believer simply strains harder, with a divine supervisor and a memorized promise for motivation. If that were what the faith offered, it would fail for the same reasons every self-improvement program fails, and a thoughtful skeptic would be right to shrug. The New Testament describes something else. Its word for the process is sanctification, the work by which God makes holy the people he has already forgiven, and its account of how that happens is odder than any program and more hopeful than despair.

The argument here turns on one distinction. Willpower self-improvement puts the self in both chairs: the self is the engineer and the self is the product, and the whole enterprise runs on a resource that was never as renewable as the brochures promised. Grace-trained habit begins elsewhere. It starts from a life already joined to Christ, and it uses practice, repetition and honest self-examination to keep that life in the way of a love that comes from outside it. Both involve effort. Only one can bear the weight of a whole human life.

## Why resolve runs out

Begin with what the secular account gets right. Aristotle saw it in the fourth century before Christ, in Book II of his *Nicomachean Ethics*. Character, he argued, does not arise in us by nature. We are able to receive it, and it is formed by practice. We become just by doing just things and brave by doing brave things, the way a person becomes a builder by building or a musician by playing. What results is a *hexis*, a settled disposition that has become second nature. Aristotle's name for virtue of character is itself built on the Greek word for habit, and he meant the connection.

He also saw the gap that resolution can't close. In Book VII he distinguishes the continent person from the temperate one. The continent person still wants the wrong thing and refrains by effort; the temperate person no longer wants it in the same way. Both may keep the same rule at the same table, but only one of them is at peace. Aristotle judged the continent man better than the one who simply gives in, but he didn't think him finished, because a life held together by force against its own desires is a life at war with itself. Most of what we call willpower is continence. It is admirable and exhausting, and it isn't what the moral tradition, pagan or Christian, meant by a changed person.

Modern psychology has reached something close to Aristotle by a different road. Wendy Wood, in *Good Habits, Bad Habits* (2019), argues that much of daily behavior is habitual, cued by context and running largely beneath deliberate intention, which is why people who succeed at changing tend to rearrange their circumstances rather than summon more resolve. The popular theory that willpower is a limited resource that runs down with use, like a tiring muscle, proposed by Roy Baumeister and colleagues in 1998, has fared less well: a preregistered replication in twenty-three laboratories, led by Martin Hagger and published in 2016, tested one standard version of the effect and found it close to zero, and what self-control actually is remains an open question. The practical verdict of the ancient and modern witnesses is nonetheless the same. You can't resolve your way into a new character. You have to be formed into one.

The Christian should concede this before saying anything else. The secular account has the symptom right. What the account can't supply are the two things the self-improvement project quietly assumes: that the self is able to choose and sustain the right habits, and that the self is the right destination for them. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), describes the modern person as a buffered self, insulated from the powers that once pressed in on the porous self of the older world, and able to treat its deepest purposes as arising from within. Give that self the old spiritual practices and it will turn fasting into a concentration aid and silence into stress relief, becoming, if it succeeds, a calmer version of the same person, still curved in on itself. I won't pretend a calmer week is nothing. But it isn't sanctification, and the Scriptures explain why.

## What Paul says actually happens

Paul's account of change in Romans begins, strangely for a self-help reader, with a death that has already happened. Romans 6 answers people who suspect that grace makes sin cheap, and Paul's reply isn't a new program of effort but a fact about who they are: "We know that our old self was crucified with him in order that the body of sin might be brought to nothing, so that we would no longer be enslaved to sin" (Romans 6:6, ESV). The verbs are past tense. The believer has been joined to Christ in his death and rising, and the command that follows rests entirely on that union: "So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus" (Romans 6:11, ESV). God first says what is true and only then says what to do. The ethic of Romans is "become what you are," and it can't be turned around into "become something by trying" without ceasing to be Paul.

Romans 7 is the most disputed page in the letter. "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing" (Romans 7:19, ESV). Is this the Christian, still at war with himself? Augustine first read it as a person under the law before grace, then, in the years of the Pelagian controversy, came to read it as the believer, Paul himself included, and he recorded the change of mind in his *Retractations* (426–427). Luther and Calvin followed the later Augustine. Wesley and many modern interpreters read it as the awakened person who knows the law and lacks the power, a portrait of life before the Spirit that chapter 8 answers. On one point, though, the readings largely agree: Romans 7 is the anatomy of willpower under law. The law names the good accurately and supplies no power to do it, and the self that tries to close the distance by resolve ends in a cry, not a victory: "Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" (Romans 7:24, ESV).

Romans 8 answers the cry, and the word Spirit fills that chapter as it fills no other in the letter. It also holds the verb that keeps the whole account from going soft: "For if you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live" (Romans 8:13, ESV). The believer does the putting to death, and it's a real act, costly and repeated. But it is done by the Spirit, which makes it the one kind of effort that doesn't draw on the believer's own reserves. Its goal isn't self-improvement at all. It is resemblance: "For those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, in order that he might be the firstborn among many brothers" (Romans 8:29, ESV). The destination of Christian change is a face, and it isn't mine.

Galatians 5 gives the same account in the language of the orchard. "But I say, walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh" (Galatians 5:16, ESV). We usually reverse the order, assuming we must conquer the desire before we can walk with God. Paul puts the walking first. He then sets the works of the flesh against the fruit of the Spirit, and the nouns carry the argument, since works are manufactured and fruit is grown. "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness," he writes (Galatians 5:22, ESV), and finishes: "gentleness, self-control; against such things there is no law" (Galatians 5:23, ESV). Paul's word for self-control is *enkrateia*, the same word Aristotle used for the continent man holding the line against his desires. Paul has taken the philosophers' name for willpower and listed it as fruit. The Scottish preacher Thomas Chalmers later called this the expulsive power of a new affection. Ordering a love out of the heart leaves a vacancy the old love soon reclaims; a stronger love that takes up residence displaces it. That's why so many of our attempts to quit something collapse. The Spirit begins with something to love, and the leaving follows.

Paul's most compressed account of how the new love takes hold is in 2 Corinthians: "And we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another. For this comes from the Lord who is the Spirit" (2 Corinthians 3:18, ESV). The verb is passive and ongoing; something is being done to us, and it isn't done yet. The means is beholding, which is to say attention; people come to resemble whatever they attend to. The pace is degree by degree, which means the change is mostly visible in retrospect, a gradient noticed across years rather than a morning's arrival.

Colossians 3 adds what the solitary reader tends to miss. It opens, like Romans 6, with a fact: "For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3, ESV). Then comes the command, "Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5, ESV), and then the image of clothing, the old self taken off and the new self put on, "which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator" (Colossians 3:10, ESV). Then the setting in which all of it happens: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God" (Colossians 3:16, ESV). The "you" is plural. Paul pictures change happening in a congregation that sings, teaches and corrects one another, not in a lone will straining at its own reflection.

## Four ways the church has read sanctification

If Scripture is this clear about the mechanism, why do Christians disagree so sharply about sanctification? Because the texts hold together things that come apart in practice: a death already accomplished and a death still to be carried out, a work wholly God's and an effort wholly ours. Paul puts both into one sentence to the Philippians. "Therefore, my beloved, as you have always obeyed, so now, not only as in my presence but much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling," he writes (Philippians 2:12, ESV), and then gives the reason: "for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13, ESV). Every tradition affirms both verses. They differ over how the halves fit and how much change a Christian should expect before death. This is a second-order question. It has divided faithful churches for centuries without dividing the faith, and each view deserves to be heard as its own defenders would put it.

The Reformed tradition teaches progressive sanctification. John Calvin, in the final edition of his *Institutes* (1559), described justification and sanctification as a double grace received together in union with Christ, distinct but never separable: we are reconciled by Christ's righteousness and remade by his Spirit. He described repentance as a lifelong dying of the old self and quickening of the new, echoing the first of Luther's *Ninety-Five Theses* (1517), which held that when Christ called for repentance he meant the whole life of believers. The Westminster Shorter Catechism (1647) gave the tradition its definition: "Sanctification is the work of God's free grace, whereby we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die unto sin, and live unto righteousness." The Westminster Confession (chapter 13) adds the tradition's realism: the work reaches every part of the person yet stays imperfect in this life, so the believer lives in a continual war of flesh against Spirit, and yet the regenerate part, strengthened by the Spirit, overcomes, and the saints grow in grace. Its strength is honesty. It won't promise the struggling Christian an escape from struggle, and it won't let him conclude from the struggle that grace has failed.

John Wesley thought this expected too little of grace. In *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection* (1766) he gathered decades of teaching on what he called entire sanctification, and it is easy to caricature. Wesley didn't teach that a Christian could be free of ignorance, mistakes, weakness or temptation. He taught that the heart could be cleansed of inward sin and so filled with love for God and neighbor that love alone governed it, that sin in the strict sense, a voluntary transgression of a known law, need no longer be committed, and that this could be received by faith in a moment, prepared by gradual growth before it and followed by more growth after. He believed it could also be lost. His texts were the promises, among them Paul's prayer: "Now may the God of peace himself sanctify you completely, and may your whole spirit and soul and body be kept blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:23, ESV). Why pray for what you never expect God to give? The strongest form of his case is pastoral. A church that expects nothing receives nothing, and a Christianity that treats Romans 7 as the permanent ceiling of the Christian life has made an alibi out of a lament.

The Keswick movement, named for the convention that began in 1875 in the English Lake District town of Keswick, grew from the Higher Life teaching that Hannah Whitall Smith popularized the same year in *The Christian's Secret of a Happy Life*. Its remedy for believers worn out by striving was a crisis of surrender: stop striving in your own strength, yield fully to Christ, and trust him to live his life through you. Sin's power, it taught, is not removed but counteracted moment by moment by the indwelling Christ, and its popular slogan became "let go and let God." It brought real relief to exhausted Christians and sent many of them into missions. J. I. Packer, who had been taught it as a new believer at Oxford in the 1940s and found it didn't deliver what it promised, made the sharpest critique in *Keep in Step with the Spirit* (1984). He argued that its passivity misread a New Testament full of commands to strive, fight and put to death, that it sorted Christians into ordinary and surrendered classes, and that for all its talk of God doing everything it left the decisive move with the believer's act of surrender. His alternative joined effort to dependence: the Christian labors at holiness precisely because God is at work in him, which is Philippians 2 stated as a way of life.

The Orthodox churches frame the question differently. Their word is theosis, deification. Its charter text is Peter's promise that "you may become partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4, ESV), and its classic statement is Athanasius's claim in *On the Incarnation* that the Word became human so that we might be made divine. Gregory Palamas, defending the monks of Mount Athos in the fourteenth century, distinguished God's essence, which no creature can share, from his uncreated energies, in which the saints truly participate. Theosis is union by grace, never by nature. Its challenge to the West is that we have often reduced salvation to a legal verdict plus a program of moral repair, when the gospel's goal was always communion, a human life drawn so deeply into God's that it shines with a light not its own.

This essay lands where Packer landed: sanctification is progressive, real and unfinished in this life, carried out by the Spirit through the believer's active and dependent effort. The strongest objection to that view hasn't been fully answered, and it is Wesley's. A tradition honest about remaining sin can grow comfortable with it, and those who hold the progressive view, this essay included, have to admit how often Romans 7 has been used to excuse what Romans 8 was written to address. The Orthodox charge stands too, though Calvin's insistence on union with Christ gives the Reformed reader somewhere to meet it. A Wesleyan or Orthodox reader who finishes this section unconvinced is still a brother or sister, and has something to teach the rest of us.

## Grace-trained habits

If willpower fails and passivity misreads the text, what does grace-trained effort look like? Thomas Aquinas answered well. In the *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274) he took over Aristotle's account of habit and then added what Aristotle couldn't. There are acquired virtues, built by practice in the way Aristotle described, and there are infused virtues, given by God, which practice doesn't create but does exercise and deepen. Grace doesn't skip the work of habit. It changes where the work starts. Aristotle assumed his students had been well raised before training could take, and Paul's reader in Romans 7 can't perform the good acts that would build the good habit. Grace breaks that circle by giving the new disposition first, so that practice trains a life already given rather than building one from nothing.

Dallas Willard spent his career making this practical. In *The Spirit of the Disciplines* (1988) he argued that Christians fail to live as Jesus taught because they try to obey in the moment of pressure without having trained for it, like someone who expects to perform in the game while never practicing. The disciplines, among them solitude, silence, fasting, study, worship, confession and service, are things within our power that make us able, over time, to do what we can't do by direct effort. They earn nothing. They place us where God can do in us what we can't do for ourselves. In *Renovation of the Heart* (2002) he gave the pattern a plain frame: a vision of life in God's kingdom, an intention to live it, and the ordinary means that make the intention real. Willard often said that grace is opposed to earning, not to effort. The Keswick error treated effort as the rival of grace. The self-improvement error treats effort as its substitute. Willard's picture is effort as the shape grace takes in a willing life.

James K. A. Smith supplies what the bookish Christian tends to miss. In *You Are What You Love* (2016), drawing on Augustine, he argues that human beings are lovers before we are thinkers, that our loves are trained more by habit than by argument, and that every culture runs its own liturgies, repeated rituals that aim desire at some picture of the good life. His best-known example is the shopping mall, and the feed and the stadium catechize us the same way, whether or not we notice. It follows that you can't think your way into new loves any more than Aristotle's continent man could argue himself into temperance. You have to be re-habituated, and the church's worship, its repeated confession, praise, hearing of the Word and eating at the Table, is a counter-liturgy for exactly that. This is Colossians 3:16 in a philosopher's vocabulary.

Put these together and the distinction comes into focus. Willpower self-improvement and grace-trained habit can involve identical outward acts. Two people can rise at the same hour, read the same chapter and skip the same meal. What differs is direction. One practice aims at the self as its product and leaves the self at the center, better managed. The other aims at God and hands the self over. Jesus gave the logic of the second in a sentence that judges every rule of life: "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matthew 16:25, ESV). Measured by that sentence, a rule of life that makes you more secure and self-possessed may be failing while it feels like success, and one that strips you of control may be succeeding while it feels like loss. I have fasted with my mind on my own progress and fasted with my hunger turned toward God, and nobody watching could have told the two apart. Only the aim distinguishes them, which is why the church has always paired its disciplines with examination.

## The examined life, lived before God

At his trial in Athens in 399 BC, Socrates told the jury that a life without examination isn't worth living for a human being (Plato, *Apology* 38a). He died for the conviction. The church inherited it, and learned that examination has a shadow. Scrutiny aimed only at the self can leave a person more anxious and more preoccupied, cataloguing failures without any hope of relief. Luther, lecturing on Romans in 1515 and 1516, described sin as the heart curved in on itself, and there is a kind of self-examination that is simply that condition practiced with a notebook.

Augustine showed the other way. His *Confessions* (397–400) examine a life in direct address to God from the first line to the last. He looks without flinching at a boyhood theft of pears he didn't even want and at a career ambition that left him, by his own account, more miserable than a drunk beggar he passed in the streets of Milan, and he can bear to look because he isn't looking alone. Christian examination happens in company. It is the prayer of the psalm: "Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts!" (Psalm 139:23, ESV), and then, "And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!" (Psalm 139:24, ESV). The psalmist asks God to search because he knows what Jeremiah knew: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9, ESV). A deceitful heart can't be its own auditor. It needs a searcher who sees more than it does and loves it anyway.

Ignatius of Loyola gave this its most durable daily form. In the *Spiritual Exercises*, approved by Pope Paul III and printed in 1548, he set out a short examination of conscience in five movements: give thanks to God for the gifts received; ask for grace to see your sins truly and turn from them; go back over the day hour by hour, in thought, word and deed; ask pardon; and resolve, with God's grace, to amend. The order is the lesson. The examen begins with gratitude, so that whatever the review uncovers is uncovered by someone who has already remembered he is held, and it ends in a request for grace rather than a verdict on the self. Ignatius knew the alternative from inside. During his stay at Manresa in 1522 and 1523 he was tormented by scruples, confessing the same sins again and again until he nearly despaired, and the *Exercises* bear the marks of a man who learned that relentless self-accusation isn't holiness. If self-examination has become that kind of trial for you, don't carry it alone; bring it to a pastor or a counselor you trust. If the accusation has turned into thoughts of harming yourself, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline today. Examination under grace never requires despair.

Paul told the Corinthians, "Examine yourselves, to see whether you are in the faith" (2 Corinthians 13:5, ESV), and he said it to a congregation, not a hermit. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing *Life Together* (1939) out of the underground seminary at Finkenwalde, argued that sin wants to stay hidden and gathers strength in isolation, and that confession to a brother or sister breaks it into the light of community. Examination kept wholly private tends to confirm its own verdicts. Those of us who preach know the danger. A preacher can warn against pride while guarding his own, and describe self-deception precisely while going on deceiving himself, and the people listening feel the hollowness without being able to say where it comes from. I have preached that way. The remedy is the one Ignatius and Bonhoeffer describe: examination before God, begun in thanks, spoken to another person, and ended in a request for grace.

## From one degree of glory to another

The person standing at the wall where this began is right about the wall. Resolve runs out, continence exhausts, and the self can't be both surgeon and patient for long. Where that person is wrong, if the gospel is true, is in reading the failure as the end of the story. Romans 7 ends in a cry, and the cry is answered by a person rather than a better plan: "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (Romans 7:25, ESV).

There is still a death in it. The old self has to be put to death again and again, and nothing in us goes quietly. But it is a death joined to his, and his didn't end in a tomb: "If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you" (Romans 8:11, ESV). The power behind the slow change is resurrection power, working at the pace of a life.

Nor is the finish line in this life. John says so with a restraint that steadies the perfectionist and unsettles the complacent: "Beloved, we are God's children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2, ESV). Beholding again. What began as a glimpse with unveiled face ends in sight, and the sight will finish what the glimpse began.

I haven't kept any of this well. There have been stretches when the public work kept growing while the life beneath it thinned, and my preaching, my marriage and my attention to my sons all paid for it. So the question left over isn't whether you have the discipline to become better. You don't, and neither do I. The question is what you are beholding, and whether tonight you'll bring the day just lived before the one who already sees it, begin with thanks, name what you'd rather not, and ask for grace to keep in step tomorrow. It won't feel like change. The work is his, the consent is yours, and the consent has to be given again in the morning.
