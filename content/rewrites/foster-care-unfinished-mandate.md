Most American churches can recite James 1:27, and very few could say how many children in their own county slept last night in a house that isn't theirs. The children the verse describes are mostly not overseas, and most are not orphans in the sense the church pictures. The federal AFCARS reports count well over three hundred thousand American children in foster care on a given day, and for much of the last decade the figure stood close to four hundred thousand. The most common reason for removal in those reports is neglect, which in real case files is hard to pull apart from poverty, addiction and untreated mental illness. Most of these children have living parents, and the most common plan in their files is to send them home.

So the question people type into a search box, what the Bible says about foster care and adoption, has an awkward first answer. Scripture says nothing about foster care, a modern legal arrangement, and Israel's law has no statute for adoption at all. What it does say is a great deal about the fatherless, and it makes adoption the name of salvation itself. The church has tended to read the second fact as a mandate to move children from worse families to better ones. The first points somewhere harder. God's answer to the solitary child is not a transfer. It is a home. In the American system that means, for most children, their own home made safe enough to return to, and for the rest a family that takes them in, and in both cases a church close enough to help.

I was raised without a father, and I know something of growing up with no one whose name covers you. That isn't the same as being taken into care, and I won't pretend it is. I have preached James 1:27 more often than I have driven anyone to a licensing class, and it has been easier to feel the verse than to reorganize a church around it. This essay is written from inside that failure, not above it.

## The fatherless in Scripture

The law's concern for the widow, the fatherless and the stranger is traced in [What Does the Bible Say About Widows, Orphans and Strangers?](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger). Two points from it matter here. The Hebrew word usually rendered orphan means fatherless, and many of those children had a living mother. And the law's first remedy kept that fragile household fed: the forgotten sheaf in the field was assigned by name, "It shall be for the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow" (Deuteronomy 24:19, ESV). Israel's first answer to the fatherless child was not to take him from his mother. It was to make sure the two of them could eat.

Psalm 68 carries the idea into worship. It opens with the words Moses spoke when the ark set out (Numbers 10:35) and celebrates God as the warrior who marched from Sinai, and in the middle of that victory song it stops to say who this God is. "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation." (Psalm 68:5, ESV). Then it says what he does. "God settles the solitary in a home; he leads out the prisoners to prosperity, but the rebellious dwell in a parched land." (Psalm 68:6, ESV).

The second verse is the one the church skips, and it holds the whole matter. The solitary are settled in a house, not handed a program or a building, and placed inside a household where someone knows their name. That is the biblical shape of care for a child without a protector, and nothing less than a family table satisfies it.

The narratives fill in what the psalm states. When Esther's parents died, "Mordecai took her as his own daughter" (Esther 2:7, ESV), which is what child welfare workers now call kinship care. Moses's story is stranger. Pharaoh's daughter raised him as her son, but his own mother was first hired to nurse him (Exodus 2:7 to 10), and when he was grown he went out to his own people and looked on their burdens (Exodus 2:11). Scripture's most famous adopted child kept his mother's milk and his people's burden.

Then there is Mephibosheth, Jonathan's lame son, whom David sought out to show him kindness for Jonathan's sake, a kindness of covenant loyalty. "So Mephibosheth ate at David's table, like one of the king's sons." (2 Samuel 9:11, ESV). Nothing in him earned it. He was brought in because of a promise made to his father.

Put the pieces together and a pattern appears. God's care for the fatherless runs first through the child's own household, then through kin, and then, where both have failed, through someone who takes the child in under covenant and gives him a place at the table. None of these stories erases the child's origin. The verse the church quotes most keeps the same order: "Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained from the world." (James 1:27, ESV). The child and the mother stand in the same sentence, and the church is sent to both.

## Adoption in Paul, and what it does not license

The New Testament word for adoption, *huiothesia*, literally the placing of a son, appears only five times, all in Paul's letters, and it names salvation from the Father's side. "For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15, ESV). To the Galatians he says the same. "And because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, 'Abba! Father!'" (Galatians 4:6, ESV). "So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God." (Galatians 4:7, ESV). In Ephesians the adoption reaches back before time: "he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will" (Ephesians 1:5, ESV).

What Paul's first readers heard in the word is a real scholarly question. Francis Lyall in *Slaves, Citizens, Sons* (1984) and Trevor Burke in *Adopted into God's Family* (2006) read it against Roman law, which his readers in Rome and the Roman colonies of Galatia would have known. Roman adoption was chiefly an instrument of inheritance. It usually concerned an older boy or a grown man, often one whose parents were alive, and moved him from one father's legal authority to another's; he took the new family's name and stood to inherit. Julius Caesar's will made his great-nephew Octavian his adopted son and heir, and Octavian became Augustus. In that world, adoption was how an outsider became the heir.

James M. Scott, in *Adoption as Sons of God* (1992), argued that Paul's real source is Israel's Scripture. Paul lists "the adoption" first among Israel's privileges (Romans 9:4), and God's promise to David's heir, "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Samuel 7:14, ESV), runs through his thought. The readings needn't compete, and either way the essentials hold. The Father initiates the adoption. The Son pays for it, sent "to redeem those who were under the law" (Galatians 4:5, ESV). And the adopted receive a name, an inheritance, and the Spirit who teaches them to call God what Jesus called him.

It is also unfinished. The same chapter of Romans that announces our adoption says we are still waiting for it. "And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies." (Romans 8:23, ESV). The legal act is done. The being at home is not. A child in care who takes years to believe the new house will still be there tomorrow is Romans 8 in miniature. Christians, after all, have been slow to feel safe with God.

Out of this theology came one of the most attractive movements in recent evangelical life. Russell Moore's *Adopted for Life* (2009) argued that adoption is a picture of the gospel itself, not a charitable sideline, and that churches should make taking in a child ordinary and supported. He did not tell every family to adopt; he argued that every Christian is called to care for orphans, and that the whole church should stand behind the families who take a child in. Many children now have parents because families answered. The instinct is right at its root: a people who were taken in should be the first to take others in.

But a distinction has to be drawn here, and the whole essay turns on it. God's adoption of sinners is pure gain. We lose nothing but our slavery. A child's adoption is a gain built on a loss. Before any child is adopted something has broken: a mother's life, a father's presence, a family's capacity to keep the child safe. The child's first family is not the slave master of Galatians 4, and some adult adoptees have said, with pain, that they were made into sermon illustrations in which their birth parents played the part of sin. The metaphor runs downward, from God to us. It does not turn every human adoption into a reenactment of redemption, and it does not license the church to treat a child's first family as the thing the child is being saved from.

## How foster care in America actually works

The modern system has a Protestant prehistory, and not all of it flatters us. George Müller's orphan houses in Bristol, begun in 1836, were a genuine work of faith. In 1853 Charles Loring Brace founded the Children's Aid Society in New York, and from the next year until 1929 its orphan trains carried, by the usual estimates, around two hundred thousand children west to farm families. Some found real homes. Many were not orphans but the children of poor Catholic and immigrant parents, and some were taken in as unpaid labor. The rescue impulse and a quiet contempt for poor families arrived together, and not for the last time.

Today's system took shape through federal laws, each correcting the last. The Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978 answered decades in which Native children were removed from their families at startling rates, and required that relatives and tribes be preferred; in 2023 the Supreme Court turned back a broad constitutional challenge to it. The Adoption Assistance and Child Welfare Act of 1980 required reasonable efforts to keep families together and reunify them. When children then drifted in care for years, the Adoption and Safe Families Act of 1997 put a clock on reunification: after a child has spent fifteen of the most recent twenty-two months in care, the state generally must move to terminate parental rights, with exceptions. A 2008 law let states extend care to twenty-one, and the Family First Prevention Services Act of 2018 opened federal money to services that keep children safely at home. This is a general description, not legal advice; anyone facing a real decision needs a caseworker or a lawyer in their own state.

A few things hold across the states. A child enters care when a court finds home unsafe. The child goes, if possible, to relatives, who now provide a large share of placements, and otherwise to a licensed foster family or, less and less, a group setting. The most common permanency plan by far is reunification, which is also the most common way children leave care. Parents receive a case plan, often treatment, housing, classes and supervised visits, and the clock starts. If reunification fails, the plan usually becomes adoption or guardianship. Tens of thousands of children whose parents' rights have been terminated wait for adoptive families at any given time, many of them teenagers and sibling groups.

Then there are the young people who reach adulthood in care without a permanent family. They are said to age out. The Midwest Evaluation led by Mark Courtney, which followed young people leaving care in Illinois, Iowa and Wisconsin to age twenty-six (its final report came in 2011), found them doing markedly worse than their peers in education, earnings, housing and involvement with the justice system. They are not failing at adulthood. They are attempting it without a household behind them.

## The adoption movement and its critics

In the 2000s American evangelicals discovered the orphan. Conferences, networks such as the Christian Alliance for Orphans, and church adoption funds turned James 1:27 into a movement, and many families adopted, a large number internationally. Its best defenders would put the case this way. Children need families, and the evidence that institutions harm them is strong. The Bucharest Early Intervention Project, reported by Charles Nelson, Nathan Fox and Charles Zeanah in *Romania's Abandoned Children* (2014), randomly assigned young children in Romanian orphanages either to stay or to go to foster families, and the children placed in families did markedly better, especially when placed young. A child left in an institution while adults debate is harmed by the debate. A critique that leaves children in orphanages has not served them, and the answer to corrupt adoption is honest adoption, not none.

That case is strong, but it is not the whole story. The journalist Kathryn Joyce argued in *The Child Catchers* (2013) that the movement's rhetoric of a vast orphan crisis, joined to the money that flowed with adoptions, created a demand for adoptable children that poor countries with weak laws began to supply. The widely repeated figure of well over a hundred million orphans came from UNICEF estimates that counted any child who had lost one parent, and most of those children lived with a surviving parent or relatives. In the mid-2000s Guatemala became one of the largest sources of children adopted by Americans, through a private, fee-driven process run largely by lawyers and notaries; investigations found mothers paid or pressured to relinquish and some babies taken outright, and adoptions to the United States were halted in 2008. Ethiopia became a leading source at the end of the decade, and reporting found children with living parents recorded as orphans. In 2018 Ethiopia's parliament banned intercountry adoption.

The most searching critic writes from inside the faith. David Smolin, a law professor at Samford University's Cumberland School of Law, came to the subject through his own family. He and his wife adopted two sisters from India in 1998 and later learned that their mother had left them at a children's home so they could be educated, and that they had been placed for adoption without her consent. In "Child Laundering" (2006) he described how children obtained by purchase, fraud or abduction could be passed through paperwork until they emerged as legal orphans, clean on the page. In later writing he pressed the point theologically: the Scripture the movement quoted keeps the orphan and the widow together, and a ministry that separates a child from a living, impoverished mother has inverted the text it cites.

Leaders within the movement, to their credit, heard much of this and moved their emphasis toward family preservation and family-based care. That is how a movement should answer evidence. But the verdict still has to be spoken plainly. A true theology of adoption and real love for children did not keep Christians from becoming, in some places, the market for a trade in other people's sons and daughters. We did not mean it. We funded it.

The domestic system has its own critics, and they cut in both political directions. Dorothy Roberts, a legal scholar, has argued from *Shattered Bonds* (2002) through *Torn Apart* (2022) that American child welfare works as surveillance of poor families, above all Black families, who are investigated and separated far out of proportion to their numbers, and that much of what gets labeled neglect is poverty. In the later book she calls for abolishing the system. At its strongest the argument is this: removal is itself a trauma, and a system that answers a family's poverty by taking its children has its priorities backward. She sees what too many Christians have refused to see. What her account can't fully hold is the child in real danger at home, and the thick, unpaid, voluntary community her alternative requires and no policy can conjure.

From the other side, conservatives charge the system with hostility to faith, and in *Fulton v. City of Philadelphia* (2021) the Supreme Court unanimously sided with a Catholic agency the city had excluded. The concern is legitimate, but a church can win its right to take part and then not take part.

The first-order conviction binds everyone: God is the Father of the fatherless, and a people bearing his name are answerable for the child without a protector. Where removal thresholds should sit, how to weigh family preservation against the permanency clock, and what to do about intercountry adoption are second-order and prudential, and Christians who love the same children land in different places. This essay doesn't settle those policy questions. It follows the order the system itself sets, reunification first in most cases and kin before strangers, and asks the church to serve that order with help for parents, honor for relatives, and families ready to take in the children who cannot go home. The strongest objection it can't fully answer is the child for whom one more chance for the parents is one more year of danger. A congregation doesn't need to settle any of it to bring dinner to a foster family on Thursday. It does need to refuse the old trick of letting the argument stand in for the casserole.

## Birth parents, kin, and children from hard places

If reunification is the most common goal, the most neglected person in the church's thinking about foster care is the parent. She is usually not a villain. She is often young and poor, wrestling with an addiction or an untreated mental illness, the child of a household that failed her in the same ways. A court may have been right to intervene. She is also, very often, the person the child most wants to see. A church that prays for the child and speaks of the mother only as the problem has taken sides against the outcome the child is most likely to live, and the child can tell. Children in care often carry a fierce loyalty to parents who hurt them, and a foster home that treats the mother with contempt asks the child to choose.

The alternative is harder and less photogenic. It looks like foster parents who speak of the mother with respect in front of her children and treat visits as the child's right rather than an intrusion. It looks like a church member who sits with her in the courthouse hallway, and a congregation that helps with the deposit on the apartment she needs before the judge will send her children home, then stays after they come home and the support disappears. None of it makes a good slide on missions Sunday. All of it is the widow in James 1:27, visited in her affliction alongside her child.

Kinship care deserves the same honor. A grandmother in her sixties who takes in three grandchildren overnight is doing what Mordecai did for Esther, often on a fixed income, with less financial support than a licensed foster family receives and little of the training. A Cochrane review led by Marc Winokur (2014) found that children placed with relatives generally did better on placement stability and several measures of well-being than children placed with strangers, while noting the limits of the studies. Churches are full of grandparents raising grandchildren. They are a foster care ministry already.

Then there is the child who arrives. Karyn Purvis, a developmental psychologist at Texas Christian University, spent much of her later career teaching parents to care for what she called children from hard places. In *The Connected Child* (2007), written with David Cross and Wendy Lyons Sunshine, she argued that early harm, whether neglect, abuse, prenatal exposure or too many caregivers too soon, shapes how a child's body and brain respond to stress. Behavior that looks like defiance is often fear. Her approach, later developed as Trust-Based Relational Intervention, starts with what she called felt safety, a child who experiences the house as safe and not merely one who lives in a safe house, and it puts connection before correction. The wider research on early adversity, including the Adverse Childhood Experiences study Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda published in 1998, points the same way.

That is information, not treatment, and this is not medical or psychological advice. Children who have been through trauma deserve professionals trained for it, and foster parents and adoptive parents should expect to need a therapist who understands trauma and attachment and a pediatrician who knows the child's history. If you suspect a child is being abused, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline is 1-800-422-4453. If a child or anyone in your home speaks of wanting to die, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

What Purvis described clinically, Romans 8 had already described theologically. The adoption is legal before it is felt, and a child can have a new name on a court order and still sleep with his shoes on. God does not require his adopted children to feel at home before he keeps them; he gives them the Spirit, who teaches them over a lifetime to say "Father" without flinching. Parents of children from hard places are asked for that same patience, and their churches are asked for it toward them.

## What a church can do when not everyone takes a child in

Two cautions deserve a full hearing. The first belongs to experienced foster parents. This is harder than the recruitment video suggests, and children who have been through trauma bring needs that exceed what most households can meet alone. A family that says yes on a wave of feeling, alone and unprepared, can harm the child and itself, and a collapsed placement is one more abandonment. The second caution belongs to the system's critics: removal is itself a wound, and a church that imagines foster care as rescue will recruit rescuers. Both are right, and neither excuses a congregation that does nothing. The first argues for preparation and the second for humility. Nothing in either argues for a church that has never asked its county what it needs.

Not every family should become a foster family, and a church that shames people into it will produce the fragile placements the veterans warn about. But every church can make it possible for some families to say yes and keep saying yes. Foster parents who quit rarely quit because they stopped loving the children. They quit because they were alone.

The answer has come to be called wraparound support, and it is ordinary work. A few people from the congregation attach themselves to one foster family. Someone brings dinner the night a new placement arrives. Someone drives to therapy, someone is approved for respite care so the parents can rest without the child going to strangers (rules differ by state, so ask the agency). Someone learns the birth mother's name and prays for her by it. Someone stays in touch with the children after the placement ends, by reunification or adoption, because children notice who stayed. Some churches work with Safe Families for Children, whose volunteer families host children while parents in crisis recover, so that an emergency need not become a state case.

Young people aging out of care need a different kind of household, not always a bedroom but always a table: a seat at Thanksgiving and Easter, an adult who answers the phone when the car breaks down or the landlord calls, a family that helps with the security deposit, comes to the graduation, and is still there at twenty-five. Most of us had these without asking. Young adults leaving care so often don't.

A church that wants to begin should begin by learning. Ask the local placing agency how many children are in care, how many licensed homes there are, and what foster families say they need. Then preach it as part of what the gospel produces, not as an annual emphasis, and budget for it. Jesus set a child in the middle of his disciples and said, "Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me" (Matthew 18:5, ESV). A congregation that takes that sentence seriously has a place in it for the family who takes the child in, the neighbor who brings the meal, and the man who drives a mother to her visit and waits in the car.

*Heaven's answer to the fatherless was never a fundraiser. It was a home, and the church is meant to be one: for the child, for the parent fighting to get the child back, and for the child who has no one left to go back to.*

## Settled in a home

The psalmist who called God the Father of the fatherless also knew the fear of being left. "For my father and my mother have forsaken me, but the LORD will take me in." (Psalm 27:10, ESV). The verse gives words to children who have lived it, and tells them where the last word lies.

He took us in at a price the psalmist could not have seen. On the cross the eternal Son, never for a moment without his Father, cried out in the words of another psalm, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, ESV). The only Son entered the abandonment so that the abandoned could be made sons and daughters. That is the adoption Paul describes. We were that child, taken in, given a name, seated at a table we did not set, and told the inheritance is ours. And the forsaken Son did not stay in the grave. His resurrection is the promise that the waiting of Romans 8 will end, and that the groaning creation, the groaning church and the child who still sleeps with his shoes on will one day be fully at home.

Until then the church lives between the adoption it has received and the homecoming it awaits, which is exactly where children in care are living too. They have been placed but not settled. A church that knows its own adoption is still being completed ought to understand them better than anyone.

Tonight, in whatever county you are reading this, there is a child asleep in a stranger's house, a mother counting the days to her next supervised visit, a grandmother who took in three children last spring and has told no one at church how tired she is, and a nineteen-year-old who aged out and has no one to call. The God who settles the solitary in a home knows every one of them, and he has placed a household of his own within a short drive of each. Whether that household opens its door, or brings the meal, or sits with the mother in the courthouse hallway, this essay cannot answer. The fatherless have been putting that question to the people of God since the first sheaf was left in the field, and it is still waiting on us.
