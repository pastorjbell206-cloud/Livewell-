Hasn't the church done terrible things? It has, and any answer that starts by softening that sentence has already lost the argument. Christians took the cross as the badge of armed pilgrimage and killed under it. Church tribunals handed condemned converts and dissenters to the state for execution. Catholic and Protestant rulers fought across Europe for more than a century under confessional banners. Preachers defended slavery from the pulpit. And within living memory, bishops and pastors shielded men who abused children and treated the victims as a threat to be managed. None of this was invented by the faith's enemies; most of it sits in the church's own archives. If the faith can't survive the truth about its own record, it doesn't deserve anyone's trust.

I came to faith from atheism, and when I was on the other side of this conversation the Crusades were part of my case. The answers Christians usually give come in two kinds: the embarrassed change of subject, or a scrubbed history in which the crusaders become misunderstood defenders. Both teach the skeptic that believers can't be trusted with their own past. What follows tells the history as the best historians now tell it, worse than the popular defense admits and more tangled than the popular charge allows, and it rests on one distinction: there is a difference between Christianity being refuted by the sins of Christians and the sins of Christians being judged by Christ.

## The Charge at Full Strength

In its crude form the objection is a list. In its serious form it is a mechanism. Christopher Hitchens argued in *God Is Not Great* (2007), surveying conflicts from Belfast to Beirut to Belgrade, that religion does not merely accompany tribal hatred but sanctifies it, handing its adherents a divine warrant no earthly authority can check. Sam Harris sharpened the point in *The End of Faith* (2004): beliefs govern action, beliefs held without evidence are dangerous in proportion to the stakes they claim, and no stakes are higher than eternal ones. Worse, he argued, religious moderates shelter the extremists by treating faith itself as beyond criticism. The biblical scholar Hector Avalos gave the case an academic frame in *Fighting Words* (2005). Religion, he argued, creates scarce resources where none need exist: sacred space, the authority of a scripture, membership in a favored group, salvation. Because none of them can be verified, no evidence can settle a dispute over them, and so religious violence is not only frequent but uniquely unnecessary. Jerusalem, a city three faiths call holy, is the obvious illustration, and the crusade indulgence, which promised remission of penance to those who took the cross, looks like the mechanism written down as policy.

A Christian should give all of that its full weight, and then notice something uncomfortable. The premise of the argument comes from Jesus. Near the end of the Sermon on the Mount he gives his hearers a test for telling true teachers from false ones:

> "You will recognize them by their fruits. Are grapes gathered from thornbushes, or figs from thistles?" (Matthew 7:16, ESV)

He is warning about wolves in sheep's clothing, and a few verses later he describes people who call him Lord, even do mighty works in his name, and are disowned on the last day. The fruit test is not a skeptic's weapon smuggled into the argument. It is Christ's own standard, and he aims it first at people who use his name.

So the church's crimes count. The real question is what they count against. If Christianity claimed that its followers would be conspicuously better people than everyone else, the record would refute it. If Christianity claims instead that its followers are sinners under the judgment of a holy Lord, the record indicts the followers on the faith's own terms.

The skeptic will rightly answer that this sounds too convenient. If good fruit confirms the faith, and bad fruit also confirms it because the faith predicted sin, then nothing could count against it. That is fair. Christianity would be refuted by its history if its teaching, followed faithfully and read in its plain sense, made people crueler; if it produced no one who loved enemies at cost; if the violence flowed from the center of the text rather than from its suppression. The faith could fail that test in principle. Running it requires telling the history honestly, and then reading the teaching.

## Clermont, Jerusalem and Constantinople

On 27 November 1095, at the Council of Clermont in central France, Pope Urban II called the knights of Latin Christendom to an armed expedition east. The background was real. Envoys of the Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos had asked Urban that March for soldiers against the Seljuk Turks, who had overrun most of Asia Minor after the Byzantine defeat at Manzikert in 1071. Urban answered with something far larger: an armed pilgrimage to relieve the Christians of the East and recover Jerusalem, with the promise that those who went out of devotion, rather than for honor or money, would have the expedition counted in place of all their penance. The speech survives only in accounts written after Jerusalem had fallen, each shaped by the victory. The response is not in doubt. Tens of thousands took the cross, the poor and whole families among them.

The first victims were not in the East. In the spring and early summer of 1096, bands moving through the Rhineland, the most notorious under Count Emicho, turned on the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms and Mainz, and at Worms and Mainz destroyed them. These were among the first great pogroms of European history, carried out by men wearing the cross. The bishop of Speyer protected most of his city's Jews; at Worms and Mainz the bishops who tried to shelter them failed. Three years later, on 15 July 1099, the main army took Jerusalem, and the crusaders massacred much of the population, Muslims and Jews alike. It happened, and it should be said as plainly as that.

The Fourth Crusade never fought a Muslim army at all. Bound for Egypt in 1202 and hopelessly in debt to Venice for its fleet, it was diverted against the Christian city of Zara and then against Constantinople, which the crusaders stormed and sacked for three days in April 1204, looting the churches of fellow Christians. Pope Innocent III had forbidden attacks on Christians and wrote in anguish afterward that the Greeks now had every reason to despise the Latins. Steven Runciman, in the last volume of his three-volume *History of the Crusades* (1951 to 1954), judged that no crime against humanity had ever been greater. The old breach between the Greek and Latin churches became a wound that has not healed.

The popular picture needs correcting in the other direction too. The image of the Crusades as an unprovoked colonial assault on a peaceful East is poor history. Thomas Madden argues in *The New Concise History of the Crusades* (2005) that they came after four centuries in which Muslim conquest had taken much of the formerly Christian Mediterranean world, from Syria and Egypt to North Africa and Spain, and that the First Crusade answered an appeal from Christians losing ground. Jonathan Riley-Smith, working through the property charters in *The First Crusaders, 1095-1131* (1997), dismantled the old story of landless younger sons chasing loot. Many crusaders sold or mortgaged their lands at ruinous rates to go, and many never came home. The dominant motive in the sources is penitential, and in an earlier essay, "Crusading as an Act of Love" (1980), Riley-Smith showed that contemporaries could describe crusading as charity toward eastern brothers.

Notice where that correction leaves a Christian. If the crusaders had been cynical adventurers, the church could disown them with a clear conscience. Riley-Smith's evidence closes that door. They confessed, fasted and prayed; before the final assault on Jerusalem they walked barefoot in procession around its walls. Christopher Tyerman, whose *God's War* (2006) is no apology for crusading, treats its religious ideology of holy war as real conviction that drove events, not a veneer over plunder. The Crusades began as a response to a real crisis and became, in the hands of sincerely pious people, a machinery of holy violence. That is worse for the church, not better. Sincerity was no safeguard.

## The Inquisition, the Wars of Religion and the Myth

The Spanish Inquisition was established in 1478, when Pope Sixtus IV granted Ferdinand and Isabella the authority to appoint inquisitors in Castile. It was not finally abolished until 1834. Its chief early targets were the *conversos*, Jews who had been baptized, many under heavy pressure, and their descendants, suspected of keeping their old faith in secret; in 1492 the Jews who would not convert were expelled from Spain. Henry Kamen, in *The Spanish Inquisition: A Historical Revision* (1997), worked from the tribunals' own records and showed that much of the popular picture came from Protestant and later Enlightenment polemic. The executions numbered in the thousands rather than the vast figures of legend, and most came in the institution's first decades.

Revision must not become absolution. Kamen stresses that the Inquisition answered to the Spanish crown and served the aim of a religiously uniform nation, a political diagnosis as much as a religious one. But every person it condemned was a person, and the apparatus rested on a conviction the church had adopted long before, that faith could rightly be compelled. Augustine, early in the fifth century, came to defend the use of imperial force against the Donatists, and he found his warrant in a line from one of Jesus' parables:

> "And the master said to the servant, 'Go out to the highways and hedges and compel people to come in, that my house may be filled.'" (Luke 14:23, ESV)

The parable is about a banquet. The master's urgency is generosity: the invited guests have refused, and he wants the poor and the lame at his table. To make it a charter for coercing conscience, the church had to read a story about hospitality as a story about police.

Then came the wars. France tore itself apart from 1562 to 1598. The Thirty Years' War began in Bohemia in 1618 and ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, leaving the German lands devastated by war, famine and plague. From these conflicts the modern West drew a story most of us absorbed without being taught it: religion made Europeans kill each other, the secular state arose to keep the peace, and faith was therefore confined, for everyone's safety, to private life.

William Cavanaugh's *The Myth of Religious Violence* (2009) is the most serious challenge to that story. Cavanaugh argues that "religion," understood as a universal category separable from politics and uniquely prone to irrational violence, is a modern invention, and that the so-called wars of religion don't fit it. Catholics fought Catholics; in the 1630s Catholic France under Cardinal Richelieu subsidized Lutheran Sweden against the Catholic Habsburgs. The rising centralized state was less the cure for the violence than one of its causes. And the myth does ideological work, teaching citizens to see other people's devotion as fanaticism while treating the nation's demand that they kill and die for it as simple reason. Peter Wilson's history of the war, *Europe's Tragedy* (2009), reaches a related conclusion: religion mattered deeply to the participants but was not the chief engine of the conflict.

A Christian should not reach for Cavanaugh too quickly. His critics answer that taking apart the modern word "religion" does not take apart what happened. The men at Jerusalem told us why they came. The Inquisition's charges were theological. Belief about God was something Christians killed over, because the church had taught them it could be defended by force. Cavanaugh's target is a double standard, and his book is no alibi for the church.

Held together, the argument and its critics leave a harder verdict. The twentieth-century regimes that set out to abolish faith, in Russia, China and Cambodia, killed on a scale no crusade approached. Those regimes did not kill in the name of atheism the way crusaders killed in the name of Christ, so the lesson is narrower than a counterpunch. Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, who survived the Soviet camps, came to see, as he writes in *The Gulag Archipelago* (published from 1973), that the line between good and evil runs not between states, classes or parties but through every human heart. The capacity for atrocity is not located in religion. It is located in us, and it will march under whatever banner is nearest, a cross, a flag, a class or a race. That does not acquit the church. It removes the church's claim to be different, which is exactly the claim the church should never have made.

## The Other Side of the Ledger

Fairness requires the rest of the record, told without triumphalism. In 362 the emperor Julian, who had abandoned Christianity for the old gods, complained in a letter to a pagan priest that the Galileans, as he called the Christians, fed the pagan poor as well as their own. Around 370 Basil of Caesarea built a complex in Cappadocia to house and treat the sick, the poor and travelers, often counted among the first hospitals. The church's care for strangers was conspicuous enough that its most determined enemy tried to copy it.

In the conquest of the Americas, the church's crimes and its protests happened in the same rooms. Just before Christmas in 1511, the Dominican friar Antonio de Montesinos told the Spanish colonists of Hispaniola that they were in mortal sin for their treatment of the native people, whom he insisted were human beings with rational souls. Among those colonists was a priest, Bartolome de las Casas, who held native laborers of his own and gave them up in 1514 after reading a passage in Ecclesiasticus about offerings taken from the poor. He spent five decades as their advocate, wrote his *Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (printed 1552), and argued at Valladolid in 1550 against Juan Gines de Sepulveda, who had used Aristotle to claim that some peoples are slaves by nature. Honesty requires one more detail. Las Casas at one stage proposed bringing enslaved Africans to spare the native peoples, and later regretted it, recognizing their enslavement as just as unjust. Both sides of the ledger ran through one man's life.

Slavery shows the same division. The Anglican Society for the Propagation of the Gospel owned plantations in Barbados worked by enslaved people for more than a century. American pastors built a biblical defense of slavery, and in 1845 the Southern Baptist Convention was formed after Baptists divided over whether slaveholders could serve as missionaries. Against that stands William Wilberforce, whose evangelical conversion in 1785 led to the long campaign that ended the British slave trade in 1807 and slavery across most of the empire in 1833, the summer he died. Frederick Douglass, in the appendix to his *Narrative* (1845), made sure no reader mistook his target: he loved the Christianity of Christ, he wrote, and for that very reason hated the slaveholding religion of the land.

The two sides read the same Bible, and a skeptic will say that proves the book settles nothing. But the readings were not symmetrical. Slavery's defenders had to lean on a handful of verses lifted from their setting and suppress a story that begins with a God who hears the cry of slaves in Egypt and leads them out. The abolitionists had the grain of the text with them, the image of God in every person and Paul's leveling sentence:

> "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28, ESV)

When one reader has to silence the center of a book to make his case and another only has to read it, that is not a tie.

A larger point came most forcefully from outside the faith. Friedrich Nietzsche, in *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887), despised Christianity for its revolt against the ancient world's values, making the weak and the suffering the objects of moral concern and teaching the strong to feel guilty. He was right about the revolt, even as he hated it. Tom Holland, writing *Dominion* (2019) as a historian rather than an apologist, argued that the West's deepest moral instincts, that victims have a claim on us and the powerful owe something to the powerless, are Christian inheritances, and that even the modern condemnation of the church draws on them. The Roman world did not generally think it wrong to expose an unwanted infant. When we call the Crusades a crime, we stand on ground the church taught the world to stand on.

None of this balances the books. A hospital in Cappadocia does not pay for a massacre in Mainz, and the victims of one Christian are not compensated by the charity of another. The point is narrower. The measuring stick by which the church stands condemned was cut largely from the story of a crucified man, which means that when the church's crimes are judged, they are judged by the church's own Lord.

## Judged by Their Own Lord

Luke marks the turn in Jesus' ministry with a single sentence:

> "When the days drew near for him to be taken up, he set his face to go to Jerusalem." (Luke 9:51, ESV)

A Samaritan village refuses to receive him because he is headed for Jerusalem. The old hostility between Jews and Samaritans turned on rival claims about where God should be worshiped, a dispute over sacred space in Avalos's terms. Two disciples respond the way religious people so often have:

> "And when his disciples James and John saw it, they said, 'Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?'" (Luke 9:54, ESV)

They had a precedent. Earlier in the same chapter they had seen Elijah on the mountain with Jesus, and Elijah had once called fire down on an idolatrous king's soldiers; some ancient copies of Luke add "as Elijah did" to their question. They had Scripture, zeal and a holy cause. Luke's answer is brief:

> "But he turned and rebuked them." (Luke 9:55, ESV)

The same thing happens at the end of the road. In Gethsemane one of his followers draws a sword and strikes the high priest's servant, the one moment in the Gospels when a disciple takes up arms for Jesus, and Jesus stops it:

> "Then Jesus said to him, 'Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword.'" (Matthew 26:52, ESV)

He adds that he could appeal to his Father for more than twelve legions of angels, and Luke records that he healed the servant's ear. Before Pilate he explains why no one fights to rescue him: "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36, ESV). The logic of the cross is a king who conquers by dying rather than by killing, and who asks his followers to carry a cross rather than a sword.

Set the First Crusade beside that road and the contrast is almost unbearable. Jesus set his face toward Jerusalem to be killed there and rebuked the disciples who wanted to burn a village on the way. More than a thousand years later, men wearing his cross attacked the Jewish communities of the Rhine and took the same city by the sword. The Crusades are not the faith taken too seriously. They are its inversion, the photographic negative of the Lord in whose name they were fought.

So the most devastating critique of the Crusades comes from Christ rather than from the atheist. The atheist says religion made them violent. The Gospels say they abandoned his way the moment they took up a sword for him. The New Testament even expected this betrayal and took the outsider's side against it. Paul, addressing a fellow Jew who boasts in God's law, turns Isaiah against him:

> "For, as it is written, 'The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you.'" (Romans 2:24, ESV)

The skeptic who condemns the church for its crimes is, on that point, standing where the church's Scriptures stand.

Return to the test the skeptic rightly demanded. Did the violence flow from the center of the teaching or from its suppression? Augustine had to turn a parable about a banquet into a warrant for force. The slaveholder had to silence the Exodus. The crusader had to set aside the plainest command in the Sermon on the Mount: "But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, ESV). Montesinos, Las Casas, Wilberforce and Douglass only had to read what was there. The history shows a text that had to be resisted before it could be used for cruelty, and that kept rising up against those who used it.

Here the Christian has to refuse the easiest escape, which is to say that the crusaders and inquisitors and slaveholding preachers were never real Christians. That won't do. They were baptized, they confessed the creeds we still confess, and they are our fathers and mothers in the faith whether we like it or not. The distinction this essay draws runs between the church and its Lord, and never between true Christians and false ones, as if the church could keep its saints and disown its sinners. The church can be terribly wrong. Christ is the one who judges it. Daniel, whom Scripture presents as faithful through the whole exile, prayed over the sins of his people in exile without once putting himself outside them:

> "we have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled, turning aside from your commandments and rules." (Daniel 9:5, ESV)

He had not committed the sins that sent Israel into exile. He said "we" anyway, because he understood that belonging to a people means inheriting its debts as well as its promises.

## Judgment Begins at the Household of God

It is easy to repent of the Crusades; condemning men dead eight hundred years costs nothing. The wound that should keep Christians awake is recent. From Boston in 2002 to the Southern Baptist Convention's own investigation in 2022, Catholic and Protestant institutions that should have protected children protected themselves, in Christ's name. That history has its own essay, [Sexual Abuse in the Church and How Churches Should Respond](/writing/sexual-abuse-crisis-in-the-church), and it deserves more than a paragraph here. No argument heals it, and the damage to faith those crimes caused is largely deserved. If you were harmed, it was a crime and not your fault: RAINN answers at 1-800-656-4673, and if a child is at risk, Childhelp at 1-800-422-4453. For the harder question of whether to go back at all, see [Church Hurt: How to Heal and Whether to Go Back](/writing/when-the-church-is-what-hurt-you).

Nowhere does the New Testament tell the church to defend its reputation; it tells the church to expect judgment and to meet it first. Peter wrote to slandered, suffering congregations in Asia Minor, and instead of assuring them of their innocence he said this:

> "For it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God; and if it begins with us, what will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel of God?" (1 Peter 4:17, ESV)

There have been moments when the church took that sentence seriously. On 12 March 2000, the first Sunday of Lent, John Paul II led a liturgy in St. Peter's Basilica that came to be called the Day of Pardon, asking God's forgiveness for sins committed by the church's members in the service of truth, against the unity of Christians, against the Jewish people, and against the rights of peoples and the dignity of women. The International Theological Commission had prepared the ground in *Memory and Reconciliation*, completed in December 1999 and published just before the liturgy. Critics said the prayers named categories rather than events and victims, and that criticism is fair. Five years earlier, in June 1995, the Southern Baptist Convention adopted a resolution repenting of the role slavery had played in its origins and of its long record of racism. Neither act undid anything. Both show the grammar of repentance, which is Daniel's grammar: we have sinned.

That grammar has to reach the present tense, or it becomes one more way of feeling superior to the dead. The crusading impulse did not die with the crusaders. Its sequence is still recognizable: the cause is baptized, the opponent is recast as God's enemy, and whatever is done to him is done for God. It shows up wherever a culture war is fought as a holy war, on the right when the nation is confused with the kingdom and on the left when a political program becomes the only acceptable righteousness. I'm not outside this. I know the pull of the sermon that picks a fight because a fight draws a crowd, and the pleasant certainty that the people I oppose are opposed to God, which conveniently makes my anger his. The Rhineland mobs thought they were the faithful. Anyone who reads their story and feels only superiority has missed its one lesson.

So the skeptic's charge stands. The church has done terrible things, some of them by devout people with Scripture in their mouths. What the charge cannot do is refute the one in whose name those things were done, because he had already condemned them, on the road through Samaria and in the garden at night. The crimes can't disprove Christ; they are among the things he will judge, and among the things he died for.

Two roads have led to Jerusalem in this history. The crusaders took the city by the sword in 1099 and lost it to Saladin in 1187. Jesus went up to the same city to be killed, refused the sword on the night he was betrayed, and was raised on the third day, and the kingdom he announced has outlived every army that marched under his name. The church has never advanced his cause by killing for him, only by people willing to suffer for him, to confess their own sins before anyone else's, and to love enemies who gave them every reason not to. Every Christian and every congregation stands at that fork again, usually in far smaller matters than a crusade, and the crusaders are a warning about how sincerely a person can take the wrong road. The question the history leaves is not whether they chose badly. It is which road we are on now.
