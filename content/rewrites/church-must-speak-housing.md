Most people who search this question aren't looking for a theology of land. They're looking at a lease renewal with a number they can't pay, or at the house their parents bought on one income that they can't buy on two, or at the block they grew up on that now has a wine bar and fewer of the families they knew. Some are on the other side of the same street: a young couple who bought the only house they could afford, in a neighborhood whose older residents say it's being taken from them, and who can't see what they did wrong.

The usual answers split along a familiar line. One side says the market prices a house like anything else, and the Christian task is personal: work, save, give, and don't covet what someone else earned. The other says housing is a right, landlords and developers are the villains, and the Christian task is to join the side that says so. Both hold something true. Neither has much to do with the way Scripture actually talks about houses, which is more specific, more legal and more demanding than either. The site treats the [Year of Jubilee](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded), [poverty and politics](/writing/is-poverty-political-the-bibles-answer) and [the widow, the orphan and the stranger](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger) at length elsewhere; this essay stays with the house and the neighborhood.

## A house in Israel's law

One sentence governs the whole of Leviticus 25:

> "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me." (Leviticus 25:23, ESV)

Israelites bought and sold land, farmed it and passed it on, but no one owned it absolutely. God was the owner and the families held a grant. Christopher Wright shows in *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* (2004) how that grant was distributed, by tribe, clan and household, so that the basic unit of the economy was a family on its own portion of ground, economically viable and socially rooted. The land laws exist to keep that arrangement from collapsing. When debt forced a sale, the law provided a way back:

> "If your brother becomes poor and sells part of his property, then his nearest redeemer shall come and redeem what his brother has sold." (Leviticus 25:25, ESV)

If no kinsman-redeemer came, the family could buy the land back when it recovered, and if neither happened the fiftieth year returned it anyway. What was really sold was the use of the land for the harvests remaining until the Jubilee, which is why the price was set by counting them. A family could lose its crops, its savings, even its freedom for a season. The law would not let it lose its place for good.

Then comes a detail most readers pass over. The law distinguishes two kinds of dwelling:

> "If a man sells a dwelling house in a walled city, he may redeem it within a year of its sale. For a full year he shall have the right of redemption." (Leviticus 25:29, ESV)

After that year the city house belonged to the buyer for good and did not return in the Jubilee. Village houses were treated differently:

> "But the houses of the villages that have no wall around them shall be classified with the fields of the land. They may be redeemed, and they shall be released in the jubilee." (Leviticus 25:31, ESV)

The Levites, who had no tribal territory, could redeem houses in their own cities at any time, because those houses were all the possession they had (Leviticus 25:32–33).

The usual reading among commentators is that the village house stood on the family's farmland and belonged to the same inheritance, so it received the same protection. A house inside a walled city was closer to what we'd call real estate, the dwelling of craftsmen and traders in a town economy not tied to the family allotment. The law allowed a real market there, with a year's grace, and refused that market in the village. The line was drawn between a house that functioned as an inheritance, the place a family belonged and made its living across generations, and a house that functioned as a commodity.

That distinction carries the rest of this essay. Israel's law wasn't against markets, or selling a house, or a buyer profiting from a good purchase. It was against the market reaching the thing that rooted a family and turning it into one more asset that could be lost permanently in a bad year. Walter Brueggemann argued in *The Land* (1977) that the biblical story moves between landlessness and landedness, and that land received as gift turns, in the hands of the settled, into land held as possession, at which point the landed forget both the giver and the landless. The Jubilee was the law's refusal to let Israel forget.

Israel's civil law is no blueprint for a modern housing code, but its moral grammar carries over. A home and an asset can be the same building and still be different things, and a society that lets the second swallow the first has lost something Scripture refused to let Israel lose.

## Joining house to house

By the eighth century BC the kinship economy the law protected was being hollowed out in both Israel and Judah. Debt, drought, royal taxation and a landed class growing around the courts were turning small holders into tenants. The prophets didn't answer with a general call to kindness. They named the mechanism.

> "Woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room, and you are made to dwell alone in the midst of the land." (Isaiah 5:8, ESV)

Isaiah's charge is consolidation. Nothing in the verse says the buyers broke a contract. They bought and bought again until villages that had held many families held one estate, and the sting is that the ones who gathered everything are "made to dwell alone." Micah, a villager from Moresheth, saw it from the side of the people losing their ground:

> "They covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away; they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance." (Micah 2:2, ESV)

Two words carry the verse. "Covet" is the verb of the tenth commandment, which in Exodus begins with the neighbor's house. "Inheritance" is the Hebrew *nahalah*, the family portion Leviticus protected.

Naboth's story in 1 Kings 21 is the Bible's fullest account of how that happens. King Ahab wants the vineyard beside his palace in Jezreel for a vegetable garden, and his first offer is reasonable by any market standard: a better vineyard in exchange, or its value in money. Naboth refuses:

> "But Naboth said to Ahab, 'The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers.'" (1 Kings 21:3, ESV)

He isn't holding out for a higher price. He's saying the vineyard isn't the kind of thing that has one. Jezebel's answer to her sulking husband, "Do you now govern Israel?" (1 Kings 21:7, ESV), is followed by the part of the story that should unsettle us most. She sends no soldiers. She writes letters under the king's seal to the elders of Naboth's town, arranges a public fast, seats two false witnesses, and has Naboth convicted of cursing God and the king and stoned, all through the forms of law. The land passes to the crown because proper procedure was followed. Elijah meets Ahab in the vineyard:

> "And you shall say to him, 'Thus says the LORD, "Have you killed and also taken possession?"'" (1 Kings 21:19, ESV)

Isaiah named consolidation, Micah named the scheme, and Elijah named a court procedure that produced a clean title to stolen ground. Each transaction looked legitimate, and God called each by its name.

We tend to read these texts as if they were about someone else: the rich, the developers, Ahab. But Micah's first charge is coveting, the sin that needs no power to begin. It can be satisfied lawfully, through a zoning vote, a tax rule or a bidding war, and never feel like taking anything at all. The most thorough dispossessions of the prophets' day were carried out by people who could point to the paperwork. We should expect ours to look the same.

## How the American map was drawn

American housing is usually described as the sum of private choices, but across most of the twentieth century law and public money drew its shape, and the shape still holds.

In 1917 the Supreme Court struck down Louisville's racial zoning ordinance in *Buchanan v. Warley*, and cities learned to draw similar lines by other means. In 1926 the Court upheld comprehensive zoning in *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty*, in an opinion that described the apartment house in a district of single-family homes as "a mere parasite." The Home Owners' Loan Corporation, created in 1933, drew maps grading neighborhoods by lending risk, with many Black and immigrant districts shaded red. The Federal Housing Administration, created in 1934, insured the mortgages that built the postwar suburbs, and its underwriting standards treated Black residents as a threat to property values. Richard Rothstein's *The Color of Law* (2017) argues from that record that American residential segregation was not the accident of private preference but the product of explicit public policy, and Kenneth Jackson's *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985) had already shown how thoroughly federal mortgage insurance, highways and the tax code paid for the move to the suburbs. The Court made racial covenants unenforceable in *Shelley v. Kraemer* in 1948, and Congress passed the Fair Housing Act in April 1968, days after Martin Luther King Jr. was killed. By then most of the suburbs had been built. The site traces that history, and the church's part in it, in the essay on [reparations](/writing/theology-of-reparations).

Two things follow for gentrification. First, the neighborhoods cheap enough to gentrify were very often made cheap. Credit was withheld from them for decades, and urban renewal under the Housing Act of 1949, followed by the interstate highways, cleared many of them outright. The people priced out today are frequently the grandchildren of people who were first redlined in.

Second, the house changed what it was. By deliberate policy the owner-occupied house became the ordinary American family's chief investment, subsidized through mortgage insurance and the tax code, its value protected by zoning, its appreciation the retirement plan. Matthew Desmond argues in *Poverty, by America* (2023) that federal housing support flows far more generously to homeowners, most of them comfortable, through tax breaks than to poor renters through direct aid.

Leviticus would recognize the problem. We made the American house a house in a walled city, a tradable asset, while asking it to carry all the weight of the village house, the place a family belongs. A homeowner fighting the apartment building down the street is often defending both his home and his retirement, and may honestly be unable to tell which. Most settled Americans, and most settled churches, have benefited from that confusion without asking about it. I've preached hospitality to rooms full of homeowners and never once asked what the rules we quietly rely on do to the people we prayed for that morning.

## Rent, eviction and the thirty percent line

Housing researchers use a simple measure. A household that spends more than 30 percent of its income on rent and utilities is cost-burdened; one that spends more than half is severely cost-burdened. The line is a rule of thumb, but past it the rent starts eating the grocery money and the savings that would absorb a bad month. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies tracks the measure in its annual *State of the Nation's Housing* reports, and in recent years those reports have counted cost-burdened renters at record levels, around half of all renter households.

Matthew Desmond's *Evicted* (2016), which won the Pulitzer Prize for general nonfiction, made numbers like that hard to read past. Desmond lived among tenants in Milwaukee in 2008 and 2009 and followed eight families through the cycle of rent, arrears and removal. His central argument is that eviction is not only a symptom of poverty but a cause of it. An evicted family often loses its belongings, its children lose their school, the adults lose work in the chaos, and the next landlord sees the record and offers only the worst units. Desmond reports that most poor renting families in America spend more than half their income on housing, and he documented how often removal happened outside court, through threats or a cash payment to leave. The book refuses the cartoon. Its landlords are people working within incentives they didn't invent, and his indictment falls on an arrangement in which housing the poor can be very profitable and being poor is very expensive. That verdict is harder than blaming a villain, because it implicates everyone who benefits without seeing it.

The law of Moses addressed this threshold directly:

> "When you make your neighbor a loan of any sort, you shall not go into his house to collect his pledge." (Deuteronomy 24:10, ESV)

The creditor had to wait outside for the pledge to be brought out, and a poor man's cloak had to be returned by sundown so he could sleep in it. The debt was real and so was the duty to repay. But the law drew a boundary around the house, the one place where even a debtor kept his dignity. A society can collect what it's owed and still refuse to let the collecting destroy the household.

If you're behind on rent or holding a notice, get real help quickly. Tenant protections vary widely by state and city, and timelines are often shorter than people expect. Local legal aid organizations, many of them free, help tenants facing eviction, and dialing 211 in most of the United States reaches a service that can point toward emergency rental assistance and legal help nearby. This isn't legal advice. But shame is one of the ways a bad month becomes a lost home. If you belong to a church, tell someone there. A congregation that doesn't know its own people are behind can't help, and many would.

## Gentrification, argued both ways

Gentrification is where the housing question turns local and bitter, and both cases deserve the form their defenders would sign.

The case against starts with the obvious: rents and taxes rise, buildings are sold and renovated, and long-term residents leave. But the planner Peter Marcuse argued in 1985 that direct displacement is only the most visible kind. There's also exclusionary displacement, the families who would have moved in the way their parents did and now can't, and there's the pressure on those who stay, as the grocery, the barber, the church and the neighbors that made the place livable give way to businesses and residents that aren't there for them. A household can keep its address and lose its neighborhood. What goes is what no appraisal records, the web of neighbors, shops and congregations that took decades to build and scatters in one lease cycle. And because the neighborhoods changing now are often the ones earlier policy filled and then starved, gentrification can complete a displacement the old maps began.

The case on the other side has better evidence than most churchgoers know. Lance Freeman and Frank Braconi studied New York in the 1990s and reported in 2004 that poor residents of gentrifying neighborhoods were, if anything, less likely to move than similar residents elsewhere. The study measured who left; it could not count the families Marcuse had in mind, who never arrived. In *There Goes the 'Hood* (2006), drawn from interviews in Harlem and Brooklyn's Clinton Hill, Freeman found long-term residents who welcomed the new grocery store and the safer streets even as they resented the sense that the improvements were meant for someone else. Edward Glaeser goes further in *Triumph of the City* (2011): the deepest cause of unaffordable housing is scarcity made by rules, zoning that permits only detached houses, height limits, historic districts, and review processes that let existing residents block new building. Where cities allow building, prices stay within reach; where they don't, every newcomer bids against the family already there. On this view the enemy is less the gentrifier than the rule that stops the next building. The movement that grew from this argument calls itself YIMBY, "yes in my backyard." On rent control, a 2019 study of San Francisco by Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade and Franklin Qian gave both sides something: covered tenants really were more likely to stay, and landlords responded by pulling units off the rental market, which, the authors estimate, pushed rents up across the city.

Grant what's true in both. Disinvestment kills neighborhoods too, and the researchers' complication of the displacement story is real. So is the loss borne by those who stay. A church that simply chants against newcomers hasn't done justice. It has joined a different tribe.

So order the question. The first-order convictions bind every Christian: "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, ESV); a household's place is under God's protection, and taking it by power is sin; and the church can't be indifferent to whether its neighbors have somewhere to live. The second-order questions divide faithful people: how much of the harm is speculation and how much scarcity, and whether the remedy is more building, stronger tenant protection, nonprofit housing, or a mix. That difference is prudence, not unfaithfulness. The third-order questions are the particular rezoning and the particular project. What none of us may do is use the difficulty of the second and third as permission to say nothing about the first.

And the first-order word judges right and left with one instrument. Scripture protects property. "Do not move an ancient landmark or enter the fields of the fatherless" (Proverbs 23:10, ESV) is a property-rights text, and the owner it guards is the weakest one. Naboth's refusal was a small owner's right to say no to the most powerful man in the country. A left that treats every homeowner's claim as selfishness, or cheers when planners clear a neighborhood for a better use, has forgotten Naboth. In *Kelo v. City of New London* (2005) the Supreme Court upheld, five to four, a city's taking of Susette Kelo's house for a private development plan. But Isaiah's woe falls just as hard on the other side. "Until there is no more room" describes a zoning code written to keep the neighbor out. A right that reads the price of a house as a verdict on the character of whoever can't afford it, while defending the rules that make houses scarce, has forgotten that a neighborhood can be coveted through a planning commission as easily as through a bid. And some of the most expensive housing in America sits where the yard signs welcome everyone and the code doesn't. The question is the same for all of us: does this law, this vote, this purchase protect a household's place, take it, or bar the door to it?

## The church owns ground

The church isn't a spectator in any of this. In most American towns it's among the larger landowners, usually tax-exempt, often in the heart of a neighborhood, frequently bought decades ago for little, and very often underused. Many congregations own parking that fills two hours a week. In gentrifying neighborhoods a building bought cheaply in the years of disinvestment may now be worth more than the congregation has ever seen.

That fact has produced one of the more hopeful developments in American housing, a movement that turned the YIMBY slogan into "Yes in God's Backyard." Congregations have begun partnering with nonprofit developers to build affordable homes on church land, keeping ownership of the ground. The obstacles are mostly the ones Glaeser describes: zoning that doesn't allow housing on church land and reviews that let neighbors stall a project for years. California responded in 2023 with Senate Bill 4, which lets religious institutions and nonprofit colleges build qualifying affordable housing on their land with far fewer local approvals. Any given project is a third-order question. Whether churches should ask what their land is for isn't.

The harder honesty points the other way. A church's balance sheet can improve with every family priced out around it. Congregations have sold to the highest bidder and followed their members to the suburbs, and some have opposed affordable housing on the lot next door in the same language of traffic and character everyone else uses. Much church planting of the last generation went into gentrifying neighborhoods carrying Jeremiah's letter to the exiles:

> "But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare." (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV)

I've preached that text to church planters moving into city neighborhoods as a commission to arrive, without once asking who was leaving. But Jeremiah wrote to the displaced. They'd been carried off from the houses they built in Judah, and God told them to build again in a place that wasn't theirs. Reading the letter only as a warrant for newcomers to invest is reading it from the wrong side, and the selectivity runs, as it usually does, toward our own comfort.

Faithfulness depends on where you stand, and none of this is legal or financial advice. For renters it means knowing your rights before you need them and refusing the lie that a housing crisis is simply a verdict on your character. For homeowners it's more uncomfortable: learning to tell your home from your investment, asking which one you're defending at the zoning hearing, and remembering that your equity is partly a gift of rules you didn't write and others were shut out of. For neighbors on a changing block it means knowing the people who were there first by name, and the newcomers the same way, so the church is one of the few rooms where both sit together. And for congregations it means taking Isaiah at his word about the fast God chooses:

> "Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house; when you see the naked, to cover him, and not to hide yourself from your own flesh?" (Isaiah 58:7, ESV)

The house in that verse is a real house. The church has spoken fluently about homelessness as a spiritual condition and much less about housing as a built one. The prophets never separated them.

## Houses and fields shall again be bought

One more detail in Naboth's story should stay with us. When the false witnesses had finished, "they took him outside the city and stoned him to death with stones" (1 Kings 21:13, ESV). A man who wouldn't sell his family's portion was condemned by lying testimony in a proceeding that looked lawful and killed outside the walls, so that a king could take his ground. The Gospels tell a story with the same outline. Jesus, who said "the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Luke 9:58, ESV), was tried on false testimony and executed at the edge of the city.

> "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood." (Hebrews 13:12, ESV)

Hebrews is comparing Jesus to the sin offering burned outside the camp, and it draws its own conclusion: believers are to go out to him there and bear his reproach, since "here we have no lasting city" (Hebrews 13:13–14, ESV). The owner Leviticus meant when it said "the land is mine" entered his world without a house and was pushed out of the city to die. The cross isn't a housing policy. But it shows where God stands in every story of dispossession, outside the gate with the ones put there, and it tells us something harder about ourselves. When the council began plotting his death, it said plainly what it feared: "the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation" (John 11:48, ESV). The place they meant was most likely the temple and their own standing under Rome, not a house. But the instinct is the one this essay has been tracing. He was put out of the city by people protecting their place.

The resurrection answers that, and the Old Testament had already rehearsed the answer in the language of land. With Babylon's army around Jerusalem, Jeremiah was confined under guard when his cousin Hanamel came with an offer that only made sense under Leviticus 25: "Buy my field that is at Anathoth, for the right of redemption by purchase is yours" (Jeremiah 32:7, ESV). It was the worst real estate deal in the Bible. The field lay in the path of the invaders and the country was about to fall. Jeremiah did exactly what the kinsman-redeemer of Leviticus 25:25 was meant to do, paid the price and had the deed preserved, and then said why:

> "For thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: Houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land." (Jeremiah 32:15, ESV)

The redeemer bought back the family ground at the moment it looked most lost, as a pledge that God hadn't finished with the land or the people on it. That's the shape of Christian hope about the earth. It isn't escape from the world of houses and fields but the promise that it will be redeemed, and Isaiah saw the end in exactly those terms:

> "They shall not build and another inhabit; they shall not plant and another eat; for like the days of a tree shall the days of my people be, and my chosen shall long enjoy the work of their hands." (Isaiah 65:22, ESV)

Every eviction, every family priced off the block where their grandparents are buried, is a small reversal of that promise. The people who built don't inhabit. And the Bible's last pages give us a city where "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Revelation 21:3, ESV), a place where no one is told there's no more room.

We don't live there yet. We live in the city with the lease notices and the zoning hearings and the church parking lot, and the promise doesn't excuse us from it. It makes the present harder to shrug at, because the God who will make that city has already told us what kind of neighbor he is. Whose place did my place cost? Who is sleeping in a car within a mile of the building where we sing about the Father's house with many rooms? What are we holding, in land and money and votes, that was never ours in perpetuity to begin with?

The land is the Lord's. We are strangers and sojourners with him, and so is every family on the next street. Jeremiah bought a field he would never farm because God had promised a future he wouldn't live to see. The church holds more ground than he ever did, and the siege isn't at our walls. What we do with it is the weight we carry out the door.
