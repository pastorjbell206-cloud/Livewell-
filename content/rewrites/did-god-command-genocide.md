There are passages in the Bible that a skeptic doesn't need to distort. He only needs to read them aloud. Before I came to faith, when I still argued as an atheist, the conquest of Canaan was one of those passages, and I think the person who reads these chapters and feels sick is reading them with more honesty than the Christian who has learned not to notice them. A command to leave nothing alive that breathes, a city whose men and women, young and old, are put to the sword, a prophet who tells a king to spare no one, including the nursing infant: these are not obscure footnotes. They sit in the middle of the story Christians call the word of God.

So the question in the title deserves a straight hearing before it gets an answer. Did the God Christians worship command the destruction of whole peoples? The honest reply is that the texts say he commanded something that looks very much like it, that the best Christian readings change the picture more than skeptics usually allow, and that none of those readings makes the difficulty disappear. This essay will not pretend the weight is gone. It will argue that the weight is different from the one the skeptic describes, that it belongs to one bounded moment in Israel's story rather than to a standing policy, and that the book which contains these chapters is the same book that finally takes the sword out of human hands.

## What the Texts Actually Command

Start with the words themselves, because every serious answer has to answer them and not a softer paraphrase. In Deuteronomy 7 Israel stands east of the Jordan, and Moses names seven peoples already living in the land: the Hittites, Girgashites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites and Jebusites. Then comes the instruction.

> "and when the LORD your God gives them over to you, and you defeat them, then you must devote them to complete destruction. You shall make no covenant with them and show no mercy to them." (Deuteronomy 7:2)

The chapter goes on to forbid intermarriage with these peoples and to order Israel to break down their altars, smash their pillars, cut down their Asherah poles and burn their carved images (Deuteronomy 7:3–5). Deuteronomy 20 then distinguishes two kinds of war. Distant cities are to be offered terms of peace first (20:10–15). The cities of the land itself are treated differently.

> "But in the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God is giving you for an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes," (Deuteronomy 20:16)

The reason given in the next two verses is religious, not ethnic: these peoples would teach Israel their "abominable practices" done for their gods, and Israel would sin against the LORD (20:17–18).

The phrase "devote to complete destruction" translates a Hebrew verb from the same root as the noun ḥerem. It is a word from the world of worship before it is a word from the world of war. Something that is ḥerem has been removed from ordinary human use and handed over irrevocably to God. In the conquest narratives it describes a city, its people and its goods placed under that ban, so that nothing is taken as plunder and nothing is kept. Israel is forbidden to profit. That's worth noticing, though it isn't comforting, because it means the violence is framed as a sacred act, which is exactly what makes a modern reader recoil.

Joshua 6 through 11 narrates the campaign in the same vocabulary. At Jericho the whole city is put under the ban, and the summary of the southern campaign sounds like a sentence carved on a monument.

> "So Joshua struck the whole land, the hill country and the Negeb and the lowland and the slopes, and all their kings. He left none remaining, but devoted to destruction all that breathed, just as the LORD God of Israel commanded." (Joshua 10:40)

Then, generations later, the prophet Samuel sends Saul against the Amalekites, the people who had attacked Israel's weary stragglers in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 25:17–18).

> "Now go and strike Amalek and devote to destruction all that they have. Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey." (1 Samuel 15:3)

That verse removes the last hiding place. You can't read it as a description of soldiers only. It names the infant, and it puts the command in the mouth of God.

The modern word needs precision. "Genocide" was coined by the Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (1944), and the United Nations Genocide Convention of 1948 defined it as acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such. Christians sometimes try to escape the charge by saying the ḥerem was aimed at religion rather than race. That escape doesn't work. The Convention lists religious groups by name. If the words of Deuteronomy 20:16 were carried out as written, against every person in every city, they would meet the legal definition. The real questions are whether they were meant and carried out as written, what they were for, and whether they license anyone else.

## The Case Against God, at Full Strength

The skeptic's case deserves to be stated the way its best defenders would state it, not the way it sounds on a bad night online. Richard Dawkins made it famous in *The God Delusion* (2006), where he opened his second chapter with a long catalogue of the Old Testament God's vices, genocide among them, and later returned to Joshua's destruction of Jericho as a test case. Dawkins is a polemicist, but his argument is sharper than his rhetoric. He contends that no modern believer actually takes his morality from these texts. Christians read Joshua and quietly decline to imitate it. They read the Sermon on the Mount and try to obey it. That act of sorting, Dawkins argues, shows that believers already carry a moral standard that stands above the book and judges it. If you can tell which parts are good, the book is not where your goodness comes from.

The philosopher Wes Morriston pressed the same point with far more care in an essay titled "Did God Command Genocide? A Challenge to the Biblical Inerrantist," published in *Philosophia Christi* in 2009. Morriston's argument is not that Christians are wicked or stupid. It is an argument about rational confidence. We have far better reason, he argues, to believe that a perfectly good God would not command the killing of children than we have to believe that the Bible is inerrant. When two beliefs collide, a reasonable person gives up the one held with less warrant. So the believer who is honest about his own moral knowledge should conclude that God did not in fact command these killings, and that the texts which say he did are mistaken. He works through the standard evangelical defenses, the wickedness of the Canaanites among them, and argues that none of them justifies the killing of the children.

Some of this is simply true, and a Christian loses nothing by saying so. It's true that the church has sometimes defended these texts with a glibness that should shame us, as if a paragraph about ancient Near Eastern idiom could settle the matter of a dead infant. It's true that the phrase "they deserved it" has been spoken by every army that ever burned a village. And it's true that I once used these chapters to win arguments, and that I was not wrong about how they read on the surface.

But the skeptic's case also rests on assumptions that deserve examination rather than deference. Dawkins's sorting argument assumes that when Christians distinguish Joshua from Jesus they are importing an outside standard, when the church has long claimed that the distinction comes from inside the canon itself, from the way the story moves and ends. Morriston's argument assumes that the texts are best read as straightforward reports of what God ordered and what Israel did, a claim about genre that the texts themselves complicate. Neither assumption is foolish, and both are contestable. Each Christian response that follows has to be weighed with its weaknesses in view, because an answer that only works when you look away from its gaps is not an answer.

## How the Ancient World Wrote About War

The first response is about genre, and it begins with what the ancient Near East sounded like when it wrote about victory. Around 1208 BC the Egyptian pharaoh Merneptah set up a victory stele listing the peoples he had crushed in Canaan, and among them he boasted that Israel was laid waste and its seed was no more. It's the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible, and it announces Israel's extinction at the very moment Israel enters the historical record. Around 840 BC Mesha, king of Moab, commissioned an inscription claiming that Israel had perished forever. The same stone describes Mesha taking the town of Nebo and killing its inhabitants because he had devoted it to Ashtar-Chemosh, and the verb he uses is the Moabite form of the same root as the Hebrew ḥerem. Israel, of course, went on existing.

K. Lawson Younger's *Ancient Conquest Accounts* (1990) laid the conquest narratives of Joshua beside the military annals of Egypt, Assyria and the Hittites and showed how much they share: stock formulas of total victory, the enemy "left none remaining," the summary that compresses a long and partial war into a single sweeping claim. His point was not that Joshua is fiction but that the language of annihilation was the conventional way to say "we won decisively," in texts written while the enemy plainly remained.

Paul Copan and Matthew Flannagan build on this in *Did God Really Command Genocide?* (2014). They argue that the phrase "man and woman, young and old" works as a stock expression for totality that need not mean women and children were actually present, and that the central command of the conquest is not extermination but dispossession: the recurring verbs are about driving out and taking the land, and the ban is aimed at the fortified centers of Canaanite power and worship. They add a sharper point from inside Deuteronomy itself. The same chapter that commands complete destruction forbids Israel to intermarry with these peoples (Deuteronomy 7:3). You don't need a law against marrying people who no longer exist.

What gives this reading its force is that the Bible refuses to let the totality stand, and it does so in the same book. Joshua 11 summarizes the campaign as though the land has been emptied, and then immediately qualifies itself.

> "There was none of the Anakim left in the land of the people of Israel. Only in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod did some remain." (Joshua 11:22)

Two chapters later, the LORD tells an aged Joshua that very much land remains to be possessed (13:1), and the narrator admits a failure that is still visible in his own day.

> "Yet the people of Israel did not drive out the Geshurites or the Maacathites, but Geshur and Maacath dwell in the midst of Israel to this day." (Joshua 13:13)

Hebron and Debir, reported as wholly destroyed in Joshua 10:36–39, have to be taken again in Joshua 15 and Judges 1. And the opening chapter of Judges reads almost like a list of the peoples who were still there.

> "But the people of Benjamin did not drive out the Jebusites who lived in Jerusalem, so the Jebusites have lived with the people of Benjamin in Jerusalem to this day." (Judges 1:21)

The same pattern appears with Amalek. The Amalekites are back in the story before 1 Samuel ends, raiding David's town of Ziklag, and when David pursues them four hundred of their young men escape on camels (1 Samuel 30:17). Either the final editors of these books were too careless to notice what sat on adjoining pages, or the language of total destruction was never intended as a census. The second is the reading the text itself invites. I spent years holding these authors to a genre they weren't writing in and calling my mistake their crime.

This reading deserves respect, and it also has real weaknesses, and an honest defender has to face them. The first is that hyperbole about a war is still about a war. Even if the numbers are rhetorical, there were cities and there were dead, and some of the dead were not soldiers. The second is that 1 Samuel 15 strains the hyperbole reading at its hardest point. Saul is condemned not for killing too many but for sparing King Agag and the best of the livestock, which suggests that someone took the command literally and faulted him for falling short of it. Critics press a third point: even if the text exaggerates what happened, it still presents total destruction as the ideal God commanded, and it is the ideal, not the body count, that troubles the conscience. The genre argument moves the question off the ground the skeptic chose. It doesn't empty the battlefield.

## Judgment, Exceptions, and a God Who Takes No Side

The second response concerns what the war was for. The skeptic's natural reading is tribal: a local deity clears land for his favorite people. The text goes out of its way to deny that reading, and it does so at the very beginning of the story. When God first promises the land to Abraham, he attaches a strange delay to the promise.

> "And they shall come back here in the fourth generation, for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete." (Genesis 15:16)

Four centuries pass before the promise is kept. The conquest is framed as judgment deferred, and deferred long. When the judgment finally comes, Israel is told bluntly that it has nothing to do with Israel's merit.

> "Not because of your righteousness or the uprightness of your heart are you going in to possess their land, but because of the wickedness of these nations the LORD your God is driving them out from before you, and that he may confirm the word that the LORD swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob." (Deuteronomy 9:5)

The wickedness in view is named elsewhere, and it includes the burning of children as offerings to the gods (Deuteronomy 12:31). Leviticus 18 describes the land itself as defiled and vomiting out its inhabitants, and then turns the warning straight around: if Israel does the same things, the land will vomit Israel out as well (Leviticus 18:24–28). The standard is attached to conduct, not to blood. Deuteronomy 13 commands that an Israelite town which turns to other gods be placed under the same ban. In Joshua 7 an Israelite named Achan, who kept plunder from Jericho, is treated as ḥerem himself. And the rest of the Old Testament tells how the covenant people were in fact expelled from the land, first the northern kingdom in 722 BC and then Judah in 586 BC, by the same logic of judgment.

Meanwhile the text keeps showing Canaanites who escape the ban by turning toward Israel's God. Rahab of Jericho, a Canaanite and a prostitute, confesses that the LORD is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath (Joshua 2:11), and she and her household are spared and live in Israel afterward (6:25). The New Testament places her in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5) and in the roll of faith in Hebrews 11. The Gibeonites trick Israel into a covenant (Joshua 9), and Israel keeps it, goes to war to defend them (Joshua 10), and generations later suffers famine because Saul broke that oath (2 Samuel 21). Deuteronomy forbids any covenant with these peoples, and the story shows God holding Israel to one. These exceptions don't abolish the rule, but they show what the rule was aimed at.

Just before Jericho, in one of the strangest scenes in the book, Joshua meets a man with a drawn sword and asks the question every soldier asks: are you for us, or for our enemies?

> "And he said, 'No; but I am the commander of the army of the LORD. Now I have come.' And Joshua fell on his face to the earth and worshiped and said to him, 'What does my lord say to his servant?'" (Joshua 5:14)

"No" is the answer to a question about sides. God is not Israel's mascot. Israel is under his command and under his judgment in the same breath.

John Walton and J. Harvey Walton offer a different and in some ways more radical reading in *The Lost World of the Israelite Conquest* (2017). They argue that the Canaanites are not presented as being punished for crimes in a legal sense, and that ḥerem is better understood as removing something from human use: the destruction of an identity, a religious and social order, rather than necessarily the killing of every person in it. The point of the conquest, in their reading, is the establishment of a covenant order in the land, and the ban targets whatever would compete with that order. It also explains why Rahab could stop being a Canaanite by joining Israel. Its weakness is that it seems to underread the moral vocabulary of passages like Deuteronomy 9:5 and Leviticus 18, which do speak of wickedness, and that redescribing the target as an identity rather than a population still leaves people inside the identity who died.

The judgment reading also has a limit it must admit. Naming the sins of Canaan explains why the text calls this judgment rather than conquest. It does not, by itself, answer for a particular child who chose none of it. Scripture gives a category here, not an anesthetic. Christians who reach for "they deserved it" as though it closed the case have not listened to their own Bible, which speaks through Ezekiel with a grief that belongs in this discussion.

> "Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, declares the Lord GOD, and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?" (Ezekiel 18:23)

The God who waited four hundred years was not eager. That doesn't make the judgment painless. It makes it the act of a Judge rather than a conqueror.

## Reading Joshua After the Cross

There has always been a simpler way out, and the church has always refused it. Around 144 a wealthy shipowner from Pontus named Marcion broke with the church in Rome over the question these texts raise. He concluded that the God of the Old Testament, the Creator who gave the law and commanded wars, was a different and lesser deity from the Father revealed by Jesus: just, perhaps, but not good. He built a Bible to match: an edited Gospel of Luke and ten letters of Paul, with the Hebrew Scriptures removed entirely.

Marcion's answer is the one many modern readers reach for without knowing his name, and it has an obvious appeal. It keeps Jesus and disowns Joshua. The church rejected it anyway. Tertullian's five books *Against Marcion*, written around 207, argued at length that the goodness and the justice of God cannot be split between two gods without destroying both: a God who is good but never judges has made his peace with evil, and a God who judges but is not good is merely power. Irenaeus had already insisted in the 180s that the one God of Israel is the Father of Jesus Christ, and that the story from creation to new creation is one story. The church kept the Old Testament not because it was comfortable with Joshua but because Jesus himself read those Scriptures as the word of his Father, and because a Christ cut loose from Israel is a Christ nobody can recognize.

Keeping the texts did not mean reading them flatly. Origen of Alexandria, preaching through Joshua around 250, read the wars as figures of the Christian's war against sin, the Canaanite cities as the strongholds of vice within the soul, and much of the early church followed him. It's easy to call this evasion, and some of it was, but it was also a real conviction: that the whole Old Testament bends toward Christ and must be read in his light.

Greg Boyd's *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God* (2017) is the most ambitious modern form of that conviction. Boyd, who firmly rejects Marcion and affirms the whole Old Testament as inspired, argues that the cross is the definitive revelation of what God is like: a God who would rather die for his enemies than kill them. If that's true, then every portrait of God in Scripture has to be read through the cross. Where the Old Testament shows God commanding slaughter, Boyd proposes that we are seeing God doing what he did at Calvary, stooping to bear the sin of his people. God allowed his covenant partners, who were conditioned by the violent assumptions of their world, to portray him in ways that reflected their fallen picture of the divine, and he bore that distortion the way Christ bore our sin. The violent portraits are inspired, on Boyd's account, not because they show us God accurately but because they show us how far God was willing to stoop.

His critics have pressed hard on this. The most basic objection is that Boyd's method lets the reader decide which parts of the text reveal God and which parts reveal Israel's distortion of God, which returns us, by a more reverent road, to Dawkins's sorting problem. A second objection is that Jesus himself did not seem to read the Old Testament this way. He spoke of the flood and of Sodom as God's judgments (Luke 17:26–29), he pronounced judgment on Jerusalem in terms borrowed from the prophets (Luke 19:43–44), and the New Testament closes with a vision of the Word of God as a rider who judges and makes war in righteousness (Revelation 19:11–13). Boyd has an answer. He does not deny judgment; he argues that God judges the way he did at the cross, by withdrawing and handing people over to the sin they have chosen, the pattern Paul names when he writes that "God gave them up" (Romans 1:24). His critics reply that this fits some judgment texts better than others, and that the cross, on the face of the New Testament, does not abolish God's judgment so much as reveal who bears it.

The reading this essay has followed doesn't need Boyd's move, though it shares his instinct that Joshua must be read from Calvary, and readers who find his proposal more persuasive remain inside the faith. The texts of the conquest belong to Scripture and are not to be cut out. They are read within one story, where the ḥerem is a real judgment, described in the maximal idiom of its age, bounded to one people, one land and one moment. And they are read in the light of the one who finally stands where the judgment falls. The strongest objection this position has not fully answered is the one Morriston names: it still asks the reader to believe that a good God, at least once, commanded a war in which children died. I don't think that objection can be argued away. I think it can only be carried, and carried somewhere.

## A Judgment That Licenses No One

The most dangerous reader of Joshua is not the skeptic. It's the believer who closes the book and feels authorized. Christians have been that reader. In 1637, during the Pequot War, English soldiers and their allies set fire to a Pequot town at Mystic in Connecticut, and hundreds died, many of them women and children. A year later Captain John Underhill defended the killing in *Newes from America* by appealing to the wars of Scripture, as though a colonial militia inherited Israel's commission. Puritan writers and preachers more than once cast Native peoples as Canaanites or Amalekites. The Osage scholar Robert Allen Warrior, in his 1989 essay "Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians," asked what the Exodus looks like when you read it from the Canaanite side, and Michael Prior's *The Bible and Colonialism* (1997) traced how the conquest narratives were put to work in more than one land. The church should hear that history without defensiveness. We did this.

The distinction that matters most is between a bounded judgment within Israel's story and a license for any people. The ḥerem was tied to one people, one land and one window of time, given by direct command through a prophet, and never generalized into a standing policy toward outsiders. Even Deuteronomy distinguishes the cities of the land from every other war. Copan and Flannagan, who defend the texts more vigorously than many would, argue that these commands belonged to a unique and unrepeatable moment in Israel's history, and that no one today has warrant to believe God has issued such a command. The New Testament closes the door from the inside. When a disciple draws a sword to protect him, Jesus stops him.

> "Then Jesus said to him, 'Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword.'" (Matthew 26:52)

When two of his disciples want to call down fire on a Samaritan village, as Elijah once did, he turns and rebukes them (Luke 9:54–55). The Christian who reads Joshua as a warrant for violence has not misread one verse. He has walked past the cross to get there.

And the cross is where the ḥerem itself goes. The Greek Old Testament often rendered ḥerem as *anathema*, the thing devoted and cursed. Paul uses that word when he says he could wish himself accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of his own people (Romans 9:3). What Paul could only wish, Christ did. In Galatians Paul uses a different word, *katara*, curse, but the movement is the same.

> "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13)

Paul grounds that sentence in Deuteronomy 21:23, the curse on one hanged on a tree. The Judge of Canaan did not stay outside the judgment. He entered it and bore it, and the canon ends with the ban abolished, in words that echo Zechariah's promise that "there shall never again be a decree of utter destruction" (Zechariah 14:11).

> "No longer will there be anything accursed, but the throne of God and of the Lamb will be in it, and his servants will worship him." (Revelation 22:3)

That doesn't return the children of Jericho to their mothers, and I won't pretend it does. It tells us the one who commanded the ban also came under it, and it tells us who we are forbidden to become. We carry these chapters as a wound in the book we trust, read at the foot of a cross where God took the curse rather than handing it to us to wield, and we wait for the day when nothing will be devoted to destruction again.
