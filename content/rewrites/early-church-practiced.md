Sooner or later someone posts it. A screenshot of Acts 2:44, usually in a translation that sounds a little like a party platform, with a caption to the effect that the first Christians were communists and the modern church has some explaining to do. Within the hour the replies arrive. It was voluntary. Read Acts 5. Peter said the land was his. The early church believed Jesus was coming back next week and sold the farm, and then Paul had to take up a collection because Jerusalem went broke. Both sides quote the Bible, both sides feel they have won, and the only thing nobody in the thread seems to want is to sit still with what Luke actually wrote.

Most of us who care about Scripture have felt both reflexes. The first reads a text that plainly rebukes how most of us hold our money and reaches for the nearest political program that seems to agree. The second hears a program being smuggled into Scripture and reaches for the verse that closes the door. Each protects something real: the first wants the Bible's demand on the rich kept sharp, and the second remembers that the last century's states confiscated land in the name of the common good and starved millions doing it. But both ask the text a question it was not written to answer.

The question in the thread is about ownership and policy: did the first Christians abolish private property, and should the government make the rest of us do likewise? Luke's question is different, and it is harder. It is whether there was anyone in need among the people who said Jesus had risen. His answer is that there was not, and he says it the way a man reports something he considers the natural result of the resurrection, not a program anyone had to vote on. Take that question seriously and the thread's two answers start to look like two ways of avoiding it. The claim is simple to state. The first Christians were not communists, and they were not libertarians. They were a family that could not bear to have a hungry member, and that is a standard every modern party fails, and most of our churches too.

## What Luke actually wrote

The first passage closes Luke's account of Pentecost, after about three thousand are baptized in a day, and it begins with the four things the new community gave itself to.

> "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers." (Acts 2:42, ESV)

The word translated "fellowship" is *koinonia*, and it sits in a list with teaching, the Lord's Supper and prayer. The sharing of goods is not an economic arrangement bolted onto a religious society. It belongs to the worship. Two verses later Luke shows what the fellowship looked like when it reached people's possessions.

> "And all who believed were together and had all things in common." (Acts 2:44, ESV)

> "And they were selling their possessions and belongings and distributing the proceeds to all, as any had need." (Acts 2:45, ESV)

Read quickly, that sounds like a single dramatic act, everyone liquidating everything in one afternoon. The Greek does not say that. The verbs for selling and distributing are imperfect, the tense Luke uses for ongoing or repeated action: they *were selling*, they *were distributing*, and the phrase "as any had need" governs the whole verse. The picture is not a commune formed by one grand renunciation. It is a community in which, whenever a need surfaced, someone sold something to meet it. The sharing kept happening because the needs kept coming.

The second summary, two chapters later, is more precise.

> "Now the full number of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own, but they had everything in common." (Acts 4:32, ESV)

Luke does not say that no one owned anything. He says the things "belonged to him," and that no one *said* they were his own. The property had an owner. What had changed was the owner's posture: he no longer held it as a fortress against his brothers and sisters. That is a distinction between title and use, and Luke draws it more carefully than either side of the modern argument. The next verse gives the source.

> "And with great power the apostles were giving their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all." (Acts 4:33, ESV)

It is easy to read past that verse as a pious interruption. It is the hinge. Luke sets the resurrection in the middle of the shared goods because he thinks the one explains the other. People who have seen death defeated hold their fields differently.

> "There was not a needy person among them, for as many as were owners of lands or houses sold them and brought the proceeds of what was sold" (Acts 4:34, ESV)

> "and laid it at the apostles' feet, and distribution was made to each as any had need." (Acts 4:35, ESV)

Again the verbs are continuous, and again the rule of distribution is need, not equal shares. Then Luke names an example. Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus whom the apostles called Barnabas, "sold a field that belonged to him and brought the money and laid it at the apostles' feet" (Acts 4:37, ESV). If everyone had sold everything as a condition of membership, there would have been no reason to single him out.

Then comes the story the libertarian side of the thread always reaches for. Ananias and Sapphira sell property, keep back part of the price, and present the rest as the whole. Peter confronts him.

> "While it remained unsold, did it not remain your own? And after it was sold, was it not at your disposal? Why is it that you have contrived this deed in your heart? You have not lied to man but to God." (Acts 5:4, ESV)

The land was Ananias's before the sale, and the money was his after it. His sin was not keeping property. It was lying, wanting Barnabas's reputation without Barnabas's cost, in a community whose shared life ran on trust. The story proves that the giving was free. It does not prove that the giving was unimportant. A community that treats a lie about generosity as a lie to the Holy Spirit does not think generosity is a private hobby.

Two details finish the picture. In Acts 6 the widows of the Greek-speaking believers "were being neglected in the daily distribution" (Acts 6:1, ESV). The sharing had become a daily, organized ministry, large enough to fail, and when it failed the apostles repaired it rather than abandoning it. And in Acts 12 the escaped Peter goes to "the house of Mary, the mother of John whose other name was Mark, where many were gathered together and were praying" (Acts 12:12, ESV). Mary still had a house. Private homes did not vanish in the Jerusalem church. They were opened.

## Did Luke idealize the church?

There is a serious objection to reading these summaries as reportage, and it deserves the form its best defenders would sign. Educated Greek readers would have heard something familiar in Acts 4:32. Aristotle, in the ninth book of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, collects the proverbs his culture used about true friendship, among them that friends share a single soul and that friends hold their goods in common. Plato knew the second proverb, and later tradition credited it to Pythagoras and told of Pythagorean circles that pooled their property. When Luke writes "of one heart and soul" and "everything in common," he is using the vocabulary of the Greek friendship ideal almost word for word. An author who dresses his heroes in a famous philosophical ideal, the argument goes, is painting a portrait, not filing a report.

The objection has a second half, from Moses rather than Athens. Luke's "there was not a needy person among them" echoes the Greek translation of Deuteronomy 15:4, down to its word for the needy, *endeēs*, which appears nowhere else in the New Testament. In the ESV the promise reads, "But there will be no poor among you" (Deuteronomy 15:4, ESV). So Luke's summary speaks to two audiences at once: to the Greek it says the church achieved what your philosophers only dreamed of, and to the Jew it says the church became what Israel was promised it would be. Ernst Haenchen's influential commentary on Acts (1956) went further and argued that Luke generalized a few striking gifts, such as Barnabas's, into a picture of the whole community. Luke Timothy Johnson, in *Sharing Possessions* (1981), reads Luke's many scenes of possessions as symbols of how a person disposes of himself before God, which makes the summaries less a model to copy than an icon of unity.

Much of that is true. Luke was not writing minutes, and anyone who treats Acts 4 as a policy memo has missed the art of it. But idealizing is not inventing, and the evidence points to a real practice Luke has interpreted rather than a fiction he has decorated. The same author who writes the glowing summaries tells, in the very next chapters, of a couple who lied about their giving and of widows neglected along lines of language and culture. A writer building an icon does not usually hang the cracks beside it. The practice was also not unheard of among Jews of the period: the Community Rule found at Qumran has members hand their property to the community after a probation, and Josephus, in the *Jewish War* (c. 75), describes the Essenes holding their goods in common. And outsiders with no interest in flattering the church noticed the same thing a century later.

Most important, Luke does not credit the sharing to good organization or high ideals. He credits it to "great power" and "great grace," the testimony of the resurrection. The philosophers said friends should share, and the Pythagoreans tried it among a trained elite. Luke says a mixed crowd of fishermen, widows, Levites and landowners did it because something had happened to them. You can disbelieve the claim, and a thoughtful skeptic will. But you cannot call Luke naive about how hard sharing is. His point is that it was not possible until the resurrection made it so, and that even then it had to be guarded against liars and repaired when it broke. Deuteronomy itself held both truths in tension. The same chapter that promises "no poor among you" says a few verses later, "For there will never cease to be poor in the land. Therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor, in your land'" (Deuteronomy 15:11, ESV). The poor will always be there, and so the hand must always be open. Jesus echoed that verse at Bethany (Mark 14:7), and a careless tradition has turned his words into a shrug. In Deuteronomy the permanence of poverty is not an excuse. It is the reason for the command.

## What the church did next

The strongest argument that Acts 2 and 4 describe something real is what the church kept doing after Jerusalem, and how the form of it changed. When a prophet named Agabus foretold a famine, which Luke dates to the reign of Claudius, the new church at Antioch responded with notable precision.

> "So the disciples determined, every one according to his ability, to send relief to the brothers living in Judea." (Acts 11:29, ESV)

Nobody in Antioch sold everything. Each gave according to ability, across ethnic and geographic lines, to people most had never met. One household of faith in one city had become a network of churches carrying one another's burdens at a distance.

Paul made that network the great project of his middle years. When the Jerusalem leaders recognized his mission, "they asked us to remember the poor, the very thing I was eager to do" (Galatians 2:10, ESV), and through most of the 50s he gathered a collection from the Gentile churches for the poor believers of Jerusalem. He told the Corinthians to set something aside "On the first day of every week" (1 Corinthians 16:2, ESV), as each prospered. That is regular, proportional giving, not liquidation. And in 2 Corinthians 8 and 9 he named its goal with a word that deserves more attention than it gets.

> "For I do not mean that others should be eased and you burdened, but that as a matter of fairness" (2 Corinthians 8:13, ESV)

> "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness." (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV)

The word is *isotes*, equality or fairness, and Paul grounds it in the manna of Exodus 16: "Whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack" (2 Corinthians 8:15, ESV). The goal is not identical holdings. It is that no member of the body go without while another has surplus. Yet the same letter insists the giving be free: "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7, ESV). And writing to Rome about the same collection, Paul says the Gentile churches were pleased to give, "and indeed they owe it to them" (Romans 15:27, ESV). Free, and owed. Paul holds both without strain, and the whole modern argument depends on pulling them apart.

The next generations kept the pattern. The *Didache*, a church manual from around the turn of the second century, tells the believer to share everything with his brother and call nothing his own, because partners in what does not die should be partners in what does. Justin Martyr, writing his *First Apology* around 155, says that Christians who once prized wealth above everything now bring what they have into a common fund for anyone in need (chapter 14), and he describes the Sunday gathering (chapter 67): after the readings, the sermon and the Eucharist, those who have means and are willing give what each thinks right, and the one presiding uses it for orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. That is Jerusalem translated into a settled congregation: voluntary, weekly, administered, aimed at need.

The pagans noticed. Lucian of Samosata, a satirist with no sympathy for the faith, wrote around 165 of a charlatan named Peregrinus who lived well off the Christians for a while, and mocked them for treating their possessions as common property so that any clever fraud could exploit them. A hostile witness is exactly the kind that counts. Tertullian, writing his *Apology* in Carthage in 197, describes the practice from inside: a monthly gift, if a member wishes and is able, since no one is compelled, spent on feeding and burying the poor, on orphans, the housebound elderly, the shipwrecked, and believers condemned to the mines. Here he records the pagan remark, *see how they love one another*, and adds a barbed line of his own, that Christians hold everything in common except their wives. By 362 the emperor Julian was complaining that the Christians fed the pagans' poor as well as their own, a story told in [What Does the Bible Say About Poverty, and Is It Political?](/writing/is-poverty-political-the-bibles-answer).

Across all of this the form changes, from Jerusalem's common purse to Antioch's relief, Paul's collection, the Sunday fund and the monthly chest, and the reach widens from brother to stranger to pagan. What never changes is the rule of distribution, which is need, and the character of the gift, which is free. John Chrysostom, preaching on Acts in Constantinople around 400, imagined aloud what his wealthy city would look like if it did what Jerusalem had done, and argued that no poor person would be left. He did not ask the emperor to seize anything. He asked his congregation. Thomas Aquinas, around 1270, gave the tradition its formula: it is lawful to own things, but their use should be held as common, ready to share with others in need, and in urgent need what a starving person takes to survive is not theft (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 66, articles 2 and 7). Ownership is real. It is also answerable.

## Two modern readings, one instrument

The modern argument over Acts began in earnest when the factory made poverty urban, visible and apparently permanent. After the Chartist failure of 1848, F. D. Maurice, John Ludlow and Charles Kingsley took the name Christian Socialist in London and set up cooperative workshops, convinced that competition was not the law of God's world. Friedrich Engels, from the other side, wrote in 1894 that early Christianity and the workers' movement shared a shape: both movements of the oppressed, both preaching deliverance, one in heaven and one on earth. Karl Kautsky's *Foundations of Christianity* (1908) read the Jerusalem church as a proletarian communism that failed because it shared only consumption and never touched the means of production. In America, Walter Rauschenbusch's *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) pointed to the first believers' shared life as evidence of a social impulse the church had later lost. And in 1971 Gustavo Gutiérrez's *A Theology of Liberation* read Luke's community of goods in the light of Deuteronomy 15, not as an ideal of poverty but as a protest against it, a people who refused to let anyone among them be poor.

At its strongest, this reading says something the church needs to hear. The Bible does not treat poverty as a private misfortune for which the comfortable bear no responsibility, and it treats wealth piled up beside a brother's hunger as a spiritual emergency. The Christian socialists were right that the market is not a neutral force of nature, and Gutiérrez was right that Luke's point is the absence of need rather than the romance of renunciation. If the church has more often endorsed the arrangements that benefit its donors than questioned them, and it has, the socialist reading is partly a judgment the church earned.

The libertarian reading is just as serious at its strongest, and it deserves the same fairness. Ronald Nash, in *Poverty and Wealth* (1986), pressed in substance the case that has become standard among Christian defenders of the free market: the sharing in Acts was voluntary, local and temporary; Peter explicitly affirmed Ananias's right to his property; Paul's collection was freely given and never enforced; and nothing in the text authorizes the state to take from some and give to others. This reading has history on its side as well as exegesis. When the sharing of Acts was made compulsory, as it was when radical reformers seized Münster in 1534 and 1535, or when a twentieth-century state collectivized the land by force, the results were not the Jerusalem church. They were terror and famine. The libertarian reading remembers that coercion cannot produce "one heart and soul," and that a gift taken at the point of law has ceased to be a gift.

Both readings see something true, and both should be measured with one instrument. Ernst Troeltsch saw the key in *The Social Teaching of the Christian Churches* (1912), when he called what happened in Jerusalem a communism of love: religious rather than political, voluntary rather than enforced, concerned with sharing what the community consumed rather than with reorganizing how society produced. Kautsky made almost the same observation and counted it a failure. Troeltsch was closer to Luke. The distinction that matters is between *koinonia* and a program. *Koinonia* is the shared life of people bound together in Christ, and it reaches their property because it reaches everything. A program is a policy for a whole society, most of whose members do not share that life and have not seen what the apostles testified to. The socialist reading takes a practice that grew out of worship and the resurrection and tries to make it a law for people who have neither. The libertarian reading takes the fact that the practice was free and concludes that it was optional. Those are opposite errors with the same result. Each moves the responsibility somewhere other than the congregation, to the state in one case and to the private conscience in the other, and in both cases the church is left doing roughly what it was doing before.

Here the distinction has to be stated as plainly as possible, because the libertarian side of the thread leans on the word *voluntary* until it bends. Voluntary is not the same as optional. Ananias was free to keep his land, and the love of God still made a claim on it. "But if anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, yet closes his heart against him, how does God's love abide in him?" (1 John 3:17, ESV). Nobody would enforce that verse. Everybody would be judged by it. Paul's collection was freely given, and he could still say the Gentiles owed it. The freedom of the gift is what makes it love. It is not a loophole in the command.

The famous slogan Marx set down in his *Critique of the Gotha Program* (1875), from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs, was older than Marx, and its two halves sound strikingly like Acts 11:29 and Acts 4:35. But it left out what Luke put at the center, the resurrection and the grace that came with it, and without that center it became a demand the state had to enforce. The libertarian answer often keeps the freedom and loses the family, so that "voluntary" ends up meaning the needy believer's hunger is between him and whoever happens to notice. Neither reading asks Luke's question: is there a needy person among you? I have to put myself inside this. It is possible to spend years arguing which reading is correct without once asking who in one's own congregation is behind on rent.

## What a household can do with Acts 4

Acts does not hand a family a budget, and it would betray the text to invent one. But it describes a posture, and a posture can be practiced in a kitchen. The imperfect tense teaches responsiveness first. The Jerusalem believers kept selling as needs kept surfacing, which means they kept looking. A household that wants to live inside this passage starts by asking whether it knows anyone's need at all, whether its money ever moves toward a face or only toward an institution. Paul told the Ephesian thief to work with his hands "so that he may have something to share with anyone in need" (Ephesians 4:28, ESV), and that small verse turns the whole purpose of a paycheck outward. We work to have something to give. That is not sentiment. Paul also wrote, "If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" (2 Thessalonians 3:10, ESV), and the church has always needed both verses to keep generosity from becoming either stinginess or enabling.

The second thing is that need has to be speakable. The Jerusalem church could meet needs because people could name them. Many American congregations are arranged so that a family can be drowning in the pew and no one will find out, because we have made our finances more private than our sins. I am not outside that. I have received tithes and overseen a budget and sent money through programs without asking whether the people who were struggling in the pews had what they needed; the machinery I worked within existed to raise and spend money, not to notice. A household cannot fix that alone, but it can refuse to keep the silence, by telling a trusted few when it is in trouble and by making it safe for others to do the same. The third thing is proximity. Mary opened her house. The *Didache* spoke of partners. The practices that endured were the ones embedded in real relationships: a table with room at it, a car lent, a debt quietly forgiven within the family of faith. The larger public questions belong to the essays on [charity and justice](/writing/charity-is-not-justice-the-difference) and [the Jubilee](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded); Luke's story happened at this smaller scale. None of this is financial advice, and a household under real strain should get competent help with its own numbers. It is a description of what a Christian home is for.

## No needy person among them

Luke put the sharing of goods and the testimony to the resurrection in the same breath, and that is the part of Acts 4 the modern argument cannot absorb. The early church did not share because it had found the right economic theory. It shared because the apostles "were giving their testimony to the resurrection of the Lord Jesus, and great grace was upon them all" (Acts 4:33, ESV). A people who believe that death has been beaten and that their future is secured in the risen Christ have lost the main reason to clutch. We clutch because we are afraid, and the resurrection is God's answer to that fear before it is anything else.

So the question the passage puts to us is finally not political but diagnostic. If there are needy people among us, and there are, and if we can afford not to know their names, and most of us can, then our problem is not a wrong reading of Acts. We are holding our goods the way people do who are not sure anything has happened. Mercy meets a church that falls short of Luke's sentence and repents. It was never meant as permission to cross the sentence out.

The first Christians did not keep it perfectly. Ananias lied, the widows were neglected, and the Corinthians ate their fill at the Lord's Table while the poor went hungry (1 Corinthians 11:21). What Luke and Paul left us is not a church that succeeded. It is a church that kept the standard in view, fell short, took correction, and began again. That road is still open, and walking it will cost something specific. Luke left one sentence lying across the path of every congregation that has come after, including mine. There was not a needy person among them. We are still deciding whether we believe what they believed badly enough to hold our houses the way they held theirs.
