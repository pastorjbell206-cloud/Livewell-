Most people who set out to read the Bible straight through stall somewhere in Leviticus, and they aren't wrong about what they found. The table of contents promises a collection: sixty-six books in the Protestant count, written over roughly a thousand years in three languages by kings and shepherds, priests and exiles, a physician and a tax collector. Between the covers sit genealogies and law codes, love poetry, laments that accuse God to his face, four overlapping lives of one man, and a closing vision of beasts and bowls and a city of gold. A reader who decides this is a shelf rather than a book has simply read the table of contents. The church's usual answer has been to hand him a method for using the shelf: find the verse for the need, the command that applies, the promise to underline. Most of us still read that way, which is why so many lifelong Christians can quote the Bible and can't say what it's about.

This essay argues for something much older. The Bible is one story, running from a garden to a city: creation, the fall, the calling of Israel, the coming of Christ, the life of the church, and the renewal of all things. Its deep grammar is the exodus. Its hinge is a Roman cross and a tomb found empty. And the distinction that matters most is between two ways of holding the book. We can treat it as a rulebook or an anthology, something we consult from outside and remain in charge of, or we can receive it as a story we find ourselves written into. The first leaves us the protagonist. The second doesn't.

## A shelf, a rulebook, or a story

Watch how the book actually gets used. The rulebook reader sorts divine instructions for a lifetime, keeps the command against stealing, drops the one against shellfish, and couldn't tell you why, because he has no story to tell him which commands belonged to which moment. The promise-box reader has a verse over the church kitchen door, "For where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (Matthew 18:20), and takes it as comfort for a thin turnout. He has rarely been told that Jesus said it about a church confronting a brother who has sinned, where every charge must be established by two or three witnesses, and that it promises Christ's presence first in the hardest thing a congregation ever does.

Neither reader lacks sincerity. What they lack is plot. They have the sentences and have lost the story, and a sentence cut from its story will say nearly anything we need it to. Those of us who preach aren't exempt. I have preached the comforting half of a passage and left the demanding half on the floor, and told myself it was for the congregation's sake when it was for mine. A book without a plot can't argue back, which is exactly why we prefer it that way.

The philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre argued in *After Virtue* (1981) that human beings are storytelling creatures, and that we can't answer the question of what we ought to do until we have answered a prior one: what story we find ourselves part of. The rulebook reading tries to answer "What should I do?" while skipping the prior question, so the commands float free of the drama that gave them their sense. The anthology reading fails more politely, leaving us to choose among many stories the way we'd pull down whichever volume suits the evening. Both leave us standing outside the book, managing it.

The story reading reverses the position. It claims the reader is already inside a drama that began long before him and will end without his permission, and that his first task is to learn where in it he stands. Erich Auerbach, writing as a literary scholar rather than a theologian, noticed the same demand. In "Odysseus' Scar," the opening chapter of *Mimesis* (1946), he set Homer's evenly lit scenes beside the spare account of Abraham and Isaac and argued that the two want different things from us. Homer lets us forget our own world for a few hours. The biblical story claims to be the only real world and sets out to overcome ours, so that the reader must fit his own life into its history of the whole. Auerbach called that claim to truth tyrannical, because it excludes every rival. A skeptic may fairly ask whether that's true or merely convenient. The answer begins with the story's shape.

## From a garden to a city: the shape of the story

Begin at the two ends, because the first pages and the last are answering each other. Genesis opens in a garden where God walks with the creatures he made in his image. Revelation closes in a city with a river and a tree of life, and a voice from the throne: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" (Revelation 21:3). Everything between is the long account of how God comes to dwell again with a people who broke the first dwelling.

Genesis 3 is the fracture the rest of Scripture addresses, and the damage spreads fast: a brother's blood crying from the ground, a world filled with violence, a tower raised to make a name. After Babel, God's answer to the scattered nations turns out to be one elderly, childless man. "In you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). The promise is particular in order to be universal. It narrows to a family, a nation brought out of Egypt, a king in David's line, a remnant carried into exile, and finally a single Israelite dying outside a city wall; from there it breaks outward to every nation, where Abraham's blessing was aimed from the first. Paul could say that Scripture "preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham" (Galatians 3:8).

Geerhardus Vos, who taught at Princeton Theological Seminary for nearly four decades, gave this a precise form in *Biblical Theology* (1948). Revelation, he argued, is itself historical, disclosed in stages that grow organically, the way a seed contains the tree without yet looking like one. That guards the story reading against contempt for the Old Testament. Leviticus is not less inspired than Hebrews. It is earlier.

N. T. Wright gave the idea its most useful image. In "How Can the Bible Be Authoritative?" (*Vox Evangelica*, 1991), and at length in *The New Testament and the People of God* (1992), he asked how a story could carry authority, since you can't obey a narrative the way you obey a command. He imagined a Shakespeare play whose fifth act had been lost. Rather than write a new script, you would steep seasoned actors in the first four acts until the characters and plot lived in them, and then ask them to work out the fifth themselves. Their improvisation would be free, and it would also be answerable, because anyone who knew the play would recognize a fifth act that contradicted the first four. Wright's acts are creation, fall, Israel, Jesus and the church, with the New Testament as the first scene of the fifth act, carrying hints of how the play is meant to end.

Craig Bartholomew and Michael Goheen adapted the model in *The Drama of Scripture* (2004), giving the church's mission and the completion of redemption an act each, so that their drama runs to six: creation, fall, redemption initiated in Israel, redemption accomplished in Jesus, the mission of the church, and redemption completed. Either way, the model refuses to flatten the Bible into timeless principles and tells the reader which act he's standing in: not the first, since the world isn't unfallen, and not the third, waiting for a Messiah who hasn't come, but after the hinge and before the ending.

That location answers the shellfish question better than any list could. When Mark remarks that Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19), he is marking a change of act. The food laws kept a holy nation distinct while the promise was held in one family; once the promise opens to the nations, that boundary has done its work. The law isn't thrown away. It's read as the part of the play that explains why the fourth act had to happen.

## The exodus: the grammar underneath the story

If creation to new creation is the story's shape, the exodus is its grammar. Israel's memory of God began with a rescue, not a definition. Before a single commandment at Sinai, God reminds the people what they have seen: "You yourselves have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself" (Exodus 19:4). The slaves are brought not merely out from Pharaoh but to God, and Exodus closes with the glory of the Lord filling the tabernacle in the middle of their camp. Freedom in the Bible is always freedom for something, and what it is for is presence.

In 1938 the German scholar Gerhard von Rad drew attention to a confession in Deuteronomy 26 that an Israelite farmer recited over his first fruits. It begins, "A wandering Aramean was my father" (Deuteronomy 26:5), and runs from Egypt to the land. Von Rad read it as a compact historical creed: Israel's faith was a recital of events before it was a catalogue of ideas. Later scholars have doubted how old that confession is, but the observation holds: the psalms go on retelling the same rescue.

The prophets reached for it hardest when the story seemed finished. Isaiah 40 through 55 speaks to exiles in Babylon who had lost land, king and temple, and promises them an exodus. "In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD; make straight in the desert a highway for our God" (Isaiah 40:3). Three chapters later the Lord who "makes a way in the sea" (Isaiah 43:16) says something startling: "Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?" (Isaiah 43:19). The new rescue will be so like the first, and so much greater, that the first can be left behind.

Then history seemed to fall short. Cyrus of Persia let the exiles go home in 538 BC, but the return was small and poor; when the second temple's foundation was laid, the old men who remembered the first wept aloud (Ezra 3:12). Isaiah's new exodus was left standing open.

The Gospels walk through that open door. Mark begins with Isaiah 40's voice in the wilderness (Mark 1:3). Matthew takes Hosea's memory of the first exodus, "out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1), and applies it to the child Jesus (Matthew 2:15). At the transfiguration Moses and Elijah "spoke of his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem" (Luke 9:31), and the Greek word behind "departure" is *exodos*. It would be accomplished at Passover. Paul says it without ornament: "For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7).

Richard Hays, in *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* (2016), calls this figural reading, drawing on Erich Auerbach's 1938 essay "Figura," which described two real events linked so that the first points toward the second and the second fulfills the first, without either dissolving into allegory. Such reading, Hays argues, runs backward. Hosea wasn't writing a coded prediction; he was remembering. The sense in which his words also point to Jesus becomes visible only from the far side of the resurrection, as John admits: "When therefore he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this, and they believed the Scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken" (John 2:22). The earlier text keeps its own meaning. It gains a second one.

Paul carries the grammar into the church. Writing to a largely Gentile congregation in Corinth, he speaks of the wilderness generation as family: "our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:1). Then he says why the old story was written down: "Now these things happened to them as an example, but they were written down for our instruction, on whom the end of the ages has come" (1 Corinthians 10:11). The word behind "example" belongs to the family of *typos*, a pattern stamped into history. The last book completes the arc. Beside a sea of glass mingled with fire, those who have conquered the beast "sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb" (Revelation 15:3). The last exodus ends where the first one did, on the far shore, singing.

## The hinge: a cross, an empty tomb, and Luke 24

Every long story has a hinge, a moment after which nothing can go back. The Bible's isn't the law on the burning mountain or the founding of David's kingdom. It's an execution outside Jerusalem, and then, on the third morning, a tomb found empty and the same man alive.

Luke says the risen Jesus himself taught his followers to read the whole story this way. On the road to Emmaus, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27). That evening in Jerusalem he said it again, more precisely: "These are my words that I spoke to you while I was still with you, that everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44). Law, Prophets and Psalms is a way of naming the whole of Israel's Scriptures. The claim isn't that a few scattered verses predicted him but that the whole library was bent toward what had just happened. Then Luke adds a line we read past: "Then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45).

Nor does the story stop at the tomb. It is written, Jesus says, that the Christ should suffer and rise, "and that repentance for the forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47). The fifth act is already inside the Scriptures he is interpreting. Abraham's blessing for all the families of the earth is about to go out on foot.

Why should the climax of a rescue be the death of the rescuer? Because of what the fracture was. Egypt was never the deepest captor, and neither was Babylon. The exile ended and the heart didn't change, because the bondage the story had been circling ran through every human being, Israelite and Egyptian alike: sin, and the death that follows it. The new exodus had to be a rescue from the thing underneath all the Pharaohs, and the New Testament claims it was accomplished as the first one was, by the blood of a lamb, except that this time the lamb is the firstborn Son himself. The early creed Paul handed on says twice that Christ died and was raised "in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3). The death and the rising didn't interrupt the story. They were what it had been waiting for.

The resurrection is also more than a happy ending. In the Jewish hope of the time, resurrection belonged to the end of history. The Easter claim is that the end has arrived in the middle, in one man, ahead of everyone else. "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). The firstfruits were the first sheaf of the harvest, the pledge of the rest. The new creation of Revelation 21 has already begun, in a garden, on a Sunday.

The early church saw this clearly because it was forced to. Around 144 a shipowner from Pontus named Marcion broke with the church at Rome. He taught that the God of Israel was a lesser deity than the Father of Jesus, cut away the Old Testament, and kept an edited Luke and ten letters of Paul. The Gnostic teachers kept the texts but rearranged them into myths of their own. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, answered both in *Against Heresies* around 180 with a single story told by a single God. He took a word from Paul, who describes God's purpose as "a plan for the fullness of time, to unite all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth" (Ephesians 1:10). The verb there means to sum up, to gather under one head, and from it Irenaeus built his teaching of recapitulation. Christ, he argued, went back over the ground Adam had lost, answering with obedience at a tree the disobedience that began at a tree, and so gathered the whole human story into himself and turned it around. For Irenaeus the unity of Scripture was the shape of what God had done in Christ: one Adam who fell, one Adam who stood, and one story from the first garden to the last city.

## Is the unity imposed? The strongest objection

The serious objection comes from people who have read the Bible with great care. In 1787, in an inaugural lecture at Altdorf, Johann Philipp Gabler drew a line between biblical theology, the historical description of what the biblical writers believed, and dogmatic theology, the church's later systematic teaching. Honest reading, on Gabler's terms, lets each text speak in its own setting first. From that line, "one story" looks like doctrine laid over the texts after the fact. James Barr, in *Holy Scripture: Canon, Authority, Criticism* (1983), warned against forcing a theological unity onto documents that are diverse and often in tension. Walter Brueggemann, in his *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997), described Israel's Scripture as a courtroom of testimony and countertestimony: the core witness to a God who acts and rescues, and beside it the voices of hiddenness and abandonment in Job, Ecclesiastes and the laments. He cautioned Christians against treating it all as a waiting room for Jesus, since the synagogue has read the same texts faithfully toward a different end.

The Jewish scholar Jon Levenson sharpened the point in *The Hebrew Bible, the Old Testament, and Historical Criticism* (1993): the very name "Old Testament" already presupposes a New one, and a reading that hears these books only as preparation can't hear them as the synagogue does. The order of the shelf makes the same point, since it does interpretive work of its own: the Hebrew Bible as Jews arrange it ends with Chronicles and Cyrus sending the exiles home, while the Christian Old Testament ends with Malachi's promise of Elijah, leading the eye straight into the Gospels. And the objection carries a moral weight Christians have earned, since reading Israel's Scriptures as a mere prologue to Christ went hand in hand, for centuries, with contempt for the Jewish people who kept reading them. On this account the "one story" is a Christian frame fitted around an Israelite library, and the frame hides as much as it shows.

Much of that should be granted without hedging. No neutral inspection of the texts would force this unity on anyone; Luke himself says the disciples had to have their minds opened. The Christian reading is a confession made from the far side of Easter, and presenting it as an obvious literary fact claims more than we know. Flattening the Bible's voices into one bland message isn't reverence; it's erasure. And Christian contempt for Jews is a sin to be repented of, not an embarrassment to be managed.

But the objection claims more than it can hold. The texts were reading one another long before any Christian arrived. Michael Fishbane showed in *Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel* (1985) how thoroughly later biblical writers reworked earlier laws, oracles and narratives from inside the canon. Isaiah reads Exodus. Nehemiah 9 prays the whole story from creation through Abraham, Egypt, Sinai, the land and the exile, and ends with the people still slaves in the land given to their fathers. Israel was narrating its Scriptures as one story before the church existed. What divides church and synagogue is where the story's center lies and how it will end. Neither ordering of the shelf closes it: the Hebrew Bible's last words are "Let him go up" (2 Chronicles 36:23). Both canons end looking forward, waiting.

Unity doesn't require uniformity, either. Hebrews opens by admitting the diversity: "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets" (Hebrews 1:1). The countertestimony Brueggemann hears is really there, because the communities that kept the song of the sea also kept Job's accusations and the laments in the same book. The story includes its own arguments, and so does its hinge. On the cross Jesus prays the opening of a lament psalm, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). The countertestimony isn't left out of the climax. It's spoken from the center of it.

Irenaeus gave the church its test. He compared the heretics to someone who pries loose the stones of a mosaic of a king, rearranges them into a dog or a fox, and insists it's the same portrait because the stones are genuine. The question for any unified reading is whether it keeps the stones where they were set. Figural reading at its best leaves Hosea still talking about Israel while it hears a second meaning after Easter. Where Christians have turned the Old Testament into mere code, the critics are right, and we should say so. The skeptic needn't finish persuaded. He should finish granting that the claim of unity is neither naive nor invented from nothing, and that the texts themselves began making it.

## Two ways of telling the one story

Christians who agree that the Bible is one story have long disagreed about how its parts fit, sometimes as if the faith itself were at stake. It isn't. That the story runs through Christ crucified and risen is first-order; the creeds bind it. How the covenants relate, and what becomes of the promises to Israel, is second-order: it divides faithful churches without dividing the faith.

Covenant theology grew out of the Reformed churches. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) and Johannes Cocceius's *Summa doctrinae de foedere* (1648) set out a covenant of works made with Adam and a covenant of grace running through every age after the fall, administered by sacrifices under the law and sacraments under the gospel. There are not two covenants of grace, the Confession says, but "one and the same, under various dispensations." At its strongest, covenant theology hears the Bible's unity in God's faithfulness: one people of God across both testaments, with Abraham the father of all who believe. "And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (Galatians 3:29). The strongest objection it hasn't fully answered is that it can flatten discontinuities the Bible itself marks, absorbing Israel into the church until God's particular promises to the Jewish people quietly evaporate. Many Reformed interpreters, reading Romans 11, have answered by expecting a future turning of Jewish people to Christ, but the objection hasn't gone away, and given how Christians have treated Jews, it isn't academic.

Dispensationalism took shape in the 1830s through John Nelson Darby and the Plymouth Brethren, and the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909 carried it deep into American fundamentalism. Charles Ryrie, in *Dispensationalism Today* (1965), named its essentials: a consistent distinction between Israel and the church, a consistently plain reading of the text, and the glory of God, rather than salvation alone, as the purpose unifying history. At its strongest, dispensationalism insists that promises made to Israel mean what they said and that God will keep them to the people he named, and it can point to Paul's words to Gentile believers about his unbelieving kinsmen: "For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29). Its strongest objection is that its classic forms divided the story into two peoples with two destinies, so that the church could appear as a parenthesis interrupting God's plan for Israel rather than the fulfillment of the promise to Abraham. Progressive dispensationalists, notably Craig Blaising and Darrell Bock in *Progressive Dispensationalism* (1993), have answered by seeing much more continuity, treating the church as the kingdom already inaugurated while keeping a future for Israel.

This essay doesn't settle the question, and its argument doesn't depend on settling it. Where we have treated this disagreement as a boundary of fellowship, we have made a tribe out of a second-order question, and both tribes owe each other some repentance. A covenant theologian and a dispensationalist who both confess the crucified and risen Christ are reading the same story, arguing about how its middle chapters fit, and waiting for the same last page.

## Written into the story

A rulebook is something we apply, and an anthology is something we browse, and in both cases we remain in charge. A story we are written into works the other way. It tells us who we are before we have decided, gives us ancestors we didn't choose, and places us in an act we didn't write. When Paul told the Corinthians that "our fathers" passed through the sea, he wasn't offering an illustration. He was telling pagans from a harbor town that they had been adopted into a family history, and that Israel's wilderness was now their past. That is what the Bible does to a reader who lets it be one story. It stops being a resource and becomes a genealogy.

There is relief in that. We are not the protagonist, and never had to be. The hero has already done what the whole plot required, and we walked in during the fifth act, after the hinge had turned. But Wright's image cuts both ways. Actors in the fifth act are free to improvise, and they are answerable to the four acts before them. Much of what passes for faithful Christianity in our time, whether it marches under a flag or dissolves into a therapy for the self, is a fifth act that has forgotten the fourth. We didn't lose the Bible's commands. We lost its plot, and without the plot we kept casting ourselves as the lead.

The same chapter that gives the Corinthians their fathers also tells them what became of most of those fathers. "Nevertheless, with most of them God was not pleased, for they were overthrown in the wilderness" (1 Corinthians 10:5). They had passed through the sea, eaten the manna and drunk from the rock. They were in the story, and they died in the middle of it, grumbling for Egypt. Paul draws the conclusion: "Therefore let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12). Being written into the story isn't the same as being faithful in it. The way out of that danger is the way the story itself provides, the repentance that Jesus said would be proclaimed to all nations in his name, beginning from the very city that crucified him.

So the church lives between the sea and the song. The Lamb has been slain, the water has been crossed, and the last page is already written: a city, a river, a voice announcing that God dwells with his people. Our page isn't finished. We are somewhere in the wilderness between the shore we came from and the shore where the song of Moses and the Lamb will be sung, carrying a book that is the memory of how we got here and the promise of where the road ends. Tomorrow, when the Bible lies open again on a kitchen table, the question it asks won't be which verse is useful. It will be whose story this is, and which act we are standing in, and whether anyone reading our lives in the fifth act could still recognize the play.
