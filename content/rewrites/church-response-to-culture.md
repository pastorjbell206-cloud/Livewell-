## Choosing a posture before a theology

Most American Christians have answered the question of culture long before they've thought about it. The mother who pulls her children out of the public school has answered it, and so has the deacon who runs for a seat on that school's board. So has the young professional who quietly stops mentioning church at the office, and so has the congregation that has made its peace with every conviction of its zip code and calls the peace hospitality. Each holds a working theory of how Christ relates to the world, and few have held it up against the alternatives.

The pressure to choose has grown because the old arrangement is ending. For most of the country's history the school calendar, the courtroom oath, the civic prayer and the moral vocabulary of the neighborhood carried broadly Christian assumptions without having to argue for them. The faith hasn't vanished from American life, but it has stopped being the assumption everyone starts from, and a faith that has to be chosen must also be taught and practiced on purpose. Many Christians feel that change chiefly as loss, and some of the grief is warranted. But grief looks for a strategy, and the strategies on offer come in pairs of opposites.

One instinct says to fight: recover the lost ground and force the culture back into a shape where the faith feels at home. I've felt that instinct. The other says to adapt: translate the gospel into whatever register the culture already finds agreeable. I've preached from that instinct as well, for longer than I like to admit, hoping to present a faith that sat comfortably beside the ambitions of middle-class America and made no heavy demands. It took me years to see that a gospel which demands nothing has nothing much to give. It can adjust a life. It can't raise one from the dead.

Both instincts are older than we are. The best map of the church's long argument about them is still a book from 1951, read alongside the people who spent the next half century telling its author what he had missed. [Why Did Christians Lose the Culture War?](/writing/why-the-church-lost-the-culture-war) tells the story of the recent American fight. This essay asks the prior question the fight assumed an answer to: what is the church's relation to the culture it lives in?

## Niebuhr's map of five answers

H. Richard Niebuhr, younger brother of the more famous Reinhold, taught Christian ethics at Yale Divinity School for three decades. His *Christ and Culture* (1951) became one of the most read works of American theology, and its vocabulary is now so common that people who have never opened it speak of Christ "transforming" culture as if the phrase came from the Bible. Niebuhr called the subject "the enduring problem," a tension built into the faith from the beginning. Christ, as he described him, leads people away from the world toward God and then sends them back into it, and the church has never settled how the two movements fit together.

By culture Niebuhr meant the whole human-made environment that people lay over the natural one: language, custom, law, institutions, technique, inherited ideas and the values that hold them together. Nobody lives outside it, the monk and the hermit included, so the question is never whether a Christian will stand in culture but how. Niebuhr sorted the church's answers into five types, and his fairness to each is the book's great virtue.

The first type is **Christ against culture**: loyalty to Christ requires a sharp break with the surrounding society. Niebuhr found it in the First Letter of John:

> Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him. (1 John 2:15, ESV)

His witnesses were Tertullian, the North African who around 200 asked what Athens had to do with Jerusalem, and Leo Tolstoy, who in *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894) rejected the state, the courts and the army as incompatible with the Sermon on the Mount. At its best this type sees what the others forget: every culture has its gods, and the early martyrs died because they would not burn a pinch of incense before the emperor's image. Niebuhr's objection was that no one can actually leave culture behind. Tertullian argued against the classical world in the polished Latin rhetoric it had taught him.

The second type, **the Christ of culture**, runs the opposite way. Jesus is received as the fulfillment of a society's best aspirations. Niebuhr traced it through the Gnostics and Peter Abelard to the culture-Protestants of nineteenth-century Germany, for whom Albrecht Ritschl was the leading theologian and the kingdom of God looked a good deal like a morally improved modern civilization. The steelman is real: the gospel does make itself at home in every language, and the historian Lamin Sanneh argued in *Translating the Message* (1989) that Christianity's translatability, with no sacred language of its own, is one of its defining marks. The danger is that the translation swallows the text. Niebuhr had diagnosed the result fourteen years earlier, in *The Kingdom of God in America* (1937), when he summed up the liberal Protestant gospel of his day as a God with no wrath leading people with no sin into a kingdom with no judgment, by way of a Christ with no cross.

The third type, **Christ above culture**, belongs to the synthesists. Culture is a good gift of the Creator but incomplete, and Christ perfects what reason and law can only begin. Niebuhr's representatives were Clement of Alexandria and above all Thomas Aquinas, whose principle that grace does not destroy nature but perfects it let the medieval church honor Aristotle and the natural law without making them revelation's equals. Its weakness, in Niebuhr's reading, was a tendency to freeze one synthesis into permanence, as if a thirteenth-century arrangement of church and society carried the authority of the gospel.

The fourth type, **Christ and culture in paradox**, is the dualist answer of Martin Luther. The Christian lives under two governments: God rules the world through law, sword and office, and the church through the gospel, and the two must not be confused. In *Temporal Authority* (1523) Luther defended the magistrate's God-given work while insisting that faith could not be enforced by it. Its strength is honesty about sin: no culture, a Christian one included, has escaped the fall. Its weakness is that it can leave the world's orders to run by their own logic, and historians still argue how far a distorted version of it helped German Protestants accept what happened after 1933.

The fifth type, **Christ the transformer of culture**, is the conversionist answer. Creation is good, the fall has perverted it without destroying it, and Christ converts human culture as he converts human beings, redirecting it toward God. Niebuhr found this type in the Gospel of John and in Augustine, heard strains of it in John Calvin, and saw it most clearly in the nineteenth-century Anglican F. D. Maurice. Its hope is its glory, and its temptation, the one we know best in America, is that "transforming culture" slides into managing it.

Niebuhr closed with a chapter titled after Kierkegaard, "A Concluding Unscientific Postscript," and declined to name a winner. Each believer and church, he argued, decides in faith, in a particular place and time, with partial knowledge. Many readers have noticed all the same that the types climb toward the fifth.

## What the map could not see

Niebuhr wrote at the high tide of mainline Protestant confidence. In 1951 the historic denominations were growing, and within a few years the nation would add "under God" to its pledge and "In God We Trust" to its paper money. A theologian in that season could easily imagine the church as the culture's trusted partner. His critics, from three quite different traditions, argued that the map carried the assumptions of that season inside its categories.

The sharpest was the Mennonite theologian John Howard Yoder's 1996 essay "How H. Richard Niebuhr Reasoned," in a volume he wrote with Glen Stassen and D. M. Yeager. It made three charges. The first was that Niebuhr treated culture as a single block, so that a Christian who refused one part of it, the sword for instance, could be filed under the type that rejected culture as a whole. But no Christian relates to culture wholesale. Every church accepts some things and refuses others, and the real question is the ground of the discrimination. The second charge was that the typology was stacked: the types formed a staircase, with the radicals at the bottom as the naive foil. The third went deepest. Niebuhr's scheme, Yoder argued, made the Jesus of the Gospels one authority to be balanced against others, so that any Christian who took the Sermon on the Mount as binding on public life looked like a sectarian. A hard note belongs beside his name: a discernment process of Mennonite Church USA, which concluded in 2015, commissioned research documenting decades of his sexual abuse of women and the church's failure to stop it, so the argument is weighed here on its logic and not on the man.

Seven years before Yoder's essay appeared, Stanley Hauerwas, deeply shaped by Yoder, and his Duke colleague William Willimon had taken a version of the argument to a wide audience in *Resident Aliens* (1989). They wrote that few books had done more than *Christ and Culture* to keep the church from seeing its situation, because the transformer type assumed a church near the levers of American power. That church had ended, they argued, and its end was a release. They distinguished the activist church, which tries to change society through politics, and the conversionist church, which changes individuals and leaves the social order alone, from the confessing church they preferred, whose first political task is to be a people whose common life makes the gospel visible, a colony in a foreign land. As Hauerwas has put it in several forms, the church does not so much have a social ethic as be one.

The third critique came from the Reformed evangelical D. A. Carson in *Christ and Culture Revisited* (2008), and it was biblical rather than sociological. Each of Niebuhr's types, Carson argued, is built on a favorite shelf of the canon. But the Bible is a single story, and its great turning points, creation and fall, the call of Israel and the giving of the law, the coming of Christ and the new covenant, and the final consummation, must all hold at once. Read that way, the types cannot be a menu of equal options. The Christ-of-culture type fails outright, since a Christ who merely confirms a society's ideals has no cross to carry. The others become partial insights held together by the whole story, so that a persecuted church may sound more like Tertullian and a church in a free society more like Augustine without either leaving the gospel.

The three critics disagree about a great deal, which makes their agreement worth noticing. Each concluded that the church cannot choose a posture toward "culture" as a single thing, and each, in his own way, pushed the question back from strategy to the church's faithfulness to the Christ of the whole Scripture.

## Three proposals for a church without the home field

Since the 1990s the argument has moved to a sharper question: what should the church do now that the culture no longer defers to it? Three books have shaped most American answers, and each deserves a hearing at full strength.

The sociologist James Davison Hunter began *To Change the World* (2010) with a demolition. Christians of nearly every stripe, he observed, assume that cultures change when enough hearts and minds change. Hunter called the theory naive. Cultures change through networks of elites and institutions at the center of cultural production, over long periods, and a movement can win millions of converts and leave the culture untouched. His account of the three political postures Christians have adopted is traced in the [culture war essay](/writing/why-the-church-lost-the-culture-war); what matters here is the shape of his alternative, faithful presence. It begins with God's own faithful presence to his people, pursuing them in love, and asks Christians to answer it in kind: faithful presence to one another, to the tasks in front of them, and within whatever sphere of influence they hold, seeking the flourishing of all of it as an act of love rather than a bid for control. Hunter frames it as a dialectic of affirmation and antithesis: the church affirms what is good in the culture and refuses what is idolatrous, and can do neither well while trying to rule. He drew the pattern from the exiles of Jeremiah 29.

Andy Crouch, in *Culture Making* (2008), came at the question from the side of the ordinary Christian. Culture, he proposed, is what human beings make of the world, in both senses: the things we make out of it and the sense we make of it. Condemning, critiquing, copying and consuming are fitting gestures on occasion, Crouch argued, but each has hardened into some generation's settled posture. The posture he commended was the one given in the garden: to cultivate what is good and create what does not yet exist. Culture changes when someone makes something that shifts what a people imagine is possible, a school, a hymn, a household with an open table. His hope reached to the end of the canon, where the redeemed city receives the treasures of the world, a theme Richard Mouw had traced in *When the Kings Come Marching In* (1983):

> They will bring into it the glory and the honor of the nations. (Revelation 21:26, ESV)

Rod Dreher's *The Benedict Option* (2017) proposed something starker. He took his title from the closing paragraph of Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981), where MacIntyre likened the modern West to the late Roman Empire and said we are waiting not for Godot but for another, very different Saint Benedict. Dreher read *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) as a sign that the flood had come, and counseled Christians to build arks: thick congregations, classical schools, intentional communities, and what the Czech dissident Václav Benda called a parallel polis. His case at its strongest is hard to dismiss. A church that has handed the formation of its children to schools, screens and markets cannot expect them to keep a faith they were never taught, and faithful minorities from the Jewish diaspora to Christians under Soviet rule survived by building exactly this kind of dense common life.

The critics answered with equal force. Many called it withdrawal under a monastic name, which Dreher denied; he meant, he said, a strengthening of the church's life for the sake of its witness, not flight to the hills. Others noted that the option favors families with money and mobility. Still others pointed out that the American Black church had lived for centuries as a minority inside a hostile order and never needed to discover the end of Christendom, because Christendom had never been theirs. Dreher's own later course sharpened the question. In 2022 he moved to Budapest, and he has written admiringly of Viktor Orbán's government, which presents itself as a defender of Christian civilization. Dreher sees no contradiction, since a state that shelters the church's institutions makes the work of building them easier. His critics see the book's argument reversed, a strategy for communities living under hostile power turning into hope in friendly power. The question reaches well beyond one writer, because churches of every stripe are tempted to trade their witness for a sympathetic government.

Set beside the two instincts the essay began with, these proposals reveal a pattern that cuts across our tribes. The instinct to fight wears the colors of the right. The instinct to adapt wears the colors of the left, and it is Niebuhr's Christ-of-culture type in its current form, a church that blesses whatever the educated culture already believes and calls the blessing prophetic. But the right has its own Christ-of-culture church, one that equates the gospel with the America of its grandparents, as [the essay on the flag in the sanctuary](/writing/the-flag-in-the-sanctuary) traces, and the left has its own culture warriors, who fight just as hard for the state's endorsement of their convictions. Both instincts spring from the same fear, that the church might become irrelevant. Fear of that kind will pay nearly any price for a seat at the table, whether the price is a political alliance or a softened creed. A church that knows who called it has less to protect. It can bear to look odd to its neighbors and let the oddness speak, since the oddness is usually what they notice first.

## Called out, and sent back in

Preachers often say that the New Testament word for church, *ekklesia*, means "the called-out ones," since it is built from *ek*, out, and *kaleō*, to call. The theology behind the point is sound, but the argument needs a caution: a word's meaning is set by its use, not its parts. James Barr's *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961) showed how often scholarship had gone wrong by reading theology into etymology, and D. A. Carson put the root fallacy first in his *Exegetical Fallacies* (1984). A first-century hearer of *ekklesia* heard "assembly," above all a Greek city's civic assembly. Luke uses it both for the riotous crowd in the theater at Ephesus and for that city's lawful assembly (Acts 19:32, 39, 41), and the Greek Old Testament often used it for *qahal*, Israel assembled before God.

The actual history is richer than the sermon illustration. The name carried two echoes at once, Israel before God and citizens gathered for public business, and neither was the vocabulary of a private club or cult. The church understood itself as a public, God's own assembly in the middle of every city, answering to a different Lord. It could never be simply against culture or of it.

The language of calling out does appear in the New Testament, only not in the word's etymology. It appears in the passage Peter wrote to scattered churches in Asia Minor, who were being slandered by their neighbors:

> But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light. (1 Peter 2:9, ESV)

The titles are Israel's, borrowed from God's words at Sinai in Exodus 19 and from Isaiah 43, and the next verse makes the point explicit:

> Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy. (1 Peter 2:10, ESV)

The calling out is from darkness, not from the city. Its result is a people, and its purpose is proclamation, which requires a hearer. Two verses later Peter tells these Christians to keep their conduct honorable among the pagans who slander them, so that their accusers may see their good deeds and glorify God (1 Peter 2:12). They are called out of one set of loyalties so that they can live in plain view of the people who still hold them. Called out and sent back in are not two strategies to choose between. They are one movement, the movement Niebuhr saw in Christ himself before he broke it into five types. Jesus prayed in that shape on the night before he died:

> I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one. (John 17:15, ESV)

Jeremiah had given the exiles in Babylon the same pattern centuries earlier, telling them to build houses, plant gardens, marry, and seek the good of the city that had carried them off:

> But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare. (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV)

The letter is read closely in [Are Christians Exiles?](/writing/exile-is-not-the-end). What matters here is how Daniel and his friends lived it at the Babylonian court, because they are Scripture's clearest refutation of treating culture as a single block. Daniel learned the language and literature of the Chaldeans, accepted a Babylonian name and served foreign kings for most of his life. Yet at the start he drew a line:

> But Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food, or with the wine that he drank. Therefore he asked the chief of the eunuchs to allow him not to defile himself. (Daniel 1:8, ESV)

Later his three friends drew another line in front of a furnace:

> But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up. (Daniel 3:18, ESV)

No single one of Niebuhr's types describes these men. They accepted Babylon's education, refused its table, served its king and defied his idol. They were discriminating, as Yoder said every faithful believer must be, and the ground of their discrimination was the first commandment. They knew what they worshiped, and so they knew where the lines fell. Paul wrote in the same key to the Christians in the capital of the empire:

> Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect. (Romans 12:2, ESV)

Notice who is transformed in that verse. The first thing Scripture asks to see changed is not the culture but the believer, whose renewed mind can then discern. Augustine wrote in *The City of God* that a brief and true definition of virtue is the right ordering of love, the *ordo amoris* (15.22). A church whose loves are rightly ordered will end up different from its neighbors, but the difference is a consequence, not a goal. A church that makes its own strangeness the point has only found a more pious way of talking about itself. The test of a community's difference is not how far it stands from its neighbors but whether the distance is spent on them.

## The shape of a people, and the cost of it

The relation of Christ and culture is not a first-order doctrine of the kind the creeds settle. Mennonite and Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed, Baptist and Methodist Christians have held different views of it in good faith, and they will go on doing so. This essay can only land where its best witnesses land, and on one point they converge. Niebuhr refused to crown a single type, Yoder argued that every faithful church discriminates within culture, and Carson argued that the whole canon holds the partial answers together. Hauerwas, Hunter, Crouch and even Dreher, who differ on almost everything else, agree that the church's first contribution to any culture is its own common life. Before it is a strategy, the church is a people, and the people is the argument.

That conclusion is less exciting than a campaign, and harder. It looks like a congregation that gathers to be formed rather than entertained, keeping covenant with people it did not choose. It looks like prayer that brings the actual state of the soul before God, Scripture read as the word of a living God rather than a source of values for a platform, and the Lord's Table, where the body broken and given stands at the center of the world instead of any nation or party. It looks like hospitality to people the culture has written off. None of this needs a cultural establishment. It formed the church in Pontus and Bithynia before any emperor was baptized, and it forms the church today where baptism can cost a person her job, her family or her life.

There is a serious objection here, and I can't fully answer it. Faithful presence can decay into comfortable invisibility, a church present everywhere and distinguishable nowhere, pleasant in its professions while the vulnerable go unprotected and the powerful unchallenged. The radicals at the bottom of Niebuhr's staircase would say that is what the transformer type becomes when it is tired, and they have history on their side. The church needs people who will say no at cost, as the Confessing Church did at Barmen in May 1934, when it rejected the claim that any area of life could belong to lords other than Jesus Christ. A theology of presence that never produces a Barmen has become a theology of accommodation, and the only guard against that drift is a people who still know the difference between the welfare of the city and the approval of the city.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a pastor of the Confessing Church who was hanged at Flossenbürg in April 1945, had already named the cost in *Discipleship* (1937). Cheap grace, as he described it, is the grace we grant ourselves: forgiveness preached without repentance, the Lord's Supper without confession, grace without discipleship and without the cross. He called it the church's deadly enemy. The church that fights for a nation and the church that echoes an educated class are both, at bottom, forms of cheap grace, a Christianity arranged to cost us nothing in the culture we happen to inhabit. I have preached that Christianity. Most of us who preach have, in one key or another.

The deepest answer to Niebuhr's question is not a type but a person. Jesus was born into a Jewish household under Roman rule, kept the festivals and taught in the images of Galilean farmers. And he was condemned by the two most admired institutions of his world, Roman law and the religion of the temple, acting together. The best culture humanity had built looked at the Son of God and chose the cross, which is why culture is never neutral ground, and neither is the church's own piety. God reversed that verdict at the empty tomb without a sword, a vote or a synthesis, and the news came first to frightened disciples who had just lost every contest the world knows how to count.

In John's vision the city of that risen Lord receives the glory and honor of the nations, cleansed of the idolatries that clung to them. That promise is why Christians can build and plant in cities that are not finally home, and refuse those cities' gods without despising their people. It also means the question this essay began with was never really about the culture. The mother, the deacon, the professional and the congregation are each deciding, week by week, what they love most and whether they love it enough to pay for it where it isn't rewarded. God's people have carried that decision through Babylon, Rome and Berlin, and nobody makes it once. It gets made on ordinary weekdays, whenever going along looks like plain good sense, and each time it is the decision the three young men made on the plain of Dura: to serve the city with their gifts and to bow to nothing in it but God. The furnace was real. So was the figure the king saw walking with them in the fire, and no posture toward culture has ever offered the church that company on easier terms.
