## Why the question won't go away

Ask a search engine whether Jesus really existed and you'll meet two kinds of confidence. One arrives in videos assuring you that Jesus is a legend stitched together from older dying-and-rising gods, and that scholars have quietly known it for a century. The other arrives from Christian sources promising that Jesus is better attested than any figure of the ancient world. Both voices sound certain, and both are wrong in ways that matter. The reader caught between them usually ends up worse off than before, holding either a doubt he can't examine or a certainty he can't defend.

The people who ask are rarely idle. Some are skeptics who suspect, with reason, that the church has a habit of hiding the soft places in its case. Some are Christians raised on the story who have just learned that historians argue about it and now wonder whether the whole thing was assembled later. The question sits underneath all the others. If there was no man, there was no cross, and what remains of Christian teaching is a moral philosophy with a mythology attached.

We in the church have earned part of the suspicion. We have overclaimed, treating a hard historical question as if a pamphlet could settle it. We have also underclaimed, preaching as though faith were a private feeling history can't touch, which quietly grants that our story floats free of the world. I came to faith from atheism, and for years I read Christian writing the way a wary buyer reads a contract, watching for the clause where a small promise is traded for a large one while your eyes are elsewhere. That wariness belongs in this subject.

Historians who have no use for Christianity agree, almost to a person, that Jesus of Nazareth lived, taught in Galilee, gathered followers and was crucified by the Roman governor of Judea. The reasons for that agreement are better than either side of the internet admits. But the Jesus that historical method can recover is a minimum, built from evidence open to any competent reader, and the church confesses a great deal more. The harder question is how the two relate, since the easy answers, that history proves the faith or that history and faith have nothing to say to each other, turn out to be two ways of keeping the man at a distance.

Whether he rose from the dead, and whether the New Testament was copied faithfully, are taken up in other essays on this site. What can a historian honestly say about Jesus, how did the discipline learn to say it, and where does its instrument stop?

## What Rome and Josephus actually said

Start with expectations, because most arguments about this go wrong before the first source is read. Jesus was a village teacher from Galilee, a backwater of a minor province, executed as one troublemaker among many. Even Pontius Pilate, who governed Judea from about 26 to 36, is known to us from a handful of writers, and until 1961 no inscription bearing his name had been found. That year archaeologists at Caesarea Maritima turned up a limestone block, reused in a later theater, that names him as prefect of Judea. Nobody concluded before 1961 that Pilate was a myth. Roman silence about a crucified Galilean is what the period would lead us to expect; the surprise is how much the outsiders say.

The fullest Roman witness is Tacitus, writing his *Annals* around 116. Describing the great fire of Rome in 64, he reports that Nero, needing someone to blame, fastened the guilt on a hated group the crowd called Christians. Tacitus explains the name for his readers: it came from Christus, who had been executed in the reign of Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate. The deadly superstition, as Tacitus calls it, was checked for a while and then broke out again, not only in Judea where the trouble began but in Rome itself (*Annals* 15.44). Tacitus despised the Christians, and the contempt in the passage marks it as his own. No Christian copyist would have called his own faith a deadly superstition. What the passage shows is that by the early second century a Roman senator with no sympathy for the movement took it as settled that its founder was a real man put to death in Judea under Pilate.

Josephus is more important, because he was a Jew of the land itself, a commander in the revolt against Rome that began in 66, and afterward a historian under Flavian patronage. His *Jewish Antiquities*, finished around 93 or 94, mentions Jesus twice. The shorter reference is the stronger one. In 20.200 Josephus describes how, in the year 62, the high priest Ananus seized the gap between the death of one Roman governor and the arrival of the next, convened a council, and had a man named James, whom Josephus identifies as the brother of Jesus "who was called Christ," stoned along with others. Almost all scholars accept this passage as Josephus's own. It is incidental, and "who was called Christ" is the phrase of an outsider identifying a man, not a believer confessing one.

The longer reference, at 18.63–64, is the famous *Testimonium Flavianum*, and here honesty requires care. In the form preserved in the Greek manuscripts, Josephus calls Jesus a wise man, adds that one might hesitate to call him a man at all, states flatly that he was the Messiah, and reports that he appeared alive on the third day. No Jew who remained outside the church, as Josephus did, would have written those lines. A few scholars defend the whole passage and some reject all of it, but most, John P. Meier among them in the first volume of *A Marginal Jew* (1991), hold that Josephus wrote a shorter, neutral notice that later Christian hands embellished. Two pieces of evidence support that middle view. Origen, writing in the third century, remarks that Josephus did not believe Jesus was the Christ, which is hard to square with a copy that said he was. And in 1971 Shlomo Pines published a tenth-century Arabic version preserved by the Christian historian Agapius, in which the claims are hedged, so that Jesus only may have been the Messiah. With the obvious additions removed, what remains describes a wise man and doer of startling deeds who won a following, whom Pilate condemned to the cross at the accusation of leading men, and whose followers did not abandon him. Even if the whole passage fell, 20.200 would still stand.

Two more Roman writers deserve weighing at their real value. Around 112 Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia and Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan asking how to handle the Christians brought before him (*Letters* 10.96). He had executed those who persisted and learned that the accused met before dawn on a fixed day, sang a hymn to Christ "as to a god," and bound themselves by oath not to steal, commit adultery or break their word. Pliny tells us nothing about Jesus's life. He tells us that within about eighty years of the crucifixion, communities far from Judea were worshiping Christ, and a Roman governor already knew them by his name. Suetonius, publishing his *Lives of the Caesars* around 121, reports that Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome because they kept rioting at the instigation of one Chrestus (*Claudius* 25.4). Many historians take this as a garbled reference to disputes over Christ within the Jewish community, the same expulsion that brought Aquila and Priscilla to Corinth (Acts 18:2). But Chrestus was a common slave name, and certainty is not available. Of the four outside witnesses, Suetonius is the weakest.

None of these writers was an eyewitness, and none tells us much. What they establish is the shape of the ancient argument. The pagan philosopher Celsus, whose attack from the 170s survives in Origen's reply, called Jesus a sorcerer and mocked the story of his birth. Nobody in antiquity, friend or enemy, is known to have argued that there had been no such man. And the earliest evidence of all remains Christian: the letters of Paul, written from around 50, some two decades after the crucifixion. Setting them aside because believers wrote them is not a historian's rule. Every ancient source has a point of view, Tacitus included. The question is always what a source knows and how it knows it.

## The case that Jesus never lived

The theory that Jesus never existed is a modern one. It surfaced among French radicals in the 1790s, was pressed in Germany by Bruno Bauer in the middle of the nineteenth century, and reached a wide audience with the philosopher Arthur Drews's *The Christ Myth* in 1909. It has never won the field, and in its present form it deserves a better hearing than Christians usually give it.

Its most capable advocate today is Richard Carrier, who holds a doctorate in ancient history from Columbia and set out his case in *On the Historicity of Jesus* (2014). Put in a form he would sign, the argument runs like this. Paul, our earliest writer, says remarkably little about an earthly Jesus. He never quotes a parable, never mentions a miracle, never names Galilee or Nazareth, and insists he received his gospel by revelation rather than from any man (Galatians 1:12). Carrier proposes that the first Christians believed in a heavenly Jesus, a divine being crucified and raised in a spiritual realm and known through visions and Scripture. The Gospels, beginning with Mark around 70, then told his story as an earthly allegory, and later believers mistook the allegory for biography. Carrier runs the evidence through Bayesian probability and concludes that the odds lean against a historical Jesus. The argument is disciplined, and it presses on real weaknesses. Our sources are late by modern standards, they were written by believers, and they fall silent exactly where a modern historian wishes they would speak.

The best reply came from someone with no stake in rescuing the faith. Bart Ehrman, an agnostic New Testament scholar at the University of North Carolina and a long-standing critic of evangelical views of the Bible, wrote *Did Jesus Exist?* (2012) to answer the mythicists directly. He calls the existence of Jesus one of the few things on which virtually every expert in the field agrees, and his reasons carry weight because he has every motive to find them weak.

The first reason is that Paul met Jesus's brother. Recalling his first visit to Jerusalem as a believer, Paul writes: "But I saw none of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:19, ESV). Carrier answers that "brother of the Lord" could be a title for any baptized Christian. But Paul calls believers brothers constantly without ever adding "of the Lord," and elsewhere he sets the Lord's brothers apart as a recognizable group: "Do we not have the right to take along a believing wife, as do the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord and Cephas?" (1 Corinthians 9:5, ESV). And Josephus, writing for a different audience entirely, independently calls James the brother of Jesus. It is hard to have a brother if you never had a body. The second reason is that Paul, for all his reticence, places Jesus in human history: "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4, ESV). He speaks of Jesus's descent from David, of a meal on the night he was betrayed, of his crucifixion and burial. The third reason is that the Gospels rest on several independent streams of tradition, several of them carrying the marks of Aramaic, the language of Palestinian Jews.

The fourth reason cuts deepest. Myths flatter their heroes, and this story humiliated its own. Jews who hoped for a Messiah expected a figure who would triumph over Israel's enemies, not one who would be stripped and nailed up by them. Paul knew exactly how the message sounded: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). A movement inventing a savior would hardly choose the most degrading death the empire had, and then date it under a named governor within the memory of people still alive. Nor does the old parallel with dying-and-rising gods hold. Jonathan Z. Smith, a historian of religion with no brief for Christianity, argued in 1987 that the category was largely a construction of modern scholars.

Consensus is not proof, and scholars have been wrong together before. But this consensus includes believers, Jews, agnostics and atheists, and it rests on evidence the mythicist has to explain away one piece at a time. The mythicist hypothesis is possible. It is not the best explanation. The honest skeptic's quarrel is not with whether Jesus lived. It is with who he was.

## From Reimarus to Schweitzer

That quarrel has a history, and the history teaches more than any single argument inside it. For most of the church's life nobody imagined the Jesus of the Gospels and the Jesus of history could come apart. The separation began in Hamburg. Hermann Samuel Reimarus, who taught oriental languages and died in 1768, left a manuscript he never dared to publish. Between 1774 and 1778 Gotthold Lessing published portions of it anonymously, the last of them blunt. Jesus, Reimarus argued, had been a Jewish political messiah who expected to set up an earthly kingdom and died disappointed; his disciples, unwilling to go back to their nets, stole the body and invented a spiritual redeemer. The theory of fraud did not survive scrutiny. The question did. Reimarus had driven a wedge between what Jesus intended and what the church taught about him, and every later quest has worked in the gap he opened.

David Friedrich Strauss widened it. In 1835, at twenty-seven, he published *The Life of Jesus Critically Examined*, and it cost him his academic career. He rejected both of the options then on offer. The supernaturalists took the miracle stories as straightforward reports. The rationalists kept the stories and explained the miracles away, so that Jesus walking on the sea became Jesus walking along the shore. Strauss argued that both misread the texts. The Gospels, he said, were largely myth in a technical sense: the first Christians, convinced Jesus was the Messiah, clothed his memory in the expectations of the Hebrew Scriptures, and the resulting stories expressed an idea rather than recorded events. George Eliot translated the book into English in 1846, and it unsettled readers well beyond the universities.

The rest of the century produced a shelf of liberal lives of Jesus, of which Ernest Renan's *Life of Jesus* (1863) was the most popular: a gentle Galilean moralist in an idyllic countryside, teaching a religion of the heart, remarkably free of anything strange. In 1892 Martin Kähler objected. The so-called historical Jesus of the scholars, he argued, was a figure assembled by modern imagination, and it concealed the living Christ rather than revealing him. The Gospels were never written as modern biography; in Kähler's well-known description, they are passion narratives with extended introductions. The real Christ, for Kähler, is the Christ the apostles preached.

Then, in 1906, Albert Schweitzer's *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* surveyed the whole enterprise from Reimarus onward and ended it, with a verdict that has never been overturned. Schweitzer showed that each age had found in Jesus its own ideals, and that each writer had drawn him according to his own character. The image most people remember from this critique, that the scholars had looked down a deep well and seen their own faces reflected at the bottom, is usually credited to Schweitzer, but it belongs to the Catholic modernist George Tyrrell, who used it in *Christianity at the Cross-Roads* (1909) about Adolf von Harnack's liberal Christ. Schweitzer's own Jesus was nobody's reflection. He was an apocalyptic prophet who expected the end of the world within his generation, threw himself on the wheel of history to turn it, and was crushed. That Jesus was strange, and Schweitzer insisted his strangeness was the point. He then left the faculty, trained as a physician and in 1913 sailed for Lambaréné in French Equatorial Africa to run a hospital. He did not treat the man he had studied as a subject to be finished at a desk.

## The second and third quests

After Schweitzer, Rudolf Bultmann, the most influential New Testament scholar of the century, largely gave up the search. In *Jesus and the Word* (1926) he judged that we can know almost nothing of Jesus's life and personality, since the sources were never written to supply a biography, and he came to hold that faith needs only the bare fact that Jesus came, not a portrait of what he was like. In October 1953 Ernst Käsemann, one of Bultmann's own students, stood before a gathering of his teacher's former pupils and argued that this would not do. A faith with no interest in the earthly Jesus, he warned, slides toward docetism, a Christ who is an idea rather than a man, and the Gospels themselves insist that the exalted Lord is the same one who was crucified. The "new quest" that followed leaned heavily on a strict test of dissimilarity.

The "third quest," a name N. T. Wright gave it in the 1980s, changed the terms by putting Jesus back inside the Judaism of his day, helped by the Dead Sea Scrolls and archaeology. E. P. Sanders's *Jesus and Judaism* (1985) began not with disputed sayings but with facts nearly everyone grants, above all the disturbance Jesus caused in the temple, and asked what kind of Jewish prophet would do such things. Wright's *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996) read Jesus as a prophet announcing the end of Israel's exile and the return of Israel's God to Zion, a return he enacted in his own person. John P. Meier's *A Marginal Jew*, begun in 1991 and running to five volumes by 2016, is the most patient of them. Meier imagined a Catholic, a Protestant, a Jew and an agnostic locked in a university library basement and not released until they had drafted a portrait all four could sign. That document is what he meant by the historical Jesus.

Beside these stood the Jesus Seminar, founded in 1985 by Robert Funk, with John Dominic Crossan among its leading figures. It deserves a fair description. Its fellows wanted to take critical scholarship out of the seminary and into public view, they were candid about their assumptions, and Funk framed the project as honest inquiry set against a fundamentalism he believed was doing real damage. They voted on the sayings of Jesus with colored beads, and in *The Five Gospels* (1993), which set the Gospel of Thomas beside the canonical four, they judged that about eighty-two percent of the words attributed to Jesus were not his. Their Jesus was a wise, subversive teacher who announced no coming end. Critics from many directions, including Luke Timothy Johnson in *The Real Jesus* (1996) and Dale Allison, objected that the Seminar dated Thomas early, stripped out the apocalyptic sayings in advance, and gave false precision to judgments no vote can settle. To its critics, the Seminar's Jesus looked a good deal like a progressive professor of the 1990s. Tyrrell's well had not gone dry.

It hasn't gone dry in our churches either. The Jesus we preach across much of American evangelicalism has looked suspiciously like a decent suburban man who shares our politics and our preferences. The right has its Jesus of national strength and the left its Jesus of pure affirmation, and each tribe is quick to see the reflection in the other's well. Everyone who looks for Jesus is tempted to find himself, and the only safeguard is to keep returning to the evidence that resists us.

## How historians sift the evidence, and what survives

To sort early material from later, the quests developed a set of tests. Multiple attestation holds that a saying or deed found in several independent sources is more likely early than one found in only one; Jesus's proclamation of the kingdom of God appears in Mark, in the sayings source, in Matthew's and Luke's own material and in John. Embarrassment holds that the church would not invent what it found awkward. Jesus submitting to John's baptism of repentance is the classic case, and Matthew's account shows the unease: "John would have prevented him, saying, 'I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?'" (Matthew 3:14, ESV). So is the admission that the Son did not know the hour of the end (Mark 13:32). Dissimilarity, formalized by Norman Perrin in 1967, accepts as secure only what could not have come from the Judaism of the day or from the later church.

The tests have real value, and they have been heavily criticized. Dissimilarity in its strict form produces a Jesus who was neither a Jew of his time nor the founder of anything, which is to say nobody. In 2012 Chris Keith and Anthony Le Donne edited a volume arguing that the whole toolkit assumed the form critics' picture of the Gospels as layers of later invention over an untouched core that could be peeled clean. No such core exists. Every report of Jesus, from the first, was already somebody's memory and interpretation of him. The more promising work now starts there. James D. G. Dunn's *Jesus Remembered* (2003) argued that what we can reach is the impact Jesus made on those who followed him. Dale Allison's *Constructing Jesus* (2010) argued that historians should trust the recurring patterns across the tradition more than any single anecdote, since memory keeps the gist better than the details, and that if the general impression is wrong we can know nothing at all. Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006) stressed the named witnesses still living when the traditions circulated. Memory bends light. It does not invent the lamp.

What survives this sifting is not small. Sanders drew up a list of facts he judged almost beyond dispute. Such lists vary a little from scholar to scholar, but most historians would sign a core like this one. Jesus was baptized by John: "In those days Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee and was baptized by John in the Jordan" (Mark 1:9, ESV). He proclaimed the kingdom of God: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ESV). He called disciples and spoke of twelve. He was known as a healer and exorcist, and even his opponents granted the deeds while disputing their source: "And the scribes who came down from Jerusalem were saying, 'He is possessed by Beelzebul,' and 'by the prince of demons he casts out the demons'" (Mark 3:22, ESV). A historian can say his contemporaries saw things they took for healings; whether God was acting is beyond what the method can weigh. He ate with people the religious avoided, and it scandalized them: "Why does he eat with tax collectors and sinners?" (Mark 2:16, ESV). He caused a disturbance in the temple. And he was crucified outside Jerusalem by Roman authority, under a charge nailed above him: "And the inscription of the charge against him read, 'The King of the Jews'" (Mark 15:26, ESV).

## The historian's Jesus and the whole Jesus

Meier drew the distinction this whole subject needs. The real Jesus, he argued, is the total reality of the man, every day he lived and every word he spoke, nearly all of it unrecorded. The historical Jesus is narrower: a modern construct, what can be recovered by methods any careful researcher can check. Kähler pressed from the other side. For him the Christ who matters is the one the apostles preached, and Luke Timothy Johnson argued in 1996 that the real Jesus is the living Lord the church knows, not a reconstruction behind the Gospels. Both warnings are needed, and neither should be allowed to set faith against history. A faith that needs historians to prove everything is hostage to the next monograph. A faith that needs nothing from history drifts, as Käsemann saw, toward a Christ who is only an idea.

Christianity chose exposure. Instead of a timeless myth, it confesses in the Apostles' Creed that the Son of God "suffered under Pontius Pilate," a phrase that fixes its center to a date and a name. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14, ESV). Flesh can be checked. Before a Roman governor and a Jewish king, Paul could say of the events he preached, "this has not been done in a corner" (Acts 26:26, ESV). The historian's minimum is where those claims touch the ground, and faith cannot contradict it without ceasing to be the faith of the creeds.

Luke tells of two disciples walking to Emmaus on the third day, and their account of Jesus is the historian's portrait almost word for word. They speak of "Jesus of Nazareth, a man who was a prophet mighty in deed and word before God and all the people" (Luke 24:19, ESV). They report "how our chief priests and rulers delivered him up to be condemned to death, and crucified him" (Luke 24:20, ESV). Every fact is right. And then comes the grief: "But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel" (Luke 24:21, ESV). They had the minimum, and it was accurate, and it could not tell them who was walking beside them. What they lacked was not better facts. It was the same facts read whole, with the Scriptures opened and the Lord himself at the table.

The fact historians are surest of is the crucifixion under Pilate, and it is the fact the church stands on: "For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Corinthians 2:2, ESV). The evidence walks the historian to the foot of the cross and stops. The confession begins at the same spot. Schweitzer, at the end of his great book, wrote that Jesus comes to us as one unknown, as he came to the men by the lake who did not know him, and speaks the same word, follow me, and that he makes himself known to those who obey in what they suffer and do. Schweitzer had exhausted the scholarship of a century and found that the man would not stay in the archive. He still won't. History can tell you, with more confidence than most people realize, that he lived. It cannot answer for you the question he asked on the road to Caesarea Philippi: "And he asked them, 'But who do you say that I am?' Peter answered him, 'You are the Christ'" (Mark 8:29, ESV). Every quest ends up standing where Peter stood. The answer is still ours to give, and there is no method that will give it for us.
