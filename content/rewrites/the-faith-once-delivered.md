Most Americans who say they have rejected Christianity have rejected something real: a faith that seemed to arrive with a voter guide, a worship band and a national anthem, that looked a great deal like the country it was preached in and changed whenever the country did. I rejected that one too. I came to Christianity from atheism, and the version I had dismissed was the only one I had ever met, recent, local and very sure of itself. I didn't know there was anything older underneath it. Many people leaving American churches, and many who never entered one, have heard a great deal about American Christianity and almost nothing about historic Christianity, and they reasonably assume the two are the same thing.

They aren't. Historic Christianity is the faith confessed in substantially the same words across two thousand years and every inhabited continent, summarized in the Apostles' Creed and set down at the councils of Nicaea in 325, Constantinople in 381 and Chalcedon in 451. It was settled in its essentials more than thirteen centuries before the Declaration of Independence, by bishops from Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor and North Africa who could not have imagined the continent where their words would one day be recited in English. The faith is older than America, and it doesn't need America.

That is a comfort, and also a correction that lands hardest on those of us who think of ourselves as the faith's defenders. American Christianity has a long habit of treating the creeds as optional, as somebody else's tradition, or as a fence the Bible never needed. "No creed but the Bible" is a slogan with a date and an address, and it is younger than the Constitution. This essay argues that the creeds are the church's confession of what Scripture says rather than a fence built around it, and that a Christianity which forgets them doesn't become more biblical. It becomes more American.

## Jude 3 in context: the faith once for all delivered

Historic Christianity's oldest name for itself comes from one of the shortest books in the Bible, whose author had meant to write a different letter. "Beloved, although I was very eager to write to you about our common salvation, I found it necessary to write appealing to you to contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (Jude 3, ESV). The next verse says why: "For certain people have crept in unnoticed who long ago were designated for this condemnation, ungodly people, who pervert the grace of our God into sensuality and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ" (Jude 4, ESV).

"The faith" here is not the act of trusting but the thing trusted, what later theologians called the faith which is believed as distinct from the faith by which one believes. Jude isn't urging his readers to feel more strongly. He is pointing to a body of content that can be kept or lost, and his words for it belong to the vocabulary of transmission. "Delivered" translates the verb Paul uses when he reminds the Corinthians of what he taught them first: "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4, ESV). "Once for all" translates *hapax*, the word Hebrews uses for the sacrifice of Christ: "he has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself" (Hebrews 9:26, ESV). The faith was delivered once because the Savior was given once. No generation revises it, because the events it confesses don't happen again.

The threat Jude names is moral as much as intellectual. The intruders are inside the church, turning grace into license and denying their Master by how they live, so contending for the faith can't be separated from holiness. And his instructions for how to contend include no coalition and no debating strategy. "But you, beloved, building yourselves up in your most holy faith and praying in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in the love of God, waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life. And have mercy on those who doubt" (Jude 20–22, ESV). The letter that tells the church to guard the deposit tells it, in the same breath, to be merciful to doubters. Many of us have used "contending for the faith" as permission for contempt, preachers in my own tradition among them. Jude gives no such permission.

Paul's last letter uses the same picture: "Follow the pattern of the sound words that you have heard from me, in the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus. By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit entrusted to you" (2 Timothy 1:13–14, ESV). The earliest church didn't assemble the faith from private reading. It received it, passed it on intact, and began summarizing it almost at once. The shortest summary may be the oldest: "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9, ESV). "Jesus is Lord" is two words in Greek, spoken aloud before witnesses, and many scholars think it was said at baptism. The church was saying a creed before it had a single council.

## The rule of faith: how the early church read its Bible

The generations after the apostles faced a problem that sounds modern: everyone had the same Scriptures, and not everyone agreed about what they meant. The gravest second-century challenge came from the teachers historians group under the name Gnosticism, who held that the material world was the work of a lesser god, that salvation meant escape from the body, and that Christ had only seemed to suffer. They kept the Bible and quoted it constantly, with real ingenuity.

Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, answered them in *Against Heresies*, written around 180. In his youth he had heard Polycarp of Smyrna, who by Irenaeus's account had known the apostle John. His best-known image describes what the Gnostics were doing with Scripture. Picture a beautiful portrait of a king made by a skilled artist out of precious stones, he says, and then picture someone prying the stones loose, rearranging them into a dog or a fox, and insisting that this was the king's portrait all along, since the jewels were genuine. The Gnostics had every stone and lacked the pattern.

Irenaeus called that pattern the rule of truth, received at baptism, and summarized it: one God the Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, made flesh for our salvation; and the Holy Spirit, who through the prophets announced Christ's birth, passion, resurrection, ascension and coming again in judgment. Then he made a claim that ought to interest any skeptic. The church, scattered through the whole world, keeps this faith as carefully as if it lived in one house, and the churches in Germany and Spain, among the Celts, in the East, in Egypt and in Libya believe and hand down nothing different. Tertullian of Carthage, writing around 200, made the same case in a lawyer's idiom. His *Prescription Against Heretics* borrows the Roman *praescriptio*, a plea that disposes of a case before its merits are argued: the Scriptures belong to the churches that received them from the apostles, and those who reject the rule of faith have no standing to argue from them. His summary of the rule closely matches Irenaeus's, one writing Greek in Gaul and the other Latin in Africa.

The rule of faith was a summary of the Bible rather than a supplement to it, the story Scripture itself tells from creation to judgment, named so that no verse could be pried loose and set into a fox. The earliest creeds answered the question of what Scripture says when it is read as one book, not the question of what to believe besides it.

A serious objection belongs here in its strongest form. In *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* (1934) Walter Bauer argued that in several regions the forms of the faith later branded heretical came first, and that orthodoxy was simply the version that won, with Rome's backing; Bart Ehrman carried the case to a wide audience in *Lost Christianities* (2003). Part of this is true. Early Christianity was diverse, and later writers sometimes tidied the story. But Bauer's reconstruction has drawn heavy criticism from historians, and the documents make a harder point. Irenaeus and Tertullian wrote more than a century before Constantine, when confessing Christ could cost a person his life. The rule they describe wasn't imposed by power. It was held against it. And its outline is the one Paul says he received and handed on in 1 Corinthians 15, a formula many scholars, skeptical and believing alike, date to within a few years of the crucifixion.

## Nicaea, Constantinople and Chalcedon: choosing words to say what Scripture says

Around 318 a presbyter in Alexandria named Arius began teaching that the Son of God, though exalted above every other creature, was himself a creature, the first and greatest thing God made. His party's slogan became notorious: there was when he was not. Arius was a biblical preacher, and he had texts. Wisdom says in Proverbs, "The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old" (Proverbs 8:22, ESV), and the Greek Bible the church used rendered the verb as "created." Colossians calls Christ "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15, ESV).

The council that met at Nicaea in 325, called by the emperor Constantine and attended by roughly three hundred bishops, faced the problem Irenaeus had described. Athanasius, a young deacon at the council and later bishop of Alexandria, recounted in *On the Decrees of the Council of Nicaea* (written in the 350s) that the bishops first tried to describe the Son in purely scriptural language, as being "from God," as God's power and image. Each time the Arian party agreed, because they could read every phrase in their own sense. So the council reached for a word the Bible doesn't use, *homoousios*, "of one being" with the Father, precisely to say what the Bible does say. The Son is "begotten, not made." The non-biblical word was the most faithful way to confess a biblical truth, because the biblical words alone had been bent into a fox.

Here the distinction this essay turns on comes into focus. A creed can be held as a fence or as a confession. A fence is built to keep people out, and its authority belongs to whoever built it; held that way, a creed lays a human boundary on top of Scripture and serves chiefly as a loyalty test. A confession says, in the church's own words, what the church hears Scripture saying. Its authority is borrowed entirely from the Scripture it confesses, so it can always be tested against the text, and it is spoken in worship before it is ever used in argument. A confession does draw a line, since to confess that Jesus is Lord is to deny that anyone else is, but the line follows from the confession rather than being its reason for existing. The Nicene Creed in its conciliar form begins "We believe," and it has been sung and prayed in the liturgy for most of its life.

The fence charge has its strongest ground at Nicaea itself. An emperor convened the council, and imperial power exiled Arius and the bishops who refused to sign. But the power soon swung the other way. Under Constantine's son Constantius, favor passed to bishops who wanted the Nicene language softened or dropped, and Athanasius, first banished under Constantine himself, was driven into exile five times in all. In 359 the twin councils of Ariminum (Rimini) in the West and Seleucia in the East were pressed into a vague formula that dropped the Nicene language, and Jerome, looking back on Ariminum in his *Dialogue Against the Luciferians*, wrote that the whole world groaned and was astonished to find itself Arian. The Nicene faith survived because churches kept confessing it when the empire wanted them to stop. At Constantinople in 381, drawing on the theology of the Cappadocian fathers, the church expanded the creed's brief line on the Holy Spirit into a confession of "the Lord, the giver of life," worshiped and glorified with the Father and the Son.

Chalcedon in 451 confessed how the one Christ is both God and man: one person in two natures, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." Those four negatives are the nearest the creeds come to a fence, and even they fence in a mystery rather than fencing out a people. They don't explain how the Word became flesh. They mark the edges beyond which the confession is lost, a Christ not truly God on one side and a Christ not truly human on the other. Chalcedon also divided the church: large parts of the churches of Egypt, Ethiopia, Armenia and Syria never accepted its language, though late twentieth-century dialogues between the Eastern Orthodox and the non-Chalcedonian churches concluded that much of the quarrel lay in terminology.

Around 434, between the councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon, Vincent of Lérins proposed a test in his *Commonitorium*: the catholic faith is *quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus creditum est*, what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all. It is a blunt instrument, and a common scholarly reading holds that Vincent had Augustine's teaching on grace and predestination in view, though he never names him. But Vincent also insisted that the faith grows the way a body grows from childhood to maturity, remaining the same body, with progress in understanding and no alteration of the faith. A deposit, on this view, is closer to a seed than to a museum piece.

## The Reformers went backward to go forward

Americans often remember the Reformation as the moment Christians threw off tradition for the Bible alone. The Reformers, accused of novelty, claimed the older ground. In the prefatory address to King Francis I that opened the 1536 *Institutes*, John Calvin argued that the fathers stood with the Reformers and that the real innovators were those who had buried the ancient faith under medieval accretions. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 begins its first article by affirming the decree of the Council of Nicaea. The Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) say in Article 8 that the Nicene, Athanasian and Apostles' creeds "ought thoroughly to be received and believed," and give a reason that states this essay's distinction exactly: "for they may be proved by most certain warrants of holy Scripture."

That clause is the Reformation's doctrine of the creeds in miniature. *Sola scriptura* meant that Scripture alone is the final and infallible authority; at Worms in 1521 Luther said councils could err. But the Reformers judged the councils by Scripture, found the first four faithful to it, and received the creeds as confessions of what the Bible teaches, never placed over it. Keith Mathison names the difference in *The Shape of Sola Scriptura* (2001): *sola scriptura*, Scripture as the only infallible rule, read within the church and alongside its teachers, is not *solo scriptura*, the individual alone with a Bible and answerable to no one. The magisterial Reformers held the first. Calvin quotes Augustine on page after page.

Not everyone grants them the fathers. John Henry Newman, who became a Catholic in 1845, wrote that year in *An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine* that "to be deep in history is to cease to be a Protestant." That argument is larger than this essay. What can't be disputed is how the Reformers understood themselves: their work was retrieval, clearing away what had grown over the ancient faith, and the creeds were the part of the inheritance they were most sure of.

## "No creed but the Bible": the American experiment

The break came later, and it came here. Nathan Hatch's *The Democratization of American Christianity* (1989) is the essential account. Hatch argues that between the Revolution and roughly 1830 populist movements, Methodists, Baptists, restorationists, Black churches and early Mormons among them, carried the democratic energy of the Revolution into religion, taking authority from learned clergy and inherited confessions and giving it to ordinary people and their own reading of the Bible. Hatch doesn't tell this as a simple fall, since the upheaval gave voice to people the old establishments had never bothered to reach. But it also taught Americans to trust the fresh reading over the old one and the individual's judgment over the church's memory.

The movement led by Thomas and Alexander Campbell, together with Barton Stone, made the principle its watchword: no creed but the Bible. The Campbell reformers spent years inside Baptist associations before the two movements parted around 1830, and Alexander Campbell's periodical, the *Christian Baptist*, attacked creeds as instruments of clerical control that divided Christians. It was a sincere hope for unity, and the movement itself later divided. The deeper irony is that "no creed" was never no tradition. The Campbells read the Bible with their age's confidence that plain facts plainly gathered would yield plain agreement, and that confidence became a creed nobody could examine because nobody admitted it existed.

Mark Noll's *America's God* (2002) traces the wider pattern. Noll argues that American Protestant theology between the 1730s and the Civil War fused evangelical faith, republican ideals and the commonsense moral reasoning of the Scottish Enlightenment, giving the ordinary reader enormous confidence in his own plain reading. It broke on slavery, when Northern and Southern Christians read the same Bible by the same method, reached opposite conclusions, and had no shared tradition of interpretation to appeal to. A church with no rule of faith beyond each reader's common sense had no way to tell the king from the fox.

The experiment has two living heirs who usually think of each other as enemies. One is a civil religion that treats Christianity as a national heritage, so that defending the faith and the nation blur into one cause. The other is a progressive Christianity that carries the old liberal slogan "deeds, not creeds" and treats Nicaea as optional furniture. Thomas Jefferson stands behind both. Around 1820 he took a razor to the Gospels and pasted together the sayings of Jesus he found reasonable, leaving out the miracles and ending before the resurrection. The nationalist and the progressive reach for different verses, but both assume that the living reader, or the living nation, holds the scissors. Both have made the faith smaller by making it answerable to us.

I say "us" because none of this is someone else's failure. I pastor a Baptist church, in a tradition that grew in exactly the soil Hatch describes, and the Christianity I rejected as an atheist was the kind that had forgotten it was ever anything else. A church that never says "begotten, not made" can easily leave its people unable to say why the Son of God is not the first and greatest creature, and to many modern ears Arius sounds perfectly reasonable. None of us set out to follow him. We simply stopped listening to the people who answered him.

## The Baptist objection, stated fairly

Written from inside a Baptist church, this essay owes the free-church suspicion of creeds the form its defenders would sign. Scripture alone binds the conscience, and no human document may stand between a believer and the Word of God. Jesus rebuked the religious leaders of his day on exactly this ground: "You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8, ESV). The Bereans were commended because they "received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (Acts 17:11, ESV), testing even an apostle's preaching against the text. Creeds are the work of fallible councils, and when they are enforced they become loyalty tests administered by the powerful, and the people who fail go to prison. Nor is the objection a quarrel with what Nicaea confessed, since Baptists have held the Trinity and the two natures of Christ from the start. It is a quarrel about who may require a confession, and about how faith comes to a person at all. A believers' church exists because faith can't be inherited or recited into anyone; each Christian must come to Christ and to the text personally, and a creed said in unison can hide how many in the room have never done so.

That last claim is Baptist memory. Thomas Helwys, who led the first Baptist congregation on English soil, published *A Short Declaration of the Mystery of Iniquity* in 1612, pleading that the king had no power over his subjects' souls; he was imprisoned in Newgate and is generally thought to have died there. John Bunyan, a nonconformist preacher whom Baptists have claimed ever since, spent twelve years in Bedford jail after 1660 for preaching without the established church's license. In 1651 Obadiah Holmes was publicly whipped in Boston for leading an unauthorized worship meeting. In 1908 E. Y. Mullins, president of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, gave the tradition a theological name in *The Axioms of Religion*, the competency of the soul in religion, each person's direct accountability to God. When Southern Baptists adopted their first convention-wide confession in 1925, its preamble called confessions guides in interpretation with no authority over the conscience.

Much of this is right. Religious liberty is one of the free churches' great gifts to the world, bought with blood, and a creed can harden into something recited and never understood. But fairly heard, the objection is aimed at the creed as fence: enforced by the state, placed over the conscience, set above Scripture. It doesn't touch the creed as confession, and Baptist history shows it. Baptists have always written confessions, from the London confessions of 1644 and 1689 to the New Hampshire Confession of 1833 and the Baptist Faith and Message. In 1678 General Baptists in England drew up a statement they called the *Orthodox Creed*, which affirmed the Apostles', Nicene and Athanasian creeds as things to be received and believed. D. H. Williams, a patristics scholar writing to free-church Protestants in *Retrieving the Tradition and Renewing Evangelicalism* (1999), argues that the ancient creeds and fathers are not a Catholic possession but the common foundation evangelicals need back, taking the suspicion seriously while refusing to let it become amnesia.

The question should be sorted by its order before it is argued. What Nicaea and Chalcedon confess, the Trinity and the full deity and humanity of Christ, is first-order, the confessional floor under every church. How a congregation uses the creeds is second-order: whether it recites them in worship, whether ministers subscribe to a confession, how much weight the councils carry beside the text. Faithful churches have answered differently and remained brothers. This essay lands on the distinction it has drawn. The Baptist conscience is right to refuse any creed that sits over Scripture, and wrong only when that refusal becomes forgetfulness of what the church has always confessed from Scripture. The strongest objection hasn't been fully answered here. If the rule of faith governs how the Bible is read, someone has to say whose rule and which councils. Protestants accept the first four councils and not Trent because the first four agree with Scripture, and a Catholic or Orthodox reader can fairly ask whether that reasoning runs in a circle. The question stays open.

## Older than the nation

In 1908, the year of Mullins's *Axioms*, G. K. Chesterton defended tradition in democratic terms in the fourth chapter of *Orthodoxy*. "Tradition means giving votes to the most obscure of all classes, our ancestors. It is the democracy of the dead. Tradition refuses to submit to the small and arrogant oligarchy of those who merely happen to be walking about." The line cuts against the nationalist, the progressive and the Baptist pastor alike, because all of us belong to the oligarchy of the living by default. The creed is how the dead get their vote.

Jaroslav Pelikan, the historian of *The Christian Tradition*, drew the line that keeps this from becoming nostalgia. In *The Vindication of Tradition* (1984) he wrote, "Tradition is the living faith of the dead; traditionalism is the dead faith of the living." The faith once delivered is the confession of people dead to us and alive to God, meant to be spoken again by the living in new languages and against new errors, the same body grown.

That confession was global long before it was Western. The kingdom of Aksum in Ethiopia had a Christian king in the fourth century, when most of northern Europe was still pagan. A stone monument raised in China in 781 records the arrival of a Christian missionary named Alopen at the Tang capital in 635. Augustine was an African. Philip Jenkins argued in *The Next Christendom* (2002) that the center of gravity of the Christian world has moved again, to Africa, Asia and Latin America. Neither the Christianity of Rome nor that of Christendom Europe was the thing itself, and neither is the Christianity of the United States.

For a post-Christian America, that is the news that matters. American Christians grieve lost cultural standing, and some of that grief is right. But much of the anxiety comes from having tied the faith to the nation, and anything tied to a nation borrows its lifespan. Rome thought it was eternal, and so did every Christian kingdom after it. The creed outlived all of them because it was never theirs. The skeptic who rejects American Christianity may be rejecting something that deserved it. What he may not have met is the faith Irenaeus said was kept in one house across the world, confessed this Sunday in Ge'ez and Mandarin and Spanish by people who have never heard of the arguments consuming us.

That faith rests on an event, not a heritage. Christ died for our sins, was buried, and was raised on the third day, and the church has been handing that on since Paul received it. If it is true, no election and no decline can touch it. If it is false, no nation can prop it up. Everything else in the creed hangs there, and the honest ways to meet it are to believe it or to reject it for what it actually says.

Jude closes his urgent letter without a strategy, in praise: "Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy, to the only God, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority, before all time and now and forever. Amen" (Jude 24–25, ESV). The people told to guard the faith are themselves kept by the God they confess, and *before all time and now and forever* is a longer span than any republic has been given. The church in America may grow smaller, and it may deserve to. The faith once delivered will still be spoken over some new believer rising out of the water, in a language none of us knows, long after our arguments are forgotten. What we are asked to do with it is harder than defending it and older than our country: to receive it whole, to confess it aloud, and to hand it on. Whether we ever believed it, or only believed in the nation we dressed it in, is the question it leaves with us.
