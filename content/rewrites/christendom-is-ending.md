## The arrangement that is ending

Many people who grew up in a small American town before about 1970 can remember when the stores closed on Sunday. Not only the Christian bookstore, but the hardware store, the bank, the diner and the car lot. In many states the law required it, and in 1961, in *McGowan v. Maryland*, the Supreme Court upheld Maryland's Sunday closing laws on the reasoning that, whatever their religious origin, they now served the secular purpose of a common day of rest. That ruling is a small window onto something enormous. A Christian practice had become so thoroughly the furniture of public life that the country's highest court could defend it by declaring it no longer religious. The stores are open now. The furniture has been carried out piece by piece, and most of us felt it go before we had a name for what was leaving.

The name is Christendom, and it means something more precise than "a lot of Christians." Christendom is a civilizational arrangement in which the Christian faith is the default setting of a society, underwritten by its laws, kept by its calendar, taught in its schools and assumed in its moral vocabulary, so that belonging to the culture and belonging to the church feel like a single act. Stuart Murray, in *Post-Christendom* (2004), describes it as the order in which the Christian story and the institutional church stood at the center of Western life, with the coercive power of the state standing behind them. Under Christendom you did not have to become a Christian. You were one, the way you were English or Danish or, later and in a softer and more complicated way, American.

That arrangement had a beginning in the fourth century, and it is ending in ours. The claim of this essay is that what is ending is Christendom and not Christianity, that the two were never the same thing, and that the church's confusion of them is sending it into the future grieving the wrong loss and reaching for the wrong remedies. Christianity is a confession: that Jesus of Nazareth, crucified under Pontius Pilate, was raised bodily from the dead and is Lord of all. Christendom is a settlement that confession once made with power. The confession is older than the settlement by three centuries and has never depended on it, though that is easier to see once you know how the settlement was built.

## How a hunted faith became the law of the empire

For nearly three hundred years the church had no legal standing. It met in houses, buried its dead in its own cemeteries, and refused the small acts of civic worship the empire asked of everyone. The last and worst persecution began under Diocletian in 303. Within a decade the world had turned over.

On October 28, 312, Constantine defeated his rival Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome. The sources disagree about what came before the battle. Lactantius, writing within a few years, says Constantine was told in a dream to mark a heavenly sign on his soldiers' shields. Eusebius, writing after the emperor's death in 337, says Constantine told him under oath of a cross of light seen in the sky with the promise that by this sign he would conquer. Whatever Constantine saw, he credited the Christian God with his victory. In 313 he and his fellow emperor Licinius agreed at Milan on a policy of toleration. What we call the Edict of Milan was neither an edict nor issued from Milan. It survives as a letter Licinius sent to provincial governors, preserved by Lactantius and Eusebius, granting Christians and everyone else freedom of worship and ordering confiscated church property returned. Galerius had already called off the persecution in 311; Milan went further and gave the church security and restitution. It made Christianity safe. It did not yet make it official.

That came under Theodosius. On February 27, 380, in a decree known by its opening words, *Cunctos populos*, Theodosius and his co-emperors commanded all the peoples under their rule to hold the faith the apostle Peter had delivered to the Romans, as taught by the bishops of Rome and Alexandria. Those who held it were to bear the name catholic Christians. The rest were branded heretics, subject to divine vengeance and to imperial punishment besides. In 391 and 392 Theodosius went further and prohibited pagan sacrifice. In less than eighty years a faith whose Lord had been executed by a Roman governor had become the religion Roman governors enforced.

It would be dishonest to tell this only as a fall. The same Theodosius, after his soldiers massacred thousands of citizens in the circus at Thessalonica in 390, was kept from communion by Ambrose of Milan until he did public penance, and he did it. An emperor knelt before a bishop because of blood he had shed. Nothing in the pagan order could have produced that scene. Christendom brought power under judgment even as it handed judgment over to power.

Eusebius, the church's first great historian, delivered an oration in 336 for the thirtieth year of Constantine's reign in which the Christian empire appears as an earthly image of the heavenly kingdom and the emperor as God's friend and deputy. Two generations later, after Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in 410 and pagans blamed the disaster on the abandonment of the old gods, Augustine began *The City of God* and gave a very different account. No earthly commonwealth, however baptized, is the city of God. The two cities, formed by two loves, are mixed together in history and will not be separated until the judgment. Augustine is the patron of every Christian who suspects that Christendom mistook itself for the kingdom. Yet Augustine also, in his long controversy with the Donatists, came to defend the state's coercion of schismatics, and he read the words of the parable in Luke 14, "compel people to come in," as a warrant for it. The sharpest critic of the imperial theology lived inside the arrangement and borrowed its sword. Nobody stood fully outside it, and that includes those of us who now write about it with sixteen centuries of hindsight.

Justinian, who reigned in Constantinople from 527 to 565, gave the arrangement its legal architecture. His Code, issued in 529 and revised in 534, opens with the law on the Trinity and the catholic faith, beginning from Theodosius's decree, so that orthodoxy stands as the first title of Roman law. In the preface to his Sixth Novel (535) he described the priesthood and the imperial authority as the two greatest gifts of God to humanity, flowing from one source and meant to work in harmony, an ideal the Byzantine world came to call *symphonia*. That word holds the whole dream of Christendom in miniature: church and throne as two voices in one song.

## One body, two swords, and the long unraveling

In the Latin West the dream took a different shape. In 494 Pope Gelasius I wrote to the emperor Anastasius that the world is chiefly ruled by two powers, the consecrated authority of bishops and the royal power of kings, and that the priestly authority is the weightier because priests must answer even for kings at the judgment of God. On Christmas Day in 800 Pope Leo III, who owed his restoration after a violent attack in Rome the year before to Charlemagne's protection, crowned Charlemagne emperor in Saint Peter's. Charlemagne's capitulary for conquered Saxony, issued in the 780s, made refusal of baptism punishable by death. In 1302 Boniface VIII's bull *Unam Sanctam* claimed that both swords, spiritual and temporal, belong to the church, the second to be wielded on the church's behalf by the hand of kings. Historians call this medieval ideal the *corpus christianum*: one Christian body, with pope and emperor, bishop and prince, as its differently ordered members. Baptism made you a member of the church and of the society in the same act, and the two memberships could not be pulled apart without tearing both.

It is a common mistake to think the Reformation ended this. It broke the body into pieces without dissolving its logic. After a generation of conflict in the German lands, the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 allowed each prince of the empire to choose Lutheranism or Catholicism for his territory, and his subjects were to conform or emigrate. Jurists later compressed the principle into a phrase, *cuius regio, eius religio*: whose realm, his religion. The phrase is often attached to the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, but the principle belongs to Augsburg. What Westphalia did, at the close of the Thirty Years' War, was amend it. It extended recognition to the Reformed churches, which Augsburg had left out, fixed 1624 as the normative year for the confessional status of each territory, and gave some protection to minorities for private worship. Westphalia exhausted the dream of a single Christendom, but it did not end the idea of a Christian territory. It multiplied it, giving each confession a Christendom of its own.

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), makes the unsettling argument that the eventual unraveling grew partly from within. His question is why, in the West around 1500, it was nearly impossible not to believe in God, while by 2000 belief had become one option among several, and for many not the easiest one to hold. Part of his answer is what he calls Reform: the long and repeated effort of Latin Christendom, from the medieval reform movements through both the Protestant and Catholic Reformations, to raise the whole of society to the standard of the devout, to discipline manners and make every Christian a serious one. That effort helped disenchant the world, and it formed a disciplined, self-contained, "buffered" self who could eventually imagine a moral order with no need of God at all. The machinery built to make everyone a Christian helped make it possible for anyone to be something else. Christendom was not only attacked from outside. It wore itself out trying to be what it claimed to be.

## The American variant: no established church, and a Christian nation

The United States never had a Christendom in the European sense, which is exactly why Americans so often miss that they had one. The First Amendment, ratified in 1791, forbade Congress to make any law respecting an establishment of religion, but it bound only the federal government. Several states kept their establishments. Connecticut ended its Congregational establishment in 1818, and Massachusetts, the last to go, in 1833.

The dissenters who paid for the colonial establishments deserve to have this part told honestly. In 1651 the Baptist Obadiah Holmes was publicly whipped in Boston after holding a Baptist meeting at Lynn. In 1773 Isaac Backus published *An Appeal to the Public for Religious Liberty* against the Massachusetts tax that supported the established churches, and in 1802 Thomas Jefferson answered the Danbury Baptist Association with his line about a "wall of separation between Church & State." The dissenters helped win disestablishment. Then, over the next century and a half, many of their heirs settled comfortably into the one establishment disestablishment had never touched, and churches whose founders had been whipped for dissent learned to enjoy the deference of the town.

What disestablishment removed was the legal church. What it left in place, and in some ways strengthened, was a cultural establishment: a broadly Protestant consensus that set the moral tone of public life without holding any legal title. Alexis de Tocqueville saw it in the first volume of *Democracy in America* (1835), observing that religion in America took no direct part in government and yet had to be counted the first of the country's political institutions. The common school read from the King James Bible. Sunday laws kept the week. In 1892, in *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, Justice David Brewer could write for the Supreme Court that "this is a Christian nation." Robert Handy, in *A Christian America* (1971), traced how Protestants built this informal establishment through voluntary societies, schools and reform movements, and how it came apart in what he called a second disestablishment in the 1920s and 1930s, as immigration, the cities and the modernist controversies broke the Protestant consensus.

The Cold War brought a late revival of its public forms. "Under God" entered the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954, and "In God We Trust" became the national motto in 1956. Then the Supreme Court ended state-composed prayer in public schools in *Engel v. Vitale* (1962) and devotional Bible reading in *Abington School District v. Schempp* (1963). Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon, in *Resident Aliens* (1989), argue that sometime between 1960 and 1980 an old and inadequately conceived world came to an end. The stores and theaters that opened on Sunday in those years did not defeat the church. They stopped deferring to it, and the church learned how much of its hold had been deference.

Taylor has a term for what that world was. He calls it neo-Durkheimian: an arrangement in which religious belonging is fused with national belonging, so that being a good citizen and being a churchgoer feel like one identity, with no established church required. That is the American form of Christendom, and its passing explains why the loss feels so personal here. Europeans watched an institution decline. Many Americans feel they are losing a country.

## What Christendom gave, and the case for bringing it back

Honesty requires beginning with the goods. Around 370 Basil of Caesarea built outside his city a complex for the sick, the poor and travelers that historians often count among the first hospitals. Tom Holland's *Dominion* (2019), written as a historian rather than an apologist, argues that the assumptions the modern West treats as self-evident (that the weak have a claim on the strong, that every human life carries equal dignity, even the secular habit of separating religion from everything else) are not the natural inheritance of humanity. They are the long sediment of Christian teaching, laid down over the centuries of Christendom until even its critics hold them without knowing the source. The atheist who protests cruelty to the powerless is, on Holland's reading, arguing in a Christian accent.

Oliver O'Donovan, in *The Desire of the Nations* (1996), offers a more theological defense. Christendom, he argues, was not simply the church grasping at power. It was the response of rulers who had heard the gospel and bowed before Christ, a political order that took the lordship of Christ to be true of public life and not only of private hearts. To repudiate it wholesale, on this reading, is to say that rulers ought never to have believed.

A sharper form of the defense has returned in the last decade under the name integralism, mostly among Catholic writers, with Protestant cousins in the revived case for a Christian nation. Put in the form its defenders would sign, it runs like this. No state is neutral. Every government teaches some account of the good through its laws, and the liberal state that claims to hold no religion simply holds its own, one that has proved hostile to the family, the unborn and the weak. Leo XIII taught in *Immortale Dei* (1885) that states have a duty to honor God and to favor the true religion. If the moral inheritance Holland describes is living on borrowed capital, the integralist asks, why not repay the loan? Why should Christians accept a public order designed to keep Christ out of it? Israel, after all, received a law that ordered worship and common life together.

That argument deserves an answer and not a sneer, and the answer comes in three parts. The first is historical. Christendom's fiercest critic was not a secularist but a Christian. Søren Kierkegaard's pamphlets and newspaper articles of 1854 and 1855, gathered in English as *Attack upon "Christendom"*, were aimed at the Danish state church. He was provoked when Hans Lassen Martensen, soon to succeed him as bishop, eulogized the late Bishop Mynster as a "witness to the truth," and he kept up the attack from December 1854 until his death in November 1855. His charge was that where everyone is a Christian by birth and by census, Christianity has quietly ceased to exist, because no one has been confronted by Christ as a decision. Christendom sheltered the faith and emptied it in the same motion. Alongside its saints it raised people who had been baptized, confirmed, married and buried by the church without ever being asked to follow its Lord, and their grandchildren are the ones now walking away without a backward look. To restore the arrangement would restore the conditions of that emptiness.

The second is biblical. Israel was a nation with a law, but the new covenant people is not its successor state. Peter wrote to scattered congregations across five provinces of Asia Minor and gave them Israel's own titles: "But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9, ESV). The holy nation of the New Testament has no territory, no army and no capital, and it is gathered from every tribe and tongue. The magistrate bears the sword for justice (Romans 13:4), and Christians rightly argue for just laws, but nowhere does the apostolic church ask the sword to produce belief. Jesus was offered the arrangement directly. The devil "showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. And he said to him, 'All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me'" (Matthew 4:8-9, ESV). He refused the kingdoms on those terms, and before Pilate he said why: "My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would have been fighting, that I might not be delivered over to the Jews. But my kingdom is not from the world" (John 18:36, ESV). Christendom was, in large part, the long effort to have the servants fight after all.

The third is the church's own later judgment. At the Second Vatican Council, in *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965), the Catholic Church taught that religious freedom is grounded in the dignity of the human person and that no one may be forced to act against conscience in matters of religion. Integralists argue that this can be reconciled with Leo XIII. Perhaps it can. But the council registered something the whole church had learned at terrible cost: a faith that must be enforced to be held is not being held the way Christ asks to be held. The goods Holland describes were the fruit of the confession working its way through a people, not of the sword that stood guard over it. The hospitals were built by people who believed, and they will be built again only by people who believe.

## Two ways to keep a seat at the table

As Christendom ends, the American church has mostly reached for one of two responses, and they look like opposites.

The first is Christian nationalism: the conviction that America was founded as a Christian nation and ought to be governed as one. Its most careful defenders would put it this way. Every people's laws express some shared account of the good, and a nation that pretends otherwise has only hidden its creed. The founding generation assumed a Protestant moral consensus, as Tocqueville saw and Justice Brewer wrote. A Christian majority acting through ordinary democratic means has as much right to shape the law by its convictions as any other majority does, and a nation that forgets the God who gave it its liberties will not keep them long. It grows from real goods, love of country, grief over moral collapse, fear for children, and it is right that the public square is never neutral. But it treats the nation as the unit of God's covenant, a role Scripture gives only to Israel and then to the church, and it reaches for the state to rebuild by law a consensus that no longer lives in hearts. It confuses the cultural establishment of 1955 with the kingdom of God. That is not only a political mistake. It is idolatry, the old *symphonia* sung with a flag in the choir.

The second is accommodation: the conviction that the church keeps faith with a changing culture by reinterpreting its teaching in the culture's terms. Its most careful defenders would put it this way. The church has always translated the gospel, from Aramaic into Greek and from Hebrew categories into the language of Nicaea, and translation is not betrayal. The church has also been wrong before and been corrected by consciences outside it, as it was over slavery, so the moral objections of the age may be one of the ways God is speaking. What is being shed, on this view, is not the faith but Christendom's accretions, and a church that insists on its old formulas will be heard by no one. It too grows from real goods, shame over the church's cruelties, compassion for the people it wounded, a longing to be heard. And it is right that the gospel must be translated; Lesslie Newbigin, a missionary in India for decades, knew that better than anyone. But translation carries the offense across into the new language, and accommodation leaves it behind. In practice it has softened the resurrection into metaphor, the atonement into example and the moral law into preference, and those are not Christendom's accretions. They are the confession. It confuses the approval of the educated public with the kingdom of God, and it pays for that approval with the confession itself. Newbigin described the shape of the problem in *Foolishness to the Greeks* (1986). Modern Western culture, he argued, divides the world into public facts, which everyone must accept, and private values, which each person may choose, and it files the gospel on the second shelf. Accommodation accepts the filing and asks only to be a well-liked value. Newbigin refused both options on offer. He would not have the church try to recover its old establishment, and he would not let it retreat into private piety. He called for a missionary encounter, the gospel addressed to the culture's own assumptions as public truth, by a church that no longer controlled the culture and no longer needed to.

Hold the two responses under the same light and they share a skeleton. Both assume the church must stand at the center of the culture to be faithful. Both measure the church by its standing. The nationalist would keep the seat at the head of the table by force, and the accommodationist by concession. That is why the accommodationist temptation is also a kind of idolatry, quieter and better mannered, with the culture's approval as the god to whom the confession is offered. Hauerwas and Willimon made the same point about the religious right and the liberal mainline of their own day: each wanted to serve as chaplain to America, and they disagreed chiefly about which America. Neither could picture a church that did not need the chaplaincy. Both are Christendom's habits outliving Christendom.

These habits are not somebody else's. I have stood in my own pulpit and preached to a town of the imagination, one that still knew the story and nodded along to the basics, when the actual town outside my door no longer knew it at all. The first thing the end of Christendom asks of us is to notice which of the two instincts we reach for when we are afraid, and to confess it.

## What comes after

There is a cheap hope that turns the end of Christendom into good news too quickly, and it should be refused. A great deal of good is being lost: a moral language most neighbors could still speak, a week with a day of rest built into it, a public memory of the story, institutions of mercy that were built by faith and may not outlive it. Grief is fitting. What isn't fitting is to grieve the seating as though it were the supper.

Among the shifts Murray says post-Christendom requires are these: from the center to the margins, from majority to minority, from settlers to sojourners, from privilege to plurality, from control to witness. Notice how much of that list describes a loss of standing and how little of it describes a loss of truth. The church is being moved from the head of the table to the edge of the room, but the gospel it carries to the edge is the same gospel it carried at the head. Paul wrote to Philippi, a Roman colony whose people prized their citizenship in Rome, "But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 3:20, ESV). He did not tell them to stop being good neighbors in Philippi. He told them where their allegiance was kept.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw the question earlier than most. Writing from Tegel prison in the spring of 1944, he asked who Christ really is for us today, and he began to sketch what he called a religionless Christianity. He paired the phrase with a hidden discipline of prayer and sacrament, so it was never a faith without worship, and in the outline for a book he never finished he wrote, in substance, that the church is the church only when it exists for others. What he was reaching for was a church that no longer relied on what he called the religious a priori, the assumption that human beings are religious by nature and will turn to God at the edges of life when their own resources fail, and that instead lived for its neighbors as Christ did, in a world that owed it nothing. He was hanged at Flossenbürg on April 9, 1945, and never saw what he had described.

Daniel's vision gave the empires of the world a statue of gold, silver, bronze and iron, and then this: "a stone was cut out by no human hand, and it struck the image on its feet of iron and clay, and broke them in pieces" (Daniel 2:34, ESV). The God of heaven, Daniel told the king, "will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed" (Daniel 2:44, ESV). The stone isn't the next metal in the sequence. Christendom, for all its grandeur and all its genuine mercies, was the long attempt to make the stone behave like the statue, to give the kingdom no hand had made the machinery of the kingdoms hands had made. It worked for a while. Statues always work for a while. Jesus described the growth of his kingdom in other terms: "The kingdom of heaven is like leaven that a woman took and hid in three measures of flour, till it was all leavened" (Matthew 13:33, ESV). Leaven never rules the loaf. It changes it from inside.

So what comes after Christendom is not a strategy to recover the center. It is a posture, and it begins with repentance before it asks anything else: for trusting the scaffolding more than the building, for mistaking a people's habits for a people's faith, for wanting the seat more than the Lord of the table. The church that comes through will be one that can be told apart from its surroundings, that persuades rather than presumes, that keeps the confession whole when it costs, and that seeks the good of its city without needing to run it. None of that is new. It is how the church lived before 312.

The last book of the Bible gives heaven a sentence to sing: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (Revelation 11:15, ESV). Christendom tried to say that sentence in the present tense, by statute, and the attempt raised cathedrals and lit pyres. The New Testament keeps it in heaven's mouth, as the verdict God will speak at the end, secured not by an emperor's victory at a bridge but by a crucified man walking out of a borrowed tomb. The church does not have to make it true. It has to be the kind of people who are still confessing it when nothing around them agrees. The West will not be Christian by default again. What remains is the harder and older question, the one the church faced in Corinth and Rome before any emperor knew its name: whether anyone watching us will be able to tell, from how we live, that we believe the sentence at all.
