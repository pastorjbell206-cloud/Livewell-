There is a man in many congregations who is doing fine by every measure the church knows how to take. He sits near the back and shakes hands at the door. He served, and on Veterans Day the pastor asks him to stand, and he stands. What no one in the building knows is that he hasn't slept through a night in years, and that the thing keeping him awake is not the memory of being afraid. It's the memory of something he did, or something he watched and didn't stop, or an order he obeyed from someone he trusted who turned out not to deserve it. He has a name for the fear, because the doctors at the VA gave him one. He has no name for the other thing, and he's fairly sure the church doesn't want to hear it.

Before anything else, a plain word to anyone reading this from inside that night. This essay is written by a pastor, not a clinician, and it is not medical or mental-health advice. If you are a veteran or service member and you are thinking about ending your life, or you are afraid you might, call the Veterans Crisis Line: dial 988 and then press 1, text 838255, or chat online at VeteransCrisisLine.net. A trained responder answers at any hour, many of them veterans themselves, and you don't have to be enrolled in VA care to reach them. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. If you love someone who served and you are worried about him, you can call the same line on his behalf. Anyone else in crisis can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

The word "trauma" has become a general-purpose container, stretched across so many kinds of hardship that it sometimes stops telling us anything. But over the last three decades clinicians who work with veterans have labored to name a specific wound that the trauma vocabulary kept missing, and they have called it moral injury. The distinction matters for medicine. It matters even more for the church, because the wound it names is a wound of conscience, and conscience is the oldest ground the church was given to tend. What a civilian congregation owes the veterans already sitting in it is its attention before its explanations. We have mostly given them the reverse.

## Where the term came from: Achilles in Vietnam

Every generation of war has had to find a word for what came home with its soldiers, and the words tell you where each age thought the damage lived. The Civil War spoke of soldier's heart, as though the injury were cardiac. The First World War spoke of shell shock, as though the concussion of artillery had bruised the nerves. The Second spoke of combat fatigue, as though a man had simply been used up. Each name located the wound in the body, and each captured something real. What none of them quite reached was the possibility that a man could come home with his nerves intact and his conscience broken.

The modern clinical name arrived with Vietnam, and it arrived because veterans insisted on it. In the early 1970s the psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton sat with groups of veterans who had turned against the war, and in *Home from the War* (1973) he described men who carried not only terror but a corrosive sense of having been made participants in atrocity. After years of pressure from veterans and their advocates, in 1980 the American Psychiatric Association added post-traumatic stress disorder to the third edition of its diagnostic manual. That was a genuine mercy. For the first time a man's suffering after war was named as a response to something that had happened to him rather than a weakness of character he had brought with him.

Yet the diagnosis came to be built around threat. The first criteria did mention guilt about surviving, but through the revisions that followed, its entry point was defined as an event that threatened death or serious injury, and its symptoms clustered around fear: the memory that intrudes, the body that stays on alert, the avoidance of anything that recalls the danger. For many veterans that described the wound precisely. For others it described the least of it.

One of the people who noticed was Jonathan Shay, a psychiatrist who spent years with Vietnam veterans at a VA clinic in Boston. In *Achilles in Vietnam* (1994) he set the stories his patients told beside Homer's *Iliad* and found that the oldest war poem in the West had already described their injury. The *Iliad* opens not with a battle but with a betrayal. Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army, seizes from Achilles the prize of honor the army had awarded him, and Achilles' rage at that violation drives everything that follows. Shay argued that what undid many of the men he treated was the same kind of thing: a betrayal of what is right, committed or permitted by someone who held legitimate authority, in a situation where the stakes were life and death. Shay borrowed the Greek word *themis* for that shared sense of what is right, and he called its violation moral injury. When it went deep enough, he argued, it did not merely wound a man. It undid his character: it shrank the world he could trust to a few comrades or to no one, and it left him braced for betrayal everywhere.

Fifteen years later a team of researchers led by Brett Litz widened the lens. Their 2009 paper in *Clinical Psychology Review*, "Moral Injury and Moral Repair in War Veterans," defined moral injury as the lasting harm that can follow when a person perpetrates, fails to prevent, bears witness to, or learns about acts that transgress his own deeply held moral beliefs and expectations. Where Shay had looked chiefly at betrayal from above, Litz and his colleagues included what the soldier himself had done. They described the wound as carried less by fear than by guilt, shame and self-condemnation, and by the withdrawal that follows when a person concludes that he no longer belongs among decent people.

The two definitions are not rivals. Together they name the two directions from which conscience can be wounded, from what was done to a person by those he trusted and from what a person did or allowed himself. Most veterans who carry this carry some of both.

## How moral injury differs from PTSD

The simplest way to hold the difference is to ask what the wound is about. Post-traumatic stress is, at its core, about danger. The mind and body learned in a terrible moment that the world could kill them, and they keep defending against a threat that has passed. Moral injury is about wrong. The person learned in a terrible moment something about what he was capable of, or what the people over him were capable of, and he cannot unlearn it. The first is a wound to the sense of safety. The second is a wound to the sense of goodness, his own or the world's.

The two often travel together, and the line between them is not clean. When the American Psychiatric Association revised its manual in 2013, it added to the picture of PTSD persistent guilt and shame and a distorted sense of blame, a quiet admission that the fear-centered account had narrowed too far. But moral injury is not a diagnosis in that manual, and many of the clinicians who use the term would resist making it one. A disorder is something to be reduced. A conscience troubled by a real wrong is not malfunctioning. It may be the healthiest thing left in the man. To treat his guilt only as a symptom is to tell him, however kindly, that the moral facts he is responding to are not really there.

That is the argument Warren Kinghorn, a psychiatrist and theologian at Duke, pressed in a 2012 article in the *Journal of the Society of Christian Ethics*. Kinghorn welcomed the clinical recognition of moral injury but argued that the mental-health professions, which work hard to stay neutral about what a human life is for, lack the vocabulary to say what has actually been injured. They can describe the distress. They cannot say whether the man ought to feel it, what he owes, or what would count as being made whole, because those are moral and finally theological questions. He concluded that the wound also needs communities that still hold a rich account of the human good and the practices that go with it.

This is where the church has to be careful in both directions. It must not pretend that a veteran's sleeplessness, rage, drinking or despair are merely spiritual problems awaiting a spiritual fix. Moral injury keeps company with depression, addiction and thoughts of suicide, and those need the skilled care of physicians and therapists. A congregation that receives a veteran well will know which counselors in town work with combat trauma and will offer to drive him to the appointment. Confession and treatment are not competitors. A man may need both, and many do. The essay on [depression, faith and the church](/writing/mental-health-and-the-church-beyond-pray-about-it) makes that case.

But the church also must not hand the whole wound to the clinic and consider its part done, because some of what the veteran carries is exactly what the church was made to address. Here a distinction helps more than any other, because moral injury is not one thing. Some of it is guilt for a real wrong: something the man chose that he should not have chosen, and he knows it. Some of it is the wound of betrayal, Shay's case, where the wrong was done by those above him and he was made its instrument or its victim. And some of it is the grief of tragedy: a split-second decision in the dark that was no sin by any fair reckoning, an outcome no one could have prevented. From inside, all three can feel identical. The man simply knows he is stained. But they call for different responses. Real guilt needs confession and forgiveness. Betrayal needs truth-telling and some measure of justice. Tragedy needs lament, and the patient refusal to let him convict himself of a sin he did not commit. A church that offers absolution for what was tragedy will confirm his worst reading of himself, and a church that offers only reassurance for what was guilt will leave him alone with it. The first work of pastoral care is to help him tell the three apart, and that work can only be done by listening.

Rita Nakashima Brock and Gabriella Lettini, in *Soul Repair* (2012), argue that moral injury is not a disorder of the individual but the reasonable response of a conscience that has been made to act against itself, and that recovery happens slowly, in relationships of trust where a person can reflect honestly on what he did and what was done to him. Their sharpest point is aimed at the rest of us. A society that sends people to fight in its name, and then treats their moral anguish as a private medical matter, has found a way to keep its own hands clean. The veteran is left holding what the nation authorized.

## What the ancient church already knew

The idea that killing can wound the one who kills, even when the killing was permitted, is not a modern discovery. When Israel's soldiers returned from the war against Midian, Moses did not send them straight back into the camp. Numbers 31 tells them to stay outside it for seven days, and every man who had killed or touched the slain had to be purified before he came home. A warrior could not walk from the battlefield into the life of the community as though nothing had happened to him. There was a threshold, and it had to be crossed with care.

The sharpest case is David's. He was the king God chose, and his wars were Israel's wars, fought under a covenant that the Old Testament itself treats as legitimate. Yet when he wanted to build a house for the Lord, he was refused, and he later told his son why:

> "But the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 'You have shed much blood and have waged great wars. You shall not build a house to my name, because you have shed so much blood before me on the earth.'" (1 Chronicles 22:8)

The text does not call those wars murder. It does not ask David to repent of them the way he had to repent of Uriah. It simply says that blood shed even in God's service left a mark that barred him from one holy task. It is close to the heart of what veterans try to explain when they say that what they did was lawful and still left something on them.

The early church kept the instinct. The Christians of the first three centuries were deeply reluctant about bloodshed, though scholars continue to argue over how thoroughly pacifist they were. Even after Christians served in the army as a matter of course, Basil of Caesarea, writing about 374 in the letter that became canon 13 of his rules, observed that the fathers before him had not counted killing in war as murder. Yet he advised, as counsel rather than law, that soldiers whose hands were not clean refrain from communion for three years. The essay on [Christians and gun violence](/writing/church-gun-violence) takes up that history; what matters here is what Basil assumed. The killing was no crime, and yet it left a wound, and the church should make room for its healing rather than pretend it wasn't there.

Augustine, whom the West credits with the just war tradition, held the same double view. In *The City of God*, finished in 426, he granted that the wise man will wage just wars, and then insisted that if he remembers he is human he will lament the necessity that forces them on him. The man who can reflect on war's evils without grief, Augustine thought, had lost something of his humanity. The origin of the just war tradition is not triumph. It is mourning.

The medieval church sometimes made that mourning concrete to the point of arithmetic. After the Norman conquest of England, a penitential ordinance issued around 1067 to 1070 under the papal legate Ermenfrid of Sion required a year of penance for each man a soldier knew he had killed at Hastings, in a campaign William had fought claiming the pope's blessing. The bookkeeping looks strange to us, but it refused to let the church's approval of the war erase the soldier's need to be reconciled after it.

Here the steelman runs the other way, and it deserves to be heard. Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* around 1271, set out the conditions of a just war: rightful authority, a just cause, and right intention. Those who stand in that tradition argue that a soldier who fights in a war that meets those conditions, and fights it rightly, has not sinned, and that to impose penance on him is to confuse him about his own conscience. Many Christians who have served, and many who love them, hear talk of moral injury as an insult to honorable service, an implication that every soldier is a penitent in waiting. They are right that it must not become that. A church that treats every veteran as a man who needs forgiving has made the same mistake as the church that treats every veteran as a hero: it has written his story before he has spoken. Whether faithfulness means the just war tradition or the refusal of violence is a second-order question serious believers have answered both ways since the early centuries, and this essay leaves it open. What both sides confess, and what the ancient practice protected, is that the soldier is a moral agent, that what he carries is real, and that the community which sent him owes him a way home that takes his conscience as seriously as he does.

## Guilt, shame and the men Scripture restored

If the church is to help, it needs one more distinction. Guilt and shame are not the same thing, though they arrive together and feel alike from inside. The psychologist Helen Block Lewis drew the line in *Shame and Guilt in Neurosis* (1971): guilt fastens on an act, I did a terrible thing, and shame fastens on the self, I am a terrible thing. June Price Tangney and Ronda Dearing, gathering years of research in *Shame and Guilt* (2002), reported that the two tend to lead in opposite directions. Guilt, painful as it is, tends to move a person toward confession and repair, because an act can be answered for. Shame tends to move a person toward hiding, withdrawal and sometimes rage, because there is nothing to repair when the problem is who you are. Scripture knew the fork long before the laboratory did: Adam's first act after the first sin is to hide (Genesis 3:10), and Paul sets godly grief that leads to repentance against worldly grief that "produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). The essay on [repentance](/writing/repentance-and-renewal) follows that distinction at length. What matters here is what it means for a man who came home from war.

Moral injury very often begins as guilt and hardens into shame. A man who did something wrong on a particular day becomes, in his own reckoning, a particular kind of man, and the second conclusion is far more dangerous than the first. The Christian gospel does not tell him his guilt is imaginary. It tells him that guilt can be carried to someone, and that shame is a lie about who he now is. And Scripture makes the case not through arguments but through three men, each of whom did something he could not undo.

The first is David, and not the David of the wars this time, but the David of Bathsheba and Uriah: adultery, and then a murder arranged through the chain of command, using the army to kill one of its own. It is, among other things, the story of a commander betraying a loyal soldier, Shay's definition seen from the commander's side. When the prophet Nathan confronted him, David made no defense: "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Samuel 12:13). Psalm 51, which its heading attaches to that moment, is the prayer of a man with blood on him who refuses both denial and despair. He has not been talked out of his guilt; his sin, he says, is ever before him. And yet he prays straight into it: "Deliver me from bloodguiltiness, O God, O God of my salvation, and my tongue will sing aloud of your righteousness" (Psalm 51:14). Bloodguilt is the thing the veteran carries, and David shows that it can be named before God rather than hidden from him, with a hope that rests on what God can do and he cannot.

The second is Peter, whose failure was the failure to act. On the night Jesus was arrested, he stood by a charcoal fire in the high priest's courtyard and three times denied knowing the man he had sworn to die with. When the rooster crowed, "he went out and wept bitterly" (Luke 22:62). That is the moral injury of the one who froze, who did not intervene, who watched his friend taken and saved himself. After the resurrection, on the shore of Galilee, the risen Christ made breakfast over another charcoal fire; John's word for it appears in only those two places in the New Testament. Then he asked Peter three times whether he loved him, one question for each denial, and it hurt:

> "He said to him the third time, 'Simon, son of John, do you love me?' Peter was grieved because he said to him the third time, 'Do you love me?' and he said to him, 'Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.' Jesus said to him, 'Feed my sheep.'" (John 21:17)

Jesus brought Peter back to the fire, back to the number three, back to the exact place of the wound, and restored him there. And then he gave him work. The man who had failed to protect his friend was entrusted with the care of others. It is the restoration of a vocation rather than a cure for memory, and it is what many veterans most need and least expect: not only to be forgiven, but to be trusted again.

The third is Paul, who before his conversion had stood by, approving, while Stephen was stoned, and then went on to hunt Christians himself. He never stopped remembering it. Decades later he wrote that he had been "a blasphemer, persecutor, and insolent opponent" (1 Timothy 1:13), and then this:

> "The saying is trustworthy and deserving of full acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost." (1 Timothy 1:15)

Notice the tense: "of whom I am the foremost." His past was not erased; it became the evidence of the mercy that met him. That is the difference between shame and forgiven guilt. Shame says the worst thing you did is who you are. Forgiven guilt says the worst thing you did is where the mercy of God was proved. None of these three men got their innocence back. All three got their lives back.

## Why the church must listen before it explains

Veterans rarely bring this to church, and the reason is usually us. The congregation that prays for the troops, puts the flag near the pulpit and frames every deployment as heroism has told the veteran, before he says a word, what his story is supposed to be. The man who came home haunted rather than proud doesn't fit the script, and he knows it. But the refusal has a second form, and it deserves the same grief. There is a posture, more common in other pews, that meets the veteran with suspicion instead of celebration, sees him as a victim to be pitied or an instrument of empire, and cannot see the man for the argument about the war. The first church cannot hear a confession because it has ruled the sin unthinkable. The second cannot hear one because it has already assigned the guilt wholesale. Both hand him a script. Neither asks him what happened.

I have done the first. I have shaken a veteran's hand at the door and thanked him for a service I never once asked him to describe, and called that honoring him. It was cheaper than listening.

Job's friends are remembered for their speeches, but their best moment came before them: "And they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great" (Job 2:13). The trouble began when they opened their mouths to explain. Bonhoeffer, in *Life Together* (1939), written after the Gestapo closed the underground seminary where he had lived with young pastors, argued that the first service Christians owe one another is to listen, and that those who cannot listen to a brother will soon be unable to listen to God. He argued too that sin wants to be alone and to stay hidden, and that confession spoken to another believer breaks that isolation in a way private prayer often cannot. James says as much: "Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16). The healing runs through people who hear the worst and stay.

That staying has a shape, and it is lament. The Psalms give the church words for protest and grief that do not hurry toward resolution, and a veteran carrying tragedy or betrayal needs those words before he needs anyone's theology of providence. The essays on [lament](/writing/lament-the-prayer-the-church-forgot) and on [grief that doesn't go away](/writing/the-weight-that-stays) take this up. The practical form is modest: a pastor or a few trusted people who will meet with a veteran over months, not once; who ask what happened and can bear the answer; who can help him sort guilt from betrayal from tragedy; who can speak forgiveness where there is sin and say plainly where there is none; and who keep walking with him to the doctor as well as to the Table.

Veterans are not the only ones. The ethicist Andrew Jameton, writing about nursing in 1984, named moral distress: the anguish of knowing what is right and being blocked by an institution from doing it. In 2018 the physicians Wendy Dean and Simon Talbot argued that much of what medicine calls burnout is better understood as moral injury, and the pandemic years that followed pressed the point on doctors and nurses who made impossible choices and watched patients die alone. Police officers carry versions of it too, from force they were trained and permitted to use, and from wrongs by colleagues they did not stop. The pews hold them as well.

At the center of the Christian faith stands a man who was betrayed by those who held legitimate authority, in the highest-stakes situation there has ever been, and who "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2). Moral injury is not foreign to him. And when he rose, he kept his wounds. He showed them to Thomas and told him to touch them (John 20:27). The risen body still bore the marks of what had been done to it, and the wounds became the place where he was recognized.

That is what the church has to offer the veteran, and it is more than an explanation. It is not the promise that he will forget, or that the dead will be undone, or that the ache will leave by next spring. It is a people who will hear what happened, a God who has carried worse, and a mercy that gives a man back his life without asking him to pretend. He is sitting in our congregations now, courteous, silent, waiting to learn whether we mean what we say about confession. The debt is still unpaid, and it comes due every Sunday.
