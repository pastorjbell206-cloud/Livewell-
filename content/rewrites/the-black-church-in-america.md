A question comes up in American churches more often than people admit, and it usually comes from a decent place. If there is one Lord, one faith and one baptism, why is there a Black church at all? The question has Scripture behind it. Paul told the Galatians, "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, ESV), and John saw the end of history as "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9, ESV). A church sorted by race at eleven o'clock on Sunday morning looks like a failure of that vision, and in one sense it is.

But the question almost always gets asked backward. The Black church didn't begin because Black Christians wanted to be apart. It began, over and over, because white Christians would not have them as equals: would baptize them but not free them, seat them in the gallery, pull them off their knees at prayer, and preach to them from a Bible with the Exodus quietly set aside. The separation was built from the other side of the aisle. What rose on the far side of it, under law and terror and with almost nothing, became the most consequential institution in Black American history and one of the truest witnesses the American church has produced.

My own formation, after I came to faith from atheism, happened in predominantly white evangelical churches that said very little about any of this. I learned it later, from books and from the patient correction of people who grew up inside the tradition, and I write with far more to receive from it than to say about it. The Black church can be understood as an ethnic institution, the church of a particular people, and it is that. It is also a witness to the whole church about suffering and hope, and the white American church has spent two centuries treating the second as if it were only the first.

## The invisible institution

Christianity came to most enslaved Africans in America slowly, and on terms their owners set. Colonial slaveholders worried that baptism might carry a claim to freedom, and Virginia settled the question by statute in 1667: baptism did not alter a person's bondage. The faith could be offered so long as it changed nothing. When planters and missionaries did preach to the enslaved, the favored text was the household code: "Bondservants, obey your earthly masters with fear and trembling, with a sincere heart, as you would Christ" (Ephesians 6:5, ESV). The plantation sermon usually stopped there. It rarely went on four verses to the line Paul addressed to the other party: "Masters, do the same to them, and stop your threatening, knowing that he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven, and that there is no partiality with him" (Ephesians 6:9, ESV). Read whole, the passage puts the master under a Lord who shows no favoritism. Read in fragments, it became an instrument of control.

The enslaved heard the fragment and refused it. Albert Raboteau's *Slave Religion* (1978) gave the scholarly name to what they built instead: the invisible institution. Away from the plantation chapel, in cabins and woods and hollows remembered as hush arbors or brush arbors, people gathered at night to pray, sing and preach on their own terms, at the risk of the lash. Raboteau drew on the testimony of former slaves, some of whom recalled turning an iron pot upside down to catch the sound of their singing. After Nat Turner's revolt in 1831, the Southern states tightened their laws against teaching the enslaved to read and against Black preaching. The planters understood that a people who could read the whole Bible were dangerous.

What they found in it was the Exodus. "Then the LORD said, 'I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings'" (Exodus 3:7, ESV). The God of the hush arbor was the God who hears a cry from the brickyards and brings a nation of slaves through the sea. In later essays, collected in *A Fire in the Bones* (1995), Raboteau pointed out the irony: white Americans told their own history as an Exodus, with Europe as Egypt and the new land as Canaan, while the enslaved, reading the same book, understood that America was Egypt and they were Israel. Both sides claimed the story. Only one of them was reading it from the brickyard.

The spirituals carried this theology where no book could go. "Go Down, Moses" told old Pharaoh to let the people go, and nobody in the quarters needed Pharaoh identified. Yet it would misread the spirituals to hear only politics in them. They are saturated with Christ: "Were You There When They Crucified My Lord?", "Steal Away to Jesus," the trumpet of judgment, the river Jordan. The enslaved kept the whole gospel along with the hope of freedom, hearing the cross as God entering their suffering and the resurrection as God overturning it. W. E. B. Du Bois, in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), gave his last chapter to these Sorrow Songs and treated them as the one spiritual inheritance the nation could truly call its own, and the greatest gift Black Americans had given it.

Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838, named the result with a precision the seminaries rarely matched. In the appendix to his *Narrative* (1845) he set "the Christianity of this land" against "the Christianity of Christ," and said he recognized the widest possible difference between them. He meant that the slaveholding church had become something other than Christianity, and that the praying slave was the one keeping the faith. Howard Thurman put the same judgment inside a single life in *Jesus and the Disinherited* (1949). His grandmother, Nancy Ambrose, born into slavery, never learned to read, and when young Howard read the Bible aloud to her she wouldn't let him read Paul's letters, except for the thirteenth chapter of First Corinthians. The master's preacher had used Paul so often to tell the slaves to obey that she had promised God that if freedom ever came, she would not read that part of the Bible. Thurman treated her refusal as discernment: a woman who had felt the difference between the Word of God and its weaponized fragments, sorting them by the light of what she had suffered. The irony is that Paul, read whole, was on her side. The preachers made sure she never met that Paul.

## From Bethel to the Great Migration

The invisible institution became visible wherever Black Christians could get out from under white control. Richard Allen, born into slavery in 1760, bought his freedom and became a Methodist preacher of real power in Philadelphia. He and other Black Methodists worshiped at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church until the morning, as Allen told it, when white trustees pulled Absalom Jones up off his knees during prayer because the Black worshipers had knelt in the wrong part of the gallery. Tradition dates it to 1787, though some historians place it as late as 1792. The Black members finished the prayer, left together and didn't come back. In 1794 Allen opened Bethel Church, and in 1816 Black Methodist congregations from several cities organized the independent African Methodist Episcopal Church, with Allen as its first bishop. The AME Zion Church followed in New York in 1821, and independent Black Baptist congregations, like the First African Baptist Church organized in Savannah under Andrew Bryan in 1788, were gathering across the South. None of them was separatist in doctrine. They separated over a question the white churches would not settle rightly: whether a Black Christian would be treated as a brother at the Lord's Table and in the governing of the church.

Emancipation turned a scattered network into the central institution of Black life in the South. When slavery ended in 1865, freed people left white-controlled congregations by the hundreds of thousands. Black Baptists formed state conventions and in 1895, in Atlanta, merged three national bodies into the National Baptist Convention, which became the largest Black religious body in the country. When Reconstruction was abandoned in 1877 and Jim Crow hardened into law, with the Supreme Court's approval in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), the church became what the sociologist E. Franklin Frazier later called a nation within a nation. It was the school, the savings society and the burial society, and the one place a Black man or woman could hold public authority without a white person's permission. It was also the church that buried the lynched. The Tuskegee Institute's long-running tally recorded more than three thousand Black Americans lynched between 1882 and 1968, often before crowds, in towns full of churches.

Then the people moved. Between the 1910s and 1970 roughly six million Black Southerners left for the cities of the North and West, the Great Migration that Isabel Wilkerson chronicled in *The Warmth of Other Suns* (2010), and the church moved with them. Storefront churches multiplied in every neighborhood the migrants filled, many of them carrying the Pentecostal fire that a Black holiness preacher, William J. Seymour, had lit at Azusa Street in Los Angeles in 1906. Detroit, an hour's drive from the church I serve, was remade by the migration, and in June 1963 C. L. Franklin of New Bethel Baptist Church helped lead the Walk to Freedom down Woodward Avenue. At its end, in Cobo Hall, Martin Luther King Jr. spoke of his dream two months before he took it to Washington.

## A movement that met in sanctuaries

Popular memory has secularized the civil rights movement. On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery city bus and was arrested. Four days later Black Montgomery filled Holt Street Baptist Church and the streets around it, and the new Montgomery Improvement Association chose as its president the twenty-six-year-old pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, Martin Luther King Jr. For 381 days Montgomery's Black residents walked, carpooled and stayed off the buses, and the boycott was run from church to church through mass meetings that were prayer meetings in form and substance. The boycott ended in December 1956, after the Supreme Court affirmed the ruling that bus segregation was unconstitutional. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference, founded in 1957, was a fellowship of ministers, and though the movement had secular wings, the church was its base: its buildings, its money, its preachers and above all its people, who had been practicing hope under pressure for three centuries.

In April 1963, jailed in Birmingham, King answered eight white clergymen who had called the demonstrations unwise and untimely. The *Letter from Birmingham Jail* is theology before it is politics, arguing from Augustine and Aquinas on unjust law and from Amos and Paul. Near the end King turns to the white church with a grief that isn't rhetorical. He had expected, he wrote, that white ministers would be among the movement's strongest allies, and instead too many had stood aside, pious about the soul and silent about the injustice in their own cities. That paragraph was written to churches like the ones that formed me, and it hasn't expired.

Five months later, on Sunday morning, September 15, 1963, members of the Ku Klux Klan set off a bomb beside the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham. Four girls were killed: Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley and Carole Robertson, who were fourteen, and Denise McNair, who was eleven. The blast blew the face of Christ out of one of the church's stained-glass windows. It took until 1977, 2001 and 2002 for three of the bombers to be convicted. It was an attack on a house of prayer on the Lord's Day, carried out by men raised in a region that called itself Christian and went on calling itself that.

## The cross and the lynching tree

James Cone gave the tradition its most famous and most contested academic voice. Raised in the AME church in Arkansas, he was teaching at Adrian College in Michigan when the Detroit uprising of 1967 and King's assassination the next spring drove him to write *Black Theology and Black Power* (1969). It argued, in deliberately explosive terms, that the God of the Bible is the God who liberates the oppressed, that in America the oppressed are Black, and that a theology which doesn't begin there is not Christian theology.

*The Cross and the Lynching Tree* (2011) is quieter and more searching. Its argument is that in America the cross and the lynching tree interpret each other. The apostles said of Jesus, "They put him to death by hanging him on a tree" (Acts 10:39, ESV), and Paul applied to him the curse of Deuteronomy, "Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree" (Galatians 3:13, ESV). The spirituals and the blues, Cone showed, had long placed Jesus beside the lynched, suffering what they suffered, and so gave Black Christians a way to look at terror without surrendering to despair. Cone's most pointed chapter concerns Reinhold Niebuhr, the most admired American theologian of his century, who wrote brilliantly about sin and power and had almost nothing to say about the lynchings of his own lifetime. Cone didn't need to call Niebuhr a bigot to make the charge land. The charge is that a sophisticated theology of the cross could flourish in white America without ever looking at the tree.

The critiques come from every direction, including from inside Black theology. His brother Cecil Cone argued in *The Identity Crisis in Black Theology* (1975) that James had leaned too heavily on white academic categories and liberation politics, when the heart of Black religion was the encounter with a sovereign God in conversion. J. Deotis Roberts, in *Liberation and Reconciliation* (1971), argued that liberation without reconciliation was an incomplete gospel. William R. Jones, in *Is God a White Racist?* (1973), asked what evidence history offered that God was in fact liberating the oppressed. Delores Williams, in *Sisters in the Wilderness* (1993), argued that Black theology had overlooked the experience of Black women, and centered Hagar, whom God met in the wilderness and helped to survive, beside Moses's story of deliverance. Conservative critics, among them the Black evangelical scholar Anthony Bradley in *Liberating Black Theology* (2010), argued that Cone let the experience of oppression become the norm by which Scripture is judged.

That last objection deserves its strongest form. The gospel is first the announcement of what God has done in Christ, the objection runs, and justice follows from it as fruit follows root. When a theology makes liberation the test of every doctrine, it sets a human outcome above revelation, and the churches that follow it tend to keep the politics and quietly lose the God. No racism is required to hold that worry. Any faithful Christian should raise the same one when a flag is wrapped around the cross.

Cone saw what almost no white theologian of his century saw, that a church could preach Calvary every Sunday while lynched bodies hung in its county. On that he was right, and the rest of us were blind. His method, in its early and angriest form, is genuinely contestable, and Black theologians contested it first. But the objection can't be turned against the Black church tradition itself, because that tradition is the standing refutation of the collapse the objection fears. The spirituals are full of blood, cross, resurrection and judgment, and the churches that organized Montgomery preached conversion as well as justice. On the first-order questions, the image of God in every person and the lordship of Christ over master and slave alike, the slaveholders' religion Douglass exposed was heresy, and the praying slaves were the orthodox ones. What stays second-order, open to argument among faithful people, is method: how liberation functions as a theological category, and where Cone illuminates and where he overreaches.

Esau McCaulley's *Reading While Black* (2020) shows the path the tradition has more often walked. McCaulley, a New Testament scholar and Anglican priest, calls it Black ecclesial interpretation: bringing the questions of Black Christians about policing, political witness, identity, anger and slavery straight to the text, and expecting the canon, read whole and patiently, to answer. He wrote against a progressive scholarship that treats the Bible as the slaveholder's book and a white evangelical reading that never asked Black questions of it, and his claim is that ordinary Black churches had long trusted the Bible over its abusers, rightly. Nancy Ambrose and Esau McCaulley stand in the same line. Neither would let the master decide what the book said.

## Arguments inside the house

A tradition is honored by being taken seriously, internal arguments included. C. Eric Lincoln and Lawrence Mamiya, in *The Black Church in the African American Experience* (1990), warned against treating the Black church as one thing with one politics. They described it through tensions it has always carried: priestly and prophetic, otherworldly and this-worldly, universal and particular, communal and private, charismatic and bureaucratic, accommodation and resistance. The model protects against the romance of outsiders who picture every Black church as a civil rights meeting, and against the cynicism of those who notice that most aren't.

The prosperity preaching heard in some Black pulpits is the hardest case, and it should be stated as its defenders would state it. For a people barred by law and custom from land, from credit and from the housing that built white middle-class wealth after the Second World War, a sermon insisting that God intends your household to flourish is not a small thing. It rejects an old theology that treated Black poverty as natural or ordained, and its defenders will say, with some justice, that critics who have never lacked for anything are quick to call ambition greed when poor people show it.

All of that is true, and the teaching is still false. When it promises that faith and giving will produce wealth, it makes God the servant of an outcome, and it lands hardest on the poorest members, who give most and are then told their continuing poverty is a failure of faith. It individualizes what the older tradition knew was structural, and it contradicts the spirituals, which never promised the enslaved that faith would make them rich, only that God saw them, suffered with them and would finally set things right. The same instrument cuts the other way, because this isn't a Black problem. The prosperity gospel is an American export preached from every kind of pulpit, and it grows from the same soil as the suburban church that promises a better marriage, a calmer mind and a rising career. A white church that condemns it in a Black pulpit while preaching success in its own has not earned the verdict.

Politics is the second argument. Since 1964, when a Democratic president signed the Civil Rights Act and the Republican nominee opposed it, Black Protestants have voted overwhelmingly Democratic, while many of their churches hold conservative convictions on marriage, sexuality and abortion. Critics on the right say the alignment has muted the church's prophetic independence and made some pulpits a party's field office. Critics on the left say its conservatism on sexuality, and the limits many bodies still place on women in the pulpit, have made it a stranger to its own children. Each critique has force, and a church whose prophetic word never crosses its party has become a chaplain, whatever its color. But the white evangelical church, which over the same decades bound itself as tightly to the other party as any religious group in the country, has little standing to deliver that lecture first. The first hand the instrument should cut is the one holding it.

## An ethnic institution or a witness to the whole church

Henry Louis Gates Jr.'s *The Black Church* (2021), subtitled *This Is Our Story, This Is Our Song*, tells the history as the story of a people: the place where Black Americans kept their music, trained their leaders, practiced self-government and imagined freedom when every other door was shut. That account is true, and it describes the Black church as the church of a particular people, doing the work of memory and survival any people's church does, which needs no apology. But if that is all the Black church is, white Christians can admire it the way one admires another family's heirloom, respectfully and without obligation: a King quotation in January, a history lesson in February. That is largely how the white church has treated it, and the treatment is itself a way of not listening.

The other reading holds that the Black church is also a witness to the whole church about suffering and hope, a gift God gave the entire body through the people the rest of the body despised. Paul wrote that "the eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you,' nor again the head to the feet, 'I have no need of you'" (1 Corinthians 12:21, ESV), and he wrote it to a church divided by status, where the well-off ate their fill at the Lord's Supper while the poor went hungry. The next line is sharper: "the parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable" (1 Corinthians 12:22, ESV). On this reading, the Black church knows things the rest of the church needs and can't learn anywhere else. It knows how to read Exodus from the brickyard, and how to hear Jesus at Nazareth announcing that he was sent "to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty those who are oppressed" (Luke 4:18, ESV) as a word addressed to it. It knows how to lament without letting go of God, and how to hope for the resurrection without pretending the crucifixion didn't happen. Thurman himself would not let the tradition stay only particular. In 1944 he helped found the Church for the Fellowship of All Peoples in San Francisco, one of the first intentionally interracial congregations in the country, because he believed the gospel he learned at his grandmother's side was addressed first to those with their "backs against the wall" and finally to everyone.

The witness has been given again in our own time, at terrible cost. In June 2015 a young white supremacist sat through a Wednesday night Bible study at Emanuel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Charleston and then murdered nine of the people who had welcomed him. Two days later, at his bond hearing, relatives of the dead spoke to him of forgiveness. The grace was costly, and it didn't cancel justice; he was sentenced to death in federal court in 2017. But for a few days the whole nation watched a Black church practice the hardest teaching of the Sermon on the Mount with its grief still raw.

What the white American church missed, then, was not a set of facts about Black history. It missed the witness, and in missing it, it missed parts of its own Bible. It read the household codes and skipped the verse to masters. It preached the cross and didn't see the tree. It kept Galatians 3:28 as a doctrine and refused it as a seating chart. What it owes is more than admiration, which costs nothing. It owes repentance with specifics. I serve a church with the word Baptist on its sign, the same word that stood over the doors of Holt Street and Dexter Avenue and 16th Street. It is also the word the Southern Baptist Convention took when it organized in 1845, after the national Baptist mission boards made clear they would not appoint a slaveholder as a missionary, and that convention took one hundred and fifty years, until 1995, to repent formally of its origins in the defense of slavery. No one who inherited the name and the pews gets to claim the first set of churches without owning the second. Repentance with specifics means telling this history in our congregations as our history, sitting under Black preaching long enough to be corrected and not merely inspired, and letting Black Christians say what reconciliation requires rather than announcing that it has happened.

## The window in Birmingham

Two years after the bombing, the congregation at 16th Street received a new stained-glass window. It was a gift gathered from ordinary people in Wales through a newspaper appeal and made by the Welsh artist John Petts, and it shows a Black Christ with his arms spread wide in the shape of the cross. The men who bombed that church meant to say that Black Christians stood outside the protection of God. The window answers that the crucified Lord stands with the ones they tried to erase, which is what the spirituals had been saying since the brush arbors.

That doesn't resolve anything. The four girls are still dead, and their killers walked free for decades while Birmingham's white churches went on meeting. The Christ in the window still has his arms stretched out as on the cross, and the resurrection the Black church has preached for three centuries is a promise it has had to hold with its back against the wall. It has held it. Bethel still stands in Philadelphia on the ground where Allen built it, the congregation at 16th Street still worships in its restored sanctuary, and Mother Emanuel opened its doors for Sunday worship four days after the massacre. None of that was optimism. It was the conviction that the God who heard the cry from Egypt had raised from the dead a man hanged on a tree, and that the last word belongs to him and not to the men with the rope or the dynamite.

The exiles in Babylon asked, "How shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?" (Psalm 137:4, ESV). Black Christians in America have been answering that question for four hundred years, in the hush arbors and the hymnals and the mass meetings. The question for the rest of the American church is heavier. It is whether we will learn the song from the people our fathers in the faith put outside the door, and hear in it the judgment and the mercy of God addressed to us. The arms of the Christ in the Birmingham window are still open, toward everyone who walks into that sanctuary, including the heirs of the churches that built the gallery. The churches that formed my own faith are among those heirs. I can't look at that window as a visitor admiring someone else's history. It is looking back at me.
