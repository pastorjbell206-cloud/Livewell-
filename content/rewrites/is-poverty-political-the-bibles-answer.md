## The question that closes a room

Ask whether poverty is political in many American churches and you can watch faces close. The word has become a smell, a sign that someone is about to carry the news cycle into the sanctuary, and so the answer comes quickly: poverty is a heart issue, it's about generosity and not policy, keep the government out of the gospel. In other churches the answer comes just as quickly from the opposite direction: poverty is a system, charity is a bandage on a wound the system keeps reopening, and a church that feeds the hungry without asking why they're hungry has made its peace with the arrangement. I understand the first instinct, and I have shared it. The second has its own force. Each side has read part of the Bible carefully.

Both answers are true about something. Scripture knows that poverty can come from a man's own choices: "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come upon you like a robber, and want like an armed man" (Proverbs 6:10–11, ESV). The same book also knows that poverty can come from someone else's power: "The fallow ground of the poor would yield much food, but it is swept away through injustice" (Proverbs 13:23, ESV). Proverbs keeps both sayings and doesn't choose. That alone should warn us that the question, as Americans usually ask it, is a trap.

The trap is the word *political*. In ordinary use it now means partisan, a matter of which team you're on. In its older sense it names the ordering of a common life: the rules by which a people decides who owns what, who owes what, and who has recourse when wronged. In that older sense Scripture couldn't keep poverty out of politics if it tried, because the Torah is the charter of a people, and much of its economic law concerns the poor. In the partisan sense, Scripture refuses to be conscripted by either side.

Everything turns on a distinction between three things the American argument keeps collapsing into one another. *Charity* is the gift given beyond what anyone could demand, freely, from a heart moved by grace. *Justice* is what is owed: the claim the poor have on a community because God has put it there, a claim that doesn't wait on anyone's mood. *Policy* is the prudential means a political community chooses to pursue justice: the tax code, the benefit rules, the minimum wage. Scripture binds the first two on every Christian and every church. It doesn't hand down the third, and on the third, faithful Christians disagree and may keep disagreeing as brothers. The right tends to fold justice into charity, so that nothing is really owed. The left tends to fold justice into policy, so that a vote discharges the debt and the neighbor never has to be met. Both lose the middle term, and the middle term is the one the Bible spends the most breath on.

> Scripture binds the obligation. It does not hand down the policy. Most of our quarrels come from pretending otherwise.

## Mercy written into the field

Some of Israel's most practical economic law concerns a harvest. "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not reap your field right up to its edge, neither shall you gather the gleanings after your harvest. And you shall not strip your vineyard bare, neither shall you gather the fallen grapes of your vineyard. You shall leave them for the poor and for the sojourner: I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 19:9–10, ESV). This isn't an exhortation to feel generous, and it isn't a tax redistributed by a central office. It's a limit written into ownership itself. The landowner's right stops short of the edge of his own field, and what grows there belongs, by God's decree, to people who gather it with their own hands. In Ruth, a widowed foreigner gleans in Boaz's barley field, and Boaz goes beyond the law by telling his young men to let extra fall for her (Ruth 2). The law set the floor; his kindness rose above it. That's justice and charity in a single chapter.

Deuteronomy 15 goes further. "At the end of every seven years you shall grant a release" (Deuteronomy 15:1, ESV), a cancellation of debts among Israelites that kept a bad harvest from becoming a lifetime of bondage. Then come two sentences that sound like a contradiction. "But there will be no poor among you; for the LORD will bless you in the land" (Deuteronomy 15:4, ESV), provided, the next verse says, "you will strictly obey the voice of the LORD your God" (15:5). Seven verses later: "For there will never cease to be poor in the land. Therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor, in your land'" (Deuteronomy 15:11, ESV). The first is the goal a faithful people could reach; the second is the realism of a God who knows they won't. The realism grounds the command rather than cancelling it: *therefore* open your hand. The law even anticipates the lender who sees the year of release coming and quietly stops lending (15:9).

Jesus quoted that second sentence in the house at Bethany, when a woman broke a flask of costly ointment over his head and some present complained that it could have been sold for the poor. "For you always have the poor with you, and whenever you want, you can do good for them. But you will not always have me" (Mark 14:7, ESV). The verse has long been used as a shrug, as though Jesus had declared poverty permanent and therefore not worth much effort. But he was quoting Deuteronomy to people who knew how the sentence continued, and its next word is *therefore*. John's account of the anointing adds a detail that should sober anyone who talks loudly about the poor: the objector there is Judas, who spoke "not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief" (John 12:6, ESV). The Bible's most famous advocate for a poverty budget was skimming from it.

The Jubilee reached deepest of all. "And you shall consecrate the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants. It shall be a jubilee for you, when each of you shall return to his property and each of you shall return to his clan" (Leviticus 25:10, ESV). The theological ground follows: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). Christopher Wright, in *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* (2004), argues that the Jubilee wasn't a scheme for equal incomes. It restored each family's access to land, the means by which a household fed itself, so that one generation's misfortune or folly couldn't dispossess its grandchildren forever. It's a structural provision that cuts two ways: it assumes families will work their land and bear their losses for a time, and it refuses to let those losses harden into a permanent class.

Whether Israel ever kept the Jubilee is uncertain. Jeremiah tells of a release of Hebrew slaves proclaimed under Zedekiah and revoked when the owners took them back (Jeremiah 34:8–17), and the Chronicler reads the exile as the land finally receiving the Sabbaths it had been denied (2 Chronicles 36:21). Yet a man with a governor's authority once enforced the spirit of these laws. When Nehemiah found nobles charging their brothers interest and taking their fields and children as security, he called a great assembly and made them swear to restore the fields and stop the lending (Nehemiah 5:1–13). In the same chapter he declined the governor's food allowance, because "the service was too heavy on this people" (Nehemiah 5:18, ESV). Public reform and private restraint, from the same man, in the same chapter.

Beneath all of this runs a pair of Hebrew words the Old Testament often joins: *mishpat*, usually rendered justice, and *tsedaqah*, righteousness. Wright shows that together they describe less the blind neutrality of a Western courtroom than a community of right relationships and the active putting right of wrongs, especially for the widow, the orphan, the sojourner and the poor. Timothy Keller, in *Generous Justice* (2010), draws the conclusion that matters here: in the Bible, giving to the poor is regularly called righteousness rather than charity, so that failing to give is not stinginess but injustice. In later Jewish usage *tsedaqah* came to mean almsgiving, as *tzedakah* still does, and some later manuscripts of Matthew 6:1 read "alms" where the earliest read "righteousness." Scripture won't let us file generosity under the optional and justice under the political. It uses one word for both.

## The prophets, and a sermon in Nazareth

Walter Brueggemann argued in *The Prophetic Imagination* (1978) that the trouble began with the monarchy itself. Solomon built what he called a royal consciousness, an economy of accumulation and forced labor that managed abundance for the few, and the prophets rose as the alternative, remembering the God who heard the cry of slaves. One needn't accept all of Brueggemann's reading to see what it explains: the prophets' anger fell hardest on people with power, and their charges were specific.

Isaiah opens with God refusing Israel's worship. The festivals burden him, the raised hands are full of blood, and the remedy isn't better liturgy: "learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause" (Isaiah 1:17, ESV). Worship is rejected for the absence of justice. Nine chapters later he names the lawmakers: "Woe to those who decree iniquitous decrees, and the writers who keep writing oppression, to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right, that widows may be their spoil, and that they may make the fatherless their prey!" (Isaiah 10:1–2, ESV). That isn't a sermon against private stinginess. It indicts legislation, rules that are legal, orderly and cruel. Amos names the merchants who couldn't wait for the Sabbath to end so they could "make the ephah small and the shekel great and deal deceitfully with false balances" (Amos 8:5, ESV), the weights and measures of an ancient market bent a little in every transaction.

Micah compresses all of it into a sentence often embroidered and seldom read in context. A worshipper asks what would satisfy God: thousands of rams, rivers of oil, his firstborn child. The answer: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8, ESV). Justice, kindness and humility are what the sacrifices were supposed to mean.

The prophets refuse the right's comfort that poverty is only a matter of hearts, since they spend most of their breath on courts, decrees, land and weights. They refuse the left's comfort too. They never located salvation in the state; the king was usually the problem. Nor did they treat poverty as a certificate of righteousness; they called the whole people, rich and poor, back to the covenant. Their instrument was the law of God, and they turned it on everyone.

In Luke's telling, Jesus opened his public ministry in the synagogue at Nazareth (Luke 4:16–21). He was handed the scroll of Isaiah and read: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty those who are oppressed, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:18–19, ESV). Then he sat down and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21, ESV). He stopped in the middle of Isaiah 61:2, before "the day of vengeance of our God." The year of the Lord's favor carries the sound of Jubilee, and John Howard Yoder argued in *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) that Jesus was proclaiming a Jubilee in earnest, with real economic content. Many scholars think Yoder pressed further than the text will go, but the echo is there, and Luke keeps his gospel concrete: his beatitude speaks of the poor, not the poor in spirit (Luke 6:20), and his woes fall on the rich and the full (6:24–25). The good news was news to actual poor people first.

Then there is the judgment of the nations in Matthew 25. "For I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me, I was naked and you clothed me, I was sick and you visited me, I was in prison and you came to me" (Matthew 25:35–36, ESV). "Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (25:40). Interpreters divide over who "the least of these my brothers" are. A long line of readers, noting that Matthew often uses "brothers" for Jesus' disciples (12:48–50; 28:10), takes them to be needy Christians; others read the phrase as reaching every hungry and imprisoned person. On either reading the passage says two things our politics prefers to separate. The ones sent away are condemned for neglect, not for cruelty; they simply didn't come. And the acts that count are face to face: food handed over, a sick man visited, a prisoner seen. No one in the story is judged for a policy position. No one is excused because a program existed.

## What the church did before there was a welfare state

The first Christians took the law's promise literally. "And all who believed were together and had all things in common. And they were selling their possessions and belongings and distributing the proceeds to all, as any had need" (Acts 2:44–45, ESV). Two chapters later, describing the same shared life (Acts 4:32–35), Luke writes a sentence that echoes Deuteronomy 15:4: "There was not a needy person among them" (Acts 4:34, ESV). The Torah's goal, which Israel missed, the Spirit had begun to reach in one congregation. Yet the sharing was not compelled. Peter tells Ananias that his field, before it was sold, "did it not remain your own?" (Acts 5:4, ESV), and his sin was the lie, not the keeping. Soon the church had to build a structure, appointing seven men to oversee the daily distribution to widows when some were being overlooked (Acts 6:1–6). Generosity needed administration almost at once.

Paul's collection for the poor in Jerusalem (2 Corinthians 8–9) holds together two words our argument pulls apart. The first is fairness: "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness. As it is written, 'Whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack'" (2 Corinthians 8:14–15, ESV). The picture is the manna in the wilderness. The second word is freedom: "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7, ESV). Fairness is the goal and freedom is the manner, and underneath both stands the ground of the whole appeal: "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). Christian giving is the grace of the incarnation taking the shape of a budget.

The watching world noticed. In the 360s the emperor Julian, trying to revive the old gods, complained in a letter to a pagan high priest in Galatia that the Galileans supported not only their own poor but the pagans' poor as well. Around 368, during a famine in Cappadocia, Basil of Caesarea preached on the rich fool of Luke 12 and told his hearers, in substance, that the bread they stored belonged to the hungry, the coat in their chest to the naked and the shoes rotting in their closet to the barefoot. Then he built a complex of shelter, food and medical care outside the city, later called the Basileias. John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch on the rich man and Lazarus, argued that not to share one's goods with the poor is to rob them, since what we hold is theirs before it is ours; elsewhere he warned against honoring Christ's body at the altar with silk while neglecting him naked in the street. Ambrose of Milan, in his treatise on Naboth, told the rich that in giving to the poor they were not bestowing what was their own but returning what belonged to the poor, since the earth was made for all in common. Paul VI quoted that passage in *Populorum Progressio* (1967).

These men were not socialists. They treated property as a stewardship and preached to the consciences of the rich. But their claim is a claim of justice: the poor were owed. The church's monasteries, hospitals and parishes carried that claim for centuries, until the state began to take the work over. When Henry VIII dissolved the English monasteries between 1536 and 1541, much of their relief went with them, and the Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601 made support of the poor a parish tax. The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 pushed relief toward the workhouse, and Victorian poor relief hardened the line between the deserving and the undeserving poor. That line still runs through every American argument about welfare, and it's older than both our parties.

## Two traditions answer the industrial age

The modern form of the question arrived with the factory, and in 1891 two great traditions of Christian social thought answered it. In May, Leo XIII issued *Rerum Novarum*, which defended private property against the socialists and, in the same breath, the worker's right to a just wage and to form associations, teaching that the state owes particular care to the poor because the rich have other ways to shield themselves. In November, Abraham Kuyper opened the first Christian Social Congress in Amsterdam with the address later published as *The Problem of Poverty*. Kuyper rejected both the individualism he traced to the French Revolution and the socialist answer, and he told the congress that Jesus, like the prophets, took the side of the poor and suffering against the powerful. His doctrine of sphere sovereignty, set out in 1880, gave the state a real but limited office: to guard justice among the other spheres of family, church, labor and enterprise, not to swallow them.

The Catholic tradition kept building. Pius XI named the principle of subsidiarity in *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931): what a smaller community can do, a larger one should not take from it. At Medellín in 1968 the Latin American bishops asked the church to give preference to the poorest, and at Puebla in 1979 they spoke of a "preferential option for the poor," the phrase by which the idea is now known. When liberation theologians fused that option with Marxist class analysis, Rome pushed back in 1984 while keeping the option itself. John Paul II spoke in *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* (1987) of "structures of sin," insisting that they are rooted in personal sins and tied to the concrete acts of people. In *Centesimus Annus* (1991) he endorsed a market economy set within a strong juridical framework, and he criticized what he called the Social Assistance State for draining human energy and multiplying bureaucracy, arguing that needs are best met by those closest to them, who act as neighbors.

Neither tradition can be filed under an American party. Both bind the obligation to the poor without apology, name structures as well as hearts, and refuse either to make the state the only actor or to let it off the hook. A reader of either will find a paragraph to embarrass the right and another to embarrass the left, which is usually a sign that someone has been reading Scripture instead of a platform.

## The American argument, stated fairly

American evangelicals had their own version of the debate, and each side deserves its strongest voice. Ronald Sider's *Rich Christians in an Age of Hunger* (1977) argued that the God of Scripture takes the side of the poor, called comfortable believers to a simpler life and a graduated tithe, and insisted that sin lives in unjust economic structures as well as in hearts. At its best it asked the prophets' question: what do the arrangements we benefit from do to people we'll never meet? David Chilton answered with *Productive Christians in an Age of Guilt-Manipulators* (1981), charging that Sider misread both the Bible and economics; later editions of Sider's book granted more to markets. Marvin Olasky's *The Tragedy of American Compassion* (1992) made the conservative case with real moral seriousness. Nineteenth-century American charity, he argued, worked because it was personal, discerning and demanding: it knew the names of the poor, asked for work where work was possible, and addressed the spirit as well as the stomach, while the welfare expansion of the 1960s replaced suffering-with by disbursement at a distance. The book fed the welfare debates of the mid-1990s. Steve Corbett and Brian Fikkert, in *When Helping Hurts* (2009), drawing on Bryant Myers's *Walking with the Poor* (1999), described poverty as broken relationship with God, self, others and creation, warned that aid which defines poverty only as a lack of things can humiliate the poor and inflate the helper, and distinguished relief in a crisis from rehabilitation and long development.

Each book is right about something the others miss, and the history refuses to let either side win cleanly. Richard Rothstein's *The Color of Law* (2017) documents how the Federal Housing Administration, from its creation in 1934, wrote underwriting rules that refused to insure mortgages in Black and mixed neighborhoods while subsidizing white suburbs, and that kind of discrimination wasn't outlawed until the Fair Housing Act of 1968. A wealth gap built by policy was later blamed on character. That is Isaiah's "writers who keep writing oppression" in a federal manual. Conservatives answer, with their own evidence, that later policy has also trapped people, that benefit rules can punish marriage and penalize a raise, and that habits of work and family still shape a life. Both sides are arguing that policy has consequences for the poor. The quarrel is over which policies, and that's a quarrel faithful Christians are allowed to have.

So name the order of the question. That Christians owe the poor, personally and together, is not open for anyone who reads the Torah, the prophets, Jesus and Paul. That the powerful may not write rules to rob the weak is not open either. How a pluralist republic arranges its taxes, benefits and labor law is a second-order question of prudence and evidence, on which believers who agree about Deuteronomy 15 may disagree about the minimum wage and remain brothers. The conclusion here is that a faith that won't follow poverty into courts and statutes has quietly agreed to leave the arrangements to someone else. The strongest objection not fully answered is that Israel's law was given to a covenant people, and the United States is not Israel. Daniel's counsel to Nebuchadnezzar, "break off your sins by practicing righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed" (Daniel 4:27, ESV), shows God holding a pagan king to account for the poor. It doesn't tell us how to translate Leviticus into a budget, and that translation will always be argued, never deduced.

What can be judged is the evasion, and the same instrument fits both hands. A man who marches for the poor and never opens his door to one has learned to love humanity in order to avoid loving a human; the program becomes his absolution. But run it the other way. The man who insists poverty is only a heart issue can hand a hungry man a meal on Tuesday and vote on Wednesday to keep the rigged court that put him on the street, and feel clean both days. Privatized compassion is not the alternative to political evasion. It's the most respectable form of it. I've preached with more clarity about individual sin than about the sin embedded in systems, because individual sin threatens no one's portfolio. That's a confession, not a boast.

## The man at the gate

Jesus told of a rich man and a beggar: "And at his gate was laid a poor man named Lazarus, covered with sores" (Luke 16:20, ESV). I've preached that parable as a story about what comes after death, which it certainly is. I was slower to notice that the rich man is never accused of harming Lazarus. He simply lived beside him, passing a dying man at his own threshold on the way in and out. Even in torment he knows the beggar's name and asks that Lazarus be sent to cool his tongue. He had known it all along.

Every one of us stands there, the marcher and the almsgiver alike. We live inside arrangements we didn't build and benefit from anyway. The shirt I'm wearing was made by hands I'll never meet, under conditions I wouldn't accept for my sons. I'm implicated before I've done one cruel thing. That's why the cross stands in the middle of the question. At Calvary God didn't meet poverty as a sentiment. He entered the machinery of an unjust order, a betrayal bought for thirty pieces of silver, a trial held by night, a death the empire kept for the bottom of its society, and let it grind him. There the law that condemns the rigged court fell on the only innocent man, and he bore it. The one who was rich became poor. The same cross that exposes our complicity pays for it, and the paying doesn't leave us comfortable. It sends us back to the gate.

The poor will always be with us, Jesus said, and the sentence he was quoting ends in a command to open the hand. Charity will still be needed when every law is just, and justice will still be owed when every pantry is full. Two questions follow a Christian into ordinary weeks: whose name do I know at my own gate, and who built the gate he's sitting outside? The God who searches the heart also audits the city. He has never once agreed to inspect the one and ignore the other.
