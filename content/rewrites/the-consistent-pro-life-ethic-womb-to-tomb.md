Few sentences sting the pro-life movement more than this one: you're pro-birth, not pro-life. It's usually said with contempt, often by people who have never met the women who run the local pregnancy center's diaper closet. Some of it is unfair, and I'll say where. But it keeps landing, because it names something the church can see in its own budgets if it is willing to look. We have been clearer about what the law should forbid than about what we ourselves would give, and louder in the public square than in the hospital waiting room. I have preached the sanctity of the unborn with more conviction than I have examined what my own congregation actually spends on the women and children who live with the consequences of that conviction. The sentence stings because part of it is about me.

This essay is not the argument about abortion itself. That case is made in its own essay on this site, and I take its conclusion as settled: the child in the womb bears the image of God. What I'm asking is what follows for the church. What does a people owe the child after birth, the mother before and after it, and every life that same image marks, from the first cell to the last breath? The answer turns on a distinction the American church rarely makes out loud. There is a pro-life position, which is a view about what the law should permit. And there is a pro-life people, a community whose common life makes it possible to receive a child. A position can be won in a courtroom. A people has to be built, slowly, by men and women willing to be inconvenienced for years. For fifty years we poured ourselves into the first. We are badly behind on the second.

## The seamless garment and its critics

The modern phrase has a date. On December 6, 1983, Cardinal Joseph Bernardin, the archbishop of Chicago, gave the Gannon Lecture at Fordham University under the title "A Consistent Ethic of Life: An American-Catholic Dialogue." Earlier that year he had chaired the committee that drafted *The Challenge of Peace*, the American Catholic bishops' pastoral letter on nuclear war, and had spent months arguing about the killing of the innocent in the cold vocabulary of deterrence. At Fordham he pressed further. The church's convictions about abortion, nuclear war, capital punishment and the care of the poor were not separate causes that happened to share a sponsor. They were applications of one principle, the dignity of human life, and they ought to be argued so that each strengthened the others. The image that attached itself to the lecture came from the foot of the cross, from the one garment the soldiers would not tear: "But the tunic was seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom" (John 19:23, ESV). Cut it anywhere and you've ruined the whole of it.

Bernardin said, then and afterward, that consistency did not mean equivalence. The issues differ in kind and in weight; a debate over deterrence and the direct killing of a child are not the same act. What consistency meant was accountability, every question answering to the same principle instead of to a party's convenience. Evangelicals had reached a version of the instinct by a different road. Francis Schaeffer and C. Everett Koop's *Whatever Happened to the Human Race?* (1979), a book and film series that shaped a generation of Protestant conviction, argued that abortion, infanticide and euthanasia belong together, because a culture that learns to decide which humans are worth keeping at the beginning of life will not stop there. That was a womb-to-tomb argument too, though it said less about the poor.

The critics of Bernardin's framing deserve their strongest form, because they were not cranks, and many of them loved the poor as much as he did. Abortion, they argued, is the direct, intended killing of an innocent human being, and in the years Bernardin spoke it was happening in the United States more than a million times a year. The death penalty concerns the guilty, and nuclear strategy and welfare policy involve prudential judgments where faithful people differ about means. Put all of these on one list and you imply they carry one weight, and in practice the list becomes a ledger. A Catholic politician who supported legal abortion could point to his record on poverty or capital punishment and present himself as the more consistent defender of life. The critics said the garment had become a cloak, and they were not imagining it. They could cite the church's own later clarifications: John Paul II's *Evangelium Vitae* (1995) carried the logic of life across abortion, euthanasia and the death penalty, yet singled out the deliberate killing of the innocent as a crime no human law can make legitimate, and in November 2019 the American bishops, in the introductory letter to *Forming Consciences for Faithful Citizenship*, called the threat of abortion their "preeminent priority."

The critics are right about the misuse and wrong about the principle. A man who uses the poor to excuse the killing of the unborn has not applied the principle across the whole span. He has dropped it at the precise point where it costs his party something. And the same misuse runs in the other direction, which the critics were slower to name. A movement can use the unborn to excuse its indifference to the poor mother, the prisoner and the dying. Each move takes one real priority and spends it to purchase an exemption from the rest. Held rightly, the consistent ethic doesn't flatten the priorities. It forbids trading them.

So the question has an order. First-order, binding every Christian conscience: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV), and the church may not choose among image-bearers for votes or coalition peace. Second-order, where faithful Christians divide: how law, policy, medicine and ministry should combine, which work comes first in a given season, what the state should do with a murderer. Particular bills and programs are third-order. A Christian who weighs a Medicaid design differently than I do is not my adversary. One who has never asked why his movement's energy runs so heavily in one direction has not yet faced the first order.

## What the early church did with unwanted children

The Roman world had a settled practice for children it did not want. A newborn, more often a girl or a child born weak, could be exposed: left at a crossroads or a rubbish heap to die or to be taken by whoever passed, often into slavery. It was legal and ordinary. A papyrus letter from Oxyrhynchus in Egypt, dated to 1 BC, preserves a working man named Hilarion writing home to his pregnant wife from Alexandria. In the same short letter in which he begs her not to worry and to take care of their little one, he tells her that if the child is a boy she should keep it and if it is a girl she should cast it out.

Into that world the church brought a different kind of household. The Didache, a manual of Christian practice from around the end of the first century, forbade killing a child by abortion and killing a newborn. The Epistle to Diognetus, probably from the second century, describes Christians marrying and bearing children like everyone else, but refusing to expose their offspring. Justin Martyr, writing his *First Apology* around 155, objected to exposure partly because so many abandoned infants were gathered up and raised for prostitution. The refusal was a practice as well as a rule, and in time it came to include taking in foundlings.

The sociologist Rodney Stark, in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), argued that this refusal helps explain how a tiny, despised movement came to fill the empire. A community that forbade abortion and infanticide, and that bound husbands to their wives for life, grew more women and more children than its neighbors. And when the great epidemics came, the plague that began around 165 and the one that swept the empire from 251, Christians stayed to nurse the sick, their own and their neighbors', while many who could afford to fled. Historians argue about Stark's reconstructed numbers; the direction of the evidence is much less in dispute. A people who treated every life as worth keeping kept more of them alive.

The same household held the widow, as Acts 6:1-6 and 1 Timothy 5:3-16 both show. By 251 the bishop of Rome, Cornelius, could report in a letter that Eusebius later preserved that his church was supporting more than fifteen hundred widows and people in distress. The Torah had already taught Israel who God is: "He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing" (Deuteronomy 10:18, ESV). The early church believed it and set a table.

It worked well enough to be resented at the top. In 362 the emperor Julian, trying to rebuild the old religion, wrote to Arsacius, a pagan high priest in Galatia, complaining that the Galileans supported not only their own poor but the pagans' poor as well, and urging his priests to imitate them. The historian Peter Brown, in *Poverty and Leadership in the Later Roman Empire* (2002), shows how new this was. Classical generosity flowed to citizens and clients. Christian bishops made the destitute, the person who belonged to no one, a public category and a claim on the whole community. The praise their age gave such a bishop, as Brown traces it, was "lover of the poor," and the title was a job description.

Notice the order in which things happened. In 374 a law issued in the names of the emperors Valentinian, Valens and Gratian, preserved in the Theodosian Code (9.14.1), finally treated the killing of an infant as a capital crime. The law came after the people. For three centuries the church had been a community in which a baby left on the rubbish heap might be carried home and a widow with no son had somewhere to eat, and that visible, costly way of life made the old practice begin to look like what it was. Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Prophets* (1962), observed that what we treat as a minor injustice the prophets treated as a catastrophe. The early church had absorbed that scale of seeing, and the law eventually followed a people who had already made the killing unthinkable among themselves.

I was raised without a father. When I read that God is "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows" and that he "settles the solitary in a home" (Psalm 68:5-6, ESV), I don't read it as a lovely abstraction. It is a description of what the church was built to be.

## A pro-life position and a pro-life people

The distinction at the center of this essay is not original here. Stanley Hauerwas pressed something very close to it in *A Community of Character* (1981), in an essay on why the public arguments about abortion keep failing. When Christians argue in the language of rights, he said, the debate becomes a standoff, and the church's deepest reason disappears: that Christians are meant to be a people who receive children as gifts rather than projects. Our opposition to abortion makes full sense only inside a community capable of welcoming the child. Take away the community and the conviction still stands, but it stands alone, a position without a people.

That is where the pro-birth sneer finds its purchase. A position can be held by anyone with a ballot. A people requires a table, a spare room, a car seat, a woman at the end of the pew who takes the baby on Tuesday nights so his mother can sleep. James put the test plainly: "If a brother or sister is poorly clothed and lacking in daily food, and one of you says to them, 'Go in peace, be warmed and filled,' without giving them the things needed for the body, what good is that?" (James 2:15-16, ESV). John sharpened it into a question about whether love is present at all: "But if anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, yet closes his heart against him, how does God's love abide in him? Little children, let us not love in word or talk but in deed and in truth" (1 John 3:17-18, ESV). Neither apostle is talking about legislation. Both are talking about what a congregation does with its own hands.

Frederica Mathewes-Green, a pro-life writer who wanted to know what would actually have changed women's minds, listened to women who had had abortions and reported what she heard in *Real Choices* (1994). What struck her was how rarely the decision came down to money alone. Again and again it came down to people: the boyfriend who said he'd leave, the parent who pushed, the sense of being entirely alone with it. A crisis of abandonment can't be answered by a statute, only by someone who does not abandon her.

The strongest evidence on the other side points the same way. Diana Greene Foster's *The Turnaway Study* (2020) followed women for years after they sought abortions, comparing those who received them with those turned away because they were past a clinic's gestational limit. The women who were turned away and carried to term faced more financial hardship, more debt and more insecurity, and it lasted. Advocates for abortion access cite the study for their position, and a pro-life reader's first instinct is to argue with it; critics have questioned how many women agreed to take part and how many stayed in the study. A better first move is to read it as a list of things the church left undone. It measures, among other things, what happens to women who carry a child to term without a people around them.

The instrument that judges this has to be the same for everyone, or it is not a principle. Apply it to the right first, since that is where most of us who hold the pro-life position live. We have too often treated the law as the finish line and the voter guide as the whole of our witness. We have been suspicious of public help for mothers and infants. Some of us cheer at executions, which even the sternest reading of Genesis 9:6 does not permit, because that verse grounds the penalty in the worth of the image the murderer destroyed and leaves no room for contempt toward the image he still bears. We have spoken tenderly about the child in the womb and with something close to scorn about the child at the border. Now apply it to the left. There the mother is seen with real care and the child is not seen at all; his moral existence begins at the edge of someone else's decision. Abortion is offered as the answer to poverty, a counsel of despair presented as kindness. The same politics that fights for postpartum coverage has often worked to defund pregnancy centers, and it is the chief engine of assisted suicide at the other end of life, from Oregon's law, in force since 1997, to Canada's in 2016. Each side holds half the garment and calls the tear a principle. Neither party's platform is the consistent ethic.

This is not a claim of perfect symmetry. The direct killing of the innocent is graver than a bad budget. But gravity is exactly the reason the church can't stop at the law. The more seriously you believe a child's life is at stake, the more seriously you have to take whatever makes his mother feel she cannot keep him.

## The mothers we did not count

The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate among wealthy nations, and not by a narrow margin; international comparisons such as the Commonwealth Fund's have placed it at the top of that table year after year. Inside that number sits a harder one. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has consistently found that Black women in America die from pregnancy-related causes at roughly three times the rate of white women, and the gap does not close among women with college degrees. The state committees that review these deaths judge a large majority of them preventable. Many fall in the weeks and months after delivery, when coverage for many poor women used to end at sixty days. Rural hospitals have closed labor and delivery units where the economics stopped working. And in 2016 a study in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* by Kelly Hoffman and her colleagues found that about half of a sample of white medical students and residents endorsed at least one false belief about biological differences between Black and white people, such as that Black people's skin is thicker, and that those who endorsed more of these beliefs rated a Black patient's pain lower in mock medical cases and made less accurate treatment recommendations. The study was not about childbirth, but it names the kind of disbelief a woman in pain can meet in any exam room. These deaths are not a mystery. They are a ledger.

The pro-life movement deserves its defense before its verdict, and the defense is stronger than its critics allow. Thousands of pregnancy resource centers, staffed overwhelmingly by volunteer women, give away ultrasounds, diapers and parenting classes, and many stay in contact with mothers for years. Christian families have been conspicuous among those who adopt and who open their homes through foster care. Since 2021 federal law has let states extend postpartum Medicaid to a full year, and most states have done so, including many with the strictest abortion laws. The people holding candles outside clinics are, very often, the same people running the diaper bank. The sneer that they forget the child at delivery is cheaper than the people it dismisses.

But a defense of the movement's people is not a defense of its priorities. Measure the energy. For five decades the legal fight over abortion commanded institutional money, pulpit time, voter guides and moral urgency. The deaths of mothers, hundreds every year, most of them preventable, concentrated among the poor and among Black women, commanded almost none of it. I am inside that indictment. I preached Sanctity of Human Life Sunday for years without once mentioning that the health data for my own region could have told me which mothers near my church were most likely to die bringing a child into the world. I could recite the abortion statistics. I could not have told you my own state's maternal mortality rate. That was not an oversight. That was formation, and I was formed.

The reproductive-justice organizations that documented these deaths most carefully did work the church should be ashamed it did not do first: they counted, named the racism in the exam room, and fought for the coverage extensions. And most of them cannot bring themselves to say the word *child* about anyone not yet born. A movement that grieves the mother and will not name the child, and a movement that names the child and will not fund the mother, are making the same move in opposite directions. Each has chosen which image-bearer it can afford to see.

When the first church found widows going hungry, it did not form a committee to argue about whose fault it was. It created an office. The deacons of Acts 6 exist because the apostles judged that the neglect of vulnerable women was not a side issue that would distract from the word of God, but a scandal that would discredit it. A tradition with that in its bloodstream, facing women dying preventably in childbirth, has no shortage of theological equipment. It has a shortage of attention.

## After Dobbs

On June 24, 2022, the Supreme Court decided *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, overturned *Roe v. Wade* (1973), and returned abortion law to the states. It was the outcome the pro-life movement had worked toward for half a century, and within months it was clear that the movement, and the church that had been its most faithful ally, had not spent those fifty years preparing for it. Some states restricted abortion sharply; others expanded access. The result was less the end of abortion in America than its redistribution, with the heaviest burden falling on women in restrictive states who had the fewest resources: no car, no flexible job, no family able to help.

The honest defense of the movement's strategy is that legal protection had to come first. You cannot build a culture of welcome around a child the law says you may kill, and movements that fight on every front at once tend to win on none. The fifty years were not misspent; they were sequential. That argument has real force. But fifty years is a long time. A movement that built advocacy and litigation networks across two generations could have built material capacity alongside them. That it mostly didn't was not forced by the work. It was a choice about what the work was for.

Amos spoke to a people who had their victories and their festivals in good order, and the Lord's answer was not congratulation: "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21, ESV). What he wanted was a changed life across the whole society: "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24, ESV). The prophet did not despise worship. He despised worship that had made peace with what happened to the poor on Monday. The post-Dobbs church stands in a similar place, and its question is what we will do with the lives the law now protects.

A pro-life people, in an ordinary congregation, would sit with a frightened woman before it advised her, because she needs evidence that she is known before she is managed. It would make sure any pregnancy ministry it supports tells women the medical truth; critics have documented centers that presented themselves as clinics they were not, and where that happens it is a sin against the very woman the ministry exists to serve. It would offer what an actual crisis requires: rent, a co-signed lease, groceries, rides to prenatal appointments, someone to call at midnight. It would still be there six months later, when the baby shower is a memory and the mother is alone with a colicky infant and a job she can barely hold, because attention that vanishes after the decision is worse than none. It would treat single mothers as members of the household of God rather than cautionary tales. It would recruit families for foster care and then refuse to leave them alone, and it would speak honestly about adoption, which is a gift and also, for the child and the birth mother, a loss that deserves to be grieved. It would bring the energy it once brought to the legislature for the unborn to postpartum care, childcare and paid leave, and accept the discomfort of finding some of those questions on the other side of the aisle. And it would not stop at the nursery door. The image of God does not fade with age or crime or dementia, so a pro-life people visits the prisoner and sits with the dying and refuses to let a grandmother who no longer knows her children be managed toward the exit.

If you are pregnant and afraid, or a new mother in a darkness that frightens you, or thinking about ending your life, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour. If the person who got you pregnant is hurting you or threatening you, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. Nothing in this essay is medical or legal advice. Abortion law has changed state by state since 2022 and is still changing; a woman with a pregnancy complication needs a physician immediately, and questions about the law where she lives need a lawyer. No church should ever make a woman afraid to seek emergency care.

## The widow of Nain

Luke tells a story that holds the whole span in a single scene. At the gate of a town called Nain, Jesus meets a funeral procession coming out: "behold, a man who had died was being carried out, the only son of his mother, and she was a widow, and a considerable crowd from the town was with her" (Luke 7:12, ESV). Everything a first-century woman had to lean on was on that bier. Her son was her provision, her protection and her future, and she had carried him in her own body before she carried him to his grave. Womb to tomb, in one procession.

"And when the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her and said to her, 'Do not weep'" (Luke 7:13, ESV). He sees the mother. Then he touches the bier, which a man careful of ritual purity would have avoided, and speaks to the dead young man, and the dead man sits up and begins to talk. And then Luke writes one of the quietest sentences in the Gospels: "And Jesus gave him to his mother" (Luke 7:15, ESV). He does not raise the son and walk on. He gives the child back into the arms that bore him, and in that one act he restores them both. The Lord of life does not choose between the mother and the child. He will not be made to.

The crowd from the town is worth noticing too. They came out with her toward the burial ground, but they could not do what she most needed, and they did not pretend they could. That is roughly the church's part. We cannot raise the dead, and we should stop acting as though a law, however just, could raise anything. What we can do is be the crowd that walks with her, carry what can be carried, and belong to the One who meets processions at the gate. The cross is where he took our deaths into his own body, the deaths we caused and the deaths we ignored, and the empty tomb is the promise that the image of God in the smallest and the oldest and the most forgotten is not going into the ground for good.

Somewhere within a few miles of every church in this country, a woman is doing the arithmetic tonight: the rent, the man who left, the parent who won't speak to her, the job that won't hold her place. She already knows what the church thinks about abortion. What she doesn't know is whether anyone there will still be answering her texts in March. We won part of the argument about the law. The Lord asked something else, whether we saw her, and he asked it in the one judgment scene he told in full, where the King says, "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40, ESV), and neither the sheep nor the goats had recognized him. A position can be held from a distance. A people has to be near enough to be found. The question every congregation carries out of this is not whether we are pro-life. It is whether she can find us.
