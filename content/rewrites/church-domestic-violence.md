A woman sits across from her pastor, or a small-group leader, or an older woman in the church she has decided to trust, and says something she has rehearsed for months. It almost always comes out smaller than the truth. He gets angry. He goes through my phone. I'm careful about what I say at home. She is testing the room, and what the listener does next will teach her whether the truth about her house can be spoken in church at all. Too often we have answered in a way that was sincere, scriptural in its vocabulary, and dangerous. We have asked what she might have done to set him off, told her to pray harder and submit more sweetly, and suggested the two of them see a counselor together. We have heard a marriage problem, and we have sent her home to it.

So before the argument, the care. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. The National Domestic Violence Hotline answers around the clock at 1-800-799-7233, and you can also text START to 88788; its advocates are trained to help you think through your safety, and the call is confidential. If you are thinking about ending your life, call or text 988. And hear this as plainly as it can be said: no teaching on submission, on marriage, on forgiveness or on the permanence of vows ever requires anyone to endure abuse. Nothing in Scripture asks you to stay where you are being hurt. Nothing here is legal advice, either. The law on protection orders, custody and reporting differs from state to state, and an advocate or an attorney where you live can tell you what applies to you.

The argument that follows turns on one distinction the church has blurred again and again: the difference between preserving a marriage and protecting a person. Both matter. Marriage is a covenant, and the Christians who defend its permanence are defending something Scripture teaches. But when one spouse is using the bond to frighten, control or injure the other, the question in front of the church is no longer how to keep a marriage together. It is how to keep a person safe. A church that confuses those questions will keep handing the person over to the institution and calling it faithfulness. I write as a pastor who has not always asked the first question first.

## What abuse is, and why the church keeps missing it

Start with a definition, because the word has been stretched in some rooms and shrunk in others. Abuse in an intimate relationship is something other than the ordinary friction of two sinners sharing a house, the sharp word both regret by morning. It is a pattern in which one partner uses fear to gain and keep control over the other, and physical violence is often not its center. The sociologist Evan Stark argued in *Coercive Control* (2007) that the older focus on discrete assaults had missed the actual shape of the harm, which is a sustained campaign of isolation, intimidation, surveillance, financial restriction and humiliation that shrinks another person's freedom until she arranges her whole life around avoiding his displeasure. A woman can live inside that for years without a visible mark.

The sociologist Michael P. Johnson, in *A Typology of Domestic Violence* (2008), drew a distinction pastors badly need. Some violence between partners grows out of arguments that escalate, wrong and sometimes dangerous but not part of a larger design; he called it situational couple violence. The other kind, which he named intimate terrorism, is violence set inside a pattern of coercive control, and in his account it is the kind more likely to escalate and to cause serious injury. Lundy Bancroft, who spent years running programs for abusive men, made a related point in *Why Does He Do That?* (2002): abuse comes less from a man losing his temper than from what he believes he is owed. That is why so many abusive men are calm and even admired in public. He can govern himself well enough. He has decided he is entitled to govern her.

The scale of this is not small. The National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, in its 2010 summary report published in 2011, estimated that 24.3 percent of American women and 13.8 percent of American men had experienced severe physical violence by an intimate partner at some point in their lives. That category is narrower than abuse itself; it counts serious physical assault and leaves out the coercion that Stark describes. The church must hear the men in those numbers too; the shame a man feels in saying it can be its own locked door. Most of what follows speaks of a woman harmed by a man, because that is the pattern church teaching has most often been bent to shelter, but everything in it holds for anyone hurt in their own home.

Why does the church miss it so often? The sociologist Nancy Nason-Clark, in *The Battered Wife* (1997), found that abused women of faith often seek help from their pastors, and that many pastors feel unprepared for what they hear; in later work she gave the silence around the subject in congregations a name, the "holy hush." The reasons for it are not hard to find. The abusive husband is often an asset to the church, generous and gifted. The woman may look unstable, because living in fear makes a person look unstable. A separation reads as failure. And the pastor, hearing two accounts, wants to be fair.

That instinct deserves respect before correction. "The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him" (Proverbs 18:17, ESV). Wisdom does not take sides on the strength of the first story told. But fairness between two parties assumes that both are free to speak and that neither is afraid of the other, and abuse is precisely the destruction of that condition. She is telling the truth while calculating what it will cost her at home. He is telling a story in comfort, and a process that weighs the two as equals has already tipped the scale. The church keeps missing abuse because it keeps looking for a fight, and abuse is not a fight. It is a regime.

## How the curtain was drawn

None of this began with the modern church. William Blackstone, whose *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (1765) shaped American law for a century, recorded the husband's old common-law power of moderate correction over his wife, set beside a master's over his apprentices and a father's over his children. He noted that the power had been doubted since the reign of Charles II, but he set it down as part of the inheritance, and American courts let go of it slowly. In 1868 the Supreme Court of North Carolina, in *State v. Rhodes*, declined to punish a man for beating his wife, reasoning that courts should not lift the curtain on domestic life except in the gravest cases. In 1871, in *Fulgham v. State*, the Supreme Court of Alabama rejected the husband's supposed privilege outright. But the principle that won in the law books took another century to reach the police station and the neighborhood. The curtain stayed drawn.

What finally drew it back was not, for the most part, the church. Some of the first modern refuges for battered women opened in the early 1970s, among them Chiswick in London in 1971 and Women's Advocates in St. Paul, Minnesota, in 1974. Congress passed the Violence Against Women Act in 1994, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline opened in 1996. In 2015 England and Wales made controlling or coercive behavior in an intimate relationship a crime in itself, carrying something close to Stark's argument into statute. Many local churches quietly sheltered women along the way, but the understanding that grew up across those decades reached our counseling rooms late, and in some places it has not arrived.

Two older habits of mind kept it out, and they came from opposite directions. The first was the conservative instinct to treat the intact family as a fortress under siege. After California passed the first no-fault divorce law in 1969 and divorce rates climbed through the following decade, many churches came to count every marriage that held together as a victory and every separation as a defeat. Divorce has real casualties, children first among them. But a church that measures its faithfulness by the marriages it keeps intact has given itself a reason to keep the dangerous ones intact too.

The second habit wore a lab coat instead of a Sunday suit. Philip Rieff described in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966) a culture in which the moral categories of sin and obligation were giving way to the categories of adjustment and well-being, where the question was less whether a person was good than whether he was functioning. Pastoral counseling absorbed much of this language, some of it helpfully. But one habit did real damage: seeing every marital problem as a dance two people create together, each partner's steps provoking the other's. For ordinary conflict that can be wise. Applied to abuse, it spreads the responsibility for one person's violence across two people and asks the one being harmed to change her steps.

The fortress and the therapy room look like rivals. They made the same mistake. One made the marriage too sacred to question and the other made the violence too mutual to name, and both left a frightened woman in the same house with the same man.

## Three ways we send her home

The first failure is the plainest: counseling a victim back into danger. It usually sounds like patience: go home, be gentle, pray for him, give God time to work. In a recorded interview from 2000, Paige Patterson, then one of the most influential leaders in the Southern Baptist Convention, described counseling a woman whose husband was abusing her to pray for him and to be as submissive as she could rather than seek a divorce, and recounted that she later came back to him injured. The recording resurfaced in the spring of 2018. Patterson said he had never condoned abuse, but the recording, together with questions about how he had handled reports of sexual assault, led trustees to remove him as president of Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary that May. It would be comfortable to treat that as one man's lapse. What set it apart was that it was recorded; many women have heard the same counsel from pastors who meant well and had never been taught to ask whether she was safe to go home.

The instinct behind that counsel deserves its strongest statement. The pastor who urges a wife to stay believes that God changes hearts, and he is right. He believes divorce harms children, and he is often right about that too. He may point to Peter's word that husbands who "do not obey the word" may be "won without a word by the conduct of their wives" (1 Peter 3:1, ESV). But Peter was writing to wives whose husbands did not share their faith, in a world where a woman's conversion could itself be taken as disloyalty to the household. He was speaking to religious difference, and nothing in the letter asks a woman to stay within reach of a man who is hurting her. Separation, far from closing the door on a changed heart, is often the only thing that opens it. A man who faces no consequence for what he does at home has little reason to repent of it.

The second failure is couples counseling. Joint counseling is a real gift to many struggling marriages, but many specialists in domestic violence advise against it where there is ongoing abuse, and Bancroft argues the point directly. Couples counseling assumes two people who can speak freely in the same room, and she cannot. Whatever she says there may be answered later, at home, where no counselor is present. And the format frames the problem as shared, which quietly confirms what he has been telling her all along: that his anger is her doing. Some researchers have studied carefully screened joint programs for couples whose conflict involves no pattern of control or fear, but those programs screen out the very cases pastors are usually asked about, and a pastor is not equipped to make that judgment. When a woman describes fear, the answer is separate help for each of them, beginning with an advocate for her.

The third failure is the misuse of Scripture itself, above all the texts on submission and forgiveness. Submission is treated at length elsewhere on this site, but one thing must be said here: Paul's instruction to wives in Ephesians 5 sits inside a sentence that begins with mutual submission "out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21, ESV) and is followed at once by commands to husbands that take up more room and cost far more. No honest reading of that passage produces a husband's right to be obeyed by force or by fear. A man who uses the verse that way has turned it inside out, and the pastor who backs him has helped.

Forgiveness has been bent just as badly. A woman is told she must forgive, and she hears that she must go back, act as though nothing happened and trust him again. But forgiveness, reconciliation and trust are three different things (the difference is traced more fully in this site's essay on abuse in the church), and in a violent house the third matters most: trust is rebuilt slowly by changed behavior, never demanded on the strength of tears. Justin and Lindsey Holcomb make this case with care in *Is It My Fault?* (2014): abuse is the abuser's choice and never the victim's fault, and forgiving him does not oblige her to remain in danger. Leslie Vernick, in *The Emotionally Destructive Marriage* (2013), draws a distinction every pastor should carry into the study: a difficult marriage and a destructive one are different things, and in a destructive marriage, quietly absorbing the harm without consequence only helps him go on sinning. Sometimes the most loving thing a wife can do is stop making his abuse easy for him.

## What God says about violence in a house

Scripture is not neutral about violence, and it is not vague. "The LORD tests the righteous, but his soul hates the wicked and the one who loves violence" (Psalm 11:5, ESV). The Hebrew word there is *hamas*, the word Genesis uses for the violence that filled the earth before the flood, the violence of the strong against the weak. God's very soul is set against the one who loves it, and there is no clause exempting the home.

The same word appears in one of the most argued-over verses in the Old Testament. Malachi 2:16 is hard Hebrew, and translations divide over who is doing the hating. The King James tradition renders it as God's own declaration that he hates divorce, and generations of women have heard that line quoted at them as a reason to stay. The ESV, with several other recent translations, takes the husband as the subject: "For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her, says the LORD, the God of Israel, covers his garment with violence, says the LORD of hosts" (Malachi 2:16, ESV). Both readings have serious defenders, and the question of divorce belongs to this site's essay on divorce and remarriage. What matters here is the word *hamas* and the man it clings to. In context Malachi is indicting husbands who were discarding the wives of their youth, and on either reading the violence stains the treacherous husband's garment, not the wife's.

The New Testament speaks to husbands with a severity the church has not always matched. "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25, ESV). The measure of a husband's love is a death, spent toward her good at his cost. Paul presses the point: "In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies. He who loves his wife loves himself" (Ephesians 5:28, ESV). And again: "For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church" (Ephesians 5:29, ESV). Nourish and cherish are the verbs of someone tending a child. A man who frightens, controls or strikes his wife is doing the opposite of every verb in the passage, whatever he calls it. In a shorter letter Paul reaches back past the blow to the temper that comes before it; the verb he uses carries the sense of bitterness: "Husbands, love your wives, and do not be harsh with them" (Colossians 3:19, ESV).

Peter speaks directly to the difference in strength that abuse exploits. "Likewise, husbands, live with your wives in an understanding way, showing honor to the woman as the weaker vessel, since they are heirs with you of the grace of life, so that your prayers may not be hindered" (1 Peter 3:7, ESV). Whatever Peter means by the weaker vessel, the conclusion he draws from it is honor. Where a husband is stronger, the text makes that strength a reason for care and gives it no license to be used against her.

Scripture also treats getting away from danger as ordinary wisdom rather than faithlessness. "The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for it" (Proverbs 22:3, ESV). When Saul sent men to kill David, his wife Michal helped him out through a window (1 Samuel 19). When a plot formed against Paul in Damascus, "his disciples took him by night and let him down through an opening in the wall, lowering him in a basket" (Acts 9:25, ESV). Nobody accuses David or Paul of a failure of faith. Leaving danger is how the faithful stay alive to keep being faithful.

Here the central distinction comes into full view. Challenged over disciples who had picked grain to eat on the Sabbath, Jesus said, "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27, ESV). He did not abolish the Sabbath. He restored its purpose. Marriage is a holy gift, and it is not disposable. But it was given for the persons in it, and when one of them turns it into an instrument for destroying the other, the church that defends the instrument against the person has forgotten what the gift was for. Separation in that case names a breach that has already happened, and the one who caused it is the one who brought fear into the house. The questions also have an order. How a marriage is ordered and when divorce is permitted are real questions on which faithful churches differ. Whether a woman should be protected from violence is not one of them, because it rests on the character of the God whose soul hates the one who loves violence.

## The man who lays power down

No honest account of abuse in the church can avoid what we have taught men about strength. The phrase most often reached for is "toxic masculinity," and it deserves more care than its champions or its enemies usually give it. Its early history is thinly documented, but it is often traced to the mythopoetic men's movement of the 1980s and 1990s, where the writer Shepherd Bliss is credited with using it for a harsh, authoritarian distortion of manhood as distinct from manhood itself. The psychiatrist Terry Kupers, writing in 2005 about the obstacles to mental health treatment in prison, gave it the definition most later writers use: a cluster of socially regressive traits that serve domination, the devaluing of women, homophobia and gratuitous violence. That use sits close to what R. W. Connell, in *Masculinities* (1995), called hegemonic masculinity, the ideal of manhood a culture ranks above the others. But in popular speech the phrase has often drifted from naming a corruption to suspecting maleness as such, which is what many critics, most of them on the right, heard in the American Psychological Association's 2018 guidelines on treating boys and men. Richard Reeves, who in *Of Boys and Men* (2022) faults the left for dismissing men's struggles and the right for wanting to restore older roles, argued that telling boys their masculinity is the problem does not make them gentler. It leaves them to listen to worse voices. The term is useful when it names a distortion and harmful when it becomes contempt.

The church has failed on both sides of that line. On the right, a generation of evangelical men was offered a warrior's picture of godliness. John Eldredge's *Wild at Heart* (2001) told men they were made for a battle to fight, an adventure to live and a beauty to rescue, and rougher preachers turned that romance into swagger; Mark Driscoll built a famous church on combative manhood and resigned in 2014 amid charges of domineering leadership. Kristin Kobes Du Mez, in *Jesus and John Wayne* (2020), traces how a militant ideal of manhood came to feel like orthodoxy to many white evangelicals. Her critics, many of them conservative reviewers, answer that the book draws heavily on celebrities and bestsellers and says less about what ordinary congregations actually taught and did. The complementarian answer deserves a fair hearing. Its leading voices have condemned abuse plainly, and W. Bradford Wilcox, analyzing national survey data in *Soft Patriarchs, New Men* (2004), found that conservative Protestant husbands who attended church regularly reported the lowest rates of domestic violence of the groups he compared, while those who claimed the label but rarely attended reported the highest. That is an association in self-reported data, not proof of cause, but it cuts against the claim that the doctrine of headship produces abusers. It does not answer the survivors' charge that some teaching on headship gives cover to abusers already there. Both can be true, and together they point to a root deeper than any doctrine: a picture of strength as the power to get one's way, which can dress itself in any theology.

On the left, the failure is quieter but real. A culture fluent about trauma and silent about evil can offer a survivor coping where she is owed justice, and a movement sure that the right views on gender protect a community has watched its own institutions shelter abusers. Contempt for men as a class has also left many boys to be raised by the loudest voices online. The instrument that exposes both failures is the same: each treats strength as the ability to impose one's will, the one celebrating it and the other only suspecting it. Neither can imagine a strength that serves.

Jesus could. Having named the way the rulers of the nations lord it over their people, he told his disciples, "But it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Mark 10:43, ESV). "For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45, ESV). Paul says the same of the eternal Son, "who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" (Philippians 2:6, ESV). All power belonged to him, and he used it to kneel, to heal and finally to die. I have five sons, and I know how much easier it is to hand a boy a script than to show him a man washing feet; the country they are growing up in offers them two loud scripts, one that dominates and one that apologizes for existing. The Christ of the Gospels answers both. His strength is the strength to absorb harm rather than inflict it, and any manhood that takes the name of Christian and uses strength to frighten a woman has taken his name in vain.

## What a church that protects people does

A church that has understood all this will sound different long before anyone discloses anything. It will say from the pulpit, in plain words and more than once a year, that abuse is sin, that coercion and intimidation count even when they leave no mark, and that no teaching on submission or marriage ever requires anyone to endure it. The woman in the fourth row should know which kind of church she is sitting in before she risks a single sentence. When she does speak, the first question will be whether she is safe, and it will come before any question about the marriage. She will be believed enough to be helped. No one will send the two of them to couples counseling while she is afraid, and no one will confront her husband without her consent and an advocate's counsel, because advocates widely warn that confronting an abuser, like the period around leaving, can sharpen the danger. Safety planning belongs with people trained for it, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline can help anyone think it through confidentially, including how to reach help if a phone may be watched. Where children are at risk, the church will know what its state's law requires and will not wait to find out. That is not legal advice, and a church should get its own.

Such a church will also remove a man who abuses his wife from leadership, whatever his gifts, because his place on the platform tells every frightened person in the building what the church will tolerate. And it will not give up on him. The gospel is for abusers too. But repentance here is measured in years of changed behavior, often through specialized intervention programs, never in a tearful apology offered to get his family back. A church that offers him quick grace offers him nothing, and offers her less.

If you are the one being hurt, hear this again, as the last word before the weight. Call 911 if you are in immediate danger. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. If you are thinking about ending your life, call or text 988. Getting out of danger is not a failure of faith, and no vow you made ever asked you to be destroyed.

And for the rest of us, the weight is this. The church names itself by a cross, the place where God in Christ took human violence into his own body rather than doing violence to us. He bore it so that it could be judged and ended, not so that the weak could be told to go on bearing it. We have sometimes preached that cross to a frightened woman as a reason to go home, turning the one place where God stood between the violent and their victims into an argument for keeping her within his reach. I have to count myself among those who were slow. The woman who will test the room is already sitting in some church this Sunday, rehearsing the sentence that comes out smaller than the truth. What she hears when she says it will tell her what we believe about God.
