Two readers open Paul's letter to the Romans at the thirteenth chapter. One closes it convinced that God has blessed the order of things exactly as it stands and that the whole of Christian political duty is to submit. The other reads on through the chapter, where Paul sums up every commandment in one word, "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Romans 13:9), and closes it convinced that no order which crushes the neighbor can claim God's blessing at all. Both are sincere. Both can read. Each walks out carrying a different God, and each is sure the other brought something to the text that he himself did not.

That scene has played out in every century, and it isn't mainly a problem with the book. The readers most dangerous to the Bible have always been the ones most certain they bring nothing to it. I know that certainty. I came to the Bible the way a former atheist comes to anything, intending to master it. I read it first for the contradictions, and after I believed, for the arguments and proof texts I could deploy. I read it fast and well by every standard I knew, and for a long time I couldn't understand why a book I now confessed as the word of God left me so unchanged. The standards were the problem.

People who ask how to read the Bible usually want either method, how to tell a promise from a proverb, or relief, because they tried and it went flat. The church hands the first group a study guide and the second a devotional, as if rigor and devotion were products for different customers. They are one need. There is a way of reading that aims to master the Bible, and a way that consents to be read by it. The first alone becomes a sophisticated way of keeping the text at arm's length. The second alone becomes a pious way of hearing your own voice. The church's best readers never chose between them.

## The proof-text habit and the invention of the verse

Start with the plain reader's strongest case. Whoever says "just tell me what it says and I'll do it" is protecting the conviction that God has spoken and that his word stands over us rather than under us. The Westminster Confession of 1646 grants that not everything in Scripture is equally plain to everyone, but insists that what is necessary for salvation is set out clearly enough that the unlearned as well as the learned, using ordinary means, can understand it. That doctrine was a gift and remains one. A book that can be interpreted into anything can no longer command you, and the plain reader is guarding the possibility that the Bible can still tell him to do what he doesn't want to do.

Grant all of that, and then notice how most of us actually read, which is in verses. Our chapter divisions go back to Stephen Langton in the early thirteenth century. The New Testament's verse numbers were added by the printer Robert Estienne in his Greek edition of 1551, many of them, his son later reported, marked while he traveled between Paris and Lyon. These were finding aids, and very good ones. But once every sentence carries its own address, it can be lifted out, printed on a mug or fired in an argument, and it looks complete. A verse cut loose from its paragraph, its book and its canon becomes a mirror that returns whatever face leans over it.

Take the favorite verse of American graduation cards: "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope" (Jeremiah 29:11). It's true, and larger and harder than the card. Jeremiah wrote it to people already deported to Babylon, and the "you" is plural. The same letter says the exile will last until "seventy years are completed for Babylon" (Jeremiah 29:10), so most adults who first heard it would die in a foreign land, and it tells them what to do meanwhile: "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7). The promise isn't that your plans will succeed. It's that God's purpose for his people outlasts a lifetime of displacement, and that they should pray for their captors while they wait.

The prosperity preacher does the same thing with a greeting. "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul" (3 John 2) became a promise of wealth and health. Gordon Fee, himself a Pentecostal scholar, showed in *The Disease of the Health and Wealth Gospels* (1985) that it is the conventional wish at the opening of an ancient letter, roughly our "I hope this finds you well." The man who makes that move believes he is honoring Scripture, which is why the habit is so hard to see from inside.

## Context, genre and canon

The correction for the proof-text habit belongs to ordinary readers. Gordon Fee and Douglas Stuart wrote *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth* (1981) for exactly them, and its central distinction has helped a great many ordinary Christians read well. Exegesis asks what the text meant to its first hearers, in their language and situation. Hermeneutics asks what it means for us now, and it comes second. Their governing rule, in substance, is that a text cannot mean for us what it could never have meant for its author and first readers. The rule keeps the reader from pouring the present into the text and calling the result revelation.

They also taught a generation to notice genre. The Bible is law and narrative, lament and love poetry, prophecy and apocalypse, letter and Gospel, and each makes its claims in its own way. Two adjacent lines of Proverbs make the point: "Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest you be like him yourself. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:4–5). Read as statutes, they contradict each other. Read as proverbs, they teach what proverbs teach, that wisdom is knowing which true thing fits this situation. A reader who asks of every sentence "what rule is this?" will misread most of the Bible with a clear conscience.

The third discipline is canon. The Westminster Confession puts it compactly: Scripture interprets Scripture, so when the true sense of a passage is in question, a sense "which is not manifold, but one," it must be sought from places that speak more clearly. Jesus gave the principle a center. On the day of the resurrection, two disciples walked toward Emmaus with a reading of Scripture that had just been broken: "But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel" (Luke 24:21). They had read their Bibles toward the Messiah they wanted, a deliverer without a cross. The stranger beside them asked, "Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?" (Luke 24:26), and then, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27). The Christ-centered reading isn't a hunt for Jesus under every rock in Leviticus, which becomes its own cleverness. It reads the whole story as moving toward the crucified and risen Christ, and lets that center correct every reading that wants glory without suffering.

The Emmaus disciples also needed a teacher, and so do we. In Acts, Philip finds an Ethiopian official reading Isaiah aloud in his chariot and asks, "Do you understand what you are reading?" The man answers, "How can I, unless someone guides me?" (Acts 8:30–31). A royal treasurer, he was asking exactly the right question. The modern reader who imagines himself alone with the text and a concordance has refused the guide the Ethiopian was glad to take.

Beneath all this sits the question the late twentieth century pressed hardest. The literary critic Stanley Fish argued in *Is There a Text in This Class?* (1980) that meaning is produced by interpretive communities, so that what a text says is finally what a community has been trained to find. The church should concede the truth in that: we read from somewhere. Kevin Vanhoozer answered with his own title, *Is There a Meaning in This Text?* (1998). A text, he argues, is something an author has done, a communicative act, so interpretation is a moral act as well as an intellectual one. The reader owes the author a hearing, as you owe any neighbor the courtesy of finishing his sentence before you tell him what he meant. If the text is only what we bring, it can never correct us, and the prophet can't indict the king. A book that can mean anything has never once set a captive free.

## Four senses, one sense, and the rule of love

Each era of the church got something right here that the next forgot. In the early third century Origen taught in *On First Principles* that Scripture, like a human being, has a body, a soul and a spirit: a plain sense, a moral sense and a spiritual sense. In the 420s John Cassian gave the West its classic illustration in his *Conferences*. Jerusalem is literally the city of the Jews, allegorically the church, morally the soul, and in its highest sense the heavenly city. The medieval church taught this fourfold sense widely: the letter tells what happened, allegory what to believe, the moral sense what to do, and the anagogical sense where you are going. It knew that Scripture is inexhaustible and meant to form its readers. Its danger was that a clever enough reader can make an allegory say anything. Thomas Aquinas set a boundary in the *Summa Theologiae*: every spiritual sense is founded on the literal, and only the literal can bear the weight of a theological argument.

The Reformers pushed that boundary further. William Tyndale argued in *The Obedience of a Christian Man* (1528) that Scripture has one sense, the literal, and that preachers had used the other three to make it say whatever suited them. Calvin, in the letter dedicating his commentary on Romans (published in 1540), held that an interpreter's chief task is to lay open the mind of the writer. They wanted the text's own voice against the reader's ingenuity, and they were right. But their literal sense was the sense God intended through the human author, and in the Old Testament it often reached forward to Christ; Luther could call the Old Testament the swaddling clothes and manger in which Christ lies. What many of their heirs inherited, especially in the commonsense Protestantism of nineteenth-century America, was a literalism without the Christward center or the formative aim. It kept the letter and dropped the love.

Love is where Augustine had placed the whole enterprise a thousand years before. In the first book of *On Christian Teaching*, begun around 397 and finished in 426, he took Jesus' summary of the law, "On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 22:40), and drew a rule from it. Whoever thinks he has understood the Scriptures, but whose understanding does not build up the double love of God and neighbor, has not yet understood them. Augustine was generous to the reader who misses what the author meant while his reading still builds love; he is like a traveler who leaves the road by mistake and reaches the destination through a field. Then Augustine adds what we tend to drop: the traveler must still be corrected, because the habit of leaving the road will eventually take him somewhere he never meant to go.

The literal sense is the floor, and love is the aim. A reading that is technically defensible and leaves the reader prouder and more comfortable with his neighbor's suffering has failed. The rule of charity without the literal sense becomes permission to call any warm reading true. The literal sense without charity becomes the most learned way there is to miss the point.

## When the plainest readers baptized an atrocity

The Americans of the early nineteenth century held one of the highest and plainest views of the Bible any people has held, and many of them read it straight into the defense of human bondage.

Among their most durable proof texts was the curse of Ham. After the flood, Noah wakes, learns what his son Ham has done, and says, "Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants shall he be to his brothers" (Genesis 9:25). Stephen Haynes traces in *Noah's Curse* (2002) how the verse became load-bearing in American proslavery thought, read as God's sentence on African peoples. Ordinary attention to context collapses that reading. The curse falls on Canaan, not Ham. It says nothing of Africa or of skin. In the flow of Genesis it anticipates Israel's later relation to the Canaanites, and it's Noah's word, not God's. Those who leaned on the verse were not reading carefully at all, and didn't notice, because the reading told them what their economy needed.

Mark Noll describes in *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis* (2006) a crisis the nation's Protestant method couldn't resolve. Proslavery readers pointed to texts that regulated slavery without abolishing it, and in a culture trained to settle questions by the plain sense of single verses, they often seemed to win. The abolitionists appealed to the whole Bible, to the image of God in every person and to Paul sending Onesimus back "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother" (Philemon 16). One side read the canon; the other read verses. It took a war to settle what the churches couldn't.

Some readers were never confused. A Bible printed in London in 1807 for enslaved people in the British West Indies left out the passages about liberation, the exodus among them. Its editors knew what the whole book would do. Albert Raboteau shows in *Slave Religion* (1978) that enslaved Christians, often forbidden to learn to read, heard the canon better than their masters. They found themselves in Israel in Egypt, before a God who says, "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exodus 20:2). Frederick Douglass, in the appendix to his *Narrative* of 1845, wrote that between the Christianity of the slaveholding land and the Christianity of Christ lay the widest possible difference. Far from rejecting the book, he was reading it whole.

Honesty requires turning the same instrument in every direction. It exposes the prosperity reading of 3 John 2. It exposes the preacher who reads God's answer to Solomon after the temple's dedication, "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves" (2 Chronicles 7:14), as a promise to the United States, when "my people" is Israel gathered at the place God chose for his name. And it exposes the reader who keeps the verses his moral sense already approves and files the rest as culturally conditioned. Thomas Jefferson did it literally around 1820, cutting the Gospels apart with a razor to keep a Jesus without miracles or resurrection. The progressive instinct has a real point: some things, the holy kiss among them, are bound to their culture, and every tradition has to discern them. But everything turns on who does the discerning. When the reader's conscience rules in advance which texts may speak, the slaveholder and the modern editor are making the same move from opposite ends, and neither can be corrected by the book in his hands.

I have to stand inside this. I've sat with a passage on a Saturday night that meant to break something in me and in the people I'd preach it to, and felt my mind go to work softening it, finding the reading that would cost the congregation something and cost me nothing. I called it interpretation. It was the oldest evasion there is, the serpent's question in the garden, "Did God actually say" (Genesis 3:1). We rarely deny the word. We adjust it until it agrees with us, and call the adjustment study.

## Reading slowly, the old way

There's a second way to make the Bible say what you want, and it needs only speed. We read everything now by scanning for the point and moving on, and we bring that reflex to Scripture and wonder why it goes flat. A pastor can read the Bible constantly for the next sermon and be fed by almost none of it. The text becomes a database, and a database answers your queries and leaves you exactly who you were.

The Bible describes itself in the vocabulary of food. The first psalm blesses the one whose "delight is in the law of the LORD, and on his law he meditates day and night" (Psalm 1:2), and pictures him as a tree planted by streams of water. The Hebrew verb behind "meditates" is *hagah*, to murmur or mutter. Eugene Peterson pointed out in *Eat This Book* (2006) that Isaiah uses it for a lion over its kill: "As a lion or a young lion growls over his prey" (Isaiah 31:4). Peterson thought of his dog with a bone, gnawing, leaving, coming back, unwilling to let go until he reached the marrow. Biblical meditation isn't emptying the mind. It's staying with the words until they give up what's in them.

Psalm 119 turns that kind of reading into a poem, 176 verses in an acrostic, eight lines for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Two of its prayers belong at the start of any reading. One asks for sight: "Open my eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of your law" (Psalm 119:18). It assumes the wonder is there and can't be seen unaided. The other confesses delight: "Oh how I love your law! It is my meditation all the day" (Psalm 119:97). That is a man read by the text long enough to love it.

The church gave the practice a name, *lectio divina*, sacred reading, and for a thousand years it was how the monasteries read Scripture. Guigo II, a Carthusian of the Grande Chartreuse, described it in a short letter often dated around 1150 and known as *The Ladder of Monks*. Reading takes a short passage in carefully. Meditation turns it over, seeking the truth hidden in it. Prayer turns to the God who spoke it and asks for what the reading has shown to be needed. Contemplation is a rest in God that can only be received. Guigo's image is a meal: reading puts the food in the mouth, meditation chews it, prayer draws out its savor, and contemplation is the sweetness itself.

What few remember is that Guigo refused to let the rungs come apart. Reading without meditation, he warned, is dry, and meditation without reading wanders into error; prayer without meditation is lukewarm, and meditation without prayer bears no fruit. The monk who never leaves the first rung has mastered a text that hasn't touched him; the one who skips it and goes straight to feeling has nothing to chew but himself. Peterson, who spent the better part of a decade translating the whole Bible, made the same refusal. Careful exegesis, he insisted, isn't the rival of spiritual reading but part of its reverence. Attending closely to exactly what someone said is one of the ways you love him.

So the old way is simple to state. Take five verses rather than five chapters. Read them slowly, then again aloud. Ask what they meant to their first hearers, with a good study Bible at your elbow. Stay with the phrase that catches you, let it become confession, thanks or request, and then be quiet for a while. You'll feel at first that you're wasting time; that feeling is the trained reflex, and it isn't telling you the truth. Reading the whole Bible in a year is good, since the whole story is the canon that corrects the verse. But coverage and communion are different goods, and a plan can be finished without the second ever happening.

Nehemiah records the scene that holds all of this together. In the fifth century before Christ, the returned exiles gathered before the Water Gate, Ezra read to them from early morning until midday, and the Levites moved among the people: "They read from the book, from the Law of God, clearly, and they gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). Then the understanding went all the way down: "For all the people wept as they heard the words of the Law" (Nehemiah 8:9). Their leaders sent them home to feast and to "send portions to anyone who has nothing ready, for this day is holy to our Lord. And do not be grieved, for the joy of the LORD is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:10). They rejoiced, the chapter says, "because they had understood the words that were declared to them" (Nehemiah 8:12). Study, grief, joy and bread for the poor sit in one scene. A reading that ends in despair has stopped too soon, one that never passes through grief was never serious, and one that never sends a portion to the one who has nothing hasn't understood the book.

## Mastering the text, and being read by it

Søren Kierkegaard saw the deepest form of the problem in 1851. In *For Self-Examination* he imagined a lover who receives a letter from his beloved in a language he doesn't understand. He works through it with a dictionary, and no one would call that labor wasted. But when he finds a request in it, he doesn't stop to write a commentary on its grammar. He goes and does what she asked. Kierkegaard's charge was that Christendom had made scholarship a reason for delay, as if every question had to be settled before obedience could be expected, and learned reading a defense against the text coming too close. He told his reader, whenever he opened the book, to say to himself: it is I to whom this speaks, and I about whom it speaks.

The first part of that book is a reading of the letter of James, which had already named the mechanism: "But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves. For if anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer, he is like a man who looks intently at his natural face in a mirror. For he looks at himself and goes away and at once forgets what he was like" (James 1:22–24). The man looks intently and walks away unchanged. That is the failure the scholar and the devotional reader share, and it's why the distinction this essay turns on isn't between study and devotion. It's between mastering the text and being read by it. You can master the Bible by study, as an expert who knows its difficulties better than its demands, or by devotion, taking the feelings you came for and leaving the rest. Either way the reader stays in charge. To be read by the Bible is to hand that charge over.

Paul's most quoted sentence about Scripture reads differently in its setting. He warns Timothy about people "always learning and never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (2 Timothy 3:7), and about a time when "people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" (2 Timothy 4:3). Those two warnings, a precise description of making the Bible say what you want, bracket the famous lines. Between them, Paul tells Timothy to continue in what he has learned, "knowing from whom you learned it" (2 Timothy 3:14), his mother and grandmother among them, and then says what Scripture is for: "All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work" (2 Timothy 3:16–17). The sentence lands on a person the text has corrected, trained and completed, and two of its four uses are things done to you against your will.

Augustine knew both kinds of reading from inside. In the *Confessions* he tells how, as a young rhetorician formed on Cicero, he found the Bible's style beneath him, and says his pride kept him from its depth. Years later, in a garden in Milan in 386, he heard what sounded like a child's chant, "take up and read," opened Paul, and read: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires" (Romans 13:14). That sentence read him. The chapter the two readers quarreled over at the start of this essay is the one that took Augustine apart, and the man undone by it went on to write the church's most careful rules for reading. The rigor and the surrender weren't competing in him. The surrender made the rigor honest.

No technique, finally, will keep us from bending the Bible toward ourselves. The will that wants to be justified goes into the holiest book ever written and comes out holding a verse that proves it was right all along. The fault lies deeper than method, in the condition the cross exists to address, and only a reader who has agreed to be exposed can stop making the text a mirror. The Emmaus disciples had built from their Scriptures a Messiah who would spare them the cross. What Jesus gave them was himself, reading the whole of it toward his own suffering, and afterward they said, "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?" (Luke 24:32). The fire came through the correction, not around it.

So bring to the text everything the church has learned: context, genre, canon, the literal sense, the long company of readers who guard you from your own face. They are how the text gets to say what it says rather than what you came to hear. Then treat the passage that wounds you as more likely to be God's word to you than the one that flatters you, and stay there. None of this is finished in a year or a lifetime, and the reader who is sure he has finally read the Bible without bending it has stopped reading it. What remains is to open it again tomorrow, expecting to be told something true and unwelcome, and then to go and do what the letter asks.
