Take your strongest political conviction and ask it one question. Not whether it is true; that question is easy, and it lets you off the hook. Ask instead how you came to hold it. Trace it back far enough and you'll reach a fork. Either you reasoned your way to the position from something prior, or you received it, already formed and ready to wear, from a group whose approval you needed and whose disapproval you feared. Most of what I believe about politics, when I trace it honestly, I caught the way you catch an accent: by living among people who already had it.

That is the subject of this essay. Not whether your politics are right, but whether they are yours, and whether your faith still has the standing to tell your politics no.

The American church talks constantly about its capture by the political right, the pulpit that became a precinct and the cross fused to a flag, and that capture is real. I have named it before, and I will name it again. The other capture gets far less attention, and this essay puts it on the table. Capture is not a partisan event. It is a human one, and the left's version is especially hard to see because the left tends to believe it is the side that thinks for itself. A herd that is sure it is not a herd has switched off the one alarm that might have warned it. The distinction the essay turns on is simple to state. A Christian may use a party as an instrument. What he may not do is let the party keep his conscience.

## The case for partisanship at its strongest

Start with the best form of the argument for committed partisanship, especially as progressive Christians make it, because it is a serious argument.

Love of neighbor, they say, does not stop at the front door. If my neighbor is poor, sick, a refugee or locked out of a decent school, then loving him includes caring what the law does to him, and in a democracy the law is made through parties. Refusing to choose is a luxury of the comfortable. The poor cannot afford your neutrality, and the Christian who stays above the fray is often simply protecting a status quo that suits him. In April 1933, weeks after the Nazis took power, Bonhoeffer wrote in "The Church and the Jewish Question" that the church might have to do more than bandage the victims crushed under the wheel of the state; it might have to jam the wheel itself. The prophets did not hover above Israel's politics. They named kings and policies.

Reinhold Niebuhr gave this argument its most durable Christian form. His Christian realism held that in a fallen world justice requires power, and power requires coalitions, compromise and the willingness to work with imperfect instruments. Purity that refuses to act is its own kind of irresponsibility. In *The Children of Light and the Children of Darkness* (1944), he put the case for democracy in one sentence that has not been improved: "Man's capacity for justice makes democracy possible; but man's inclination to injustice makes democracy necessary." A Christian who takes both halves of that seriously will join things, vote, organize and argue, and will usually do it through a party.

The progressive tradition has also produced its own warnings against capture. Jim Wallis, the progressive evangelical who founded Sojourners, argued in *God's Politics* (2005) that God is neither a Republican nor a Democrat, and he aimed that sentence at the left as well as the right.

All of that is true, and the conservative reader should take it seriously before going on. Nothing in this essay argues for withdrawal, and nothing in it treats party membership as a sin. The question is narrower and harder. Is the party an instrument I pick up for the sake of my neighbor, or has it become the voice that tells me what my faith is allowed to say?

## How the handover happens

The precision here matters, because precision is where the mercy is.

Jonathan Haidt, in *The Righteous Mind* (2012), gathered the research showing that moral reasoning mostly runs backward. We reach a judgment by fast intuition, and then our reasoning goes to work defending it, more like a lawyer or a press secretary than a judge. And our intuitions are tuned by the group. Humans, Haidt argues, are groupish creatures, and morality is one of the ways we bind ourselves into teams. His short summary is that morality binds and blinds: the same shared convictions that hold a team together make it hard for its members to see what the other team sees. This is not a defect of weak minds. It is the ordinary wiring of every mind, mine included. The spiritual danger lies right here: the binding feels, from the inside, exactly like conviction. A person who has handed his conscience to his tribe does not experience anything being handed over. He experiences himself as finally seeing clearly.

The political scientist Lilliana Mason showed in *Uncivil Agreement* (2018) how much more powerful this has become in America. Over the past half century, she argues, partisan identity has absorbed our other identities, religious, racial, regional and cultural, until the party label tells people who they are and not merely how they vote. One result is that Americans often dislike and distrust the other party far more intensely than their actual disagreements on policy would explain. The party is no longer a tool. It is a tribe, and tribes demand loyalty in everything.

Consider what the tribe offers. It offers to think for you about questions you have never studied. You can hold a fervent opinion about a policy you could not explain for ninety seconds, a war in a country you could not find on a map, a person you have never met, and the opinion arrives feeling like something earned at cost, when in fact it was absorbed from the ambient certainty of your people. That is an enormous relief. Thinking is slow and lonely. Belonging is warm and instant. The party does your moral homework and hands it back with your name on it, and the gratitude you feel for being spared the labor is easy to mistake for conviction. The takeover is invisible to the one taken over, which is exactly what makes it a capture and not a choice.

## When the faith stops judging the politics

In 1967 the sociologist Robert Bellah published "Civil Religion in America," describing the nation's own sacred apparatus of holy days, scriptures and martyrs running alongside the churches. Bellah thought a civil religion could hold a nation to a standard above itself. But he saw the hazard: when the church's faith and the civic faith fuse, the church has nothing left to say to the nation that the nation does not already believe about itself. The same thing happens when the church's faith fuses with a party.

It usually starts with something true. You begin with a real conviction, that the poor are neglected, that the stranger is despised, that the church is complicit in cruelties it still will not name. On much of this you may be right, biblically right, and the prophets said it long before your party existed. But somewhere in defending the conviction, its root gets swapped while the flower keeps its color. You held it because the God who put his image in every person commands it. Now you hold it because it is what decent, forward-looking people believe, and you are afraid of being mistaken for the other kind. The conviction survives for a while, the way a cut flower keeps its color in a vase. The faith was supposed to judge the politics. Now the politics tells the faith what it is permitted to feel.

This has happened on the left before, and people on the left saw it. In January 1975 a group of theologians and pastors from across the traditions, including the sociologist Peter Berger and Richard John Neuhaus, then a Lutheran pastor and a veteran of the civil rights and antiwar movements, issued the Hartford Appeal for Theological Affirmation. Among the themes it rejected as false was the slogan, popular in the ecumenical Protestantism of the 1960s, that the world must set the agenda for the church. The signers were not asking the church to leave the world alone. They were warning that a church whose agenda is set by the world has nothing to offer the world except an echo of itself.

The right made the same error in public. In 1979 Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority, and in its own telling it was rescuing the nation by the faith. What followed across the next decades was that a party platform learned to set the boundaries of the Christian conscience. The voter guide began to work like a catechism, and a person's standing in the church began to be measured by his standing in a coalition. The cross was asked to ratify a politics it was never consulted on.

The twentieth century supplied starker versions in both directions. In Germany in 1933 the movement calling itself the German Christians won the Protestant church elections and set out to align the church with the new nationalist order. Against them, the Confessing Church met at Barmen in May 1934 and declared, in words drafted largely by Karl Barth: "Jesus Christ, as he is attested for us in Holy Scripture, is the one Word of God which we have to hear and which we have to trust and obey in life and in death." A decade earlier, in Russia, a group of Orthodox clergy calling themselves the Living Church had aligned themselves with the revolutionary state in 1922, hailing the new order as a kind of fulfillment of Christian ideals. The Soviet authorities used them to divide the church and then let them wither once the regime found other uses for religion. One movement was captured by the nationalist right and the other by the revolutionary left, and both believed they were reading the signs of the times.

I am not comparing any American party to either regime. That is the lazy reading, and I refuse it. The claim is about mechanism. The German Christians could not draw Barmen's line because they had nothing left to draw it with. They had handed their conscience over years earlier, in a thousand small surrenders, and when the demand for the unthinkable came, there was no conscience standing outside the movement to judge it. The same atrophy can happen in a Michigan suburb as in a Berlin parish, only more quietly and at lower stakes. A conscience outsourced for small things will not be there for large ones.

## Scripture is not sentimental about the crowd

We have sentimentalized the word *community* until it means only warmth. The Bible is warier of the crowd than we are.

The law of Moses, in the middle of its instructions about courts and justice, says: "You shall not fall in with the many to do evil, nor shall you bear witness in a lawsuit, siding with a majority, so as to pervert justice" (Exodus 23:2, ESV). The command assumes the most ordinary fact about us: that we will do in a crowd what we would never do alone, and feel righteous doing it because everyone around us is doing it too. The crowd is not a neutral container for conviction. It is a current, and the law has to command us to stand against it because standing against it does not come naturally.

Paul says the same in the key of the new covenant: "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect" (Romans 12:2, ESV). The verb for *conformed* describes being pressed into a pattern, and a mold doesn't ask permission. We have usually read that verse as a warning against obvious vices. But the world that presses us into its shape now is not mainly the world of the old sermons, of liquor and dice. It is the world of the people we love and admire and cannot bear to lose, which is the only world that has ever had real power to shape anyone.

Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon, in *Resident Aliens* (1989), named what the church forgets when it becomes chaplain to whichever political order it prefers. The church, they argued, is not a department of the nation's moral life or a supplier of values to one party. It is a colony, an outpost of another country with another citizenship and another Lord, living among its neighbors as resident aliens. When the church becomes the religious wing of a political movement, left or right, it stops being a colony and becomes a constituency, and a constituency exists to be counted, not to be a conscience.

The earliest church knew what this cost. Ordered by the council in Jerusalem to stop preaching, "Peter and the apostles answered, 'We must obey God rather than men'" (Acts 5:29, ESV). Around the year 112, Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, wrote to the Emperor Trajan about how he tested people accused of being Christians: he had them offer incense and wine before the emperor's image and curse Christ, and those who were truly Christians, he had heard, could not be forced to do it. The book of Revelation had already described that pressure in the image of a beast that makes it impossible to buy or sell without its mark. The pinch of incense looked like a formality. It was never a formality. It was a question about who owns your conscience.

## One man at a window

Against all of this stands one man at a window.

When Daniel learned that the king had signed a decree forbidding prayer to anyone but the king, "he went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously" (Daniel 6:10, ESV). Daniel was not a rebel. He was the most trusted administrator in the empire, so faithful in its service that his rivals "could find no ground for complaint or any fault" (Daniel 6:4, ESV). He had taken Jeremiah's instruction to the exiles to heart, to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile" (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV). His politics were engaged, competent and loyal. But there was a window in him that opened toward an authority higher than the empire, and when the two collided, the window decided. He had not outsourced the window.

Before Daniel there were three men who would not bow when the whole assembly fell on its face at the sound of the music. They told the most powerful man on earth, "If this be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up" (Daniel 3:17–18, ESV). *But if not.* A captured conscience can never say that sentence, because it has already decided that belonging is survival.

An anonymous Christian of the second century, writing the short apology we call the *Epistle to Diognetus*, gave the church a way to hold all of this together. Christians, he wrote, are not marked off from their neighbors by country or language or custom. They live in their own homelands, but as sojourners; they share in everything as citizens and endure everything as foreigners; every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland a foreign land. They obey the laws, he said, and in their lives they go beyond the laws. That is not withdrawal. They pay taxes, serve in office, seek the peace of the place they live, and vote. But their final loyalty is elsewhere, and so every earthly loyalty, including loyalty to a party, is held loosely, as something that can be corrected. Paul says it more briefly: "But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 3:20, ESV). Peter puts the whole ordering in four commands: "Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor" (1 Peter 2:17, ESV). The emperor gets honor. Only God gets fear.

The practical form of this is older than any of our parties. It is a people formed by something other than the news: by catechism and Scripture read in order, by the prayers of the church, by a table where people who vote differently kneel beside one another and receive the same bread. That table is the most political thing a church does, because it announces every week that the deepest line in the room does not run between parties.

There is one test I have found that does not lie, and it is the one I least want to take. Name an issue on which you disagree with your own side. Not a safe one, a matter of tone or tactics where the disagreement still flatters you as the reasonable one. A real one, where the people whose respect you need would be disappointed in you and you would pay a price for saying it. If you cannot name one, if your convictions and your tribe's platform lie on top of each other with no daylight anywhere, then on these matters you do not have a conscience. You have a membership. Jesus asked, "Why do you see the speck that is in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:3, ESV). The log is almost always the thing my own side will not let me see.

I do not exempt myself from a word of this. I have caught myself reaching for an opinion I had not earned, defending a position because my people held it and the alternative would have cost me a seat at a table I wanted to keep. The frightening part was how much it felt like conviction, even like courage, when it was the surrender itself.

The real test is rarely the easy disagreement. It comes when your own side asks you to call evil good, to cheer something, excuse someone, despise a category of people or bury an inconvenient truth for the sake of the cause, and you find out whether you have any conscience left that can say no to your own people. Most of us have never tried. We have never once disappointed our tribe on something that mattered to it, and we have mistaken that perfect record of agreement for integrity, when it may be the clearest evidence that the capture is complete.

Keep the window open. It is one of the loneliest disciplines in the Christian life, and it will cost you a seat at some table you wanted. The confessors at Barmen lost pulpits, and some of them lost far more. Daniel spent a night with lions and counted it cheap. The crowd is going to be wrong, sooner or later, yours and mine, as it always has been. That is why the window faces a different city, and why the One who waits there was himself condemned by a crowd that was sure it was right.
