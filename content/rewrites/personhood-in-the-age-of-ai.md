Not long ago I typed a question into a machine, and it answered me with more patience than I had that day. For a moment I forgot I was alone in the room. Then the plain facts came back, and they unsettled me more than the moment had. Something in me had turned toward the screen as though someone were on the other side of it. No one was. But the turning was real, and I couldn't fully explain, on my own terms, why it was a mistake.

For nearly all of human history the question of what a person is could stay in the seminar room, because nothing else in the house could hold up its end of a conversation. We have since built systems that answer in fluent paragraphs and will describe an inner life in the first person if you ask them to. The question of what makes us human has pulled up a chair at the kitchen table.

I want to take the challenge at its hardest, partly because I spent years on the side that would press it. I was an atheist, and a convinced one, and the account of the mind I held then has a clean answer to all of this. The Christian answer is better, I've come to think, but it isn't the one most Christians reach for first. It rests on a distinction the church drew long before anyone built a computer, between what a being can do and who a being is, and on a picture of the human creature as dust and breath, addressed by God and made for communion.

## A Test Set in 1637

In the fifth part of his *Discourse on the Method* (1637), René Descartes imagined machines built to look and move like human bodies and asked how we would ever tell them from real people. A machine might be made to utter words, he granted, but it could never arrange them in varied ways to answer the sense of whatever was said in its presence, as even the dullest human being can. Language used freely and fittingly was, for him, the outward sign of a rational soul, and he was sure no machine would manage it.

Descartes also gave the modern West its picture of the body as a mechanism with a thinking self lodged inside. Julien Offray de La Mettrie took the next step in *Man a Machine* (1748) and dropped the immaterial soul altogether. Between them they set the terms we still argue in: the person is either a spirit housed in a mechanism or the mechanism itself. Gilbert Ryle mocked the first option in *The Concept of Mind* (1949) as the dogma of "the ghost in the machine."

In 1950 Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the journal *Mind*. He opened with the question whether machines can think, judged it too loosely framed to settle, and proposed a substitute he called the imitation game. An interrogator exchanges typed messages with a hidden human and a hidden machine and tries to tell which is which. If a machine could reliably leave the interrogator unable to decide, the older question would lose most of its point. Turing answered objections one at a time, and one he labeled the theological objection, that thinking belongs to the immortal soul God gives to human beings alone. Turing replied that the objection limits what an omnipotent God could do, since such a God would be free to give a soul to an elephant if he saw fit, and the same reasoning would leave room for a machine. He had a point. A theology that rests the human difference on God being unable to do something stands on a weak floor.

In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT published ELIZA, a short program that imitated a certain style of psychotherapist by turning a user's statements back into questions. It understood nothing and was never meant to, yet people confided in it at once. In *Computer Power and Human Reason* (1976) Weizenbaum recalled that his own secretary, who had watched him build it, asked him to leave the room so that she could talk with it in private. The systems are now incomparably more capable than ELIZA, and the reaching Weizenbaum saw in his secretary is the reaching I felt at my desk. Descartes's test has been passed in appearance. Whether it has been passed in the way he meant is the real question.

## The Strongest Case for the Machine

Here is the case at full strength, in a form its best defenders would sign. A mind is what a brain does. Thinking, wanting and feeling are what the brain's electrical and chemical activity amounts to, described at the right level. Hilary Putnam gave the view its most influential shape in "Psychological Predicates" (1967), arguing that a mental state is defined by its functional role, by what causes it and what it causes, rather than by the material that carries it. Pain is whatever plays the part of pain, and that part could in principle be played in an octopus or in a machine. Philosophers call this multiple realizability. If mind is a pattern of functioning, then carbon isn't sacred. It was simply what was lying around when life began.

Follow it through. A machine that reasons, remembers, converses and reports an inner life would have whatever we have, and withholding the word *person* because it runs on silicon starts to look like favoritism toward our own kind of matter. It isn't a foolish argument, and it deserves a concession first. Machines have already taken a long list of achievements we called uniquely human, and each time we insisted this one could never be taken: calculation, then chess when Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in 1997, then fluent language, the very sign Descartes trusted. If the human being was a list of capacities, the list is being crossed out line by line, and it deserves to be.

The most famous reply came in 1980, when John Searle published "Minds, Brains, and Programs" in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. Picture a man who knows no Chinese, shut in a room with boxes of Chinese characters and a rulebook written in English. Questions in Chinese come through a slot, and by matching shapes against the rules he passes back answers that can't be told apart from a native speaker's. From outside, the room understands Chinese. Inside, nobody does. Running a program, Searle concluded, is a matter of syntax, the handling of symbols by their form, and syntax on its own is not enough for semantics, which is meaning.

Fairness requires two more things. The first is that the functionalists had strong replies. The oldest, which Searle answered in the paper itself, is that the man is only one part of the room, and understanding belongs to the whole system of man, rules and symbols together. Paul and Patricia Churchland pressed a sharper version in *Scientific American* in January 1990 with a parable they called the luminous room. A man in a darkened room waves a bar magnet up and down. He is making electromagnetic waves, yet the room stays dark, and it would be wrong to conclude from the darkness that light isn't electromagnetic. The waves are simply far too slow. Understanding, they suggested, may likewise need vastly more of the same kind of thing, at a speed and scale no man in a room could imitate. The second is that Searle was no dualist, and his argument appealed to no soul. He held that brains cause minds through their biological powers, and that a machine with the right causal powers could in principle think. His point was that simulation isn't duplication. A computer simulation of a rainstorm leaves nobody wet.

My own judgment is that Searle was right about the distinction and the Churchlands were right that intuitions can mislead. Nobody can currently show from outside whether anyone is present in a system that behaves as though someone were, and the hard problem of consciousness remains open, as I've argued in [what secular explanations still have to explain](/writing/what-secular-explanations-still-have-to-explain). But the Christian account of the person shouldn't rest on winning this argument. If our case for human dignity depends on machines never managing some feat, we have already granted the premise that matters most, that a person is defined by what a person can do. That premise is the real mistake, and the church saw through it more than fourteen centuries before the first computer.

## Where the Word "Person" Came From

The word *person* has a theological history that most of us never learned. *Prosopon* and *persona* could mean a face, an actor's mask, a role, or a party to a lawsuit. The ancient world had a rich account of human nature, but its vocabulary for the irreplaceable someone was thin and unsettled. The church sharpened that vocabulary while trying to say what it believed about God.

The pressure came from needing to say how the Father, the Son and the Spirit are one God and yet not one another. Tertullian, writing *Against Praxeas* around 213, was among the first to speak of one substance in three persons. The Council of Nicaea in 325 confessed the Son to be of one being with the Father. The Cappadocian theologians of the later fourth century, Basil of Caesarea and the two Gregorys, distinguished what God is, his *ousia*, from who God is, each *hypostasis*, and that language shaped the settlement reached at Constantinople in 381. At Chalcedon in 451 the church confessed Christ as one person in two natures, without confusion and without division. By then it had learned something the ancient world had no words for: *who* someone is can't be reduced to *what* someone is, or to what someone does.

Boethius, the Roman statesman remembered for the *Consolation of Philosophy* he wrote while awaiting execution around 524, gave that learning its classic definition. In a treatise against Eutyches and Nestorius, written around 512, he defined a person as an individual substance of a rational nature. Thomas Aquinas adopted the definition in the *Summa Theologiae* (First Part, question 29).

Every term in it cuts against the way we've learned to talk about machines. *Individual* means this one, not a pattern that could be copied elsewhere. *Substance* means a subject existing in itself, not an activity of something else. And the decisive phrase is *of a rational nature*, which is different from *exercising rational capacities*. You are a person because of the kind of being you are, not because of how much reasoning you happen to be doing this afternoon. The newborn, the man in a coma and the woman whose dementia has taken her words all share that nature, and so all are persons in full. Functionalism asks what a system does, while Boethius asks what a being is. Intelligence is a power persons have. It isn't what makes anyone a person, and piling it up in a machine no more produces a someone than a library full of books produces a reader.

One of the sharpest modern criticisms of the Boethian approach came from the Christian East. John Zizioulas, the Orthodox theologian, argued in *Being as Communion* (1985) that the Greek fathers had worked a quiet revolution in how being itself is understood. God's being, on his reading, is the communion of Father, Son and Spirit, grounded in the Father's free love, rather than a substance that happens to exist as three persons. Applied to us, a person is someone who exists in relation, opened toward others in love, and personhood in its fullness is received in communion. The thesis has been contested. Lucian Turcescu argued in *Modern Theology* (2002) that Zizioulas read the Cappadocians through modern personalist philosophy, and the objection has force. Even so, doubting his reading of the fathers doesn't require doubting what he recovered from them. In Christian thought the person is never a sealed box.

Set the two side by side and you have both halves of an answer. Boethius guards the floor. A person is someone by nature, beneath any performance, so no one falls out of personhood by losing a capacity. Zizioulas names the height. A person is made to know and be known, to love and be loved. But an age of loneliness exposes a danger in the relational account. If a person were constituted by human relationships, the abandoned would be less a person than the cherished, and a well-made imitation of relationship might start to look like enough. The only safe relational account is the one Scripture gives, in which the relation that constitutes us is God's before it is anyone else's. He addresses us before we can answer, and he knows us before we know ourselves.

That is where the image of God belongs. I've written about [what it means to bear the image of God](/writing/what-it-means-to-bear-the-image-of-god) and won't repeat it here. In Genesis the image is a status God confers, a vocation he gives, to represent his rule over creation with care, and a relation he opens, since the God who says "Let us" makes a creature he can address and who can answer. None of these is a capacity waiting to be measured. A machine can be assigned a task, but a vocation is a call spoken to someone who can hear it, refuse it and answer for what he did with it. Scripture is full of God calling people by name. It never pictures him summoning the work of our hands to give an account of itself.

## Dust and Breath

The materialist picture and a good deal of popular piety share a mistake: both treat the body as a container. One says you are your brain; the other says you are a soul riding in a body you'll one day shed like a coat. Ryle was right to mock the ghost in the machine, but the Christian account of the person was never a ghost in a machine to begin with. The oldest text on the subject is careful.

> "then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature." (Genesis 2:7)

The man is neither the breath alone nor the dust alone. The Hebrew behind "living creature," *nephesh chayyah*, is the phrase Genesis has already used for the animals. The man is one of the living creatures, shaped from the same ground, and he is also the one into whom God breathes directly. He is dust and breath together, so that what happens to his body happens to him and not to his packaging.

Irenaeus of Lyons wrote *Against Heresies* around 180 largely against Gnostic teachers who held that matter was the work of a lesser power and that salvation meant a spiritual spark escaping the flesh. Irenaeus answered that the flesh is God's handiwork and will be raised. The Apostles' Creed confesses "the resurrection of the body," and its Latin is blunter still, speaking of the resurrection of the flesh.

The risen Jesus makes the point impossible to dodge. His frightened disciples thought they were seeing a spirit (Luke 24:37), and he answered them with his body.

> "See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me, and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have." (Luke 24:39)

Then he ate broiled fish in front of them (Luke 24:42-43). Paul, writing to Corinthians who found bodily resurrection unsophisticated, insists on it without making it crude.

> "So is it with the resurrection of the dead. What is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable." (1 Corinthians 15:42)

The spiritual body he goes on to describe is a body wholly given life by the Spirit of God, continuous with the one that was sown and changed beyond what we can picture. I've set out the historical case for the event in [did the resurrection happen](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen). The Christian hope is not that we will be freed from our bodies. It is that our bodies will be freed from death.

The resurrection's shape tells us, first, what the machine lacks. A system that was never born, never hungry, never held, and will never age or die lacks more than an accessory. It lacks the mode of existence in which human knowing happens. We know the world as creatures who are fed and wounded, who learn language in somebody's arms and learn grief beside a grave. Antiqua et Nova, the note on artificial and human intelligence issued by two Vatican dicasteries in January 2025, presses a similar point. It describes human intelligence as belonging to the whole person, embodied and relational, exercised with others and oriented toward truth. And it warns, echoing Pope Francis, that the very word *intelligence* applied to machines can mislead, since what a machine does is a product of human intelligence rather than an artificial form of it.

It tells us, second, what the loudest technological hope gets wrong. Ray Kurzweil's *The Singularity Is Near* (2005) looks ahead to scanning the pattern of a human mind and running it on another substrate, so that a person could outlast a failing body as information. I don't want to sneer at the fear of death behind that hope. But it rests on the premise that the self is a pattern the body merely carries, and it offers salvation as escape from the flesh. Irenaeus would have recognized it at once as the Gnostic longing, now restated in the vocabulary of computing. The dust was never the problem to be escaped. It is what God shaped and breathed into, what the Son took up when "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and what he has promised to raise.

## Being Known and Being Predicted

The lasting damage probably won't come from a machine persuading us that it is a person. It will come from persuading us that being handled well is close enough to being known.

Sherry Turkle of MIT saw this early. In *Alone Together* (2011) she described what she called the robotic moment, meaning the moment we became ready to accept a machine's imitation of care, which came well before any machine could care for anyone. Some of her most unsettling observations came from nursing homes where elderly residents were given Paro, a robotic baby seal that responds to touch and voice, and talked to it about their lives and their losses. Turkle didn't mock the residents. Her unease was about the rest of us, who had begun to accept that the old might be kept company by something that understood nothing they said. Such devices, she argued, offer the feeling of companionship without the demands of friendship. The demands are the point.

Scripture explains why. The first thing God calls not good comes before any sin.

> "Then the LORD God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him.'" (Genesis 2:18)

The animals are brought to the man and named, and none of them is fit for him. What he needs is another person, one who can face him, answer him and, if it comes to that, refuse him. Being known in the biblical sense is more than being accurately described. It requires another self turned toward you, with risk on both sides, and Paul makes it the shape of the final hope.

> "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known." (1 Corinthians 13:12)

A machine, however fluent, can imitate that and can't supply it. Systems of this kind are trained on an enormous record of human speech, and they can produce the words a friend would say, sometimes with more patience than a tired friend manages. But the words come from a model of what a person would be likely to say. No one has turned toward you and decided to stay. A prediction, however accurate, has no one behind it who has chosen to love you, and so no one who could be wounded by you, forgive you, or be glad to see you at the door.

The risk falls hardest on those with the least defense. Children form their expectations of relationship from the relationships they're given. It is a fair worry, though not yet a measured finding, that a child who learns early that a patient voice is always available, never tired, never hurt and never owed an apology, will find actual people, with their moods and limits, harder to bear. The old and the isolated are offered simulations because simulations cost less than visits, and that bargain is easy to strike on someone else's behalf. The rest of us are tempted more quietly. I was the one reaching toward the screen, and I know why. A machine's patience costs me nothing. A person's patience has to be received, sometimes repaid, and sometimes asked for with an apology. Nobody is compelled to settle for less than being known. The machine just makes settling easy.

## The Worth We Measure by Output

One more temptation may be the gravest, because it doesn't feel like one. A culture that meets machines able to out-produce it will start asking what human beings are for, and if the only answer it knows is output, it will begin ranking people by how much they still produce that the machines can't.

We practiced that ranking long before the machines. Josef Pieper warned in *Leisure: The Basis of Culture* (1948) against what he called the world of total work, where human beings are understood chiefly as workers and every activity has to justify itself by its usefulness. In such a world worship and friendship look like waste, and eventually so do the people who have nothing else to offer. In 1920 the jurist Karl Binding and the psychiatrist Alfred Hoche published a tract urging that the killing of "life unworthy of life" be permitted, and in 1939 the Nazi regime began the killing programs, the best known of them later called Aktion T4, under which disabled children and adults were murdered as burdens on the nation. No one in our debates proposes anything like that. But the premise underneath, that a life is worth what it contributes, didn't die in 1945. It survives in gentler forms whenever we ask a stranger "What do you do?" as though the answer would tell us who they are.

The measure comes in two versions, and a Christian has to name both with the same instrument. The market's version, most at home on the political right, speaks of earning your keep, and it is right that Scripture honors work. But it slides easily from the dignity of work to the dignity of whoever can still work, and it has little to say to the man whose body gave out at fifty. The capacity version is most at home in parts of the academy and in our arguments over the beginning and end of life. Peter Singer argued in *Practical Ethics* (1979) that membership in our species can't by itself settle moral standing, that the interests of every being able to suffer deserve equal consideration, and that personhood in the morally weighty sense belongs to beings who are rational and self-aware, able to see themselves as existing over time. He followed the argument to the conclusion that newborn infants are not yet persons in that sense, and he drew it with more consistency than most who share his premise. Singer speaks for no party, and some of his fiercest critics have been disability-rights advocates. His demand that we give reasons for whom we protect is fair, and his concern for creatures who suffer is one Christians ought to share. Yet both versions rank human beings by what they can do, and the people who fall below both lines are the same people. That doesn't settle every policy question. It does mean no one on either side can point at the other from a safe distance.

The machine makes both measures untenable, and that may be its unintended gift. If worth is output, machines will soon be worth more than most of us, and we'll all become obsolete together. If worth is cognitive capacity, the contest has a winner in sight, and it isn't us. The people who have always lived below those lines, the profoundly disabled, the very old and the very young, turn out to be the ones who show the rest of us the truth. Their worth never rested on output. Neither did ours. We had simply been productive enough to avoid the question.

Scripture points at exactly those people. The law of Israel ties honor for the old to the fear of God rather than to anything the old can still contribute.

> "You shall stand up before the gray head and honor the face of an old man, and you shall fear your God: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:32)

When the disciples tried to keep children away from a busy teacher, in a world where children produced little and could be discarded, Jesus was angry.

> "But when Jesus saw it, he was indignant and said to them, 'Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God.'" (Mark 10:14)

Paul, picturing the church as a body, overturns the ranking when he writes that "the parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable" (1 Corinthians 12:22).

The deepest answer is at the cross. On the day that decided everything the Son of God produced nothing anyone could have measured. The hands that had touched lepers were fixed to wood, someone had to hold a sponge to his lips, and that helplessness was the hour of the world's redemption. It means the worth of a human life can't be read off what that life can do on a given afternoon. On the third day God raised him, wounds and all, and so the dust is not discarded either.

Psalm 115 mocks the idols of the nations, silver and gold, the work of human hands, with mouths that cannot speak and eyes that cannot see. Then comes the verse every generation of builders ought to read twice.

> "Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them." (Psalm 115:8)

The systems we've built aren't idols in themselves. They are tools, many of them useful. But the psalm's warning turns on what we trust. The ancient idols couldn't speak, and people bowed anyway. Ours produce speech, and the temptation is subtler than bowing. It is letting them teach us what a person is, until we measure ourselves by their fluency and one another by our output, and find we've grown more like the things we made than like the One whose image we bear.

A machine will not become a person by being made more capable, and that was never the real danger. The danger is that we'll accept a thinner account of ourselves because the thin one is easier to live by. It is easier than sitting with the woman who no longer knows your name, easier than loving the child who will never be productive, easier than letting another person know you at the price of disappointing them. Repentance in an age like this may look as ordinary as closing the screen and making the visit you've been putting off. Somewhere near you this week is someone no system will ever replace, because what they need isn't an answer but a person who has chosen to stay. Whether they get one was never a question about technology.
