A great many married Christians sit through years of sermons without hearing one careful word about the part of their marriage that hurts most. They share a mortgage, a last name and a pew, and from the outside they look like any other couple. But they haven't touched each other in months, some of them in years, and neither knows how to say so. One lies awake feeling unwanted. The other lies awake feeling like a failure, or like a debtor who can't pay. Each has built a private story about what the silence means, and each story is probably wrong.

The church gave most of these couples a theology of sex made of two words. Before the wedding: don't. After the wedding: do. Neither word prepared anyone for a marriage in which desire runs at two speeds, or the body hurts, or an old wound turns touch into alarm. So couples take the question to the internet, where two answers wait. The culture says sex is a need, a sexless marriage is a failed one, and you owe it to yourself to find fulfillment. A certain kind of church says Paul told you not to deprive your spouse, so stop refusing.

Some of both is true. The culture is right that the loss is serious. The church is right that Scripture expects sexual union in marriage and treats its long absence as a danger. But both answers treat the body as something owed, in one case to the self and in the other to the spouse, and neither has noticed what Paul was doing when he wrote the sentences everyone quotes. The Bible's teaching on sex in marriage is neither a license nor a quota. It's a doctrine of mutual belonging, in which each spouse's body is given to the other. What is given can be offered, received, waited for and grieved. It can't be seized. The question turns on the difference between a body that belongs to your spouse and a body your spouse owns.

### What Paul was correcting at Corinth

The seventh chapter of 1 Corinthians opens with Paul answering a letter: "Now concerning the matters about which you wrote: 'It is good for a man not to have sexual relations with a woman'" (1 Corinthians 7:1). The ESV puts that second sentence in quotation marks for good reason. Gordon Fee argues in *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* (1987) that it is the Corinthians' own slogan, quoted back before Paul corrects it. Some in that church had concluded that the Spirit had lifted them above the body, and that the holiest thing a married believer could do was to stop sleeping with a spouse.

The same congregation had the opposite problem a chapter earlier. There the slogan was "All things are lawful for me," and some were using it to justify prostitutes. Paul's answer is that the body belongs to the Lord: "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:19–20). Corinth had a libertine party and an ascetic party, one indulging the body because it didn't matter and the other refusing it for the same reason, and Paul answers both from one conviction. The body matters to God. Then he writes:

> The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband. For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does. Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer; but then come together again, so that Satan may not tempt you because of your lack of self-control. (1 Corinthians 7:3–5)

Read slowly, the passage does four things its misusers skip. First, it's symmetrical, and in that world the symmetry was startling. Under Augustus's law on adultery, the Lex Julia of 18 BC, a wife's infidelity was a public crime, while a husband's use of slaves and prostitutes wasn't treated as adultery at all. Everyone assumed a husband had rights over his wife's body. Almost no one said a wife had rights over her husband's. Paul says it in the same breath and the same words, which meant a Christian husband's body was not his own to take anywhere else.

Second, the verb for "authority" in 7:4 is the one Paul had just used against the libertines: "'All things are lawful for me,' but not all things are helpful. 'All things are lawful for me,' but I will not be dominated by anything" (1 Corinthians 6:12). The man who claimed authority over his body is told that in marriage it has passed to a spouse who handed over his or her own body in the same act. That makes two people who each belong to the other, not a master and a servant.

Third, the language of debt is real, and it matters where Paul points it. The word behind "conjugal rights" is the ordinary word for what is owed. But every command in the passage is addressed to the one who gives. Let the husband give. Let the wife give. Do not deprive. Nowhere does Paul tell a spouse to exact or collect. It's a debt that can be paid and can't be collected, and the same letter defines love as the thing that "does not insist on its own way" (1 Corinthians 13:5). Fourth, even abstinence has to be mutual. "By agreement" translates a word from which we get *symphony*. A pause is legitimate when both choose it, for a time and a reason, and its goal is return.

The passage most often used to pressure reluctant spouses was written to stop spouses from treating refusal as holiness. Read whole, it condemns the withholding that uses the body as a weapon and the pressure that uses the verse as one. A husband who reads "the wife does not have authority over her own body" as a warrant to press his wife has taken the clause that limits him and turned it into a claim. That isn't a strict reading of Paul. It's a reversal of him.

This needs saying without softening. Consent and care are not optional features of Christian marriage. Sex obtained by threat, force, guilt or relentless pressure is abuse, whatever verse is quoted over it, and no faithful reading of Scripture asks anyone to endure it. If that describes your home, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233, and you deserve a pastor or counselor who will take you seriously.

### A book of desire in the middle of the Bible

Paul wasn't grudgingly permitting sex. He was defending something Scripture had celebrated from the beginning. "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh. And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:24–25). The one-flesh union belongs to the world before the fall, in the chapter God pronounces good. And the first thing sin does in the next chapter is not to invent desire but to poison exposure: "Then the eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked. And they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves loincloths" (Genesis 3:7). Their bodies hadn't changed. Being seen had become dangerous. When Scripture names the sexual act, it often uses the ordinary verb for knowing, "Now Adam knew Eve his wife" (Genesis 4:1), the same verb the prophets use for knowing God. Sexual union is a form of personal disclosure, which is exactly why it wounds so deeply when it's used without love.

Then there is the Song of Songs, eight chapters of love poetry in the canon, frank about bodies and unembarrassed by desire. The woman speaks more than the man, and the book opens with her longing. For most of Christian history the church read it chiefly as allegory: Origen in the third century read it as the love of Christ and the soul, and Bernard of Clairvaux preached eighty-six sermons on it between 1135 and his death in 1153 without getting past the opening of the third chapter. The allegory isn't baseless, since the prophets describe God's covenant with Israel as a marriage and Ephesians carries that image to Christ and the church. But as a replacement for the plain sense, it was a way of keeping the book while losing its meaning.

Two lines from the Song bear directly on marriages where desire has gone quiet. One is a refrain the woman repeats three times: "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles or the does of the field, that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (Song of Songs 2:7). Love can be tended and invited; it can't be summoned by force. The other is her declaration near the end: "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me" (Song of Songs 7:10). The Hebrew word for desire there appears only twice elsewhere, both in Genesis, including the judgment of Genesis 3:16, where desire between the man and the woman is bent into struggle and rule. In the Song the word returns healed. Desire runs toward the woman instead of against her, and belonging is mutual. Within the lovers' covenant, something of Eden comes back.

Proverbs 5 is a father's warning to his son about adultery, and its remedy is delight, not suppression: "rejoice in the wife of your youth, a lovely deer, a graceful doe. Let her breasts fill you at all times with delight; be intoxicated always in her love" (Proverbs 5:18–19). The Bible's own antidote to betrayal is a marriage where desire is welcome at home. Hebrews gathers it into one sentence that guards the marriage bed from both sides: "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous" (Hebrews 13:4). Honor it, against those who despise it. Keep it undefiled, against those who betray it. The ascetic and the adulterer are answered by the same verse.

John Paul II, in the Wednesday audiences of 1979 to 1984 later gathered as the *Theology of the Body*, called this the nuptial meaning of the body: in marriage the body says with flesh what the vows say with words. A Baptist needn't share his sacramental theology to see the point. When sex is used to take rather than give, the body is made to tell a lie, and both people feel it.

### How the church learned to distrust desire

If Scripture is this clear, how did the church come to sound so embarrassed? The honest answer begins with something admirable. Peter Brown shows in *The Body and Society* (1988) that early Christian renunciation was not simply hatred of the body. In the Roman world a citizen's body belonged to the city, which needed heirs and soldiers. Young men and women who chose lifelong virginity were declaring that their bodies belonged to the age to come, not to the empire's demand for children. Jesus said that in the resurrection "they neither marry nor are given in marriage" (Matthew 22:30), and Paul wished others could be single as he was (1 Corinthians 7:7). The celibate are a sign that marriage is not the final thing, and the church still needs that witness.

But the honor given to virginity slid into contempt for marriage. In 393 Jerome wrote *Against Jovinian*, attacking a monk who taught that virgins, widows and married Christians stood equal before God. Jerome ranked them like the harvests in the parable of the sower, a hundredfold for virgins, sixtyfold for widows, thirtyfold for the married. Jovinian was condemned, and Jerome's rhetoric outlived them both.

The decisive voice was Augustine's. His *Confessions* (c. 397–400) records the young man's famous prayer for chastity and continence, but not yet, and the partner of many years, the mother of his son, whom he sent away when a respectable marriage was being arranged. Augustine was no simple body-hater. He had broken with the Manichees, who taught that matter was evil, and in *The Good of Marriage* (c. 401) he defended marriage against Jerome's excess and named its three goods: offspring, faithfulness and the unbreakable bond. But he also taught that intercourse sought for more than the begetting of children, even between husband and wife, was a pardonable fault. In the fourteenth book of *The City of God* he pointed to the way arousal refuses to obey the will, coming when unwanted and failing when wanted, as the plainest sign of the fall, and in later works he treated this disordered desire, concupiscence, as the channel by which original sin passes to children. Julian of Eclanum argued against him that sexual desire was a natural good given by God, and that sin lay in its excess. Augustine won, and the Western church inherited his side for a thousand years.

Augustine saw something true. Desire after the fall really is disordered, and anyone long married knows it from both directions, as a compulsion that won't stop and an absence that won't be summoned. But he placed the disorder in the pleasure rather than in the self-turned heart, and so made delight itself suspect. The long result was a church that called marriage good while treating what happens in the marriage bed as something God forgives rather than something God made.

Medieval canon lawyers then took Paul's language of what is owed and built a legal category out of it, the marital debt. Paul's word to the giver became a claim for the creditor, and the idea traveled far. Sir Matthew Hale, a devout English judge whose treatise on criminal law was published in 1736, taught that a husband couldn't be guilty of raping his wife, because by marrying she had given a consent she could not retract. That doctrine shaped Anglo-American law for more than two centuries, and not until 1993 was marital rape a crime in some form in every American state. It wasn't the libertines who built this. It was pious men who had turned a gift into a debt.

The Reformers began the recovery. Martin Luther's *The Estate of Marriage* (1522) called marriage God's own work, and on 13 June 1525 he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun who had fled her convent two years earlier. Yet the recovery was incomplete. In the same treatise Luther could still say that intercourse is never without sin, though God excuses it by his grace because marriage is his work. The shift came slowly and can be dated. The Book of Common Prayer of 1549 listed three causes for which marriage was ordained, and the "mutual society, help, and comfort" of husband and wife came third, after children and the remedy against sin. The Westminster Confession of 1646 put the mutual help of husband and wife first. The tradition was learning, clumsily, that the love of the spouses was not an afterthought.

### Two revolutions and the same mistake

The twentieth century brought two revolutions, usually told as opposites. The first oral contraceptive was approved in 1960, *Griswold v. Connecticut* struck down a state ban on contraception for married couples in 1965, and within a decade much of the West had rewritten its sexual ethic.

The sexual revolution deserves a fair hearing, because it named things the church should have named first. It exposed a double standard as old as the Lex Julia and gave voice to wives whose desire nobody had asked about. But it rested on an account of the person that Carl Trueman traces in *The Rise and Triumph of the Modern Self* (2020), from Freud through Wilhelm Reich and Herbert Marcuse: sexual expression is central to who we are, so restraint is oppression and fulfillment is a right. Its disappointments have been documented by writers with no interest in defending the church. Mary Eberstadt argues in *Adam and Eve After the Pill* (2012) that the revolution's own results contradict its promised happiness, especially for women and children. Louise Perry, a secular feminist, argues in *The Case Against the Sexual Revolution* (2022) that its freedoms served men who wanted sex without commitment far better than the women who were told it would free them. The revolution got the symptom right and the anthropology wrong. It saw that the church had shamed desire, and it didn't see that desire made sovereign becomes a master.

The church's answer in the 1990s was purity culture, and it too deserves a fair hearing. The Southern Baptist True Love Waits campaign began in 1993, and Joshua Harris published *I Kissed Dating Goodbye* in 1997. The parents behind it had watched the revolution's wreckage and wanted to spare their children, and chastity is a biblical call: "For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from sexual immorality; that each one of you know how to control his own body in holiness and honor" (1 Thessalonians 4:3–4).

But the movement taught chastity through shame and fear. It made girls responsible for the lust of boys. It promised that waiting would be rewarded with a wonderful marriage, a prosperity gospel for the bedroom. And it said almost nothing about how a body trained for years to treat desire as danger was to become, overnight, a body that welcomes it. In 2018 Harris withdrew his book from publication. Linda Kay Klein's *Pure* (2018) gathered the testimony of women who carried the movement's shame into adulthood. Sheila Wray Gregoire, Rebecca Gregoire Lindenbach and Joanna Sawatsky, in *The Great Sex Rescue* (2021), reported on a survey of roughly twenty thousand Christian women: those who had absorbed teachings such as a wife's obligation to provide sex whenever her husband wanted it reported lower satisfaction and more sexual pain. The obligation teaching, meant to protect marriages, was draining desire from them.

The two revolutions made the same mistake. Both put sex at the center of the self. One made it an idol to be served and the other an idol to be feared, and an idol feared is still an idol. Neither could see sex as a good and ordinary part of a covenant between two people who belong to God before they belong to each other. Those of us who preach should be honest here. We found it easier to denounce the culture's appetites than to say one frank, careful word about the marriages in our own pews. The silence that couple is living in didn't come only from Hollywood. We built some of it.

### Why a marriage goes quiet

The bedroom is rarely where the trouble starts. It's usually where the trouble shows up, so before a couple asks what's wrong with their sex life, they have to ask what it's reporting. They also need one distinction before the conversation can go anywhere. "I don't want you" and "I can't find desire right now" are different sentences with different answers. The spouse who wants more tends to hear the first when the second is being said, and the spouse who wants less often can't tell from the inside which is true.

Most causes are no one's moral failure. Grief can shut the body down for a long season after the loss of a child, a parent or a pregnancy, and spouses often grieve on different schedules. Illness can suppress desire or make sex physically hard, and so can common medications, including many antidepressants. The body changes after childbirth, in menopause and with age. Sexual pain is more common than most couples know and often treatable, as is erectile difficulty, which can also be an early sign of heart disease. And a parent who has been needed by small children for eleven hours may have nothing left by nine at night, which is not a verdict on the marriage.

Other causes are wounds. A spouse who carries abuse or assault from before the marriage may find the body bracing or freezing even when the mind wants closeness and the spouse is gentle, and a betrayal inside the marriage can do something similar. That isn't a spiritual failure, and it isn't healed by trying harder or believing harder. Healing is real, but it's slow, it can't run on the timeline of the spouse who wants it over, and it usually needs a counselor trained in trauma. The spouse married to someone carrying that wound is asked for one of the most Christlike things marriage ever asks: to want the other's healing more than one's own satisfaction, and to be a safe place long enough that safety becomes believable.

Pornography should be named plainly. C. S. Lewis distinguished in *The Four Loves* (1960) between Eros, the love that wants the beloved person, and Venus, the sexual appetite, which on its own wants a sensation rather than a person. Pornography is Venus cut loose from Eros and sold by the hour, training desire toward images that ask nothing and leaving a real spouse measured against a fantasy. Jesus didn't treat this as private: "But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:28). It betrays the one-flesh promise even when no one else is touched, and the spouse who discovers it is right to grieve it. The way back runs through confession, real accountability and often a counselor, because a habit formed in secret rarely breaks in secret.

And some quiet marriages are simply resentful ones. The body refuses what the heart can't yet give. A spouse who has been dismissed at dinner, or has carried the household for months unseen, may find that desire has left the room, as protection rather than punishment. Then the absence of sex isn't the problem to solve. It's the symptom of an unanswered wrong, and pressing on the symptom only proves the resentment right.

What helps? Start with a physician, and consider going together, because some of this is chemistry with a name and a treatment. Pelvic floor physical therapy helps many women with sexual pain. A counselor trained in sexual health can often name what a couple can't. A pastor who won't flinch helps too, but never as a substitute for medical or clinical care, and a church that discourages such help because prayer should be enough has abandoned couples to a problem they can't solve alone. This is not medical advice, only a plea to take the body as seriously as God does.

The conversation itself belongs in daylight, at a time when nothing is being asked. The spouse who wants more has to find the truer sentence under the frustration, usually "I need to know you still want me." The spouse who wants less has to find the truer sentence under the avoidance, which may be "I'm so tired I can't find myself," or "I've never stopped being afraid of this," or "I don't know why, and I want to find out." Neither is an animal or a fraud. Both are grieving, and both are allowed to.

Some couples object that they simply aren't very physical and are content. That can be true; illness, age and long seasons of caregiving can make the body a smaller part of a marriage's language, and no one is handing out a quota. But Paul assumes a pause is chosen by both and ends in return, so a couple should ask honestly whether their peace is rest or a truce they called contentment because trying kept ending in hurt.

### Naked and not ashamed

Under every cause and remedy lies the oldest line in the Bible's account of marriage: "the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25). The nakedness is of the body and more than the body, the condition of being completely seen with nothing managed, and the miracle is that it didn't frighten them. After the fall the man says what every human being has said in some form: "I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself" (Genesis 3:10). Most of us suspect that if we were fully known we'd be left, so we manage what our spouse may see, and a marriage can go on for decades that way, sexually active or not, without either person being known. Most couples are less under-skilled than under-known. The revolution offered nakedness without the promise not to leave, which is only exposure, and purity culture offered a promise wrapped in fear, and fear keeps the fig leaves on.

The gospel answers at exactly this point. Paul, having quoted Genesis 2:24, adds: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). The marriage bed was always meant to point past itself. On the night before he died, Jesus took bread and said, "This is my body, which is given for you" (Luke 22:19). He seized nothing and gave everything, and the next day that body was stripped and exposed in public, so that people who had hidden since Eden could be seen completely and not abandoned. Every Christian marriage lives under that sign. The body is given, because the body that saved us was given.

And the body matters enough to God that he will raise it: "And God raised the Lord and will also raise us up by his power" (1 Corinthians 6:14). It cuts two ways. The body is not trivial, so a sexless marriage is a real loss and not a spiritual achievement. And the marriage bed is not ultimate, so a marriage that has lost this for a season, or for good, through illness or age or a wound that won't fully heal in this life, has not lost the thing marriage was finally for.

None of this resolves tonight. The couple in the pew will go home to the same bed and the same silence, and an essay can't change chemistry or heal a wound. What it can change is what they think they're holding. If the body is owed, the only options are to collect or to default. If the body is given, there are others: to confess, to wait, to make the appointment, to say the true sentence in daylight, to keep offering what can't be demanded and receiving what can't be earned. They belong to each other now, and both belong to a God who has already seen them naked and did not turn away. The question they carry out the church door is not whether one of them owes the other something. It's whether either of them is willing to be seen.
