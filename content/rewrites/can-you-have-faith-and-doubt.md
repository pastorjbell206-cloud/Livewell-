Most people who doubt in church are not undone by the question. They're undone by the thought that follows it. The first comes uninvited, often at two in the morning: what if none of this is true? The second arrives right behind it and does far more damage: a real Christian wouldn't be thinking this. The first is a question. The second is a verdict, and it is usually the verdict, not the question, that empties the pew. People conclude that the doubt proves the faith was never there, and they stop praying because praying felt like lying.

I came to faith from atheism, which means I walked into the church carrying more doubt than most of the people already in it. Nobody could tell me it disqualified me, because it was the door I came in through. But I am also a pastor, and a church can teach that second thought without ever saying it aloud. We teach it in the testimony that rounds every struggle up to a victory, and in the worried face and single verse handed to the teenager who finally said the frightening thing out loud. Pastors teach it, and I'm one of them. Where sureness is the price of admission, everyone pays in the same counterfeit, and a room full of people carrying the same question sits in silence, each convinced he is the only one.

This essay is for the believer who doubts, and its claim is that the church has scrambled two distinctions. The first is between doubt and unbelief. Doubt is a question asked from inside trust; unbelief is a settled refusal to trust. The second is between certainty and confidence. Certainty, in the sense the modern world taught us to crave, is a state the knower must produce and defend. Confidence rests on the faithfulness of the one who is known. Scramble them and the church ends up shaming the very people Jesus went out of his way to meet.

## What Scripture does with its doubters

Start with the hymnbook. The lament is the most common kind of psalm in the Psalter, and Israel sang its complaints in the same worship as its praise. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). "Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he in anger shut up his compassion?" (Psalm 77:9). Psalm 88 goes down into the dark and never turns, and its last line is "my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18). The editors of Israel's prayer book read that ending and kept it. But look at how the psalm begins: "O LORD, God of my salvation, I cry out day and night before you" (Psalm 88:1). A man who feels nothing but God's absence addresses the absence to God, and calls him the God of his salvation while he does it. That is the shape of doubt inside trust. Nobody files a grievance with a God he has stopped believing in.

Then there is John the Baptist. Matthew tells us that John "heard in prison about the deeds of the Christ" and sent his disciples with a question: "Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" (Matthew 11:2–3). This is the man who baptized Jesus. He had preached a Messiah with an axe: "Even now the axe is laid to the root of the trees" (Matthew 3:10). Now that Messiah was eating with tax collectors while John sat in a cell. His was the doubt of a faithful man whose God was not doing what he had expected.

Jesus answers with evidence, and the evidence is drawn from Isaiah: "the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them" (Matthew 11:5). The same prophet also promised "liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to those who are bound" (Isaiah 61:1), and the answer Jesus sent does not include that line. It is hard to imagine a man in Herod's prison failing to notice. Then comes a word aimed at the exact place where John's doubt lived: "And blessed is the one who is not offended by me" (Matthew 11:6). As soon as the messengers leave, Jesus turns to the crowd and defends the questioner: "among those born of women there has arisen no one greater than John the Baptist" (Matthew 11:11). He answered the question, and then he honored the man who asked it. John died in that prison. The answer he received was true, and it did not open the door.

Thomas has carried his nickname for two thousand years, and it flattens him. Earlier in John's Gospel he is the disciple willing to die with Jesus (John 11:16). After the crucifixion he was absent when Jesus came, and he refused to rebuild his life on a secondhand report: "Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into his side, I will never believe" (John 20:25). Then comes a verse easy to skip: "Eight days later, his disciples were inside again, and Thomas was with them" (John 20:26). He stayed in the room. He kept company for a week with people who claimed to have seen what he could not bring himself to believe. When Jesus came, he offered Thomas exactly what he had demanded: "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe" (John 20:27). The Greek reads, roughly, *do not become unbelieving, but believing*. Jesus speaks to Thomas as a man at a fork, and names the direction rather than condemning the question. Thomas answers with the highest confession in the Gospel: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).

Matthew kept a sentence a more cautious editor would have cut. The eleven go to the mountain in Galilee, "And when they saw him they worshiped him, but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17). Matthew does not tell us who. He tells us that worship and doubt stood on the same mountain in front of the same risen Lord. What happens next matters more: "And Jesus came and said to them, 'All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me'" (Matthew 28:18). He came closer, and gave the commission to the whole group, doubters included. The church's mission was handed to a company that had not finished wondering.

None of this makes the Bible sentimental about doubt. Jesus caught Peter as he sank in the sea, and then asked him, "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" (Matthew 14:31). James warns that "the one who doubts is like a wave of the sea that is driven and tossed by the wind," and calls such a man "double-minded" and "unstable in all his ways" (James 1:6, 8). Hebrews warns against "an evil, unbelieving heart, leading you to fall away from the living God" (Hebrews 3:12). Scripture keeps its doubters, but it does not tell them they have arrived. It tells them that where they go from here matters.

## Doubt is not unbelief

The most useful account of where the doubter stands is Os Guinness's *In Two Minds*, first published in 1976 and reissued in 1996 under the title *God in the Dark*. Guinness begins with the word itself. The English "doubt" descends from the Latin *dubitare*, which is related to the word for two. To believe is to be of one mind in accepting something as true, and to disbelieve is to be of one mind in rejecting it. To doubt is to be in two minds, not yet settled into either. From that Guinness draws his central claim: doubt is not the opposite of faith, and it is not the same as unbelief. It is a state of suspension, and a suspension can resolve in either direction. The doubter is not yet lost. He is also not safe to stay where he is forever.

The New Testament's vocabulary bears Guinness out. The verb Matthew uses for Peter on the water and for the disciples on the mountain, *distazō*, appears in the New Testament only in those two places, and it carries the picture of standing two ways. The verb James uses for the wave-tossed doubter, *diakrinomai*, is the same verb Jude uses when he tells the church to have mercy on "those who doubt." The same word draws two responses, and the difference is instructive. James speaks to the doubter about his own responsibility. Jude speaks to the church about how to treat him.

Read James in his setting and his warning sharpens rather than softens. The double-minded man in James 1 is asking God for wisdom in the middle of trials (James 1:5). His word for double-minded, *dipsychos*, appears in the New Testament only once more, also in James, and the second use tells us what he means: "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-minded" (James 4:8). The context is friendship with the world, which James calls enmity with God (James 4:4). His double-minded man is a man of divided allegiance, praying to God while keeping the world as a backup plan. That is a different creature from the father in Mark who cried out, "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24). The one hedges his loyalty. The other confesses his weakness to the only person who can help, which is loyalty under strain.

Unbelief, in Scripture, is something else again. Hebrews reaches back to the wilderness generation, which saw the sea divided and the manna fall, and it warns, "do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion" (Hebrews 3:8). Its verdict on that generation is blunt: "So we see that they were unable to enter because of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:19). That generation did not lack evidence. What it refused was trust, and the refusal settled into a direction of life. Doubt asks its hardest questions at the table. Unbelief has already stood up and walked to the car. The questions may be identical. The direction is not.

Guinness also insists that doubt comes in kinds, with different causes and different remedies. Some of the kinds he sorts are matters of the mind, some of the emotions, some of a faulty picture of God. The practical point is plain enough. Some doubt has real intellectual content: the reliability of the Gospels, the problem of suffering. That kind deserves investigation. But much doubt is not an argument at all. It is exhaustion, or grief, or a wound from a church that hurt you, or a season of anxiety that swept God up along with everything else. That doubt is just as real, and an argument will not reach it, because it did not come from one. Bringing a syllogism to a heartache fails the person twice.

Tim Keller argued in *The Reason for God* (2008) that every doubt rests on some alternative belief that is itself taken on faith, so the honest course is to doubt your doubts as rigorously as your faith. Keller also wrote that "a faith without some doubts is like a human body without any antibodies in it." Frederick Buechner, in the entry on doubt in *Wishful Thinking* (1973), said it with a lighter hand: "Doubts are the ants in the pants of faith. They keep it awake and moving."

## Where the demand for certainty came from

If doubt is so ordinary among God's people, why does it feel like an emergency in ours? Part of the answer is a date. In 1641 René Descartes published his *Meditations on First Philosophy*, in which he resolved to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted until he struck something that could not. Like Archimedes asking for one immovable point from which to move the earth, he wanted a single unshakable point for knowledge, and he found it in the thinking self. The method was brilliant for mathematics. But over the following generations it quietly changed what Western people meant by knowing. To know came to mean to be certain beyond the possibility of doubt, and whatever fell short of that standard fell short of knowledge.

The church answered the Cartesian standard in two ways, and both accepted its terms. One answer tried to meet the standard. In the years of *The Fundamentals* (1910–1915) and the fundamentalist-modernist controversy that followed, many conservative Protestants came to defend the faith as though it could be demonstrated with the certainty of a laboratory result, and to treat any wavering as betrayal. That movement was defending real doctrines against real losses, and many of its writers were careful scholars. But a faith built to match the certainty of a proof inherits the fragility of a proof. One hard text or one unanswered prayer, and the whole structure seems to fall, because the person was taught that a crack and a collapse are the same thing.

The other answer fled the standard. In 1799 Friedrich Schleiermacher addressed his *Speeches on Religion* to the "cultured despisers" of faith and relocated religion away from knowing and doing into intuition and feeling, where the critics of doctrine could not reach it. That move gave the liberal Protestant tradition a shelter, and its critics have argued ever since that the price was the claim that Christianity is true about the world. The two answers look like opposites. They share a root: both assume that faith must either be as certain as mathematics or retreat into something that makes no claims at all.

Much of the popular evangelical faith of the last half-century managed to combine them. It asked a feeling to carry the weight of a proof. "I know that I know that I know" is the confession of a faith that has located its certainty in the believer's inner state. When the feeling fades, as feelings do with sleep and grief, the faith seems to have died with it.

This is where certainty becomes an idol, and the word is not too strong. Isaiah describes a man who cuts down a tree, burns half of it to warm himself and bake his bread, and carves the rest into a god: "He prays to it and says, 'Deliver me, for you are my god!'" (Isaiah 44:17). The logic of the idol is that we make it and then ask it to save us. Certainty of the Cartesian kind is made of our own materials, our arguments and our feelings, and then we ask it to hold us up. A faith that rests on its own certainty has put its weight on something it carved.

The church before Descartes did know a kind of certainty. The Reformers prized it, and insisted that a believer could be sure of God's favor. The Council of Trent answered them in its 1547 decree on justification, teaching that no one could know with the certainty of faith that he had obtained God's grace. Trent deserves to be read at its strongest here: the same chapter says that no devout person should doubt God's mercy, Christ's merit or the power of the sacraments, and its target, as the chapter's heading puts it, was "vain confidence," the person who boasts of being certain his sins are forgiven and rests on that certainty alone. The Reformers answered that assurance never rested on the believer's own certainty. In Ford Lewis Battles's translation of the *Institutes* (final edition 1559), John Calvin defines faith as "a firm and certain knowledge of God's benevolence toward us, founded upon the truth of the freely given promise in Christ" (III.2.7). The certainty rested on the promise, outside the believer. And a few sections later (III.2.17) Calvin concedes that he can imagine no certainty untinged by doubt and no assurance free of anxiety, and that believers live in constant conflict with their own unbelief. Luther preached assurance while suffering lifelong *Anfechtung*, assaults of dread and despair. For the Reformers, certainty belonged to the reliability of the one promising. Descartes moved it into the mind of the one knowing. That shift is the whole story in small.

Lesslie Newbigin gave the recovered distinction its name. The title of his *Proper Confidence* (1995) is the argument in two words: the church had fought the Cartesian battle on borrowed ground, and the confidence proper to a Christian is of another kind. It rests on the faithfulness of the God who has made himself known in Jesus Christ, and it is held as a response to his call, not as a possession guarded against every question. That is the Reformers' certainty without the Cartesian frame. It does not ask the believer to feel sure. It asks him to trust someone who has kept his word.

## The case for certainty, and against it

The sharpest recent statement of this argument is Peter Enns's *The Sin of Certainty* (2016). Enns contends that much evangelical faith has equated trusting God with holding correct beliefs about God with complete certainty, so that when the beliefs are strained, trust itself seems to fail. He reads Ecclesiastes, Job and the laments as Scripture's own witness that God's people have always lived without that kind of certainty, and he argues that seasons of doubt are often the place where God weans us from trusting our ideas about him and teaches us to trust him.

His critics deserve their strongest form, because they are right about important things. First, they argue that Enns sets trust against belief in a way no one can actually live. Enns would reply that he is loosening the grip on beliefs, not discarding them, but the critics' point survives the reply. You cannot trust a person about whom you believe nothing. Thomas's confession is an act of trust, and also one of the largest claims ever made about a human being. Second, the New Testament wants its readers to know. Luke writes so that Theophilus "may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught" (Luke 1:4). John writes "that you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13). Paul, in chains, says, "I know whom I have believed, and I am convinced that he is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me" (2 Timothy 1:12). Assurance is a gift Scripture holds out, not a vice it warns against. Third, the celebration of doubt can harden into its own performance. A culture that wears questions as a badge and despises anyone who still has answers has built a tribe as closed as the one it left. A question that is never permitted an answer is not humility. It is another way of staying in control.

The certainty that will not let a question be asked and the questioning that will not let an answer be given, on the right and the left alike, are the same refusal. Both keep the self at the center, one guarding its grip and the other its freedom.

Where, then, does the argument land? Enns is right about the idol. A faith that rests on the believer's felt certainty has put its weight in the wrong place, and many have been crushed under it. His critics are right that faith has content, and that the confidence the apostles commend is real knowledge of a real person, not a mood of openness. The line runs between the object and the grip. Paul's confidence rests on "whom I have believed" and on what "he is able" to do, not on the strength of his hold. Certainty about Christ is something Scripture offers. Certainty in my certainty is the idol. The strongest objection to this settlement is one this essay cannot fully answer: from the inside, the difference between a question held in trust and a slow drift out of it is not always visible, and "confidence" can become a softer word that excuses us from confessing anything definite. The best guard the essay can offer is to keep the questions where Thomas kept his, in the room, and to let the answer be a person rather than a position.

## When the dark does not lift

Some doubt is not a question at all but an absence. The prayers go up and nothing comes back. The church has known this for a long time. The sixteenth-century Carmelite John of the Cross gave it the name it still carries, the dark night of the soul, and he taught that God sometimes withdraws the felt consolations of faith in order to draw a person beyond dependence on the feelings to dependence on God himself.

The most famous modern witness is Mother Teresa of Calcutta. When her private letters were published in 2007 as *Come Be My Light*, edited by Brian Kolodiejchuk, readers learned that soon after she began her work among the poorest of Calcutta, around 1948 and 1949, she entered an interior darkness that, apart from a brief reprieve, lasted until her death in 1997. She wrote to her confessors, and to Jesus himself, of emptiness, of feeling that God was absent, of feeling at times that she had no faith at all. Skeptics read the letters as the confession of a woman who had stopped believing and kept up appearances. The letters are genuinely dark, but that reading has to account for what she did with the darkness. She kept praying. She wrote her anguish to the one whose absence she felt, as the author of Psalm 88 did, and kept tending the dying for nearly fifty years. And in 1961, with the help of the Jesuit theologian Joseph Neuner, she came to understand the darkness as a share in Christ's own suffering, part of the work rather than a verdict on it. Her letters are not the record of a fraud. They are the record of hands held up after the feeling had left the arms.

The Hebrew word for faithfulness, *emunah*, appears in an unlikely place. When Israel fought Amalek and Moses grew too tired to hold up his arms, Aaron and Hur held them up, "So his hands were steady until the going down of the sun" (Exodus 17:12). The word translated "steady" is *emunah*. Faithfulness, in that scene, is arms held up under load, partly by other people.

Care is needed here, because not every darkness is spiritual. Some of what feels like losing God is depression, grief or anxiety that has swept God into the fog with everything else. If your doubt is tangled with sleeplessness, hopelessness or a heaviness that is plainly larger than any question, tell someone you trust and see a doctor or a counselor. Treating it is not a betrayal of faith. This essay is not medical advice. If the darkness has come to include thoughts of ending your life, call or text 988, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, today. The feeling that God is gone is real. The conclusion despair draws from it, that there is no way through and no one who would want you to find one, is false.

## What the church owes the one who doubts

Much of the fault here belongs to us. The church has too often treated questions as rebellion, and it has done so less from malice than from fear. A community whose faith rests on certainty cannot afford a question, because every question threatens the structure. So we hand the doubter a verse and a worried look, or quietly move him out of the ministry he loved until the symptoms pass. We have all done versions of each, pastors first among us. The doubter learns that if he wants to keep his questions, he will have to keep them somewhere else. Many did. We did not lose them because they asked. We lost them because we would not let them ask among us.

Jude's short letter shows a better way, and it is worth reading his instruction in full: "And have mercy on those who doubt; save others by snatching them out of the fire; to others show mercy with fear, hating even the garment stained by the flesh" (Jude 22–23). Jude distinguishes among people in trouble. Some are in the fire and must be pulled out; some must be handled with fear. The doubter is to receive mercy. The church's recurring mistake has been to treat every doubter as though he were already in the flames, and to reach for the fire tongs when Jude asked for mercy.

Mercy in practice looks ordinary. It means pastors who can say "I don't know" in public. It means churches where the laments are sung, so a person in the dark discovers that Scripture already gave him words. It means the person who finally says *I'm not sure I believe any of this anymore* is met by someone who leans toward him rather than away.

And the doubter owes something too, and it is not certainty. It is the discipline Thomas practiced without knowing he was practicing it: stay in the room. Keep praying the honest prayer, the one with the doubt left in it. Keep company with people who confess what you cannot yet confess, and let them hold your arms up for a while. Investigate your questions, and doubt your doubts too. Don't make a permanent decision in a temporary state. Certainty that arrives suddenly in the middle of the night, in either direction, is worth waiting out.

On the cross, Jesus prayed the opening line of a lament: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46, quoting Psalm 22:1). He did not doubt the Father's existence. He bore the forsakenness that belonged to us. But he prayed it in the psalm's own grammar, as a cry addressed to "my God," which means that the prayer of the one who feels abandoned has been prayed by the Son of God himself. There is no darkness a believer can enter that her Lord has not already addressed to the Father.

And on the other side of that cry stands a risen man who still carries his wounds. When Jesus came to the doubter, he did not offer an argument or a feeling. He offered his hands. Then he spoke past Thomas: "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29). The blessing lands on the people who would have to believe with their questions still open, which is every one of us. It does not promise that the questions will close. John died in his cell with his question answered and his prison still locked. Mother Teresa died with her darkness unlifted. Some of what you are carrying may still be with you when you die. The question you walk out with is not whether you will ever be certain. It is which direction you will carry the question, toward the table where he offers his hands or away from it, and whether you will let anyone hold your arms up while you decide.
