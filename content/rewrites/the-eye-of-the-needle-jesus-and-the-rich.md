Most people who know one thing Jesus said about money know the camel. "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God" (Mark 10:25, ESV). A surprising number of them also know the explanation that takes the sting out of it. There was, the story goes, a small gate in the wall of Jerusalem called the Needle's Eye. A camel could just get through it if its owner unloaded the packs and made the animal kneel, and so the rich can enter the kingdom after all, provided they stoop a little and travel lighter. It's a tidy lesson, and it preaches well. It is also a story no one has ever been able to find in the ancient world.

The more searching question is why we wanted it so badly. A sentence doesn't generate a legend unless it frightens someone, and this one has frightened the church for as long as there has been a church. The reason isn't hard to find. Nearly everyone who reads this essay in America is, by the measure of the world Jesus spoke into and the world we live in now, the man in the story. I am. I own things I've found good reasons to keep, I've given some of my anxieties the respectable name of prudence, and there are comforts I'd be slow to part with even to follow the one I claim to follow. When I read the camel saying, I'm not reading about someone else. That is exactly why the gate is so attractive.

**The claim is this. Jesus named an impossibility, and every softening the church has invented, the gate, the rope, the purely inward reading, the two-tier ethic, even the heroic renunciation that makes poverty a new achievement, works the same way: it turns the impossible into the merely difficult, and the difficult is something we can manage. "All things are possible with God" doesn't reverse that verdict. It moves the power to save from the rich man to God, which rules out both despair and management, and it leaves wealth exactly as dangerous as Jesus said it was.**

## The man who ran, and what Mark set around him

Mark puts this story on the road to Jerusalem, and he frames it with two scenes that interpret it before and after. Immediately before it, people bring children to Jesus, the disciples try to send them away, and Jesus is indignant. Then he says, "Truly, I say to you, whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child shall not enter it" (Mark 10:15, ESV). A child in the first-century world had no standing, no property and nothing to trade. The kingdom, in this saying, is something received by people who bring nothing to the transaction. He takes the children in his arms and blesses them, and in Mark's next sentence a man comes running up to Jesus, kneeling in the road, and asking what he must do to inherit eternal life. The placement is the commentary. The one who can do nothing receives; the one who can do a great deal asks what to do. Even his verb gives him away, because an inheritance is the one kind of wealth no one earns.

Jesus answers first by questioning the compliment. "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18, ESV). Then he recites the commandments that govern life with a neighbor, and Mark's list has one item the other Gospels lack. "You know the commandments: 'Do not murder, Do not commit adultery, Do not steal, Do not bear false witness, Do not defraud, Honor your father and mother'" (Mark 10:19, ESV). Many commentators have suggested that "Do not defraud" stands where the tenth commandment's prohibition of coveting might have been, and whatever its origin, it names the characteristic sin of the landed rich in an agrarian economy: the withheld wage, the squeezed tenant, the debt called in at the worst moment. The Law had already warned about exactly that (Deuteronomy 24:14–15), and James would warn about it again (James 5:4). Jesus is not accusing the man. He is simply walking him along the edge of the place where fortunes were usually made.

The man says he has kept all of these from his youth, and nothing in the text suggests he's lying. Then comes the sentence on which the whole encounter turns. "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me'" (Mark 10:21, ESV). This unnamed man is the only person in Mark's Gospel of whom Mark says that Jesus loved him, and the command is what that love prescribed. Notice what the "one thing" turns out to be. It isn't an eleventh commandment. It is a person. Selling and giving clear the man's hands, and "come, follow me" tells him what the empty hands are for. Mark records the outcome without comment. "Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:22, ESV).

Only then does Jesus turn to the disciples. "And Jesus looked around and said to his disciples, 'How difficult it will be for those who have wealth to enter the kingdom of God!'" (Mark 10:23, ESV). They are amazed, and he says it again, this time addressing them with a word that reaches back to the scene with the children. "But Jesus said to them again, 'Children, how difficult it is to enter the kingdom of God!'" (Mark 10:24, ESV). Then the camel, and then a reaction far out of proportion to a warning about attachment. "And they were exceedingly astonished, and said to him, 'Then who can be saved?'" (Mark 10:26, ESV). Their shock makes most sense against an assumption they shared with most of their neighbors: that prosperity was a sign of God's favor, the covenant blessings of Deuteronomy 28 made visible in fields and barns. If a man God had visibly blessed, who had kept the commandments from his youth, could not get in, then no one could. They heard the saying correctly. Jesus doesn't correct their arithmetic. He corrects the direction they were looking. "Jesus looked at them and said, 'With man it is impossible, but not with God. For all things are possible with God'" (Mark 10:27, ESV).

The passage doesn't stop there, and the rest of it matters. Peter points out that the disciples have left everything, and Jesus promises that no one who has left house or family or lands for his sake and the gospel's will fail to receive "a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:30, ESV). Prosperity teachers have long read that hundredfold as a promised return on giving, and their case deserves to be put the way they would put it. The text does say houses and lands, and it does say "now in this time," not only in the age to come. The God of Deuteronomy 28 promised that obedience would show up in fields and barns, and Paul told the Corinthians that "whoever sows bountifully will also reap bountifully" (2 Corinthians 9:6, ESV). On this reading the rich man's failure was a failure to trust God's generosity. Had he sown, he would have reaped, and the passage is less a warning about wealth than an invitation to put it in God's hands and watch it multiply. (The site's essay on [the prosperity gospel](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel) traces where that teaching came from.)

The reading has to leave a good deal out to work. The hundredfold is a household before it is an estate: brothers and sisters and mothers and children, which no one receives a hundredfold except in a new family, the one Jesus had already named when he looked around a crowded room and called those who do the will of God his brother and sister and mother (Mark 3:35). Father appears in the list of what is left behind and drops out of the list of what is received, and many readers have taken that as deliberate, since this family has one Father. The promise comes "with persecutions," a clause that seldom survives into sermons on the hundredfold. Even Paul's harvest is enrichment for the sake of more giving: "You will be enriched in every way to be generous in every way" (2 Corinthians 9:11, ESV). The one who sells finds a hundred homes open to him, and persecution with them.

Then Mark sends the whole company on up the road, with Jesus walking ahead and the disciples afraid (Mark 10:32), toward the third prediction of his death. The chapter's summit comes a few verses later: "For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45, ESV). The man who could not let go of his possessions is set, in Mark's arrangement, a few steps behind the one who is about to let go of everything.

Matthew and Luke tell the same story with their own emphases, and the familiar name "the rich young ruler" is actually a composite of all three. Matthew calls him a young man, adds "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" to the commandments (Matthew 19:19), and frames the command conditionally: "If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me" (Matthew 19:21, ESV). Luke calls him a ruler and says he "became very sad, for he was extremely rich" (Luke 18:23, ESV), and then, a chapter later, lets us watch a rich chief tax collector named Zacchaeus come down from a tree and give half his goods away. A great deal of later theology hung on Matthew's "if you would be perfect," and Luke's Zacchaeus is the Gospels' own proof that the camel can get through, though not in any way the camel could arrange.

## A gate that was never there, and a rope that doesn't help

The Needle's Eye gate has no support in any ancient source. No first-century writer mentions it, no excavation of Jerusalem's walls has turned it up, and it seems to have entered Christian preaching many centuries after the Gospels were written. Even on its own terms it fails. A camel that kneels and is unloaded and squeezes through a low door is still a camel getting in by effort, and if that were the picture, the disciples would have had no reason to ask who could be saved, and Jesus no reason to answer with the word "impossible."

The rope theory is more learned and more tempting. The Greek word for camel is *kamēlos*; a rarer word, *kamilos*, means a rope or ship's cable. In later Greek the two vowels had come to sound alike, and a handful of late Greek manuscripts read "rope," a minor variant that a few ancient interpreters also favored. A cable through a needle is a tidier image than an animal, which is exactly why the suggestion has lasted. Most scholars reject it for three reasons. The manuscript evidence for "camel" in all three Gospels is overwhelming. Scribes tend to make hard readings easier, not the reverse, so the rope looks like a correction rather than the original. And the correction doesn't even achieve what it's for, since a ship's cable won't go through a needle either.

The saying is proverbial hyperbole, and Jesus reached for the same animal when he wanted to name something absurdly large: "You blind guides, straining out a gnat and swallowing a camel!" (Matthew 23:24, ESV). The camel was the largest animal a Galilean saw; the eye of a needle was the smallest opening anyone handled every day. The Babylonian Talmud, compiled centuries later in a land where the elephant was the biggest beast, uses the same figure with an elephant: a dream, it says, never shows a person an elephant passing through the eye of a needle, because no one has ever imagined such a thing (Berakhot 55b). Hyperbole of this kind is truth spoken at the volume it deserves.

What the gate and the rope share is a motive. Both convert an impossibility into a difficulty, and a difficulty is something a capable person can manage with enough humility, discipline or technique. The gate is not an archaeological mistake. It is a theological one, and the reason it keeps getting preached is that it says what capable people want to hear: that the kingdom is a hard squeeze but a possible one, and that the one doing the squeezing is you.

## Clement's question: can the rich be saved?

The most serious attempt to answer the saying without dissolving it came early. Around the year 200, Clement of Alexandria wrote a short treatise known as *Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?* for well-to-do Christians in one of the great commercial cities of the Roman world. His motive was pastoral, and it deserves to be stated as his defenders would state it. Some wealthy believers had heard Mark 10 read aloud and concluded they were shut out, and having lost hope, they had stopped trying to live as Christians at all. Clement wanted neither to flatter them nor to abandon them.

His argument, paraphrased, runs like this. Jesus cannot mean that the mere disposal of property saves, because pagan philosophers had given away their estates without thereby gaining life, and if outward poverty were the key, every beggar would stand nearest the kingdom by default. What Jesus commands is that the soul be stripped of its passions for wealth, the anxious grasping and false opinions that make money a master. Wealth itself is an instrument, which can be used well or badly, and a person who owns nothing cannot feed the hungry or shelter the stranger as Jesus elsewhere commands. The New Testament agrees at several points. Women of means supported Jesus' mission "out of their means" (Luke 8:3, ESV). Paul's instruction to Timothy assumes rich believers who remain in the church and gives them a charge rather than an eviction: "As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17, ESV). Jesus did not command every disciple to sell everything. Zacchaeus kept half.

Much of this is right, and the site's essay on [what the Bible says about money and wealth](/writing/what-the-bible-actually-says-about-money-and-wealth) gives Clement's account of wealth as a tool its full hearing. The question here is narrower: what Clement's reading has to set aside to stay gentle. Mark's man does not go away because of passions in the abstract. He goes away "for he had great possessions." The disciples did not hear an inward saying, and Peter's reply, that they had left everything, referred to real boats, real nets and a family trade left on the shore (Mark 1:16–20). The deeper trouble is that the inward reading is invisible. No one can check whether I have sold my passions for wealth. The man in Mark could see exactly what he was being asked, and so could everyone watching. Clement himself insisted that the rich give, and give freely. But once his reading is cut loose from his insistence, it becomes the quietest gate of all, one that every rich Christian can pass through without any change a poor neighbor would ever notice.

## Sixteen centuries of arguing with one verse

Not everyone took Clement's road. Athanasius's *Life of Antony*, written around 360, tells how a young Egyptian Christian walked into church around the year 270 just as Matthew 19:21 was being read, heard it as spoken to him, and went out and gave away the land he had inherited. The most famous conversion story of early monasticism is this verse taken at its word. In the 360s Basil of Caesarea preached on the same story in a sermon known as *To the Rich* (c. 368) and pressed on the one detail Matthew adds. The young man claimed to have loved his neighbor as himself; Basil asked how that could be true of a man who held so much while his neighbors lacked so much. His wealth was the evidence against his claim. Basil had given away his own inheritance, and in the years after the famine he built a complex of poorhouse, hospice and hospital outside Caesarea that people came to call the Basileias.

Peter Brown's *Through the Eye of a Needle* (2012) shows that the argument turned urgent only in the last decades of the fourth century, when genuinely wealthy Romans began entering the church in numbers. Among the circle around Pelagius, a treatise *On Riches* argued that riches themselves were the barrier, and reports of that teaching in Sicily reached Augustine, who answered in a letter around 414 (Letter 157). Abraham was rich and was called God's friend, he replied, and what shuts a person out is the love of riches and trust in them, though those who give up everything for Christ do a greater thing. The irony is worth noticing. The most rigorous reading of the camel saying came from a movement convinced that human beings can do what God commands, and in its hands renunciation became an achievement. The man's own question, "What must I do?", had come back dressed as an ascetic.

Francis of Assisi heard the verse differently from both. His early biographers tell of Francis and his first companion opening the Gospel book around 1208 and finding this command, and the rule he wrote for his brothers, the *Earlier Rule* of 1221, sets Matthew 19:21 near its opening and forbids the brothers to receive money. For Francis poverty was neither a price of admission nor a spiritual technique. It was the shape of following a Lord who had been poor, and the testimony of those who knew him is that it was joyful.

The medieval church found a way to honor this without requiring it of everyone. Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* (I-II, q. 108, a. 4, c. 1270), distinguished precepts, binding on all, from counsels, offered to those who would be perfect, and Matthew's "if you would be perfect" was the text beneath the counsel of poverty. The distinction took the command seriously and also noticed that Jesus did not give it to everyone. Its cost was two classes of Christian. The camel became someone else's vocation, the friar's and the monk's, while the merchant kept the precepts. Martin Luther's *On Monastic Vows* (1521) rejected that division and put every Christian under the whole gospel. That was a real recovery, but Protestantism, having closed the monastery, didn't always find anywhere else to put the command, and in many of our churches it went quietly back into the inward reading.

John Wesley tried to give it an address in ordinary economic life, and his three rules in "The Use of Money" (1760), gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can, made it neither the monastery nor the private attitude but a discipline anyone could see. And in 1937, running an illegal seminary for the Confessing Church, Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote in *Discipleship* that the church had learned to hear Jesus' concrete commands in what he called a paradoxical way, keeping the goods while claiming inward freedom from them, and that when this reading replaces obedience rather than guarding it, grace has been made cheap. He did not claim every Christian must sell everything. He claimed that the command came to a particular man as a call, and that escaping into a principle is one of the oldest ways to refuse it.

## What "all things are possible with God" does and doesn't soften

The whole argument turns on one distinction: between a difficulty and an impossibility. A difficulty is a problem for the person facing it. An impossibility is a problem only God can solve. Every gate the church has built turns the second into the first, and Jesus' answer to the disciples refuses to let that happen. He doesn't say the saying was an exaggeration or that it applies only to some. He says that with man it is impossible, and then he names the only one for whom it isn't.

That answer does two things. It rules out despair, because no rich person is beyond the reach of God, and Luke shows it happening in the very next chapter when Zacchaeus opens his hands before anyone has asked him to. It also rules out management, because entering the kingdom was never the rich man's accomplishment to begin with. The camel saying and the children saying are the same saying. Everyone enters the way a child does, with nothing to trade, and wealth is dangerous precisely because it is the thing most likely to persuade us that we have something to trade.

What the answer does not do is make wealth safe. God's possibility, when it arrives in the Gospels, doesn't look like a rich man who stays exactly as he was with a new attitude inside. It looks like Zacchaeus. Gary Anderson's *Charity* (2013) shows that in the Judaism of Jesus' day, giving to the poor was widely understood as a deposit made with God, a way of storing treasure where it could not be lost, and Proverbs had already said it: "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD, and he will repay him for his deed" (Proverbs 19:17, ESV). So "treasure in heaven" in Mark 10:21 isn't a vague consolation. It names where Jesus wanted the man's security moved. Grace that is real eventually shows up in the ledger. None of this is financial advice, and this essay can't tell any household what to sell or keep. It can only say that the passage will not leave our money alone, and that the God who makes the impossible possible usually does it by loosening a grip.

## The rich man Jesus let go, and the church that runs after him

Mark's scene has a detail churches tend to hurry past. The man walked away, and Jesus let him go. He didn't call after him with a more accommodating offer. The church has rarely managed the same restraint, and the reasons deserve a fair hearing. The wealthy need the gospel as much as anyone, and treating their souls as less worth reaching would be its own snobbery. Ministry costs money. Nicodemus sat on the ruling council, Joseph of Arimathea was rich enough to own a new tomb, and the church has had generous, influential members from its first days. Influence also travels through networks, and a converted executive may shape more lives than many others. None of that is frivolous.

Scripture's objection isn't to the rich being welcome. It is to their becoming the center. Paul reminded the Corinthians of the church's actual makeup: "For consider your calling, brothers: not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth" (1 Corinthians 1:26, ESV). James described the gathering where a man with a gold ring is shown to a good seat and a poor man is told to stand, and then asked the question that settles it: "Listen, my beloved brothers, has not God chosen those who are poor in the world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom, which he has promised to those who love him?" (James 2:5, ESV). And Jesus told a host whom to invite: "But when you give a feast, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind" (Luke 14:13, ESV), because they cannot repay, and the repayment comes "at the resurrection of the just" (Luke 14:14, ESV).

The history shows how quietly the center moves. Brown traces the patrons who became indispensable to the late Roman church. In 1889 Andrew Carnegie's essay "Wealth" recast the great fortune as a trust the rich man must administer for the public good, declaring that the man who dies rich dies disgraced, a Gilded Age Clement without the cross. In the same years Russell Conwell, a Baptist pastor in Philadelphia, delivered his lecture "Acres of Diamonds" thousands of times, telling audiences it was their duty to get rich and implying that most poverty was earned. I am a pastor, and I can't read that history from a safe distance. I have weighed which neighborhoods promised growth and which people were likely to give, and called it stewardship. Some of it was. Much of it was simply drifting in the direction the money pulled.

The distinction that matters is between a church that welcomes the rich to a table set around those who cannot repay, and a church organized around the comfortable that adds a program for the poor. The first is a family. The second is a clientele with a charity attached, and the skeptic who says so is usually seeing clearly; James said it first. For a household choosing a church, or a doubter deciding whether the church is honest, the question isn't the size of the building (the site's essay on [church wealth and the widow's mite](/writing/church-wealth-world-poverty) takes that up) but who sits at the center of the church's life, and whose comfort sets its tone.

## The man who went away

Mark gives the rich man no name and no ending. He goes away sorrowful, and the story walks on without him, up the road toward Jerusalem, with Jesus out in front and the disciples afraid of where he is going. That is the last thing the passage wants us to see. The one who told the man to sell everything and follow was himself about to give up everything, not a portion but his life, as a ransom for many. Paul would later compress the whole transaction into a sentence: "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). The needle was never a test the camel could pass. It was the measure of a poverty only Christ would enter, and the only way through it is to be carried by the one who went first.

That is why the saying has to stand at its full height. If it is only difficult, I will manage it, and I will manage it the way comfortable people always have, by admiring it from a good seat. If it is impossible, I am the disciple asking who can be saved, and I am finally standing where grace can reach me. But grace that reaches a rich man does not leave his hands where they were. Repentance here is more than a feeling about money; it is the slow, visible, costly loosening of a grip, in a family of believers where no one's security is meant to be held alone.

Nothing in the text says the man never came back. It is possible that he heard, after the cross, that the thing he could not do had been done for him, and came back lighter. The text leaves that open, and it leaves us in his place on the road, with the same love looking at us and the same command still standing. The question Mark hands the reader is not whether the rich can be saved. God has answered that. The question is what we are still holding, and whether we would rather keep it or follow.
