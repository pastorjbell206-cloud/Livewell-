The search usually begins somewhere private. A wife types the question late at night because someone has handed her a verse, and she wants to know whether it really says what she was told. A husband types it because he suspects the verse gives him something. A skeptic types it because she has heard that the Bible tells women to obey men and wants to see for herself how bad it is. All three are handed the same sentence, almost always on its own: "Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:22, ESV).

One thing needs to be said before anything else, because some who read this are reading it from inside danger. Nothing in this passage, and nothing anywhere in Scripture, requires a wife or a husband to endure abuse. A husband who uses the word headship to control, frighten, isolate, or hurt his wife isn't exercising a biblical role badly. He is sinning, and the church should call it sin. If that describes your home, you can call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, any hour of the day, and if you are in immediate danger, call 911. This essay returns to that at length near the end. It is set here first so that no one has to read four thousand words to find it.

The rest of the question deserves more patience than it usually gets. Submission can close the faces in a room of Christians before a sentence is finished, and outside the church it has become shorthand for everything a thoughtful person suspects is wrong with the faith. So here is the claim this essay has to earn. The verse begins a sentence earlier than it is usually quoted. Paul took a genre written to secure the power of the man at the top of the household and turned its heaviest command on that man. And faithful Christians still disagree, for serious reasons, about how much order remains once he has done it. That last part is where an honest answer has to begin.

### Two readings, each in its strongest voice

There are two broad positions among Christians who take the whole Bible as God's word, and both deserve to be heard as their best defenders would state them.

The first is usually called complementarian. Its fullest modern statement is the volume John Piper and Wayne Grudem edited, *Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood* (1991), which grew out of the Danvers Statement of 1987. It runs like this. God made man and woman equal in dignity, both bearing his image, and distinct in calling, and the distinction isn't a leftover of ancient culture but part of the goodness of creation. When Paul grounds his teaching about men and women, he reaches past the first century to Genesis (1 Corinthians 11:8–9; 1 Timothy 2:13). In Ephesians the analogy he chooses is Christ and the church, and that relationship is not symmetrical: "Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands" (Ephesians 5:24, ESV). Christ does not submit to the church. Paul never tells husbands to submit to their wives, though it would have been the simplest sentence in the world to write if he meant it. Grudem argued, in a 1985 survey of more than two thousand ancient uses of the Greek word for head, that it regularly carried the sense of authority, and he has argued that the "one another" of verse 21 can mean some to others, as roles require. Headship, on this reading, isn't privilege. It is the heaviest responsibility in the home, a man answering to God for the people entrusted to him, and its best defenders insist that the characteristic sin of the modern husband is less tyranny than absence. Take the distinction away, they say, and you haven't freed the woman. You've erased something God said at the beginning.

The second is usually called egalitarian, or mutualist, and its careful statement can be found in *Discovering Biblical Equality* (2004), edited by Ronald Pierce and Rebecca Merrill Groothuis with Gordon Fee. It runs like this. The first thing Scripture says about man and woman is that both together bear God's image and both together receive the charge to fill and rule the earth: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The first time a husband rules his wife is in the account of the curse, "he shall rule over you" (Genesis 3:16, ESV), which describes what sin did to marriage rather than what God designed. Redemption runs back toward the garden: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, ESV). Ephesians 5 opens with a command laid on every believer before anyone is singled out, and the same Paul tells the Corinthians that the wife has authority over her husband's body just as he has over hers (1 Corinthians 7:4). Gordon Fee argued in his commentary on 1 Corinthians (1987) that Paul's word for head points more naturally to source, the one from whom life flows, than to command. And the same household code that tells wives to submit tells slaves to obey, which no one now reads as God's permanent blessing on slavery. Consistency, the egalitarian says, asks us to read both instructions as the gospel working patiently inside a fallen social order it was already beginning to dissolve.

Each position is trying to honor the same Scripture, each can point to real texts, and each is grieved by what the other's worst adherents have done. The Bible holds a genuine tension here and does not resolve it as tidily as either camp would like.

That tells you what kind of question this is. The Nicene Creed does not mention it, and neither does Chalcedon; nothing about the Trinity, the person of Christ, the cross, or the resurrection hangs on it. It is a second-order question, the kind that divides faithful churches without dividing the faith. It shapes which congregation a family joins and how a denomination writes its confession, and it is worth arguing carefully. But a complementarian and an egalitarian can kneel at the same table, and if they cannot, the problem isn't their doctrine of marriage.

### The sentence begins at verse 21

Ephesians 5:22 is the middle of a sentence. The thought begins at verse 18: "And do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery, but be filled with the Spirit" (Ephesians 5:18, ESV). Paul then describes what a Spirit-filled church looks like through a string of participles: "addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody to the Lord with your heart, giving thanks always and for everything to God the Father in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:19–21, ESV). Submission first appears here as a fruit of the Spirit's presence in a worshiping community, alongside singing and thanksgiving, before it is ever a social arrangement.

Then comes the detail that changes how the whole passage sounds. In the oldest manuscript of Paul's letters we have, a papyrus copied around AD 200, and in the great fourth-century Codex Vaticanus, verse 22 has no verb at all. It reads, in effect, "wives, to your own husbands, as to the Lord." Later copyists supplied a verb to make the sentence stand on its own, which is exactly what we have been doing with it ever since. In its earliest form the instruction to wives borrows its verb from verse 21, and grammatically it is part of the command to mutual submission.

The complementarian is right that this doesn't make the word soft. The Greek verb *hypotassō* means to arrange oneself under, and the New Testament uses it for submission to governing authorities (Romans 13:1) and for the church's relation to Christ. But three features of Paul's usage matter. First, it is addressed to the wife herself, as a moral agent choosing her own conduct before God, in a letter that nowhere tells husbands to produce or enforce it. Second, Paul had a separate word for obedience, *hypakouō*, and he uses it a few verses later: "Children, obey your parents in the Lord" (Ephesians 6:1, ESV), and "Bondservants, obey your earthly masters" (Ephesians 6:5, ESV). He does not use it for wives. (Honesty requires adding that 1 Peter 3:6 does use it of Sarah, a point complementarians rightly raise.) Third, the wife's submission is "as to the Lord," which does not make the husband a stand-in for God. It does the opposite. It places the whole arrangement under a prior loyalty, and the apostles had already said what happens when a human command collides with that one: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29, ESV).

Here is the distinction everything else turns on. Submission in this passage is something a person gives. It is never something another person takes. A wife can order herself toward her husband's good as an act of devotion to Christ. A husband who demands it has shown that he doesn't understand what the word is, because submission that is extracted is no longer submission. It's subjugation, and Scripture has other names for that, none of them approving.

Then Paul turns to the husband and stays far longer: three verses to wives, roughly nine to husbands, and none of the nine is about authority. "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25, ESV). The man with all the legal power in the house is given, as his pattern, not the emperor or the master but the crucified. "In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies. He who loves his wife loves himself. For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church" (Ephesians 5:28–29, ESV). The image is precise. The husband who has to be told that his wife is hurting has already failed the standard, because a man doesn't need to be told when his own hand is burning.

Then Paul lifts the whole thing out of the household. He quotes Genesis, "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh," and adds, "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31–32, ESV). The marriage is a small enacted picture of a larger covenant, in which the one with every right to be served came to give himself away.

### A household code turned toward the powerful

To hear how this passage first sounded, you need to know its genre. Scholars call it a household code, and it had a long pedigree. In the first book of his *Politics*, written in the fourth century before Christ, Aristotle analyzed the household as three relationships, master and slave, husband and wife, father and child, and argued that the male is by nature fitter to command than the female. The discussion is addressed to the man who rules. The others are the ruled, and they are discussed rather than spoken to. Around the end of the first century, Plutarch's *Advice to the Bride and Groom*, a gentle and in places affectionate little book, counseled a wife to worship only the gods her husband believes in and to shut the door on foreign religions. A wife's religion belonged to her husband's household.

Against that background, three things about the New Testament codes stand out. They speak directly to the wife, the child, and the slave, addressing them as people with consciences before God. They speak to the powerful and lay duties on them, which the genre did not do: fathers are not to provoke their children (Ephesians 6:4), and masters are to "stop your threatening, knowing that he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven, and that there is no partiality with him" (Ephesians 6:9, ESV). And they reframe the whole household under a Lord who outranks its head. The shorter code in Colossians carries the same shape in two verses: "Wives, submit to your husbands, as is fitting in the Lord. Husbands, love your wives, and do not be harsh with them" (Colossians 3:18–19, ESV).

First Peter is the most striking of the three. It tells wives, "Likewise, wives, be subject to your own husbands, so that even if some do not obey the word, they may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives" (1 Peter 3:1, ESV). Set that beside Plutarch. Peter is writing to women who have taken up a faith their husbands don't share, which is precisely what the respectable moralists of the empire said a good wife must never do. His instruction to "be subject" assumes a woman whose conscience already belongs to Christ and not to her husband. And he ends with the husband: "Likewise, husbands, live with your wives in an understanding way, showing honor to the woman as the weaker vessel, since they are heirs with you of the grace of life, so that your prayers may not be hindered" (1 Peter 3:7, ESV). Co-heirs. A husband who dishonors his wife will find, Peter says, that heaven stops listening to him.

David Balch argued in *Let Wives Be Submissive* (1981) that Peter's code was partly apologetic, answering the charge that this new movement wrecked households, and Craig Keener made a similar case for Paul in *Paul, Women and Wives* (1992). The early Christians used a form their neighbors recognized, so the gospel would not be slandered, while filling it with content their neighbors would not have recognized at all. Keener draws the obvious conclusion: the same passage regulates slavery, and nobody reads Ephesians 6:5 as a permanent endorsement of owning people.

The egalitarian says that logic of accommodation applies to the wife as much as to the slave. The complementarian says it doesn't, because Paul never grounds slavery in creation, while he grounds marriage in Genesis 2:24 and in Christ's relation to the church. The distinction between accommodation and endorsement is the right one to argue over. What neither camp can honestly claim is that Paul simply copied the household code of his world. He kept its form and broke its logic, and the break runs straight through the husband.

### How a verse about dying became a license

The church has not always read the passage whole, but it has never lacked witnesses who did. John Chrysostom, preaching on Ephesians in the late fourth century, kept the wife's subjection and yet told husbands that Christ's example obliged them to care for their wives as Christ cares for the church, even if it meant suffering anything and giving their lives for them. That is the long tradition at its best. The trouble is that across most of Christian history the surrounding culture supplied the content of the word head, and the culture's picture was the Roman father, the lord of the manor, the man whose word was law in his own house. The cross was affirmed and the throne was practiced.

Two recent historians have traced how this worked in American evangelicalism, and their arguments deserve to be heard without either being swallowed whole or waved away. Beth Allison Barr argues in *The Making of Biblical Womanhood* (2021) that much of what is taught as biblical womanhood is the product of particular historical turns, the Reformation's narrowing of women's callings to the household and the nineteenth century's doctrine of separate spheres among them, rather than of Scripture read on its own terms. Kristin Kobes Du Mez argues in *Jesus and John Wayne* (2020) that for many white evangelicals since the middle of the twentieth century, the Jesus of the Gospels was gradually replaced by a militant masculinity, so that male authority in the home and a certain kind of power in public came to be defended as one package. The history they tell is real, and some of it is damning. It explains how men could memorize Ephesians 5:22 and never feel the weight of 5:25, and how, when the Southern Baptist Convention added to its confession in 1998 that a wife should graciously submit to her husband's servant leadership, the servant half of that phrase was so often left to fend for itself.

But the same instrument has to be turned in the other direction, or it becomes a tribe's weapon rather than a diagnosis. A history of how an interpretation was abused doesn't settle what a text says, as complementarian scholars rightly insist. And the reaction against domination has produced its own failure, quieter but not harmless. Robert Bellah and his colleagues described in *Habits of the Heart* (1985) the expressive individualism that shapes American life, a self whose first duty is to its own fulfillment and whose relationships last as long as they serve it. Inside that grammar, any submission of any kind sounds like self-betrayal, and a marriage can quietly become two sovereign selves negotiating terms. That isn't what careful egalitarians teach; mutual submission is at least as demanding as anything a complementarian asks. But it is what much of the culture hears, and it leaves homes where no one goes first and the shared spiritual life of the marriage dies of neglect while both people congratulate themselves on not being domineering.

Beneath the tyrant and the absentee lies the same grammar: both assume power is the prize. One man grasps it and calls it headship; another avoids every cost of leading and calls it respect. The passage condemns both with one sentence, because it defines the husband's place by what he must spend.

I have to stand inside this, not outside it, and I won't pretend I have always read this text well. Any man who has been married long enough knows how easy it is to call self-protection leadership, and how much more comfortable headship is when it means deciding than when it means absorbing the cost. I was raised without a father, so whatever I understand about being a husband I did not learn by watching one at home. And every father of sons, and I have five, has to reckon with the fact that each boy will one day read Ephesians 5 and decide what he thinks it permits him, and that what he has seen at home will teach him more than any sermon.

### Where I land, and what I have not answered

I read verse 21 as the roof over the whole passage. The mutual submission it commands is not a preface to be discarded once the real instructions begin; it is the frame that governs them. At the same time, I don't think Paul's distinct words to wives and husbands can be flattened into perfect symmetry without doing to the text what the tyrant does, reading our century back into his. Paul kept an order. He addressed wife and husband differently, and I take that seriously. But he filled that order with a content that can no longer serve the purpose orders usually serve, which is to secure the comfort of whoever sits at the top. The asymmetry I find in the passage is this: within a marriage where both spouses defer to one another out of reverence for Christ, the husband carries a particular calling to go first into self-giving, to initiate the costliest love, and to answer to God if the home is not being loved that way. The wife's submission is a gift she gives, freely and "as to the Lord," to a husband who is called to lay down his life. It is never a tax he collects. Complementarian readers will think this gives away too much. Egalitarian readers will think it keeps too much. I hold it with conviction, and without contempt for either.

The strongest objection I haven't fully answered comes from my complementarian brothers, and I want to state it the way they would recognize as fair. Paul's analogy is not only the cross. It is also "as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands" (Ephesians 5:24, ESV). The church's submission to Christ is not reciprocal. Christ does not defer to the church's judgment when the two disagree. If the analogy holds, then the asymmetry in the passage looks like an asymmetry of authority and not only of cost. My answer is that Paul immediately defines Christ's headship by what Christ does with it, which is to give himself up, to sanctify, to nourish and cherish, so that whatever authority the husband has is authority to serve and never to command his wife's conscience. That answers much of the force of the objection. It doesn't answer all of it, and I would be lying to say the phrase "in everything" disappears when I read it. It doesn't.

From the other side, the slavery parallel presses hard. If I read Paul's instructions to slaves as accommodation to a structure the gospel would eventually dissolve, why not his instructions to wives? My partial answer is the complementarian's: Paul roots marriage, unlike slavery, in creation and in Christ's covenant with his people. I think that answer is sound, and I hold it with humility, because Christians once used arguments from creation to defend orders the gospel later exposed.

A thoughtful Christian who lands a step to either side of me is still my brother or sister. What neither of us may do is leave the passage with a husband who is served instead of spent.

### What submission never means

Whatever one decides about the order Paul preserves, some things fall outside every faithful reading. Submission never requires anyone to endure abuse. Not physical violence, not sexual coercion, not threats, not the slow campaign of control that isolates a woman from her friends, her money, and her own judgment. The instruction is "as to the Lord," and the Lord does not ask his people to hand their bodies or consciences to cruelty. When a husband uses Ephesians 5 to demand compliance, he has read the one verse addressed to his wife, skipped the nine addressed to him, and laid the weight meant for himself on the person it was written to protect. He isn't exercising headship badly. He isn't exercising it at all. Headship understood as domination is sin, and it should be named in the church with the same plainness we name any other sin.

A wife who leaves to get safe has not rebelled against Scripture. A church that tells her to go home and submit harder has failed her, and has failed Christ. Peter told wives to "do good and do not fear anything that is frightening" (1 Peter 3:6, ESV), and a church should never be one more thing she has to fear. Pastors and friends who hear of abuse should believe the person telling them, put her safety ahead of the reputation of the marriage or the congregation, involve the authorities when the law requires it or safety demands it, and resist treating abuse as a communication problem to be solved in joint counseling, a practice many advocates caution against. Reconciliation, if it ever comes, comes after truth, repentance proven over time, and safety, never as a shortcut around them.

### Going first

Many Christian marriages suffer less from too much headship or too much submission than from something quieter. A husband and wife attend church, pray over a meal, and have almost no spiritual life together: no honest prayer in each other's hearing, no conversation about what God is doing in them, no confession offered to the one they have wronged. Both are waiting for the other to go first, and the waiting can outlast the marriage. Praying honestly in front of the person who knows your failures isn't too holy for most couples. It's too naked.

If the husband's particular calling is to go first into self-giving, then it includes going first into this. Be the first to confess when you were wrong. Be the first to say let's pray, even when your voice shakes, and to pray the true sentence rather than the polished one. Be the first to forgive, and the first to ask your wife how your leadership feels to her and hear the answer without defending yourself. That is the head who "nourishes and cherishes" on an ordinary Tuesday. And to the wife whose husband will not go first: mutual submission means you are not required to let the shared life of your marriage die while you wait for him. Go first yourself, gently and without contempt, inviting rather than shaming. Your faithfulness was never contingent on his.

In the end the question is not really who leads. It's who dies first, and on that the New Testament is not ambiguous at all. "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). He went first, before there was anything in us to answer him. On the night he was betrayed, the one with every right to be served took a towel and knelt: "If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet" (John 13:14, ESV). Every Christian husband is asked to take that towel, and every Christian wife is asked to kneel beside him, and if the passage has a hierarchy at all, it's a contest over who gets lower.

That leaves every reader with something to carry out of the room. The man who came looking for a verse that puts him in charge has found one that puts him on a cross, and he will have to decide tonight whether he wants it. The woman who came in fear has found that the text she was struck with was written to protect her. And those of us who stand in pulpits, and I include myself, have to reckon with how long the church read half a sentence and called it the will of God. The other half is still there on the page, waiting for a church honest enough to read it aloud, and to live in a way that makes the reading believable.
