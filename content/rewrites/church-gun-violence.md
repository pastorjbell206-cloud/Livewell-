There is a Sunday every American pastor now knows how to recognize. Somewhere during the week there was a shooting, and the sermon written on Tuesday suddenly sounds as if it were meant for another country. I have stood in that pulpit with two sermons in my head and preached the safer one. We grieve with real warmth, we pray with real sincerity, and then we move on, because the subject feels political and ministry is supposed to stand above politics. We call that posture pastoral wisdom. Too often its truer name is lost nerve, and I am describing the man I have been before I describe anyone else.

The silence is understandable, because the room really is divided among people who love Jesus. On one pew sits a man who hunts every November, taught his daughters to shoot, and keeps a pistol in the nightstand because he has thought hard about what he owes his family. A few rows back sits a mother who has buried a son, or a teacher who has practiced locking her classroom door with six-year-olds. He hears every call for new laws as an accusation. She hears the church's quiet as a verdict on whose grief counts. Both are in the building because of Christ, and neither is a caricature.

This essay tries to be fair enough that both of them finish it feeling understood. That means stating the Christian case for armed self-defense and the Christian case for nonviolence in the form their best defenders would sign, describing the law accurately, and letting the evidence say only what it can bear. It also means refusing the easiest move, which is to treat the whole subject as a policy fight the church may decline to join. Some of it is exactly that. But some of it is a question about the image of God, about whose deaths we have trained ourselves to see, and about what we trust when we are afraid.

## What the numbers say, and what they don't

Start with the federal government's own mortality data, because the numbers are less contested than the arguments built on them. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention compiles the nation's death certificates and publishes the counts through its CDC WONDER database. By that data, 48,204 Americans died from gunfire in 2022. The news covers mass shootings and street homicide, yet the largest share of those deaths was neither. More than 27,000 of them, about 56 percent, were suicides. Homicides made up most of the rest, more than 19,600. Suicide has been the larger part of American gun death for decades.

In 2022 three researchers at the University of Michigan, Jason Goldstick, Rebecca Cunningham and Patrick Carter, reported in the *New England Journal of Medicine* that in 2020 firearm injuries had passed motor vehicle crashes as the leading cause of death for Americans aged one to nineteen. And the dying is not spread evenly. Gun homicide falls with crushing weight on young Black men in a relatively small number of neighborhoods, while gun suicide falls most heavily on white men, disproportionately older and rural.

Each side's favorite image misleads. The gun-rights movement has been slow to speak of suicide as a gun question, though it is most of the toll and the dead are disproportionately the older rural men who fill many of its churches. The gun-control movement tends to build its public case around mass shootings and military-style rifles, though mass shootings are a small fraction of the dead and handguns account for most gun homicides. Christians on both sides have mostly adopted their side's picture without checking it against the count.

The numbers do not settle policy. They settle something prior, and Scripture is blunt about it:

> Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those who are stumbling to the slaughter. (Proverbs 24:11)

The next verse closes the exit most of us reach for:

> If you say, "Behold, we did not know this," does not he who weighs the heart perceive it? Does not he who keeps watch over your soul know it, and will he not repay man according to his work? (Proverbs 24:12)

The proverb turns knowledge into accountability. Once a community knows that tens of thousands of its people die this way every year, the profession of helplessness stops being innocent. After the first murder the Lord said to Cain, "What have you done? The voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground" (Genesis 4:10). The blood speaks whether or not we are listening, and God hears it whether or not it fits our politics.

## The case for the armed defender, at full strength

The Christian who keeps a gun to protect his household stands in a line of moral reasoning that runs through the Torah, the medieval schools and the civil rights movement. The law of Moses assumes a man may defend his home:

> If a thief is found breaking in and is struck so that he dies, there shall be no bloodguilt for him, (Exodus 22:2)

The next verse sets the limit:

> but if the sun has risen on him, there shall be bloodguilt for him. He shall surely pay. If he has nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft. (Exodus 22:3)

In the dark, the householder cannot know whether the intruder came to steal or to kill, and the law does not make him find out first. In daylight, when he can see and call for help, the same blow becomes bloodguilt. Even this oldest text permits force only inside a boundary of necessity.

Then there is the passage every Christian gun owner has heard quoted. On the night of his arrest, Jesus tells the disciples, "But now let the one who has a moneybag take it, and likewise a knapsack. And let the one who has no sword sell his cloak and buy one" (Luke 22:36). He ties it to prophecy: "For I tell you that this Scripture must be fulfilled in me: 'And he was numbered with the transgressors.' For what is written about me has its fulfillment" (Luke 22:37). The scene ends abruptly:

> And they said, "Look, Lord, here are two swords." And he said to them, "It is enough." (Luke 22:38)

The passage is genuinely contested. One reading takes it plainly: the season in which Jesus' presence protected them is over, and they will need a purse, a bag and the means of defense in a hostile world. Those who hold it add that in the garden Jesus stopped a blow that would have obstructed the cross, and in Matthew's account told the disciple to put his sword back into its place, not to throw it away. The other reading takes the command as a figure for coming hostility that the disciples hear too literally. On that view "It is enough" closes a conversation gone wrong, two swords could never have defended the band of disciples, the swords serve to let Jesus be counted among outlaws as Isaiah foretold, and within hours Jesus meets the first use of one with "No more of this!" and heals the wounded man's ear (Luke 22:51). Neither reading is foolish, and the passage alone does not settle the question either way.

Augustine, the great architect of Christian thinking about force, was reluctant about private self-defense. In *On Free Choice of the Will* (388 to 395) he grants that a law permitting a traveler to kill a robber is just as law, yet doubts the killer is free of sin, since he killed to keep a life he was bound to hold loosely. Augustine accepted public force for the common good and hesitated at private force for the self. Thomas Aquinas answered him in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, question 64, article 7). An act, Thomas argued, can have two effects, one intended and one beside the intention. The man defending himself intends to preserve his life; the attacker's death is not his aim. The act is lawful if proportionate and unlawful the moment it uses more force than necessary. No one is bound to forgo moderate self-defense to spare the aggressor, but a private person may never make the attacker's death his purpose. That is the root of what later moralists called double effect, and it remains the tradition's most careful account of when a Christian may strike.

The twentieth century sharpened it. Reinhold Niebuhr argued in "Why the Christian Church Is Not Pacifist" (1940) that in a sinful world love for the neighbor sometimes requires resisting the aggressor, and that refusing can abandon the weak to the strong. Paul Ramsey, in *War and the Christian Conscience* (1961), grounded the Christian justification of force not in self-interest but in love for the neighbor under attack. Heard at full strength, the defender's case is finally about the child down the hall rather than about his rights.

American history adds a witness the suburban debate forgets. In 1964 in Jonesboro, Louisiana, Black men, many of them veterans, organized the Deacons for Defense and Justice and carried rifles to protect civil rights workers and their families, because local law enforcement would not and in places rode with the Klan. Charles E. Cobb Jr. documents in *This Nonviolent Stuff'll Get You Killed* (2014) how much of the Southern freedom struggle was quietly guarded by armed neighbors, and Adam Winkler's *Gunfight* (2011) records that Martin Luther King Jr., after his home was bombed in 1956, applied for a permit to carry a pistol and was refused. For people with that memory, distrust of a state monopoly on force is simply what history taught them.

A thoughtful Christian gun owner would sign that case, and much of it is true. Whatever the church says next has to be said with it still standing.

## The case for laying down the sword, at full strength

The Christian who refuses to kill, even to save his own life, is usually not naive about evil. He has read the same Gospels and stopped where they stop: in a garden at night, the one plainly innocent man in history being seized unjustly and a friend drawing steel to defend him. If force was ever justified, it was then. Jesus forbade it:

> Then Jesus said to him, "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword." (Matthew 26:52)

For this tradition the scene is the cross's pattern for everyone who follows, not an exception carved out for Jesus alone. He had already taught, "Do not resist the one who is evil. But if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matthew 5:39). And Peter, the man who swung the sword, later wrote that Christ left us an example: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23).

Roland Bainton argued in *Christian Attitudes Toward War and Peace* (1960) that the church before Constantine was predominantly pacifist. Later scholars have complicated that, pointing to Christians in the Roman army by the late second century. What is not disputed is the depth of the reluctance about shedding blood. Tertullian, in *On Idolatry* around 200, argued that when the Lord disarmed Peter he disarmed every soldier. Origen, answering the pagan Celsus around 248, said Christians served the emperor by prayer rather than arms. Even after the church accepted military service, Basil of Caesarea, in a letter of about 374, noted that the fathers before him had not counted killing in war as murder, yet advised, as counsel rather than rule, that soldiers who had killed abstain from communion for three years, since their hands were not clean. Much of it concerned the army and its pagan oaths more than the private householder. But the instinct is plain: killing, even when permitted, left a wound on the one who did it.

John Howard Yoder gave the tradition its most influential modern form. In *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) he argued that Jesus was offered, and refused, the Zealot path of bringing in God's kingdom by the sword, and that the cross was the price of the refusal. The church is called to faithfulness, not to making history turn out right by whatever force it takes. A hard note belongs here: a discernment process of Mennonite Church USA, which concluded in 2015, commissioned research documenting decades of his sexual abuse of women and the church's failure to stop it, so the argument must stand on the Gospels rather than on the man. Stanley Hauerwas argued in *The Peaceable Kingdom* (1983) that Christian nonviolence is less a strategy for reducing harm than the shape of a people who believe the resurrection, and so can afford patience, because the world's survival does not rest on their willingness to kill. Nor is this passivity. Pacifists insist the question "What would you do if someone attacked your family?" has many answers short of killing: shielding, restraining, fleeing, taking the blow.

Set side by side, the two traditions agree on more than their partisans do. Both forbid a Christian to aim at another's death; Thomas says so as plainly as Yoder. Both demand the least force that will do. Both insist the attacker is an image-bearer for whom Christ died. Where they divide, the church has held the question open within the creeds since Augustine, which makes it second-order: brothers and sisters may disagree without either leaving the faith. Each side carries an objection it has not fully answered. The defender must reckon with the fact that the one time Jesus faced his exact scenario, an innocent under attack and a disciple with a blade, Jesus stopped the blade. The pacifist must reckon with Ramsey's neighbor, the child whose life may hang on someone's willingness to act. This essay does not settle what the church has not settled.

## What the law says, and what the evidence can bear

The Second Amendment, ratified in 1791, reads: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." For most of the twentieth century courts read it largely through its militia clause. *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008) changed that. Writing for a five-to-four majority, Justice Antonin Scalia held that the amendment protects an individual right, unconnected to militia service, whose core is self-defense in the home, and struck down the District's handgun ban. The same opinion said the right is not unlimited, naming as presumptively lawful the bans on possession by felons and the mentally ill, limits on carrying in sensitive places such as schools, and conditions on commercial sale. *McDonald v. City of Chicago* (2010) applied the right to the states. In *New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen* (2022), a six-to-three majority led by Justice Clarence Thomas struck down New York's requirement that applicants show "proper cause" to carry in public, and held that a gun law must be consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation rather than justified by weighing costs and benefits. *United States v. Rahimi* (2024) then upheld, eight to one, the federal law disarming people under domestic-violence restraining orders that find them a credible threat. This describes what the Court held and is not legal advice. The law differs by state and is still moving; anyone deciding what to own, carry or store should learn the law where they live and, when it matters, consult a qualified attorney.

What the Constitution cannot do is tell a Christian what to do. A right to own a gun is not a duty to own one, and a law that survives the Court is not thereby wise. Paul drew the same distinction for the Corinthians: "'All things are lawful,' but not all things are helpful. 'All things are lawful,' but not all things build up" (1 Corinthians 10:23). The rule he gave next governs Christian liberty on every side: "Let no one seek his own good, but the good of his neighbor" (1 Corinthians 10:24).

The evidence humbles both parties. The RAND Corporation's *The Science of Gun Policy* (first edition 2018, since updated), a systematic review of the research, found its strongest support for laws requiring guns to be stored away from children, which reduced youth firearm suicides and self-injuries and unintentional shootings of children, and found moderate evidence that "stand your ground" laws increase homicides. For many measures argued over most loudly, including assault-weapons bans, it judged the evidence limited or inconclusive. Christians who share every conviction about human life will read these studies and land in different places.

So the disagreement has to be ordered. Some things bind every conscience. Each of these deaths ends a life made in God's image, and Scripture grounds the gravity of killing exactly there: "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image" (Genesis 9:6). Lament, truthfulness about who is dying, and refusal to sort the dead by their usefulness to our side are not optional. Whether to own a gun, whether a Christian may kill in self-defense, and which mix of background checks, storage laws, red-flag orders and carry rules would reduce the dying are second-order questions, and the division over them is not apostasy in either direction.

*The church has rarely failed here by choosing the wrong policy. It has failed by going quiet on what binds every conscience to avoid arguing about what doesn't.*

The failure runs both ways. There are pulpits where a gun bill is preached as the plain meaning of the Sermon on the Mount, and there are men's retreats where the rifle has become an emblem of Christian manhood, printed on the shirt beside the cross. Both have conscripted the faith. The skeptic watching sees two camps using one God to bless opposite arsenals and concludes the God is decoration. We gave him the evidence.

## The death we don't talk about

The American most likely to die by a gun is a man, often middle-aged or older, alone with his own gun on the worst night of his life. That fact sits at the center of the numbers and almost nowhere in the church's speech. For centuries the church answered suicide with shame, at times refusing Christian burial to those who died by it, and the shame has outlived the rules. It lives on in men who cannot bring themselves to tell anyone, least of all a pastor, what they are thinking.

Much of the foundational research here comes from the Harvard Injury Control Research Center. David Hemenway set out the public-health case in *Private Guns, Public Health* (2004), and in 2008 he and Matthew Miller summarized the evidence in the *New England Journal of Medicine*: across American states, higher household gun ownership goes with higher suicide rates, the difference coming from gun suicides while suicides by other means run at similar rates. Their explanation is not that gun owners despair more than anyone else. Many suicidal crises are brief, a gun is far more lethal than most other means, and most people who survive an attempt do not later die by suicide. The means at hand in the worst hour often decide whether there is a next day.

That evidence should be stated no more strongly than it goes. State comparisons show association, and critics rightly note how hard it is to rule out everything else that differs between places. It says nothing about the character of any particular owner, most of whom will never harm anyone. But it has grown firmer. In 2020 a team led by David Studdert of Stanford followed millions of adult Californians over more than a decade and found that men who owned handguns died by suicide at markedly higher rates than men who did not, almost entirely by gun. The finding concerns access in a crisis, and it can be heard in the language gun owners already speak, which is safety.

So the church has a word here that needs no legislature. Any Christian can learn to ask a hurting friend whether there is a gun in the house, and offer to hold it for a while, the way a friend holds the car keys at the end of a bad night. Families can decide in advance that when someone is in the dark, the guns go to a trusted relative until it passes. State laws on handing over a gun, even for a few days, differ, and some gun shops and police departments will store one temporarily. This comes from a pastor, not a clinician, and it is not medical or legal advice; a doctor or licensed counselor belongs in the help.

Scripture does not flinch from this dark. After his greatest triumph Elijah sat under a broom tree and asked to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers" (1 Kings 19:4). God did not argue with him first. He let him sleep, and an angel touched him and said, "Arise and eat" (1 Kings 19:5). The first ministry to a man who wanted to die was bread, rest and company.

If you are in that place now, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour, and ask someone you trust to keep any gun in your home for you tonight. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Your despair is telling you something false about your life.

## When the shooting comes to church

On June 17, 2015, a young white supremacist joined the Wednesday night Bible study at Emanuel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Charleston, South Carolina, sat with the members for about an hour, and then killed nine of them, including their pastor, Clementa Pinckney. Two days later, at his bond hearing, relatives of several of the dead spoke to him of forgiveness. On November 5, 2017, a gunman killed twenty-six people during worship at First Baptist Church of Sutherland Springs, Texas. An Air Force court-martial had convicted him of assaulting his wife and stepson, which should have barred him from buying a gun, but the Air Force never sent the record to the FBI's background-check database. As he left, a neighbor, Stephen Willeford, ran out with his own rifle, shot him, and helped chase him down.

After Sutherland Springs, one side pointed to the armed neighbor and the other to the broken background check. Both were telling the truth, and neither wanted the other half.

Since then churches have argued about armed security, and it is a real argument among faithful people. Some elders conclude that a trained, accountable team is part of what shepherds owe the flock, since the hired hand is the one who "sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees" (John 10:12). Others conclude that a sanctuary guarded by guns preaches something about where safety comes from that no sermon can unsay, and that the Charleston families gave a witness no security team could. This is prudential, and a congregation should decide it slowly, with its most vulnerable members in the room.

Beneath it lies the question the church must put to every side. The idol is never the object; it is the trust. The psalmist wrote in a nation with an army and did not tell Israel to melt its chariots. He told it where confidence belonged: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (Psalm 20:7). John Calvin observed in the *Institutes* (1559) that human nature is a perpetual factory of idols. It builds them from whatever lies nearest. The gun owner has to ask whether the weapon has become where his hope goes when he is afraid, and whether the industry that sells him fear is catechizing him more than his church. The advocate has to ask whether he believes the right statute can secure what only God secures, and whether the politics that raises money from his fear is catechizing him too. "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18).

The state is the hardest part, because each side's idol is the other's legitimate fear. The governing authority "does not bear the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4), which should chasten any Christian who imagines his rifle as the final check on lawful government. Yet the Deacons knew a state that would not protect them, which should chasten any Christian who imagines the state's monopoly on force as simply safe. Most of us feel only one of those pulls, and that is the one to distrust.

## The watchman and the blood that speaks

The Black church has been burying its young from gunfire for generations, while many white congregations found the subject urgent mainly when it reached schools and sanctuaries that looked like their own. Walter Brueggemann argued in *The Prophetic Imagination* (1978) that a comfortable society survives by numbness, and that the prophet's first task is to bring denied grief into public speech. I can tell you which shootings interrupted my sermon preparation and which did not, and the pattern embarrasses me.

Ezekiel was given an image for this responsibility:

> But if the watchman sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet, so that the people are not warned, and the sword comes and takes any one of them, that person is taken away in his iniquity, but his blood I will require at the watchman's hand. (Ezekiel 33:6)

The watchman does not design the city's defenses or settle how the wall should be built; the Christian legislator, officer, gun owner and advocate will answer those questions differently. He is responsible for saying aloud what he sees. The church can leave the second-order questions open and still refuse to pretend it has not seen tens of thousands of image-bearers a year, most dead by their own hand, thousands of them children and teenagers, all known to God by name. And the watchman needs his own psalm: "Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain" (Psalm 127:1). The gun owner may be the one friend a despairing man trusts with the question, and the one who can take the gun for a while.

The blood of Abel still cries from the ground. Hebrews answers it with another: "and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Hebrews 12:24). The better word is that God entered our violence, took it into his own body at the hands of men with swords and a state behind them, and would not summon the legions of angels he said were his to call (Matthew 26:53). Whatever a Christian decides about the nightstand drawer, he decides it under that cross, as someone whose life was bought by a death absorbed rather than avenged.

So watch your first reaction when the next alert crosses your phone. If the first question is which side this helps, the numbness has done its work, in you and in me. The blood at the gate has names, and God knows every one of them. What remains is whether the watchmen on the wall will learn them too.
