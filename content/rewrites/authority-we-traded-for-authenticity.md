Listen to how believers talk now, and then to how they talked a generation ago, and you'll hear one sentence quietly replace another. The old sentence was *the Bible says*. The new one is *I feel like God is*. They sound like cousins. In practice they point in opposite directions. The first places the self under a word that stands over it. The second places the word under a self that stands over it. The change came slowly, a phrase at a time, and for reasons that looked like mercy, and it has settled more in the progressive church than any single doctrine ever did. It did not change what we believe so much as it changed who decides.

That is the question this essay is about. When my experience and the Scriptures disagree, which one is the judge? It is a real question, and the people who answer it differently from the historic church are not fools or villains. Many of them have been badly hurt by people who said *the Bible says* while meaning something much smaller. So the argument has to begin with their case at its strongest. It also has to admit that the conservative church, which thunders about biblical authority, has made the same trade in a louder voice. And it has to end somewhere other than critique, because a faith that only tears down is not the faith the church confessed.

The claim, stated plainly, turns on one distinction. Experience has a real and honored place in the Christian life, and it has corrected the church before. But it has done so as a witness brought into court, never as the judge seated on the bench. The progressive church did not so much abandon biblical authority as quietly move the bench, from a word outside the self to the experience inside it. A faith ruled by the self cannot confront the self, and a self that cannot be confronted cannot be rescued.

## The strongest case for experience

The case its best defenders would make goes something like this.

First, *the Bible says* has a history, and much of it is ugly. In the American South before 1865, preachers and professors said the Bible sanctioned slavery, and they had verses to point to: Abraham held servants, the law of Moses regulated slavery rather than abolishing it, and Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon. The men who said *the Bible says* in those years were often the most confident readers in the country, and they were catastrophically wrong. Anyone who has been on the wrong end of that confidence has reason to distrust it.

Second, experience has corrected the church's reading of Scripture before, and Scripture itself records it. For centuries Israel had read circumcision as the permanent sign of belonging to God's people; Genesis calls it an everlasting covenant. Then Peter walked into the house of a Roman centurion, and while he was still preaching the Holy Spirit fell on uncircumcised Gentiles. When the church met in Jerusalem to decide what to do, Peter argued from what he had seen: "And God, who knows the heart, bore witness to them, by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us" (Acts 15:8, ESV). The church changed a boundary it had held for generations because of what God was plainly doing in people's lives. The progressive Christian asks, reasonably, why the Spirit could not be doing the same today.

Third, the tradition has always given experience a seat. John Wesley's movement taught that the believer may know by the Spirit's inward witness that he is a child of God, and in the twentieth century the Methodist historian Albert Outler summarized Wesley's method as a "quadrilateral" of Scripture, tradition, reason and experience. Half a century after Wesley's conversion, and far more radically, Friedrich Schleiermacher wrote *On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers* (1799) to readers who had written Christianity off. He argued that religion is not first a set of doctrines imposed from outside but a living awareness at the root of human experience, what he later called the feeling of absolute dependence on God. His motive was missionary. He was trying to win people back.

All of that deserves to be granted. The church has used the phrase *the Bible says* as a club. Reading a text within its own world is not unbelief; it is often repentance for a long history of careless reading, and it has prevented a great many cruelties. The Spirit does work in human lives in ways that send us back to the Scriptures to see what we missed. Anyone who wants to answer the progressive case honestly has to begin by agreeing with most of it.

## How the bench moved

The trouble lies in what happened next, and it happened over two centuries.

Schleiermacher's move, grounding faith in religious experience, became the working method of liberal Protestantism through the nineteenth century. In 1922 Karl Barth, himself trained in that tradition, turned against it in a lecture later published as "The Word of God and the Task of the Ministry." His charge, put in paraphrase, was that liberal theology had tried to speak about God by speaking about humanity in a loud voice. Barth had watched his own teachers sign a public statement in support of the Kaiser's war aims in 1914, and he concluded that a theology built on human religious experience had nothing to say when human experience went badly wrong. It could only echo the age.

Later in the century the sociologists found the same shift in the pews. In *Habits of the Heart* (1985), Robert Bellah and his colleagues gave it a human face. They interviewed a young nurse they called Sheila Larson, who described her faith this way: "It's Sheilaism. Just my own little voice." Bellah did not mock her. He treated her as the logical end of a long American road, a religion with as many denominations as there are believers, each one its own pope and its own canon. Sheila was not a rebel against the church. She was simply saying out loud where the bench had been moved.

The trade almost never arrives as a declaration that Scripture has no authority. It would feel like betraying something still loved, and the love is often real. It arrives as a method of reading. Passages that wound the self are quietly reassigned to their ancient context and their human authors. Passages that console the self are received as the timeless heart of the gospel. The hard sayings become *that was their world*; the comforting ones become *this is who God really is*. The standard that sorts the two piles is never named, because naming it would end the game. The standard is the self.

The honest version of context-reading and the self-serving version look almost identical from outside. The difference is which way correction runs. In the first, I let the text's world correct my reading of the text, and I sometimes discover the passage says something harder than I thought. In the second, I let my world correct the text, and it never does. There is a test, and it's merciless: when did the Scriptures last win an argument against me? Not against my opponents. Against me. If every reading lands where I had already arrived by other roads, then I do not hold the Bible as an authority. I hold it as a mirror, and a book that only ever says what I already believe is not my Lord. It is my echo, bound in leather.

## A witness, not a judge

Go back now to the progressive case at its strongest, to Jerusalem in Acts 15, because the passage settles more than its defenders notice.

Peter tells what God did in Cornelius's house. Barnabas and Paul tell of signs and wonders among the Gentiles. That is experience, brought into the assembly as testimony. Then James stands up, and his first move is to take the testimony to the Scriptures: "And with this the words of the prophets agree, just as it is written" (Acts 15:15, ESV). He quotes Amos, who had said centuries earlier that the nations would be called by the Lord's name. The experience was received because it matched what God had already promised. And the verdict the council sent to the churches was not *we feel that*, but "it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (Acts 15:28, ESV), a judgment reached together, tested against the prophets, and sent out under apostolic authority. Experience testified. Scripture judged. The church submitted.

The same pattern holds for Wesley. On 24 May 1738, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street in London, someone was reading aloud from Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans, and Wesley wrote in his journal, "I felt my heart strangely warmed." It is the most famous religious experience in Protestant history, and notice what it confirmed: a text, read aloud, about justification by faith. Outler himself later complained, in a 1985 article, that his "quadrilateral" had been widely misread as four equal authorities, when for Wesley Scripture stood first and the others served it.

And the same pattern holds, most importantly, for slavery. Mark Noll argues in *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis* (2006) that the proslavery side often won the verse-by-verse argument under the commonsense way of reading that dominated American Protestantism, and that the nation finally settled with armies what its theologians could not settle with exegesis. But look at who saw clearly. Frederick Douglass, in the appendix to his *Narrative* (1845), drew a line between "the Christianity of this land" and "the Christianity of Christ." He did not appeal from Scripture to his own feelings. He appealed from a corrupt reading to the Lord of the text. The experience of the enslaved was a witness, and it was true testimony, because it exposed what the slaveholders' reading had hidden: that the image of God in every person, the exodus from bondage, and the Christ who took the form of a servant stood in judgment over the whole institution. The slaveholders were the ones who had let the self sit on the bench. Their interests edited the text while their mouths said *the Bible says*.

That is the distinction. Experience brought as witness can expose a false reading and send the church back to the text to hear it more truly. Experience seated as judge can do none of that, because it has abolished any court above itself. And Scripture is blunt about why the judge's chair cannot belong to the self. "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9, ESV). The verse is not a relic of a darker theology. It is the one diagnosis a faith built on inner experience cannot afford to hear, because the whole structure rests on trusting the inner voice. Jeremiah says the inner voice is the one witness that must never testify without corroboration, and corroboration comes, by definition, from outside.

## The same trade on the right

I have to turn this on my own side of the room now, because an essay that only convicts the people I disagree with is positioning, not prophecy.

The conservative who claims full submission to biblical authority while reading the text through nationalism, or through the unregulated market, or through a picture of the 1950s American household that appears nowhere in Scripture, has made the identical trade. He has installed his politics as a canon within the canon and shouted about authority loudly enough to drown out the sound of his own editing. The proslavery preachers of 1850 were the conservatives of their day, and they are the clearest example in American history of a self, and a class, sitting in judgment on the text while claiming to kneel before it. The prosperity preacher who builds a gospel of health and wealth out of a handful of verses has done the same thing with a smile. Paul warned Timothy that "the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" (2 Timothy 4:3, ESV). Every tribe in the American church has its curated feed of such teachers. The only difference is which passions they suit.

Alasdair MacIntyre argued in *After Virtue* (1981) that once a shared authority is lost, moral argument decays into emotivism, the reduction of every moral claim to an expression of preference: *I approve of this; do so as well*. Our arguments become interminable because nobody can appeal to anything the other side is bound by. That describes the progressive church when it argues from experience, and it describes the conservative church when it argues from a tradition it has quietly reshaped to fit its politics. Both have dismantled the court and kept shouting at each other in the empty room.

This is not the left's private failure. It is the oldest human one. In the garden the serpent did not offer rebellion, which is a hard sell. He offered a self that would decide: "you will be like God, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5, ESV). And the book of Judges ends its account of a people in freefall with a sentence that reads like a coroner's report: "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25, ESV). The horror of those last chapters is not that people chose evil knowing it was evil. It is that they did what seemed right to them, sincerely, with no king over their sincerity.

I stand behind a pulpit with this book open, and I know how easily I reach for the passage that confirms what I already came to say. The indictment does not land on the progressive church first. It lands on every pulpit, mine included.

## Reading under the creed

The answer to the progressive trade is not a return to the conservative one, the lone interpreter with a Bible and a flag. The church has an older way of reading, and it answers both.

In the second century the church faced teachers who quoted the same Scriptures and drew an entirely different god out of them. Irenaeus of Lyons, writing *Against Heresies* around 180, did not answer them with a better private reading. He answered with the rule of faith, the summary of the apostles' teaching that the scattered churches had received and held in common: one God the Father, maker of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, made flesh for our salvation; one Holy Spirit. The rule did not replace Scripture. It was the shape in which Scripture had been handed on, the thing that let a church in Gaul and a church in Asia recognize the same Lord in the same book. Our creeds grew from it. Two and a half centuries later Vincent of Lérins, in his *Commonitorium* (434), offered a test for any new teaching: hold to what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all. That test cuts against innovation from every direction. It rules out the progressive who revises the faith to fit the moment, and it rules out the conservative who mistakes his grandparents' politics for the faith once delivered.

Reading under the creed means reading with the communion of saints, the whole church across twenty centuries, and not merely with the people who agree with me this year. C. S. Lewis made the practical point in his introduction to Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* (1944). Every age, he argued, has its own characteristic blindness, and we cannot see ours because we are inside it. The old books help, not because their authors were wiser than we are, but because their mistakes are different from ours and so they catch what we cannot. That is why the Bereans remain the model. Luke praises them because they "received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (Acts 17:11, ESV). They tested the apostle by the Scriptures, not the Scriptures by their preferences. When Luther stood before the emperor at Worms in 1521 and refused to recant, he said his conscience was captive to the Word of God. The principle had teeth because it bound the man invoking it. Lesslie Newbigin spent the last years of his life, in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989), insisting that the gospel is public truth, an announcement of what God has done, and not a private preference ratified by the inner sense of the believer.

The cost of losing all of this does not show up as cost at first. It shows up as freedom, and the bill comes later, on the night the inner voice turns. A self that is its own authority does well until the self begins to drown, until the voice that was supposed to be the truest thing about me starts saying that I am worthless, that the marriage cannot be saved, that the dark is right about me. In that hour a faith grounded in experience has nothing to offer, because it can only return me to the self, and the self is what has failed. If that voice is telling you tonight that you would be better off dead, do not argue with it alone. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour, and a pastor or a friend should hear it from you too.

What a drowning person needs is a word from outside the cell, a voice that can say *you are not what your worst hour says you are* and be believed against the whole weight of his own feeling. John wrote exactly that word: "for whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything" (1 John 3:20, ESV). No one can derive that sentence from inside the heart. When the heart condemns, a religion of the heart has already lost the case, because it made the heart the judge.

We thought authority was the cage and the inner voice the open door. We had it backwards. The sovereign self is the smallest room in the world, and its only window faces inward. The Word that stands over us, that we did not write and cannot revise, is the only word that can reach into that room, and it does not come to flatter. It comes the way a physician comes to a patient who has begun to trust the disease, with a diagnosis and a cure, and the cure was paid for on a cross by the One who is the Word. The progressive church was right that people have been crushed by counterfeit authorities that deserved to fall. It was wrong to think the answer was no authority but the self. The question each of us carries out of this essay is a small and searching one, and it will not go away: when did the Word last tell me I was wrong, and did I let it?
