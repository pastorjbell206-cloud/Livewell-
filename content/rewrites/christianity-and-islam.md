Most conversations about Christianity and Islam run on one of two slogans. The first says the two faiths are one religion in different clothes, divided mostly by language and history. The second says they have nothing in common, that the God of the mosque is a stranger or an idol and the story between the two peoples is war from beginning to end. The slogans sound like opposites, but they do the same job. Each excuses the speaker from finding out what the other person actually believes.

I have said both, at different times in my life, for the same reason: I didn't want to look closely. When I was an atheist the first was especially convenient. If every religion was the same hope in different robes, I never had to weigh any one of them as a claim about what is real. The second was just as lazy. It spared me the work of meeting a Muslim on his own terms.

The real difference between Christianity and Islam is not whether God is one, or great, or merciful. Both faiths confess all three, and many Muslims confess them with a seriousness that should embarrass the average Christian. The difference runs through two questions that are easy to blur. The first is who God is. The second is whether he has shown himself, finally and in person, in Jesus of Nazareth. Most of the heat in this conversation comes from collapsing those two questions into one.

## Islam as Muslims Would State It

Begin where a Muslim begins, with the confession every convert speaks aloud: there is no god but God, and Muhammad is the messenger of God. The first half of that sentence holds Islam in miniature. Its theological name is tawhid, the oneness of God, and it means more than a count of one. God is one in his being, one in his attributes, and alone worthy of worship, without partner, rival, equal or offspring. Surah 112 says in four brief lines that God is one, that all things depend on him, that he neither begets nor is begotten, and that nothing is comparable to him. The gravest sin in Islam is shirk, setting any created thing beside the Creator.

God sent prophets to every people, and the Qur'an names many whom Jews and Christians know, from Adam and Abraham to Moses, David and Jesus son of Mary, each bringing the same summons to worship the one God. Muhammad is the last, the seal of the prophets (Qur'an 33:40), and the Qur'an confirms what was true in the earlier scriptures and corrects what Muslims believe was lost or altered in them.

Calling the Qur'an the Muslim Bible undersells it. Muslims receive it as the speech of God himself, given to Muhammad through the angel Gabriel between about 610 and his death in 632, in Arabic, word for word; qur'an means recitation. Its status was fought over. Beginning in 833 the caliph al-Ma'mun tried to impose the view that the Qur'an was created, and scholars who refused were punished, most famously Ahmad ibn Hanbal, who endured prison and the lash rather than agree. The inquisition collapsed within two decades, and Ibn Hanbal's position became the settled conviction of Sunni Islam: the Qur'an is the uncreated speech of God, eternal with him. And because that speech was given in Arabic, Muslims commonly treat a translation as an interpretation of its meaning rather than as the Qur'an itself.

Out of that word comes a life with five pillars: the confession of faith; ritual prayer five times a day facing Mecca, with washing beforehand and prostration in its course; zakat, a fixed portion of wealth owed each year to the poor as a debt to God; the fast from dawn to sunset through Ramadan; and the pilgrimage to Mecca, once in a lifetime for all who are able. Over all of it stands the phrase that opens every surah but one: in the name of God, the Merciful, the Compassionate.

Now let the Muslim state his objection in a form he would sign. If God is truly one, the Trinity sounds like three gods defended with a formula. If God is above all created things, calling anyone his son drags the Most High into the begetting that belongs to creatures. And if God is victorious, a messiah stripped, mocked and executed on a Roman cross is an insult to his glory. God would not abandon a great prophet to that.

The skeptic usually assumes that objection comes from ignorance. It comes from reverence. Every clause guards God's honor against our habit of shrinking him, and a Christian who cannot feel that has not earned the right to disagree. But disagree we must, since respect that won't name a real disagreement has decided the other person's convictions aren't worth arguing with.

## God, the Word, and Jesus Son of Mary

Surah 4:171 tells the People of the Book not to exceed the bounds of their religion, calls the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, only a messenger of God, and then, strikingly, God's word conveyed to Mary and a spirit from him. It forbids Christians to say "three" and declares God far above having a son. Surah 5:73 says those who call God the third of three have disbelieved, and 5:116 pictures Jesus denying that he ever told people to take him and his mother as gods beside God.

Christians have long noticed that a divine family of God, Mary and Jesus is not the Trinity any creed has confessed; neither Nicaea in 325 nor Chalcedon in 451 put Mary in the Godhead. Muslim scholars reply, fairly, that the refusal covers any partner set beside God, and that a Son who is fully God is still, from inside tawhid, a second beside the One. The disagreement survives the clearing away of misunderstandings.

What Christians confess is that God's oneness is the unity of a life rather than the solitude of a point. The one God exists eternally as Father, Son and Holy Spirit, three persons in one undivided being. Christians did not reach this by speculation. Jewish monotheists who prayed daily that the LORD is one found themselves worshiping a man they had eaten with, and they needed language that betrayed neither the Shema nor the Lord Jesus. "Anyone who does not love does not know God, because God is love" (1 John 4:8, ESV). A God who was solitary before creation would have begun to love only once there was a creature to receive it. The Trinity says love belongs to what God has always been.

Islam has its own question of the Word, and John of Damascus pressed it in the eighth century. If Jesus is God's word and a spirit from him, then either God's Word is eternal with God or there was a time when God had no Word. Sunni theology would later hold that God's speech is uncreated and eternal, something of God eternal with God and yet no second god, which is nearer to the Christian instinct than either side usually admits. The difference is where that eternal Word comes to rest. Seyyed Hossein Nasr, a Muslim philosopher writing for Western readers in *Ideals and Realities of Islam* (1966), drew the parallel plainly: the Word of God in Islam is the Qur'an, and in Christianity it is Christ. The Canadian scholar of religion Wilfred Cantwell Smith is often credited with a similar observation. In Islam the Word became a book. In Christianity he became a man.

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14, ESV). John's verb for that dwelling is the verb of the tabernacle, God pitching his tent among his people, and it explains why the Bible looks so unlike the Qur'an. The Gospels are four accounts by human writers, and Luke says plainly that he investigated before he wrote (Luke 1:1-4); Scripture is the inspired witness to a Word who is a person. Lamin Sanneh, a Gambian scholar raised Muslim who became a Christian and taught at Yale, traced one consequence in *Translating the Message* (1989). Because the Arabic Qur'an is God's own speech, Muslim worship stays in Arabic wherever it goes. Christianity has had no sacred language since its Gospels carried Jesus's words out of Aramaic into Greek, and it has translated its Scriptures from the start into the languages of those who received them. A Word who is a person can be heard in any tongue.

Then there is Isa. In surah 3:45 the angels bring Mary good news of a word from God whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, honored in this world and the next and among those brought near. The Qur'an affirms his virgin birth and his miracles, Muslim tradition awaits his return, and Christians who speak as if Islam despised Jesus are not telling the truth. But the honor has a ceiling: in Islam Jesus is a servant and prophet of God, nothing more. In the Gospels he forgives sins committed against God and receives worship, and Paul, writing within about thirty years of the crucifixion, says of him, "who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" (Philippians 2:6, ESV). The Qur'an honors Jesus as the one who pointed most faithfully to God. The New Testament worships him as God come near, and no amount of goodwill can find a shelf between those two.

## The Cross, and Whether Grace Can Be Sure

The sharpest disagreement concerns an event. Surah 4:157 answers those who said they had killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary: they did not kill him or crucify him, the verse says, but it was made to appear so to them, and the next verse says God raised him to himself. The mainstream reading among Muslim commentators, gathered by al-Tabari in the early tenth century, is that someone else was made to resemble Jesus and died in his place, while Jesus was taken bodily into heaven, from which he will return. The commentators disagreed about who the substitute was, and the verse does not say. This substitution reading is what most Muslims believe today.

It was never unanimous. Todd Lawson's *The Crucifixion and the Qur'an* (2009) argues that the substitution story is the commentators' interpretation rather than something the verse itself spells out, and shows that some medieval Muslim thinkers, notably among the Ismailis, accepted that Jesus died on the cross while denying that his enemies had truly killed him. The Lebanese-born Muslim scholar Mahmoud Ayoub, in a 1980 article in *The Muslim World* on the death of Jesus in the commentary tradition, read the verse as denying not the death but the human boast of having defeated God's messenger. Such readers point to 3:55, where God tells Jesus he will take him and raise him to himself with a verb that often refers to death, and to 19:33, where Jesus speaks of the day he will die. These are minority views, not what most Muslims believe, but they are Muslim readings and deserve to be known.

The historian has a simpler task. The crucifixion of Jesus under Pontius Pilate is among the best attested facts of ancient history. Tacitus, no friend of Christians, recorded in his *Annals* (15.44), written around 116, that Christus suffered the extreme penalty under Pilate in the reign of Tiberius, and Paul's letters, written within about twenty-five years of the event, rest everything on it. "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3, ESV). A Muslim can answer, consistently, that revelation may correct what men thought they saw. The skeptic will notice that only one of these claims can be checked, and that the evidence runs one way.

For Islam the cross would be an indignity God could not permit. For Christianity it is the center of the story. "But we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). Paul preached it knowing it offended every religious instinct of his world. The two faiths do not interpret one event differently. One denies that it happened, and the other says that without it nothing else it teaches can stand.

That leads to grace. Christians who describe Islam as a crude religion of earning have not listened to it. Surah 39:53 tells those who have wronged their own souls not to despair of God's mercy, for God forgives all sins. A saying of the Prophet preserved in the collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim teaches, in paraphrase, that no one enters paradise by his deeds, and when his companions asked whether that included him, he answered that it did, unless God covered him with his mercy. Islamic piety at its best holds fear and hope together and counts presuming on either as a danger. From there comes the Muslim objection to the atonement. Surah 6:164, echoed elsewhere, says no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another. If God wishes to forgive, he forgives, and he needs no death to do it. Punishing another so the guilty go free looks unnecessary and unjust, and the objection lands hardest on preaching that makes the cross sound like an angry Father venting on an innocent third party.

The Christian answer depends on the Trinity, which is why the disputes cannot be separated. The one who bears the burden is God the Son, one with the Father, so that no stranger is punished; God takes the cost of forgiveness into his own life. Anselm of Canterbury asked in *Cur Deus Homo* (1098) why God became man, and his answer, whatever its limits, saw the point: the debt was humanity's to pay, and only God could pay it. Forgiveness that costs the forgiver nothing is one kind of mercy. The cross shows another, a mercy that bears the wound it pardons.

Out of that comes the most practical difference, assurance. In Islam the final weighing is still ahead, and the believer's hope rests on God's mercy in it. In Christianity the verdict has been spoken in advance. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV). "I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God that you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13, ESV). We should be honest about what we have done with that gift. Dietrich Bonhoeffer warned in *Discipleship* (1937) against cheap grace, forgiveness taken without repentance, and much of American Christianity has treated assurance as permission to stop fearing God. A Muslim watching us often sees presumption where the New Testament promised gratitude, and he is not always wrong.

## Fourteen Centuries of Meeting

Damascus fell to the Arab armies in the 630s, and for generations Christians remained a large share of the population under Muslim rule in Syria and Egypt. John of Damascus was born into that world around 675, in a family that had served the Umayyad caliphs' treasury, and he grew up among the Muslim ruling class before he became a monk at Mar Saba near Jerusalem. In *On Heresies*, the catalog within his *Fount of Knowledge* (usually dated to the 740s), chapter 100 treats Islam as the heresy of the Ishmaelites, a deviation from the faith rather than a new religion. The chapter is polemical and sometimes mistaken, but it sees the hinge. He reports that Muslims called Christians associators, for setting a partner beside God, and that Christians replied by calling Muslims mutilators, for cutting God off from his own Word and Spirit. The argument over 4:171 is that old. Sidney Griffith's *The Church in the Shadow of the Mosque* (2008) shows what followed, as Christians under Islam wrote the first Christian theology in Arabic and learned to defend the Trinity in the vocabulary of Muslim debate.

The Crusades, treated at length in another essay on this site, belong here too. Pope Urban II's call at Clermont in 1095 answered an appeal from Byzantium after centuries of conquest in once-Christian lands, and the capture of Jerusalem in 1099 ended in a massacre of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants under the sign of the cross. Both are true. Muslims remember the Crusades far more vividly than most Christians do, and a Christian who wants to be heard must know what his hearer remembers.

In 1219, during the Fifth Crusade, Francis of Assisi crossed the lines at Damietta in Egypt to see the sultan, al-Malik al-Kamil. He was received, he preached, and he came back unharmed. John Tolan's *Saint Francis and the Sultan* (2009) shows how every age since has remade the scene in its own image, the medieval church making Francis a would-be martyr and ours a pioneer of dialogue. Firmer ground is Francis's own Earlier Rule of about 1221. Friars who go among Muslims, its sixteenth chapter says in substance, may live in two ways: without quarrels or disputes, subject to every human creature for God's sake and confessing that they are Christians, and then, when they see it pleases God, proclaiming the word. That posture belongs neither to the crusader nor to the relativist. It belongs to a man who believed the gospel enough to cross enemy lines to speak it, and enough not to need the sword.

Seven centuries later Rome wrote esteem for Muslims into formal teaching. The third section of *Nostra Aetate*, promulgated by the Second Vatican Council on October 28, 1965, says the Church regards Muslims with esteem, since they worship the one God, merciful and almighty, Creator of heaven and earth, who has spoken to humanity. It notes that they revere Jesus as a prophet though not as God, honor his virgin mother, await the judgment, and prize prayer, almsgiving and fasting, and it urges both sides to leave past quarrels behind.

On 13 October 2007, a year after Pope Benedict XVI's lecture at Regensburg had provoked anger across the Muslim world, 138 Muslim scholars and leaders published "A Common Word Between Us and You," which located the common ground of the two faiths in the two great commandments, love of God and love of neighbor. Scholars at Yale's Center for Faith and Culture, Miroslav Volf among them, drafted a Christian response, "Loving God and Neighbor Together," which ran as a full-page advertisement in the *New York Times* that November with roughly three hundred initial signatories, many of them evangelicals. Near its opening it asked forgiveness for Christian sins against Muslims, the Crusades among them. Evangelical critics objected that it said too little about Christ and treated love of God as common ground when the two faiths disagree about who God is. Both documents were serious acts of goodwill. The critics were asking the right question.

## Do Christians and Muslims Worship the Same God?

That question stopped being academic for American evangelicals in December 2015. Larycia Hawkins, a tenured political scientist at Wheaton College, widely reported as the first Black woman tenured there, announced on Facebook that she would wear a hijab through Advent in solidarity with Muslims facing a wave of hostility, and wrote that Christians and Muslims worship the same God, citing Pope Francis. Within days Wheaton placed her on paid administrative leave, saying it needed time to explore the theological implications of her statements; in January 2016 it moved to dismiss her, and in February the two announced that they would part ways. Wheaton's defenders said a confessional school has the right and duty to guard its confession. Hers said she had affirmed that confession without reservation, that Rome had taught the same thing for fifty years, and that the response might have been different for a different professor. Both sides had a point.

The strongest case for yes is Miroslav Volf's *Allah: A Christian Response* (2011); Volf, a Croatian-born theologian at Yale, also wrote in Hawkins's defense. He distinguishes between whether two people refer to the same God and whether they describe him the same way. Two people can argue fiercely about one man, and the argument presupposes that he is the same man. Christians and Muslims, Volf argues, share enough of the core description to be speaking of one God: that there is only one God, that God created everything that is not God, that God is radically different from everything that is not God, that God is good, and that God commands us to love him with our whole being and our neighbors as ourselves. Arabic-speaking Christians called God Allah before Islam existed and still do, and Christians have always said that Jews worship the true God while denying the Trinity. He also draws on Nicholas of Cusa's *De pace fidei*, written after the fall of Constantinople in 1453. None of this, Volf insists, makes the faiths equally true. Muslims aim their worship at the only God there is and are wrong about him in ways Christians must name.

The strongest case for no begins with how the Bible identifies God, by his acts rather than by a list of attributes a philosopher could draw up. Robert Jenson argued in the first volume of his *Systematic Theology* (1997) that for Christians God is the one who raised Jesus from the dead, having first raised Israel out of Egypt. Those who answer no build on that account: a description of God without the Son is a portrait of someone else, and Islam does more than omit the Son; it denies him by name. "Whoever does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him" (John 5:23, ESV). John is starker still. "No one who denies the Son has the Father. Whoever confesses the Son has the Father also" (1 John 2:23, ESV). The critics add that the Jewish analogy limps, since Israel's Scriptures are the ones Jesus fulfilled, while the Qur'an came six centuries after the gospel and contradicts it.

The order of the question matters. The Trinity and the deity of Christ are first-order convictions, bound by the creeds. How to describe another people's worship is a different kind of question, and orthodox Christians answer it differently: Rome says yes, many evangelicals say no, and both confess Nicaea. The New Testament holds together what our debate pulls apart. At the well in Samaria Jesus told a woman whose people claimed the God of Jacob, "You worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews" (John 4:22, ESV). He did not tell her she worshiped a different god, and he did not tell her that her worship was enough. In Athens Paul claimed an altar to an unknown god. "What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you" (Acts 17:23, ESV). Of his own people he wrote, "For I bear them witness that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge" (Romans 10:2, ESV).

Here the two questions come apart. On who God is, the one Creator, the God of Abraham, Christians and Muslims overlap more than either slogan admits. On whether he has shown himself, finally and in person, in Jesus of Nazareth, they part, and nothing softens it. This essay concludes that Muslim worship is turned toward the same God, the one who made the world, and that Christians must still say, with Jesus at the well, that it is worship of him without knowing him as he has made himself known. The strongest objection is John's, that no one who denies the Son has the Father, and it has not been fully answered here. A reader who weighs it more heavily and answers no has not left orthodoxy, and should not be treated as if he had.

## What Christians Can Learn, and What We Cannot Give Away

Two failures stand on either side of this subject, and one commandment judges both. "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16, ESV). The first is the caricature: the claim, repeated in some evangelical tracts, that Allah is a pagan moon god; the habit of treating the world's Muslims as a security threat; the lifting of violent verses from the Qur'an out of context by people who resent skeptics doing the same with Joshua. The second is the relativism that tells a Muslim the differences don't matter. It sounds kinder and is just as untruthful, because it tells him he doesn't mean what he says in surah 4:157 and surah 112. Both erase the actual person. Most of us have committed both, and the second is harder to catch because it feels like a virtue.

Honest disagreement makes honest learning possible, and the first lesson is prayer. A Muslim who stops five times a day to wash and bow to the ground, at work or in an airport, practices what most American Christians admire and few do. We never needed to borrow it. Daniel knelt three times a day at a window open toward Jerusalem after the king's decree had made it a crime (Daniel 6:10). "Now Peter and John were going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1, ESV). The *Didache*, among the earliest Christian writings outside the New Testament, tells believers to pray the Lord's Prayer three times a day, and Benedict ordered his monks' whole life around the hours. We owned this discipline and mislaid it.

The second is reverence. Muslims remove their shoes and put their foreheads to the floor, while much of our worship has grown so casual it is hard to tell whether we think we are in anyone's presence. The third is public faithfulness: the teenager fasting through Ramadan in an American high school, bearing a visible witness many Christians gave up for fear of seeming strange. None of this is flattery. Discipline does not prove truth, many Muslims are as nominal as many Christians, and fixed prayer can harden into a burden. But a Christian who meets a devout Muslim and feels only superiority has not understood what he saw.

What we cannot give away is the reason Christian prayer sounds different. Islam's highest title for a human being is servant of God, and it is a noble one. The New Testament keeps the servant and adds what no servant could claim. "For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15, ESV). Anyone raised without a father hears more than a figure of speech in that cry. The discipline we should learn from our Muslim neighbors answers a welcome already given; it was never meant to earn one.

Five times a day, all over the world, the call to prayer declares that God is greater. Christians agree. The quarrel is over what his greatness looks like. Islam, out of reverence, says he is too great to let his messiah be crucified. Christianity says the cross is where his greatness was finally shown, that the one who was in the form of God took the form of a servant and went all the way down into death so that servants could become sons, and that the grave could not hold him. If that is false, we have built our lives on a terrible mistake. If it is true, then what we owe our Muslim neighbors is a life that looks as if it was bought at that price, and the courage to say, gently and without fear, who paid it. Most of us have given them neither. That is the question this subject finally puts to the Christian, and it will not be answered at a debate table.
