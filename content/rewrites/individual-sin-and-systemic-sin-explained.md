Say the word *systemic* in a church Bible study and the room sorts itself before anyone has weighed an argument. To some it sounds like the gospel finally speaking to the whole of life. To others it sounds like a political program in a theological coat, with a quiet accusation underneath: you are guilty of things you never did, and no confession will ever settle the account. Many Christians sit between, uneasy in both directions. They know the prophets talked about unjust courts and cheated scales. They also know that a doctrine which lets every sinner blame the arrangement has stopped being Christian. So they stay quiet, and the quiet feels like prudence.

The question underneath deserves better than a password. Is sin only personal, or does the Bible know about evil that lives in laws, markets and institutions? The answer this essay argues for is more demanding than either tribe would like. Sin is always personal, and it is never only private. Structures are made by persons, and then they shape persons. Scripture holds both halves of that sentence at once, and the church has spent a long time choosing whichever half costs its own people less.

I have to begin with my own half. For the better part of a decade in ministry I preached sin as if it were always and only a personal transaction: a man lusts, a woman deceives, a person steals, and the remedy is conviction, repentance, grace and change. None of that was false. It was incomplete. I read Amos as if he were addressing private hearts when he was indicting grain merchants, courts and a royal sanctuary, and I called the incomplete version faithful because it never cost me anything to preach.

## What the Bible calls corporate sin

The Old Testament has a small, stubborn image for this kind of wrong. It is the boundary stone. "You shall not move your neighbor's landmark, which the men of old have set" (Deuteronomy 19:14, ESV). A landmark is not moved in a fit of passion. It is moved quietly, by someone with enough standing to make it stick, and once the courts have ratified the new line, the next generation inherits the stolen field as ordinary property. Hosea saw it happening at the top of his society: "The princes of Judah have become like those who move the landmark; upon them I will pour out my wrath like water" (Hosea 5:10, ESV).

Isaiah takes the further step. "Woe to those who decree iniquitous decrees, and the writers who keep writing oppression, to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right, that widows may be their spoil, and that they may make the fatherless their prey!" (Isaiah 10:1–2, ESV). The woe does not fall on a thief in the night. It falls on legislators and scribes. Nobody in that picture had to break the law to rob the widow, because the law was the instrument. And the prophet does two things at once. He names persons; their hands are on the pen, and the woe is theirs. But the harm runs by category, "the needy," "the poor of my people," a class removed from justice by statute. Isaiah does not choose between the scribe and the decree. He indicts the scribe for the decree.

Amos, preaching in the northern kingdom around 760 BC under the long, prosperous reign of Jeroboam II, sees the same thing in the courts and the market. "They hate him who reproves in the gate, and they abhor him who speaks the truth" (Amos 5:10, ESV). The gate was where Israel's disputes were heard and contracts enforced, the institution through which a poor man received justice or lost it. Amos hears the grain merchants impatient for the Sabbath to end, "that we may make the ephah small and the shekel great and deal deceitfully with false balances, that we may buy the poor for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 8:5–6, ESV). Individual men were cheating, but a market where the short measure is standard practice punishes the honest merchant until he cheats or fails. Amos then turns to people who never touched a scale: "Woe to those who are at ease in Zion," who "anoint themselves with the finest oils, but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph!" (Amos 6:1, 6, ESV). Their sin is an absence. The arrangement delivers their oil without showing them the cost.

The story of Achan in Joshua 7 pushes the corporate category as far as it goes. One man takes plunder devoted to God, and the narrator reports it this way: "But the people of Israel broke faith in regard to the devoted things" (Joshua 7:1, ESV). God's verdict is plural: "Israel has sinned" (Joshua 7:11, ESV), and the defeat at Ai falls on soldiers who never saw the silver. Yet the chapter will not let the corporate swallow the personal. The lot narrows from tribe to clan to household to man, and Achan confesses in the first person: "Truly I have sinned against the LORD God of Israel, and this is what I did" (Joshua 7:20, ESV). The ending, in which his household dies with him, is among the hardest pages in Scripture, and the law elsewhere forbids punishing children for their fathers' sins (Deuteronomy 24:16). Achan is not a template for courts. He is a picture of covenant solidarity, in which one member's hidden sin becomes everyone's problem.

The prayers of the exile carry that solidarity somewhere more searching. Daniel, around 539 BC, confesses sins he did not commit: "To us, O LORD, belongs open shame, to our kings, to our princes, and to our fathers, because we have sinned against you" (Daniel 9:8, ESV). Daniel sums up his own prayer: "While I was speaking and praying, confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel" (Daniel 9:20, ESV). Nehemiah, nearly a century later, prays the same way: "confessing the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6, ESV). Neither man had bowed at Manasseh's altars. Both said *we*.

Set those prayers beside Ezekiel 18 and a distinction appears that the whole argument depends on. The exiles had a proverb: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" (Ezekiel 18:2, ESV). It was a corporate account of sin, used as an excuse, and God refuses it: "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (Ezekiel 18:20, ESV). Guilt, in Ezekiel's sense, is not inherited. Daniel and Nehemiah show that belonging is. The same corporate facts can be spoken in two directions. Spoken outward, as the proverb spoke them, they locate sin somewhere other than in me. Spoken inward, as Daniel spoke them, they become confession. Scripture condemns the first use and models the second.

## Rulers and authorities: the debate over Paul's powers

The New Testament carries the corporate category into a vocabulary that has been argued over for seventy years. "For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 6:12, ESV). At the cross God "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Colossians 2:15, ESV). And "none of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (1 Corinthians 2:8, ESV). Who are these rulers and authorities?

For most of Christian history the answer was that they are personal spiritual beings, fallen angels ranged against God. The twentieth century reopened the question. Hendrikus Berkhof, a Dutch theologian writing *Christ and the Powers* (1953) in the years after his country's occupation, argued that Paul's powers include the structures of earthly existence: tradition, custom, the state, the ordering of society. Colossians 1:16 says the rulers and authorities were created through Christ and for him, and Berkhof took that seriously. The powers were made good, as the scaffolding of life together. They fell by making themselves absolute. At the cross they were unmasked, because the best religion and the best law of the ancient world combined to kill the Son of God and so showed what they had become. John Howard Yoder translated Berkhof into English in 1962, and through Yoder the reading entered American theology.

Walter Wink pushed it furthest. In a trilogy that closed with *Engaging the Powers* (1992), he argued that every institution has an outer form and an inner spirit, and that the language of powers names the inner spirit: the corporate culture that forms its employees, the national myth that forms its citizens. He called the whole arrangement the Domination System, and his theology of the powers came down to three claims: they are good, they are fallen, and they must be redeemed. Wink's strength is real. He names what anyone who has worked inside an institution recognizes, the way a system forms people toward ends none of them would own alone.

The traditional reading answers with serious objections. Scholars such as Clinton Arnold, in *Powers of Darkness* (1992), argue that Paul's readers in Ephesus, a city steeped in magic and astral religion, would have heard these words as naming real personal beings, and that Ephesians 6:12 insists on it: the powers are "cosmic," "spiritual," and "in the heavenly places," a strange way to describe a tax code. On this view Wink demythologizes the text, turning the devil into an institutional mood. The traditional reading need not deny that evil works through human arrangements; its claim is that the powers are not reducible to them. The worry is not pedantic: a church that believes all evil is institutional will stop praying against the evil one.

This is a second-order question on which faithful readers differ. The reading that seems to honor the whole text is that the powers are real spiritual agencies whose work becomes visible in human arrangements. Paul can say the rulers of this age crucified Jesus, and the Gospels can name Pilate and Caiaphas, without contradiction, because the powers work through courts, councils and crowds. Berkhof's insight survives, and so does the traditional one: the struggle is "not against flesh and blood," so the persons inside a structure are never the final enemy. The strongest objection this reading has not answered is simple. How exactly does a personal power bind itself to a law or a market, and does "working through structures" say more than that evil is everywhere? That stays open.

## How the church learned to name structures

The idea that sin builds cities is not new. Augustine began *The City of God* in 413, three years after Alaric's Goths sacked Rome, and finished it in 426. The book is an argument about what human communities are made of, and his answer is love. "Accordingly, two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self" (14.28). A people, he says later, is a multitude bound together by agreement about what it loves (19.24). That is a theory of structure: a disordered love, shared long enough, becomes a way of life that teaches the next generation what to love. Augustine called the ruling love of the earthly city *libido dominandi*, the lust to dominate, and he did not flatter empires about it. "Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" (4.4). His pirate tells Alexander that he does with one ship what the emperor does with a fleet. In one heart the lust is called crime; crowned, it is called order.

The modern West narrowed that vision. The revivals of the 1820s and 1830s, Charles Finney's above all, taught that sin is a voluntary choice and conversion a decision the sinner can make tonight. There was real gospel in it, and those revivals fed abolition. But American evangelical grammar became individual: my sin, my decision, my relationship with God.

Walter Rauschenbusch, a German Baptist pastor who had served on the edge of Hell's Kitchen in New York, wrote *A Theology for the Social Gospel* in 1917 to recover what that grammar had lost. He did not deny personal sin or the fall. He argued that sin is passed on not only by descent but by social tradition, and that it takes up residence in what he called super-personal forces: institutions, customs and interests that outlive the people who built them and train each new member in their ways. He named the accumulated whole the kingdom of evil. Amos would have recognized the insight. Its weakness lay in the liberal optimism around it, in which the kingdom of God could shade into social progress, as though reform were redemption.

Reinhold Niebuhr corrected the Social Gospel from inside it. In *Moral Man and Immoral Society* (1932) he argued that individuals can sometimes be moved by conscience beyond their own interests, but groups (nations, classes, races, corporations) almost never are. The collective amplifies self-interest and dilutes conscience, and it runs on its members' virtues. Patriotism, on his account, takes the unselfishness of individuals and converts it into the egoism of the nation. Loyalty and diligence keep the collective wrong running. Niebuhr had no patience for the liberal hope that love would simply scale up to society, and just as little for the conservative hope that converting individuals would make structures take care of themselves.

The Catholic tradition drew the line most carefully. In *Reconciliatio et Paenitentia* (1984), John Paul II affirmed that social sin is real, and that every case of it is the accumulation and concentration of many personal sins, while warning against any use of the idea that blames a vague entity (the situation, the system, society) and so dissolves the personal sin behind it. Three years later, in *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* (1987), he spoke of "structures of sin," insisting that they are "rooted in personal sin" and tied to the concrete acts of individuals who introduce them, strengthen them and make them hard to remove. He traced them to two attitudes, the all-consuming desire for profit and the thirst for power, and he applied the diagnosis to both blocs of the Cold War, criticizing liberal capitalism and Marxist collectivism in the same document. Structures are real, they are sinful, and they never have authors other than persons.

## The case against the category, at full strength

The objections to systemic language are not mere defensiveness, and they deserve their strongest form.

The first is that the category dissolves personal responsibility. When we say a system sinned, we create a sinner who can never be summoned. A structure cannot kneel, weep or be baptized. Tell a man the system is guilty and you have handed him the most comfortable absolution ever devised, a guilt with no face, least of all his own. Sin becomes weather, and no one can repent of weather. Worse, if I am held guilty for a system I did not build and cannot see, my conscience is not awakened but crushed, and the gospel, which forgives sins committed by persons, seems to have nothing to say. This objection guards the link between sin and the will. John Paul II shared it, and Ezekiel 18 is in the canon for exactly this reason.

The second is that the category has become a partisan tool. In American use, *systemic* comes bundled with a policy program and with secular theories whose account of human nature is not Christian, and it tends to assign guilt by group membership rather than by act. Treating every disparity as proof of structural sin makes the claim impossible to test. A Christian who resists the vocabulary may simply be refusing to let the church be conscripted.

The third is that real change comes through converted hearts. Jesus located evil within: "For from within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts" (Mark 7:21, ESV), and the list that follows includes theft and coveting, the raw material of every unjust arrangement. Structures reformed without new hearts get captured by the old sins; revolutions have a way of producing new pharaohs. History offers a strong case. William Wilberforce was converted in 1785, and his conversion drove the long campaign that ended the British slave trade in 1807 and, in the year of his death, slavery itself in 1833.

Each objection is right about something. But the individualist side has a blind spot too, and it has been studied. The sociologists Michael Emerson and Christian Smith, in *Divided by Faith* (2000), studied how white American evangelicals understand racial division, drawing on a national survey and extended interviews. They found a cultural toolkit made of three tools: accountable freewill individualism (people answer for their own choices), relationalism (life turns on personal relationships), and antistructuralism (a reluctance, sometimes an inability, to see social structures as real causes). With those tools, racial inequality looked like the sum of individual choices and broken friendships, and the remedy looked like what the authors called the miracle motif: if everyone became a Christian, the problem would resolve itself. Black evangelicals, holding much the same theology, were much more likely to see structures at work.

The toolkit deserves its own steelman, because it is made of true things. Each of us will give an account of himself to God (Romans 14:12). Reconciliation is personal before it is anything else. Emerson and Smith did not find malice. What they found is in some ways harder to repent of: sincere people with good theology and real affection for their neighbors, using tools that could not see what they were not built to see, and so reproducing a divided society they genuinely opposed. The third objection at its best is Wilberforce. As a habit of mind it becomes the miracle motif, a faith that treats the heart as the only place sin lives and never has to notice what the heart has already built.

## Always personal, never only private

Now the distinction can do its work. Sin is always personal. Every structure had authors, and nothing in Scripture lets a person hide inside an institution. But sin is never only private. What persons do settles into law, custom, market and habit, and those settlements outlast their authors and form the people born into them. The child who inherits the moved landmark did not move it. Once he knows, he is not neutral about it either.

Scripture supplies three different relations a person can have to a structural wrong, and keeping them apart answers most of the fear. There is guilt, which attaches to what I have done, and Ezekiel guards it: no one bears the guilt of another's act. There is complicity, which attaches to what I knowingly benefit from and consent to, and Proverbs refuses its standard excuse in advance: "If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this,' does not he who weighs the heart perceive it?" (Proverbs 24:12, ESV). Complicity is not the innocence of the man who could not have known. It is the arranged innocence of the man who made sure not to. Pilate's basin is its emblem, and the priest and Levite who passed by on the other side of the Jericho road are its parable. And there is responsibility, which attaches to what I am called to repair whether or not I caused it. Daniel was not guilty of his fathers' idolatry, yet he confessed it as *ours*. Nobody is asked to confess guilt for acts he never committed. Everybody is asked to stop washing his hands, and some are asked to rebuild.

The distinction also exposes a common grammar under positions that look opposed. The American right, which often resists systemic language, uses structural analysis freely when the structure belongs to someone else. Conservatives argue that no-fault divorce laws, beginning with California's in 1969, reshaped a generation's expectations of marriage, and that the law on abortion teaches a culture what a child is. That is structural reasoning, used without apology. The refusal tends to come when the structure benefits people like us. The American left, fluent in systems, often lets the language point outward, toward the other party, class or region, so that the systemic account becomes a way to locate evil somewhere other than in my own heart. Its secular forms carry accusation without absolution, confession demanded and never finished. Both moves are the sour-grapes proverb. One says the fault lies in individuals, meaning other individuals. The other says it lies in structures, meaning structures other people benefit from. Shared roots do not mean equal weight in every case, and a given structure's history may put far more on one side than the other. But the instrument that judges both is the same: which direction does our corporate language face?

There is a Baptist example of the prayer said in the right direction. The Southern Baptist Convention was organized in Augusta, Georgia, in 1845, largely to defend the right of slaveholders to serve as missionaries. At its hundred-and-fiftieth anniversary in 1995 it adopted a resolution on racial reconciliation. The messengers acknowledged the role slavery had played in the convention's formation, lamented and repudiated slavery as a historic evil, repented of racism, and apologized to African Americans for condoning and perpetuating individual and systemic racism in their own lifetime. That resolution did what Nehemiah did.

I cannot write any of this from above the basin. I pastor in a comfortable town, and my comfort has a supply chain I have not traced. I have preached against materialism to people who worked in industries built to produce and reward it, and I called that prophetic, when it was the comfortable half of the witness with its most expensive demands removed. I have watched a particular group of people plainly suffering, calculated the cost of saying something, decided I could not afford it, and dressed the calculation in careful language about prudence and political entanglement. The language was not entirely wrong. It was not entirely honest either. The honest name for most of my careful neutrality was self-protection.

The same honesty reaches the church's mercy. A food pantry is obedience to Christ, but a pantry that feeds workers paid too little to eat, and never asks why, quietly absorbs the pressure that might have changed the wage. Isaiah's fast holds both: "to let the oppressed go free" and "to share your bread with the hungry" (Isaiah 58:6–7, ESV).

## Where the persons and the powers were judged

The place Scripture finally answers the question is the cross, where personal guilt and structural evil meet in one event and are judged together. Ask who killed Jesus and the text names persons. Caiaphas reasoned "that it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish" (John 11:50, ESV). Pilate "took water and washed his hands before the crowd, saying, 'I am innocent of this man's blood; see to it yourselves'" (Matthew 27:24, ESV). Ask again and the answer is also a system: Rome's machinery of order and the temple's machinery of religion, working exactly as designed, protecting their stability by spending an innocent life. Caiaphas's sentence is the logic of every structure of sin. Sacrifice the one to preserve the whole. The early church prayed both answers in one breath: "there were gathered together against your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed, both Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel" (Acts 4:27, ESV). Rauschenbusch saw it, arguing that Jesus bore the public sins of organized society, and he named the ones that combined to kill him: religious bigotry, the alliance of graft and political power, the corruption of justice, the spirit and action of the mob, militarism, and class contempt. Paul said it in the language of the powers: the rulers of this age crucified the Lord of glory, and there he disarmed them.

Look closely at that crowd and the claim that complicity cannot be real guilt breaks down. Caiaphas was protecting his nation, the soldiers were following orders, and Pilate had a basin. Each had a defensible story and a narrow role, and together, with no one owning the outcome, they crucified the Son of God. If distributed, respectable guilt were not real guilt, then no one killed Jesus. The church has always known better. The creed says he was crucified under Pontius Pilate, and the hymns say he was crucified for me.

That is why the cross is the only ground on which structural sin can be confessed without denial or despair. The first objection feared that structural guilt would crush the conscience because it could never be forgiven. The cross forgives exactly this. It names the comfortable guilt of the decent without shrinking it, and it bears it. The man who needs to believe he is innocent will defend the system forever, because the system is his alibi. Only the man who knows he is forgiven can afford to see what he is part of. Grace does not tell me I was innocent. It tells me I was guilty and am forgiven, and I know of no other word that makes a complicit man brave.

Repentance, then, has a recognizable shape, and Luke gives it a face. Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector in Jericho, a local operator in Rome's system of tax farming, which let its agents collect more than the state required. When Jesus came to his house he did not abolish the Roman tax system, and he could not have. He stood and said, "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8, ESV). The law asked a thief who confessed to restore what he took and add a fifth (Numbers 5:6–7). Fourfold was the penalty for a stolen sheep that had been sold or slaughtered (Exodus 22:1), and Zacchaeus imposed it on himself. He took his hand out of the machine and repaired what it had taken through him. Jesus answered, "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9, ESV). The heart changed first, and the change reached the ledger the same afternoon. Personal before it was anything else, and never content to stay private.

The landmark is still where it was moved. Someone moved it, someone wrote the law that let it stay moved, and most of us live on fields whose lines we never walked. The prophets named the scribes, Daniel said *we*, and Jesus went to a cross where a governor washed his hands and the rulers of this age did their work. None of that settles what any particular reader owes to any particular structure, and I will not pretend it does. It leaves a prayer, the one Nehemiah prayed with his own name in it. *Even I and my father's house have sinned.* What each of us carries out of this essay is whether we can pray it toward ourselves before we pray it about anyone else.
