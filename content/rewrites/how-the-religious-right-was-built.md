### The story each side tells

Most American Christians under sixty have never known a church that wasn't sorted into political camps. The voter guide in the foyer, the assumption about how the person in the next pew votes, the quiet suspicion of the believer who votes the other way: all of it feels like the weather, the climate faith has always lived in. It isn't. The movement we call the religious right is younger than many of the people who belong to it. It was built on purpose, by people whose names we know, in years we can count. And the older Protestant establishment it rose to oppose had been built the same way a generation before.

Each side tells a tidy story about how this happened. The movement's own story goes like this. In January 1973 the Supreme Court handed down *Roe v. Wade*, and evangelical Christians, roused by the killing of the unborn, reluctantly left their churches for the public square because the stakes were too high for silence. The critics' story is its mirror image. The religious right, on this account, had nothing to do with babies. It began as a defense of segregated Christian schools against the tax collector, and abortion was adopted later as a more respectable banner for a movement whose real origins couldn't be spoken aloud.

I held the first story myself before I read the history, and I have preached to rooms full of people who hold it still, so I'm not correcting strangers. I came to faith out of atheism with no church background to sort things against, and the first Christianity I received was already fused with a party. Nobody lied to me on purpose. They handed me what they had been handed. For years I mistook a political reflex for the obedience of Christ, and I didn't know there was a difference, because the people who discipled me didn't know either.

The honest history is less flattering than the first story and less tidy than the second. What it shows is not that Christians were wrong to care about the public order. They weren't. What it shows is that a people who had real convictions about the unborn and about the freedom of their churches allowed the machinery of partisan politics to decide which convictions counted, how loudly they were held, and who was a faithful Christian. The mainline had done the same thing earlier, on the other side of the aisle.

### After Dayton: retreat, rebuilding and the new evangelicals

In July 1925 a high school teacher named John Scopes was tried in Dayton, Tennessee, for teaching evolution in violation of state law. William Jennings Bryan, three times the Democratic nominee for president and the most famous evangelical layman in the country, helped prosecute. Clarence Darrow defended. Scopes was convicted and fined, and the verdict was later set aside on a technicality, but the courtroom was never the point. H. L. Mencken's dispatches turned the trial into a national verdict on fundamentalism itself, and Bryan died in Dayton five days after it ended. George Marsden's *Fundamentalism and American Culture* (1980) traces how a movement that had been, before the First World War, a respectable coalition of Protestant scholars and preachers defending the supernatural claims of the faith became, in the popular mind after Dayton, a byword for rural backwardness.

The usual account says fundamentalists then withdrew from public life for fifty years. Some of that is true. Much of the movement had absorbed the dispensational premillennialism popularized by the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), which read history as a steady decline toward judgment, and in that frame the reform of society looked like polishing brass on a sinking ship. But Joel Carpenter's *Revive Us Again* (1997) showed that the retreat was a relocation. Between the 1920s and the 1940s fundamentalists built a parallel world of Bible institutes, radio programs, summer conferences, publishing houses and mission agencies. They weren't gone. They were building infrastructure, and it would matter later.

Nor were they ever as apolitical as the story claims. Daniel K. Williams argues in *God's Own Party* (2010) that conservative Protestants were politically engaged throughout the century: in the long campaign for Prohibition, in the anticommunism of the 1950s, and in the fight over the Supreme Court's school prayer decisions of 1962 and 1963. And in the South, white evangelical churches were among the load-bearing institutions of Jim Crow. A church that blesses the existing order is making a political choice even when it never registers a voter. The quiet of those decades was selective.

The first serious attempt to bring conservative Protestants back into the center of American life came from inside the movement. In 1942 a group of ministers formed the National Association of Evangelicals, with Harold John Ockenga as its first president. In 1947 Carl F. H. Henry published *The Uneasy Conscience of Modern Fundamentalism*, a short and searching book arguing that fundamentalism had abandoned the social implications of the gospel and so had forfeited any right to be heard on the great questions of the age. Henry wasn't asking for a party. He was asking for a mind, a Christianity that could think about justice, labor and war with the same seriousness it brought to the virgin birth. *Christianity Today* began in 1956, with Henry as its first editor and Billy Graham as its chief founder.

Graham was the new evangelicalism's public face. His Los Angeles campaign of 1949 made him a national figure, and his New York crusade of 1957, organized with the cooperation of mainline churches, made the break with separatist fundamentalism final. By the mid-1950s he had stopped allowing his audiences to be segregated, and in 1957 he invited Martin Luther King Jr. to pray at the New York crusade. Those were real acts of courage. Graham also did something whose consequences he couldn't see. He became the friend of presidents, praying with Eisenhower, advising Johnson, and growing close enough to Richard Nixon that the release of the Watergate tapes left him shaken and ashamed. Late in life he said publicly that he regretted having come so near to partisan politics.

The Eisenhower years matter here for another reason. Kevin Kruse's *One Nation Under God* (2015) shows how the 1950s fused a generic, anticommunist public piety with national identity: "under God" was added to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954, and "In God We Trust" became the national motto in 1956. Kruse argues that much of this was promoted by business leaders who saw religion as an ally against the New Deal. By 1960 a great many American Christians of every kind had learned to hear the nation's voice and the church's voice as one sound. That habit would be put to work.

### The mainline got there first

The religious right did not invent the political church. It inherited the idea from the people it defined itself against.

At the start of the twentieth century the great Protestant denominations, Methodist, Presbyterian, Episcopal and Congregational among them, formed what historians call the Protestant establishment. They were the churches of presidents, university founders and newspaper editors, and they assumed, not unreasonably, that the moral tone of the nation was their responsibility. Out of that assumption came the Social Gospel, whose best theologian, Walter Rauschenbusch, argued in *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) that the kingdom of God made claims on wages, housing and working hours and not only on private souls. In 1908 the new Federal Council of Churches adopted a Social Creed calling for the abolition of child labor, a reduction of working hours, a day of rest in seven and protection for workers. Many of those demands are now law, and Christians should be glad of it.

The mainline's finest public hour came a half century later. When Martin Luther King Jr. called clergy to Selma in March 1965, hundreds of ministers, priests and rabbis came, and James Reeb, a Unitarian minister from Boston, was beaten on a Selma street and died of his injuries. On race, the mainline's leadership was on the side of justice while much of white evangelicalism was not, and that difference is part of the record, not a footnote to be balanced away.

But the same decades formed a habit. Through the National Council of Churches (1950) and denominational social action offices, the mainline increasingly measured its public faithfulness by the positions it took on legislation, and those positions increasingly tracked one political coalition: civil rights, then opposition to the Vietnam War, then the liberalization of abortion law. In 1967, six years before *Roe*, Protestant and Jewish clergy in New York formed the Clergy Consultation Service on Abortion to refer women to physicians willing to perform abortions. By the early 1970s it was difficult to find an issue on which a mainline denominational office and the liberal wing of the Democratic Party disagreed.

Dean Kelley, himself an official of the National Council of Churches, published *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing* in 1972. He contended that churches grow when they make serious demands and offer a clear account of meaning, and that the mainline, in trading its distinctive demands for broad social agreement, had become less necessary to its own members. The mainline's long decline had many causes, including lower birth rates and the drift of its children into no religion at all. But Kelley named something that the next movement should have heard: a church whose public witness can be predicted from a party platform has made itself redundant.

Robert Wuthnow's *The Restructuring of American Religion* (1988) supplied the sociology. After the Second World War, he argued, the deepest divisions in American religion stopped running between denominations and began running through them, dividing religious liberals from religious conservatives inside nearly every tradition. A Methodist liberal came to have more in common with an Episcopal liberal than with a Methodist conservative. The two camps organized through special-purpose groups built around causes, and those groups lined up, over time, with the two parties. The religious right was the conservative half of a restructuring the liberal half had already begun.

This is the parallel case, and it has to be told with care, because the evidence doesn't support equal blame. The causes were not morally interchangeable, the scale of the two movements' political power was not the same, and on civil rights one side was simply right. But the grammar was shared. In both cases the church's public faithfulness came to be expressed chiefly in the currency of policy, and in both cases the policies arrived as a package assembled by a coalition, not by the church's own reading of Scripture. The mainline showed where that road ends. The religious right took it anyway.

### Two convictions and a tax case

Before the verdict, the conviction deserves to be stated in the form its own defenders would sign, because much of it is true.

The first conviction concerned the unborn. Christians did not invent their concern for the child in the womb in 1973. The Didache, a church manual from the late first or early second century, forbids killing a child by abortion in the same breath as it forbids murder, and the early church was known in the Roman world for taking in infants their parents had left to die. The psalmist's confession, "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13, ESV), is a prayer before it is a proof text, but it's a prayer that makes the child in the womb someone God already knows. Daniel K. Williams shows in *Defenders of the Unborn* (2016) that a serious pro-life movement existed well before *Roe*, led largely by Catholics, and that it made its case in the language of human rights rather than sectarian doctrine. Evangelicals came to the cause later, and many came through the work of Francis Schaeffer and C. Everett Koop, a pediatric surgeon who would become Surgeon General. Their book and film series *Whatever Happened to the Human Race?* (1979) argued that abortion, infanticide and euthanasia were the logical fruit of a culture that had lost the biblical account of the human person, and it changed minds. If the unborn child is a human being, then "Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute" (Proverbs 31:8, ESV) applies to her, and a church that stays silent has failed a neighbor. This essay holds that.

The second conviction concerned the freedom of the church. The Christian school movement grew rapidly in the 1960s and 1970s, and many of its schools were founded for reasons that had nothing to do with race: the school prayer decisions, a secularizing curriculum, the desire to teach children in a Christian frame. Parents who built those schools with their own money experienced the federal government's scrutiny as the state claiming the power to decide which Christian ministries deserved to exist on equal terms. When the apostles were ordered to stop preaching, Peter and John answered, "Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge" (Acts 4:19, ESV). The instinct that the state does not own the church is an old and honorable one.

Now the history that complicates the founding story. In 1970, under pressure from a lawsuit brought by Black parents in Mississippi, the Internal Revenue Service announced that private schools practicing racial discrimination would no longer qualify for tax exemption. Many of the affected schools were segregation academies founded across the South as public schools desegregated. Bob Jones University, a fundamentalist school in Greenville, South Carolina, admitted no Black students until 1971 and afterward prohibited interracial dating and marriage. The IRS revoked its exemption in 1976. In 1978 the Carter administration's IRS proposed tougher rules for private schools founded or expanded during local desegregation, and the protest from Christian school parents was so heavy that Congress blocked the rules the following year. In 1982 the Reagan administration moved to restore Bob Jones's exemption and then reversed itself under public criticism. In May 1983 the Supreme Court ruled against the university, eight to one, in *Bob Jones University v. United States*.

Meanwhile the evangelical record on abortion before 1979 was mixed. In 1971 the Southern Baptist Convention passed a resolution calling for laws that would permit abortion in cases of rape, incest, severe fetal deformity and danger to the mother's emotional, mental or physical health, and it returned to similar language in the middle of the decade. W. A. Criswell of First Baptist Dallas is reported to have welcomed *Roe* at first, though he later changed his view. By 1980 the convention was calling for legal protection of the unborn.

Randall Balmer builds on this record in *Bad Faith* (2021), extending an argument he had made for years. He contends that the religious right was mobilized first by the tax fight over segregated schools, and that abortion became its public cause only in the late 1970s. His most cited witness is Paul Weyrich, the conservative strategist who helped found the Moral Majority, who recalled at a 1990 gathering, in Balmer's account, that he had failed for years to rouse evangelicals over abortion, school prayer or the Equal Rights Amendment, and that what finally moved them was the government's action against Christian schools.

Balmer's critics are serious historians, and their objections deserve the same hearing. Williams argues that evangelical opposition to abortion grew steadily through the 1970s and that pro-life conviction was real, not a cover story. Many who fought for the Christian schools experienced that fight as a question of religious liberty, and their testimony counts too. And Weyrich's recollection, given more than a decade after the fact by a man who blamed Carter for a policy begun under Nixon and Ford, is a slender foundation for a whole thesis. The fairest reading I can give is that the strands were braided rather than sequential. The pro-life conviction was real, and Schaeffer and Koop's tour and the Moral Majority's founding happened in the same year. But the defense of institutions built around racial exclusion was woven into the movement's origins far more deeply than its founding story admits. A movement that remembers itself as born purely to defend the unborn has edited a harder chapter out of its own genealogy, and an honest descendant has to put it back.

### The machine, 1979 to 1989

The pieces came together quickly. In 1976, the year *Newsweek* called the year of the evangelical, a Southern Baptist Sunday school teacher named Jimmy Carter won the presidency speaking openly of being born again. Four years later many of the same voters turned against him, disappointed by his support for the Equal Rights Amendment, his administration's handling of the family and the schools, and the IRS proposal. In June 1979 Jerry Falwell, pastor of Thomas Road Baptist Church in Lynchburg, Virginia, founded the Moral Majority with Weyrich, Howard Phillips and Richard Viguerie, and Weyrich supplied the name. Christian Voice and the Religious Roundtable organized alongside it. In August 1980 Ronald Reagan told a Religious Roundtable gathering in Dallas, in substance, that since they could not endorse him he would endorse them. Reagan had been divorced, and as governor of California he had signed the 1967 law that liberalized abortion in that state. White evangelicals chose him over the born-again Baptist anyway.

That choice is often told as hypocrisy. It is better understood as the moment a religious movement began reasoning like a coalition. Coalitions don't ask who shares their faith. They ask who will deliver. The Moral Majority closed in 1989, and in the same year Pat Robertson, fresh from his failed 1988 presidential campaign, founded the Christian Coalition, with Ralph Reed as its executive director. Reed's strategy was patient and effective: activists on precinct committees, county parties and school boards, and voter guides distributed through churches. Frances FitzGerald's *The Evangelicals* (2017) tells the whole arc as a long quarrel within white Protestantism over how to relate to the culture, and she shows how a political movement led by men like Falwell, a fundamentalist Baptist, and Robertson, a charismatic broadcaster, came to stand in the public mind for evangelicalism as such.

Here is the problem, stated as precisely as I can. It was not that Christians entered public life. It was that political power began setting the terms of faithfulness. A believer may have excellent Christian reasons to oppose abortion and to defend the freedom of Christian schools. But a coalition bundles its commitments, and so the believer's convictions about the unborn arrived packaged with positions on tax rates, gun law, foreign policy and much else that Scripture addresses indirectly or not at all. Over a generation the package became the test. Which issues counted, how loudly they were held, which sins were named from the pulpit and which were passed over, who was suspected of compromise: more and more of that was decided by what the coalition needed. Mark Noll opened *The Scandal of the Evangelical Mind* (1994) with a sentence that stung because it was fair: "The scandal of the evangelical mind is that there is not much of an evangelical mind." A faith organized to win stops doing the slow work of understanding, because understanding complicates the fight.

The clearest measure came with character. In 1998, during the Clinton scandal, the Southern Baptist Convention passed a resolution insisting that the moral character of public officials matters. In 2011 the Public Religion Research Institute found that about three in ten white evangelical Protestants agreed that an elected official who commits an immoral act in private can still behave ethically in public; in October 2016 the figure was about seven in ten. Convictions about character did not change because Scripture changed. They changed because the candidate did.

The same instrument has to be turned on the left, and on those of us who would rather it weren't. The evangelical left has its own founding document, the Chicago Declaration of Evangelical Social Concern (1973), and its own temptation, which is to discover that its prophetic voice grows quiet when its party governs. James Davison Hunter argues in *To Change the World* (2010) that the Christian right, the Christian left and even many of their critics share a single assumption: that the public life of the church is chiefly a matter of politics, and that faithfulness means gaining or resisting the power of the state. The scale is not equal and the consequences have not been equal. The grammar is.

The movement achieved its signature goal in June 2022, when *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* overturned *Roe*. Whatever one thinks of the decision, the victory poses a question the coalition cannot answer for the church: whether fifty years of political mobilization formed a people who will care for mothers and children now that the law no longer carries the argument for them. That question was always the church's, not the party's.

### The king's sanctuary

Scripture saw this long before Lynchburg. When the kingdom of Israel split after Solomon, Jeroboam, the first king of the north, feared that if his people kept going up to the temple in Jerusalem their hearts would turn back to the southern king. So he set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan and gave his people a worship of their own (1 Kings 12:26 to 29). Bethel was built to serve a political need. Nearly two centuries later, when the herdsman Amos came north and preached judgment on the house of the second Jeroboam, the priest of Bethel, Amaziah, reported him to the king and sent him home: "never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom" (Amos 7:13, ESV). Amaziah didn't need to believe anything false about God. He only needed to accept that the sanctuary belonged to the king, and that a word which endangered the kingdom's interests could not be spoken there. Amos's answer was to deny that he had any standing the court could grant or take away: "I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son, but I was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs" (Amos 7:14, ESV).

Every political church, right or left, is tempted to become a king's sanctuary. It rarely happens by a single decision. It happens when a pastor learns which sermons cost members and which don't, when a denominational office learns which statements keep its allies close, when a congregation learns to hear a challenge to its party as an attack on its faith. None of it requires anyone to deny a creed. It only requires the quiet transfer of the sanctuary's ownership.

The temptation of Jesus puts the same offer in its plainest form. The devil "showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. And he said to him, 'All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me'" (Matthew 4:8 to 9, ESV). The kingdoms were real, and the price was a single bow. Jesus refused, and he went on refusing all the way to Pilate's hall, where he said, "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36, ESV). He did not mean that his kingdom had nothing to say to Rome. He meant that it did not come by Rome's means. Then the most sobering sentence in the passion narrative was spoken, not by the Roman governor but by the religious leaders of Israel, men who had made their peace with power in order to protect their people and their temple: "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15, ESV). They were not pagans. They were the guardians of the sanctuary, and on the day that mattered most, they confessed the wrong king.

I read that verse and don't get to stand outside it. Anyone who has preached in an election year knows what it's like to measure a sentence by how it will land with the people who share their politics, and how easy it is to call that prudence. The history in this essay is not the story of other, worse Christians. It is the story of how people very like me, with real convictions about the unborn and the freedom of the church, and in the mainline with real convictions about justice for the poor and the oppressed, handed the ordering of those convictions to a coalition and called it faithfulness.

The way back is older than both sides. Augustine wrote *The City of God* after Rome was sacked in 410, to Christians who feared that the fall of the empire meant the failure of the faith. He argued that two loves have made two cities, the love of self and the love of God, and that the two are mixed together in every earthly order until the end. That means no party is the city of God, and no party is merely the city of man. Christians can serve the earthly city, and should, because "in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV). But they cannot hand it the keys to the sanctuary. One of the earliest Christian confessions, "Jesus is Lord" (1 Corinthians 12:3), was a political sentence in a world where Caesar claimed the title, and it was political precisely because it refused to belong to any faction's program.

Repentance here is ordinary and concrete. It looks like a church whose care for the unborn shows in its nurseries, its adoptive homes and its help for poor mothers as plainly as it ever did at a rally. It looks like Christian schools telling the truth about how some of them began, and mainline offices asking whether their statements ever tell their own members anything unwelcome. It looks like pastors who can preach Amos in an election year and trouble both halves of the room, and a table where people who vote against each other kneel side by side, because the same Lord died for both and will judge both.

The religious right was built, and so was the political mainline before it. That is the hopeful part of the history, because whatever was built by people in years we can count can be examined, confessed and rebuilt by the grace of God. The harder part is that the rebuilding can't begin with the other side's sanctuary. It begins with ours, and with the question Amaziah never asked: whose house is this? The chief priests answered it on the worst day in history. Christ rose on the third day to answer it again. Every congregation in America now has to decide which answer it will give, knowing that it will give one either way.
