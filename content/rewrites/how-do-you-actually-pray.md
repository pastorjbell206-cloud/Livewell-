There is a particular silence that falls in a hospital room when someone finally says, "I think we should pray," and nobody knows who goes first. I have stood in that silence more times than I can count. A family that can talk fluently about the surgery, the insurance and the drive home goes quiet the moment the conversation turns toward God. They want to pray. What they discover, in the hour it matters most, is that they don't know how the words are supposed to leave the body.

They're in good company. Luke tells us that Jesus "was praying in a certain place, and when he finished, one of his disciples said to him, 'Lord, teach us to pray, as John taught his disciples'" (Luke 11:1, ESV). The disciples had watched him heal the sick and silence the scribes, and none of that sent them looking for lessons. What undid them was watching him pray. Teachers of the day, John the Baptist among them, gave their followers a characteristic prayer that marked out whose disciples they were. These men wanted a way of speaking to God that belonged to Jesus, so that it could belong to them.

Most people who ask how to pray aren't short on sincerity. They're short on words, and they've been told the words are supposed to come from inside them, so the silence they find there feels like a verdict. This essay argues something older and, I think, kinder. Prayer is not a performance you give God, and it is not a feeling you produce in yourself. It is answering a Father who spoke first. And because he spoke first, the church has never asked anyone to pray from an empty tank. It hands us his words: the prayer Jesus taught, the Psalms Jesus prayed, and the set prayers that have carried believers through centuries of nights when nothing else would come.

## Praying to the wrong audience

Jesus's teaching on prayer in Matthew begins not with a method but with a warning about audiences. The Lord's Prayer sits at the center of a passage on giving, praying and fasting (Matthew 6:1–18), and each practice is set against a kind of theater. The hypocrites "love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners, that they may be seen by others" (Matthew 6:5). The Greek *hypokritēs* meant a stage actor. The problem isn't that these men pray in public. It's that their prayer has an audience other than God, and the audience has already paid them. Then Jesus names a failure from the other direction: "And when you pray, do not heap up empty phrases as the Gentiles do, for they think that they will be heard for their many words. Do not be like them, for your Father knows what you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:7–8). The first failure performs for people. The second performs for God, as though he were a reluctant power who could be worn down by volume or pried open with the right formula.

Both are alive in us, and I should own the first before I point at anyone. Anyone who prays in public for a living, and I do, knows the pull to pray toward the room. The second lives on in what Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton found when they studied American teenagers in *Soul Searching* (2005). The working faith they named moralistic therapeutic deism treats God as something between a divine butler and a cosmic therapist, and the teenagers, they found, had learned it from the adults around them. In that religion prayer is a requisition slip.

There is a third audience, the one most of us actually pray to: ourselves. We measure prayer by what it produces inside us, and that habit has a history. For most of the church's life prayer was a practice kept at appointed hours whether the heart was warm or cold. Then Friedrich Schleiermacher's *On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers* (1799) defended faith to the Romantics of Berlin by locating its essence not in doctrine or duty but in feeling and intuition. He meant to rescue religion from its critics, and he also moved its center into the self. By the time Robert Bellah and his colleagues described the reigning American ethos in *Habits of the Heart* (1985) as expressive individualism, the spontaneous had come to mean the sincere and the scripted had come to mean the dead.

The objection to all this deserves its weight. Scripture is severe about words without the heart; God complains through Isaiah that "this people draw near with their mouth and honor me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me" (Isaiah 29:13). Better, then, to wait until you mean it. I understand that view from the inside. I didn't pray for years because I didn't believe anyone was listening, and that at least was honest. What surprised me after I came to faith was a second kind of not-praying among people who believed completely. They were waiting to feel like it, and while they waited their prayer lives died of natural causes.

The view is right that God wants the heart and wrong about how the heart gets there, and it confuses two things Jesus keeps apart. Hypocrisy is pretending, before an audience, to feel what you don't. Coming to God when you feel nothing and telling him so is the opposite of hypocrisy, because nothing is being faked and no one is watching. Jesus's cure for theatrical prayer isn't to wait for better feelings but to change the audience: "But when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you" (Matthew 6:6). Prayer is address, not expression. Expression is judged by how well it captures what is in me. Address is judged by whether it reaches the one I'm speaking to, and that depends on him, not on my eloquence or my temperature. Your Father knows. The knowing came first.

## The prayer Jesus gave, clause by clause

The prayer reaches us twice, and the settings teach different things. In Matthew it stands in the Sermon on the Mount as the alternative to theater and babbling, introduced with "Pray then like this" (Matthew 6:9). There it's a pattern. In Luke it answers the disciples' request with "When you pray, say" (Luke 11:2). There it's a text, and a shorter one: "Father, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come. Give us each day our daily bread, and forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone who is indebted to us. And lead us not into temptation" (Luke 11:2–4). The church has never had to choose between them. It prays the words and learns the pattern.

The Didache, a church manual most scholars date to the late first or early second century, tells Christians not to pray like the hypocrites, writes the prayer out in its Matthean form, and adds a plain instruction: pray this way three times a day (Didache 8). Around 200, Tertullian wrote the earliest surviving treatise on it, *On Prayer*, and described it as a summary of the whole gospel, as though Jesus had folded nearly everything he taught into a few lines.

*Our Father in heaven.* Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, writing *On the Lord's Prayer* around 252 for a church just through the Decian persecution, stopped at the first word. Jesus doesn't teach us to say *my* Father. Our prayer, Cyprian argued, is public and common, prayed for the whole people because the people are one. You cannot say this prayer and stay an only child. Then *Father*. When God sent Moses to Pharaoh, the message was "Israel is my firstborn son" (Exodus 4:22), a claim of rescue from slavery. Jesus addressed God as *Abba*, a word Mark preserves on his lips in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36), and Paul says the Spirit teaches adopted children to say the Son's own word (Romans 8:15). Joachim Jeremias argued in *The Prayers of Jesus* (1967) that this address had no real parallel in the Jewish prayer of the time; James Barr's 1988 essay "Abba Isn't Daddy" corrected the sentimental version of that claim, since *abba* was the family word a grown son used as readily as a child. It carries the trust of a son who knows his Father's house, and *in heaven* keeps that trust from shrinking God to the size of our own fathers.

*Hallowed be your name.* This isn't a request that God become holy. It echoes a promise to a people in exile: "And I will vindicate the holiness of my great name, which has been profaned among the nations, and which you have profaned among them" (Ezekiel 36:23). God's own people did the profaning. So the petition asks God to keep his promise and then turns back on the one praying it, as Cyprian saw: we ask that the name already holy be kept holy in us. Notice the architecture, too. Three petitions about God come first, his name, his kingdom, his will, and only then three about us. Most of our private praying runs in the opposite order.

*Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.* This asks for God's healing rule to arrive in the world, not for escape from it. It is also the costliest sentence in the prayer, since you cannot ask for God's will to be done while keeping one corner of your life where your own will is supposed to win. In the garden Jesus prays, "My Father, if this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done" (Matthew 26:42), the words of the hillside now prayed with his face on the ground.

*Give us this day our daily bread.* After the kingdom, the kitchen table. The Greek word translated *daily* is so rare that Origen, in his own *On Prayer* (c. 233), said he could find it in no other Greek writer and thought the evangelists had coined it. Its likeliest senses are "bread for the coming day" or "the bread we need," and either way the petition refuses hoarding, as Israel learned when manna kept till morning "bred worms and stank" (Exodus 16:20). To pray it is to confess that the food on your plate is gift, and the *us* means it is never only your plate.

*And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.* Luke has "sins" and then "everyone who is indebted to us," and many scholars note that in Aramaic one word could carry both senses. This is the only petition Jesus comments on afterward: "For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you, but if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses" (Matthew 6:14–15). God will not let his mercy become something we receive while withholding it from the person down the hall. Pray this line slowly and a particular face will rise in your mind. The prayer puts it there on purpose.

*And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil.* The Greek *peirasmos* means trial or testing as well as temptation, and God "tempts no one" (James 1:13). We are asking to be spared the test we would fail and rescued from evil, or, as the ESV notes, from "the evil one." It is what Jesus urged on Peter in Gethsemane, "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Mark 14:38), hours before Peter denied him. The prayer ends in need, not triumph, and the honesty is a mercy.

Then there's the line most of us add: *For yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory, forever. Amen.* The earliest Greek manuscripts of Matthew, including the fourth-century Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, end at "deliver us from evil," and Luke has no closing praise at all. But the Didache already ends the prayer with a shorter doxology, "for yours is the power and the glory forever," echoing David's prayer in 1 Chronicles 29:11, so the church was closing with praise very early. Fuller forms entered later manuscripts from worship, and English readers came to know them through the Reformation-era Bibles translated from those manuscripts, the King James Version of 1611 among them. The ESV rightly puts it in a footnote: it is the church's fitting answer to what Jesus said rather than part of what Matthew recorded him saying.

## Borrowed words: Luther's barber and the prayer book of the Bible

In 1535 Martin Luther's barber, Peter Beskendorf, asked his old friend how to pray, and Luther answered with a short booklet, *A Simple Way to Pray*. It assumes the reader has no words of his own to start with. Luther tells Peter to make prayer the first business of the morning and the last of the night. He borrows an image from Peter's trade: a barber who lets his mind wander while he chatters will cut his customer's nose or throat, and prayer needs that kind of attention. His method is to take a petition of the Lord's Prayer and pray it through in your own words, or to take a Commandment and turn it into instruction, thanksgiving, confession and petition, a garland of four strands. He admits he still feeds on the Lord's Prayer like a child and never exhausts it. And he adds a line every beginner should hear: if, while you're praying the given words, the Holy Spirit begins to preach in your heart, stop and listen.

Four centuries later Dietrich Bonhoeffer made the same move with the Psalms. In *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* (1940) he argued that praying does not simply mean pouring out one's heart. It means finding the way to God and speaking with him whether the heart is full or empty. A child learns to speak because his father speaks to him, and we learn to speak to God because God has spoken to us; the Psalms are the words he has given us to speak back, and they are the prayer of Jesus Christ, who prays them still with his church. In 1940, to hold up the Hebrew Psalter as the church's prayer book was itself a confession against a regime that despised the Old Testament. It was the last book he published in his lifetime; the next year the authorities forbade him to publish at all.

Eugene Peterson carried the argument into pastoral life in *Answering God* (1989): prayer is not self-expression but answering speech, and the Psalms are tools for learning to answer a God we didn't invent. Tim Keller's *Prayer* (2014), which draws directly on Augustine's letter to Proba and Luther's booklet for his barber, describes prayer as both conversation and encounter, continuing a conversation God has already started through his Word and his grace. You don't generate prayer from nothing. You answer.

That is why the church has always kept set times and set words. "Evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint and moan, and he hears my voice" (Psalm 55:17). After Pentecost, "Peter and John were going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1). The Didache's three times a day grew, in the monasteries, into the eight daily offices of Benedict's Rule (c. 530). Then in 1549 Thomas Cranmer's first Book of Common Prayer compressed the monastic hours into two daily offices, Matins and Evensong, gave them to parish churches in English, and arranged for the whole Psalter to be read through every month. His collect for purity, adapted from an older Latin prayer, still addresses the God "unto whom all hearts be open, all desires known, and from whom no secrets are hid." A prayer like that doesn't ask you to feel anything first. It tells you the truth about the One you're speaking to, and your heart can catch up.

Someone will object that Jesus warned against "vain repetitions," as the old translation had it. But he warned against heaping up phrases to extract an answer, and in the next breath he gave his disciples words to repeat. Set prayer is not a cage. It is a trellis, and on the days your own words don't come, it holds you up.

## When prayer goes dark

Anyone who prays for years and not weeks will reach a season when the warmth goes out of it, and nothing has obviously gone wrong, which is the cruel part. I have knelt in that place with nothing in my chest, the ceiling as blank as it was when I was an atheist, and wanted to get up and call it honesty.

The first thing the church offers here is lament, the largest single category in the Psalter. Walter Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms* (1984), sorted the psalms into orientation, when the world holds together; disorientation, when it falls apart; and new orientation, when God surprises his people on the far side. Most modern worship sings the first and the third. The Psalms linger in the second. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1). Psalm 88 never turns the corner; its last line is "my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18). Complaint addressed to God is not the failure of faith. It is faith refusing to take its grief anywhere else. In Psalm 42 you can watch the psalmist talk back to his own flat soul: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God" (Psalm 42:11). He is preaching to himself against his own feelings.

The second thing the church offers is a map of dryness, and its famous phrase has been worn smooth: "the dark night of the soul" now means any hard stretch of life. For John of the Cross, who wrote the poem around his escape from a monastic prison in Toledo in 1578 and the commentary *The Dark Night* some years later, it meant a work of God, not a mood. God gives beginners real sweetness in prayer, and they come to love the sweetness more than they know, so God withdraws it, the way a loving mother weans a child, to purify a love that was partly about its rewards. John carefully distinguished this night from two things it resembles. It isn't lukewarmness, which leaves you indifferent to God; in the night you are pained because you think you're failing him. And it isn't what he called melancholy, a sickness of body and temperament. That distinction matters now. If what you're carrying looks more like depression than dryness, it deserves a doctor as well as a spiritual guide (this isn't medical advice). If it has you thinking about ending your life, please call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the United States, today. Reaching for help is not a failure of faith.

The strangest witness to the night is recent. When Brian Kolodiejchuk published Mother Teresa's private letters as *Come Be My Light* in 2007, readers learned that from soon after she began her work among the poor of Calcutta in 1948 until her death in 1997, she felt almost no sense of God's presence, with only brief breaks. Some took the letters as proof she had quietly stopped believing. Read whole, they show something harder. With the help of the Jesuit Joseph Neuner she came to understand the darkness as a small share in Christ's own desolation, and she kept rising before dawn to pray to a God she couldn't feel, for decades. Whatever you make of her, her prayer did not depend on its rewards.

C. S. Lewis saw the same truth from the enemy's side. In *The Screwtape Letters* (1942) the senior devil advises teaching the patient to judge each prayer by whether it produced the feeling he was trying to manufacture. Later Screwtape admits what frightens hell most: a human who no longer desires but still intends to do God's will, who looks around at a universe from which every trace of God seems to have vanished, asks why he has been forsaken, and still obeys.

Beneath all of it Paul places the promise that makes a dry prayer a real one. Romans 8 moves through three groanings: the whole creation groans in the pains of childbirth (Romans 8:22), we ourselves groan as we wait for adoption (8:23), and then, "Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). In its setting the verse is about a world in labor and the Spirit of God groaning within our groaning. When the only prayer you can manage is silence with your face in your hands, prayer is still happening, underneath you and on your behalf. "I feel nothing and I came anyway" may be the purest prayer you'll ever pray, because there is nothing in it for you but God.

## Asking, and hearing no

The skeptic has a sharper question than dryness: the asking seems not to work. Families pray for a sick child and the child dies. Researchers have even tried to test it. In 2006 the *American Heart Journal* published the Study of the Therapeutic Effects of Intercessory Prayer, led by Herbert Benson, which followed cardiac bypass patients prayed for by strangers and patients who weren't. It found no benefit from being prayed for, and a somewhat higher rate of complications among patients told for certain that others were praying for them. The skeptic is entitled to ask what Christians think they're doing.

The first honest answer is a concession. If prayer were a mechanism that reliably moved outcomes in the right dose, it would be magic, and Jesus condemned exactly that picture when he warned against thinking we're heard for our many words. That doesn't settle the skeptic's grief. But it means the Christian account of petition was never "ask correctly and receive." It was both bolder and humbler.

The bolder part is that Jesus tells us to ask anyway, and shamelessly. Right after the prayer in Luke comes the man who bangs on his friend's door at midnight and gets bread "because of his impudence" (Luke 11:8). "And I tell you, ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you" (Luke 11:9). Then Jesus ends with a surprise: "If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!" (Luke 11:13). The gift guaranteed to asking is not every item on the list but God himself. Augustine, writing to a widow named Proba around 412, explained why a God who already knows would want us to ask: not to inform him, but because asking stretches our desire and enlarges us to receive what he means to give.

The humbler part is that the Son of God asked and was told no. In Gethsemane he prayed, "Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36). It asks plainly for what he wanted, and it yields. The cup wasn't removed. Yet Hebrews, almost certainly remembering that night, says that "Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence" (Hebrews 5:7). He was heard, and he died. The answer came on the far side of the grave.

Paul learned a smaller version of the lesson. "Three times I pleaded with the Lord about this, that it should leave me. But he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness'" (2 Corinthians 12:8–9). The thorn stayed, and what he received instead was a strength he could only find by staying weak. Keller puts the church's long conclusion this way: God gives us either what we ask or what we would have asked if we knew everything he knows. That doesn't make the hospital room easy, but it keeps the asking honest. We are told to ask for the cup to pass, with loud cries if need be, and then to say the sentence we learned on the hillside.

## The Father who was heard

At the worst hour in the history of the world, the Son of God did not compose a prayer. "And at the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, 'Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?' which means, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Mark 15:34). He prayed a psalm (Psalm 22:1). When his own words would not come, he reached for the words Israel had been given for exactly that darkness and prayed them aloud to the God who seemed to have gone. If the Son of God prayed borrowed words from the bottom of the dark, you are allowed to.

It matters that he died praying, and it matters more that he rose. The one who taught us "Our Father" isn't a dead teacher whose words we recite in his memory; "he always lives to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25). The prayer of Gethsemane was heard. The lament of Golgotha was answered on the third day. And every dry prayer since, every Lord's Prayer mumbled in a hospital room, rises to the Father alongside the prayer of a Son who has been where the one praying is and come out the other side.

None of this makes prayer easy. The prayer Jesus gave still makes us say *our* when there is someone we've shut out, and *your will be done* over the one thing we most want to keep in our own hands, and *as we also have forgiven* while that face rises in the mind. The dark still comes, and sometimes it stays for years, and sometimes the answer to the plainest request we've ever made will be no.

But you were never meant to pray well. You were meant to answer. The disciples began where you're beginning, by admitting they didn't know how, and he didn't grade them. He gave them words, and then he went to the garden and the cross and prayed those words to the end. So tomorrow, before the day gets its hands on you, say them slowly. *Our Father in heaven.* You may feel nothing at all. Say them anyway, to the One who was listening before you opened your mouth, and who knows exactly what it cost his Son to teach you.
