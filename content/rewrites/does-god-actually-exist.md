## Why the debate never ends

Type the question into a search bar and you will find hundreds of hours of it: two speakers at two podiums, a moderator with a timer, and a comment section in which both sides declare victory. The format promises a verdict and never delivers one. That isn't only because debaters are stubborn. "Does God exist?" is several questions bundled into one, and the people answering it are often not using the word *God* to mean the same thing. Before anyone can say whether the arguments work, someone has to say what they are arguments for.

I came to faith from atheism, and nobody brought me there by winning a debate. That cuts two ways. It makes me wary of anyone who sells the arguments as a machine, where you feed in a skeptic, turn the crank, and collect a believer, because people who try that usually confirm the skeptic's suspicion that believers are selling something. But it doesn't make the arguments worthless. What changed for me was the slow discovery that the account of the world I was defending couldn't carry what I actually knew, and the classical arguments turned out to be the most careful statements of what that account had left out. They didn't carry me across. They cleared the ground I eventually crossed.

The question also has a history that shapes how it feels before any argument begins. Charles Taylor asks in *A Secular Age* (2007) how the West moved from a society, around 1500, in which it was nearly impossible not to believe in God, to one, around 2000, in which belief is one option among several. The arguments weren't refuted in the meantime. What changed was the background sense of the plausible, which Taylor calls the immanent frame, a world understood as a closed order of natural causes. Inside it God feels like an extravagant hypothesis, while the world's existence feels like nothing that needs explaining. Both feelings were learned, and believers carry them as surely as skeptics do.

This essay is a map, and several regions on it have essays of their own. Consciousness, reason and a universe balanced for life are taken up in [Can Science Explain Everything?](/writing/what-secular-explanations-still-have-to-explain); the moral argument in [Can You Be Good Without God?](/writing/can-you-be-good-without-god); faith and evidence in [Is Faith Irrational?](/writing/is-faith-irrational); the supposed war with science in [Does Science Disprove God?](/writing/faith-and-science). What belongs here is the older core: the arguments from the bare fact that anything exists and from the very idea of God, the best objections to both, and a question the debates rarely ask, which is what kind of God these arguments, if they worked, would have found.

**The classical arguments are better than their critics say and weaker than their defenders hope. Their real service is less to settle whether God exists than to force the question of which God is meant: a perfect person without a body who made the world, or being itself, on which everything that exists depends for its existence at every moment. Serious Christians still divide over that question. Either way, the difference between proving a conclusion and recognizing a presence is close to the difference between winning a debate and coming to faith.**

## Why is there anything at all?

The most famous arguments begin from the plainest fact available: something exists. They are usually lumped together as "the cosmological argument," as though there were one, and much of the confusion in popular debate comes from that lumping. There are at least three distinct arguments here, and they stand or fall on different premises.

The first belongs to Thomas Aquinas. Opening the *Summa Theologiae*, written between 1265 and 1274, he asks whether God's existence is self-evident, and answers that it is self-evident in itself but not to us (ST I q.2 a.1). We don't see God's essence, so we have to reason toward him from his effects (a.2). Then come the Five Ways (a.3). The first reasons from change: whatever passes from being able to be something to actually being it is brought there by something already actual, as a staff moves only because a hand moves it, and such a series can't run on without a first member that changes others without itself being changed. The second reasons in the same way from efficient causes. The third reasons from possibility and necessity: things that come to be and pass away don't account for their own being, and the argument ends at something necessary of itself. The fourth reasons from the degrees of goodness, truth and nobility we find in things to a maximum that causes them. The fifth reasons from the fact that things without minds act regularly toward ends, as an arrow flies to a target only because an archer aims it. Each Way closes by saying, in one form or another, that this is what we call God.

What most summaries miss is that Aquinas was not arguing for a first moment. He held by faith, on the authority of revelation, that the world began in time, but judged that reason alone could not demonstrate it (ST I q.46 a.2). The series in the Five Ways is not a row of dominoes stretching back through the past. It is a present stack of dependence, the hand moving the staff right now. Edward Feser, restating these arguments in *Five Proofs of the Existence of God* (2017), distinguishes an accidentally ordered series, like a line of ancestors, from an essentially ordered one, where every member acts only while the one above it acts. A father can die and leave his son alive. A staff can't keep moving once the hand lets go. Aquinas's question is what holds the whole stack up at this moment, and it would stand even if the universe had no beginning at all.

The second argument belongs to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. In his *Principles of Nature and Grace* (1714) he asks the question in its sharpest form, why there is something rather than nothing, and adds that nothing would be simpler and easier than something. His *Monadology*, from the same year, rests on the principle of sufficient reason: for anything that is, there is a reason why it is so and not otherwise. Each contingent thing finds its reason in other things, but the whole collection of contingent things, however long, doesn't explain itself, so its reason must lie in a substance that exists necessarily and carries the reason for its existence within itself. Leibniz had answered the obvious reply in 1697, in *On the Ultimate Origination of Things*. Suppose a book of geometry had always existed, each copy made from an earlier copy. You could explain every copy by the one before it and still have explained neither why there is such a book at all nor why it says what it does. An infinite regress of explanations explains each link and leaves the chain hanging in the air.

The third argument is different in kind, because it depends on a beginning. It is called the kalam argument, after the tradition of Islamic theology in which it was sharpened. Al-Ghazali pressed it in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, written around 1095 against Avicenna and the Aristotelian philosophers who held the world eternal, asking, among other paradoxes of the infinite, whether an infinite number of past revolutions of the heavens would be odd or even. William Lane Craig revived the argument in *The Kalam Cosmological Argument* (1979) in the form now repeated everywhere: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause. Craig defends the second premise philosophically, arguing that an actually infinite series of past events can't be completed one event at a time, and scientifically, from Big Bang cosmology. He then argues that the cause must be beyond time and space, enormously powerful, and personal, since a timeless cause producing an effect with a beginning is best understood as an agent who freely chose to act.

Sorting the three matters. The kalam has the most intuitive force and the most exposed premise, since its second step is hostage to the philosophy of the infinite and to physics, where it is still debated whether our universe had an absolute beginning. Aquinas and Leibniz don't need to win that debate. Their arguments work, if they work, even in an eternal universe, because they ask about dependence rather than chronology. Their exposure lies in the principle that the world must have a sufficient reason at all, and that is exactly where the best critics have aimed. The arguments from design and fine-tuning, a related family, are weighed with the naturalist replies at full strength in [the essay on what secular explanations still have to explain](/writing/what-secular-explanations-still-have-to-explain).

## The argument from the idea of God

The strangest argument in the history of the question was written as a prayer. Anselm, then prior of the abbey of Bec in Normandy, composed the *Proslogion* in 1077 and 1078, addressing it to God from its first page. He says he first called it *Faith Seeking Understanding*, and that he does not seek to understand in order to believe but believes in order to understand. The most famous proof of God was worked out by a monk who already believed, on his knees, as an act of thought offered back to the one he was thinking about.

The argument runs like this. God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived. Even the fool who denies God understands the phrase when he hears it, so the thing it names exists at least in his understanding. But a thing that exists only in the understanding could also be thought of as existing in reality, which is greater. So if that than which nothing greater can be conceived existed only in the mind, we could conceive something greater than it, which contradicts what it is. Therefore it exists in reality as well. In the following chapter Anselm pushes further: such a being can't even be conceived not to exist.

The fool comes from the Psalms. "The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1, ESV). Anselm treated him as a logician with a faulty premise, but the psalm has something else in view. The Hebrew fool is a moral figure before he is an intellectual one, a man who lives as though no one will ever call him to account, and the rest of the line describes what such people do rather than what they think. The psalm then refuses to let the fool stay a separate category of person: "They have all turned aside; together they have become corrupt; there is none who does good, not even one" (Psalm 14:3, ESV). Paul quotes the line in Romans 3 to include Jew and Gentile alike, and it includes the believer who recites the creed on Sunday and lives unaccountable by Tuesday. Practical atheism is the kind the church rarely argues against, because so many of us practice it.

Objections came at once. Gaunilo, a monk of Marmoutier, answered in *On Behalf of the Fool* that by the same reasoning one could prove the existence of a lost island more excellent than all other lands. Anselm replied that his reasoning applies to that than which nothing greater can be conceived and to nothing else, and later defenders, Plantinga among them, sharpened the point: an island has no intrinsic maximum, since one could always conceive it with one more palm tree. Aquinas also rejected the argument (ST I q.2 a.1), holding that it shows only that God is thought of as existing, not that he exists outside the mind. The standard objection came from Immanuel Kant in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), who gave the argument its modern name, the ontological argument. Existence, Kant argued, is not a real predicate. It adds nothing to the concept of a thing; it says the concept has an instance. A hundred real coins contain not a coin more, in concept, than a hundred imagined ones. The difference lies in my purse, not in the idea.

Alvin Plantinga revived the argument in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974) in a form built to step around Kant. Define maximal excellence as including omnipotence, omniscience and moral perfection, and maximal greatness as having maximal excellence in every possible world. The argument asks whether maximal greatness is possible, then uses a principle of modal logic, that what is possibly necessary is necessary, to conclude that if such a being exists in any possible world it exists in all of them, ours included. The logic is valid. Everything turns on the first premise, where the argument meets its mirror image: if it is possible that no maximally great being exists, the same logic proves that none does. Plantinga saw this and said so. He claimed that his argument shows the rational acceptability of theism, not its truth, because a reasonable person could accept its key premise without being compelled to.

That modest claim matters more than it looks. The ontological argument has persuaded few people who didn't already believe, but it teaches something the other arguments teach less clearly. If God is what Anselm meant by the word, his existence can't be a lucky accident. The question is not like asking whether there is life on a distant moon, which might turn out either way. On Anselm's terms God exists necessarily or not at all, and a God who merely happened to exist, who might have failed to be, would not be God in the sense he meant. Not every theist accepts those terms, as a later section shows, but anyone who argues about God has to decide whether to.

## The case against, at full strength

The strongest critic of these arguments wrote them into a conversation. David Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* were published in 1779, three years after his death. Cleanthes argues from the design of the world, Demea from reason alone, and Philo, the skeptic, usually has the best lines. Against design, Philo argues that the analogy between the world and a machine is weak, and that even granted it yields at most a finite designer, perhaps several, perhaps a beginner whose first rough attempt this world was. Against the a priori argument, Hume gives Cleanthes the sharpest objection in Part IX: nothing can be demonstrated to exist unless its denial is a contradiction, and whatever we conceive as existing we can also conceive as not existing, so "necessary existence" has no consistent meaning. Explain each of twenty particles of matter, Cleanthes adds, and it is unreasonable to ask for a further cause of the twenty. And if something must be necessary, why not the material universe itself? Yet in Part XII Philo concedes, in a passage scholars still argue about, that the cause of order in the universe probably bears some remote analogy to human intelligence.

The same objections came to life on the radio. On 28 January 1948 the BBC Third Programme broadcast a debate between Bertrand Russell and Frederick Copleston, a Jesuit historian of philosophy, and Copleston chose Leibniz's argument from contingency. Russell answered on two fronts. He denied that "necessary" can sensibly describe a being at all, since necessity, on his view, belongs to statements, not to things. And he charged the argument with a fallacy of composition: every human being has a mother, but it doesn't follow that the human race has a mother. Pressed on whether the universe as a whole needs an explanation, Russell replied, "I should say that the universe is just there, and that's all." Copleston observed that if one refuses even to sit down at the chessboard, one can't be checkmated.

Both men scored. Russell's point about composition is fair against careless versions of the argument, though Leibniz's version doesn't depend on it: its claim is that a collection made entirely of things that could have failed to exist could itself have failed to exist, the way a wall built only of red bricks is red. Russell's "just there" is not a refutation but a stopping place. Every account of reality stops somewhere. The theist stops with a being that could not have failed to be; Russell stopped with a universe that happens to be. Their real disagreement was whether reality may finally be an unexplained fact, and whether the question "why anything?" deserves an answer or only a shrug.

J. L. Mackie gave the case against God its most careful modern form in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982). The title borrows from Hume's remark, at the end of his essay on miracles in 1748, that no reasonable person could believe the Christian religion without a miracle working in him. Mackie weighed theism as a hypothesis and was scrupulously fair to his opponents. He argued that the principle of sufficient reason is not self-evident, that the case against an infinite past fails, that the modal ontological argument gives no reason to prefer its key premise to its mirror image, and that, everything weighed, the balance tips toward atheism. A believer who hasn't read Mackie hasn't yet met the case against belief at its best.

Graham Oppy, an Australian atheist, pressed a more radical conclusion in *Arguing about Gods* (2006). He defines a successful argument as one that ought to persuade every reasonable person who doesn't already accept its conclusion, and after examining the ontological, cosmological and design arguments in detail he concludes that none succeeds. What makes Oppy unusual is that he applies the same standard to his own side. He doesn't claim that the arguments against God, those from evil included, meet it either; his conclusion is that, in his demanding sense, there are no successful arguments on either side of the question.

Two more objections are felt by far more people than have read them. Horrific evil gets the length it deserves in [If God Is Good, Why Is There Suffering?](/writing/if-god-is-good-why-suffering). And J. L. Schellenberg argued in *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason* (1993) that a perfectly loving God would not permit what he calls nonresistant nonbelief, people who are open to God and not resisting him yet still can't find their way to believing he exists. The popular question "Who made God?" is weaker than either, because it misses its target. Each argument above ends at something uncaused because it never began or could not fail to be, and asking for its maker quietly swaps in a different God from the one the arguments were aimed at.

So the long argument has ended, for now, in something like a stalemate at the level of demonstration. Oppy is probably right that no argument on either side compels every reasonable mind. But it is not a stalemate about nothing. Nine centuries of argument have narrowed the disagreement to one point, which is whether the world can be its own explanation, and that point turns out to depend on what one means by God.

## Which God is being argued about

David Bentley Hart argues in *The Experience of God* (2013) that what the loud atheists deny, and what many believers defend, is a demiurge: a very powerful being within reality, an engineer standing alongside the universe as one more thing, only larger. That, Hart argues, is not what the classical traditions of Judaism, Christianity and Islam meant by God, and he is sharpest with believers who defend it. For Aquinas, God is *ipsum esse subsistens*, subsistent being itself (ST I q.4 a.2), the one in whom what he is and that he is are identical (ST I q.3 a.4). God is not the biggest item in the inventory. He is the reason there is an inventory. When Moses asked the name of the God who spoke from the bush, the answer was this: "God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And he said, 'Say this to the people of Israel: I AM has sent me to you'" (Exodus 3:14, ESV). Aquinas took "He Who Is" as God's most proper name (ST I q.13 a.11). Hebrew scholars rightly caution that in its setting the phrase is first a promise of presence to slaves in Egypt, not a treatise on being. Readers in Aquinas's line still find his point in the scene: the bush burns and is not consumed, as though the God present in it doesn't compete with the creature for room.

Many serious Christian philosophers hold another view, which the Dominican philosopher Brian Davies, in *An Introduction to the Philosophy of Religion*, calls theistic personalism. Richard Swinburne, in *The Coherence of Theism* (1977), defines God as a person without a body, present everywhere, the creator and sustainer of the universe, perfectly free, all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good, and he argues there that God's existence, though it depends on nothing, is not logically necessary. Plantinga questioned the classical doctrine that God is simple, without parts or distinctions, in his 1980 lecture *Does God Have a Nature?* Their case at its strongest is that Scripture shows a living God who hears, answers, grieves and relents, that divine simplicity is hard even to state coherently, and that the classical account risks making God remote, a principle rather than a Father. They would add that Hart's demiurge is a caricature of their view, since none of them thinks God is a part of the universe or a product of it.

This is a second-order question, and faithful Christians stand on both sides of it. The ecumenical creeds confess the one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, without settling it in so many words. The later confessions, from the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 to the Westminster Confession in 1646, with its God "without body, parts, or passions," do speak in the classical voice, a point classical theists press and personalists have to reckon with. The arguments themselves pull in different directions. Whatever the Five Ways reach, if they reach anything, is pure actuality, uncaused and necessary, and Feser spends much of *Five Proofs* arguing that the classical attributes follow from it. The kalam, in Craig's hands, ends at a personal agent who freely chose to create, and Swinburne's cumulative case presents a personal God as the simplest explanation of the world. Each view carries an objection it hasn't fully answered. The classical view has to say how an unchanging God truly responds to prayer; its reply, that the change lies in the creature's relation to God, satisfies some careful readers and not others. The personalist view has to say why a God whose existence is not logically necessary exists at all; Swinburne's answer is that God is the simplest place for explanation to stop, and its critics reply that this stops where Russell stopped, only with a different fact at the end. This essay leaves the question where the church has left it, open, and readers who settle it on opposite sides remain brothers and sisters in the faith.

Here the distinction the essay turns on comes into view, and the classical account states it most sharply. A being among beings could be proved as a conclusion, the way an unseen planet is inferred from its effects and added to the list. The ground of being, on the classical view, can't be found that way, because it is not on the list. It can only be recognized, the way you realize that you have been standing on a floor the whole time you were searching the room. Aquinas said as much when he wrote that God's existence is self-evident in itself but not to us. What comes last in the order of our knowing is first in the order of being. The personalist reaches a similar place by another road, since a person is finally known by meeting, not by inference from his footprints. Paul told the philosophers of Athens that the God they worshiped as unknown was not far from any of them, "for 'In him we live and move and have our being'; as even some of your own poets have said, 'For we are indeed his offspring'" (Acts 17:28, ESV). You cannot step outside the one in whom you exist to inspect him from a neutral distance. That is part of why the arguments never quite close. It is also why Hart can say that an atheist who rejects a cosmic engineer has rejected something the classical tradition rejected first.

## How anyone actually comes to believe

Blaise Pascal was one of the finest mathematical minds of his century, and he distrusted the proofs. In a fragment of the *Pensées*, published in 1670 after his death, he observes that the metaphysical proofs of God are so remote from ordinary reasoning that they strike few people, and that those they do persuade are persuaded only while the demonstration is in front of them; an hour later they fear they were mistaken. On the night of 23 November 1654 Pascal had an experience of God that he wrote down and sewed into the lining of his coat, where it was found after his death in 1662. The God he met, he wrote, was the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, not of the philosophers and scholars. He wasn't renouncing reason. He was marking the difference between knowing that there is a first cause and knowing whom you are dealing with. Pascal quoted the prophet, "Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Savior" (Isaiah 45:15, ESV), and in its setting the verse is no complaint about absence. It stands in the chapter that opens with Cyrus, a pagan king God used without the king knowing him. God is hidden in history the way an author is hidden in a story, present on every page.

John Henry Newman gave this experience its most careful account in *An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent* (1870). He distinguished notional assent, which accepts a proposition as an idea, from real assent, which grasps it as a thing and engages the whole person. And he named the faculty by which people reach certainty where no single argument settles the matter: the illative sense, the trained judgment that sees when converging probabilities, none decisive alone, amount to certainty. His example was that we are certain Great Britain is an island, though few of us have sailed around it, on a convergence of evidence too varied to set out as a syllogism. Newman had written years earlier, in the *Tamworth Reading Room* letters of 1841, "Life is not long enough for a religion of inferences," and he reprinted the passage in the *Grammar*. This isn't faith against reason. It is the ordinary way reason works about everything that matters most: whether a friend can be trusted, whether a marriage is sound, whether a crucified man rose.

That is what the arguments are for. They clear the ground. They show that belief in God is not a failure of intelligence, that the question "why anything?" is real, and that the God in question is not a cosmic engineer waiting for a gap in the science. They didn't make me a believer; what they did was make unbelief expensive, which is a different thing. Recognition came another way, and Luke tells the story of it. Two disciples walked to Emmaus beside the risen Jesus, heard him open the Scriptures from Moses and the prophets, and still didn't know him. Then he broke bread at their table: "And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And he vanished from their sight" (Luke 24:31, ESV). The arguments on the road were not wasted. Their hearts burned while he spoke. But what they received at the end was not a conclusion. It was a face they had been walking beside all afternoon.

The Christian claim is stranger than anything the philosophers reached, and it is where the question finally leaves us. Paul says of Christ that "he is before all things, and in him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:17, ESV). That is the language of the one who holds the stack of dependence up at every moment, whichever account of him proves truer. Three verses later the same letter says what that one did: "and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross" (Colossians 1:20, ESV). The being in whom all things hold together entered the world he holds and was killed in it by people who, like the fool of the psalm, preferred to live unaccountable. We are those people, believers included. The question was never only whether God can be proved. It is whether we will recognize, before we are finished, whose ground we have been standing on and whose bread we have been eating, when every breath we have spent arguing the matter was already his gift.
