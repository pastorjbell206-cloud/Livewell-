## Where the objection starts

What kept me out of Christianity, when I was an atheist, was not the resurrection. I closed the book on its first page, because I assumed that believing it meant believing the earth was a few thousand years old, and I knew enough about rocks and starlight to think that settled things. As far as I could tell, the price of admission was the surrender of geology, and I wasn't willing to pay it. The cross never got a hearing, because the first page had already lost the case.

Plenty of people stand at that door now, and some of them were raised inside the house. They were handed one reading of Genesis as though it were the faith itself, and nobody mentioned that Christians have never agreed about the days. When that reading cracks under what they learn later, they assume the faith cracked with it.

The skeptic's objection deserves its full strength. If Genesis 1 makes claims about how and when the world was made, and those claims are false, then every Christian re-reading looks like a retreat: believers read the days as days until geology made that embarrassing, and then discovered the text had been poetry all along. The charge has some truth in it. Science has more than once sent Christians back to a text to ask whether they had read it well, and sometimes they hadn't. Nobody today thinks a fixed earth is an article of faith, though some of the church's best minds once did.

But the charge misses two things. The first is historical: Christians disagreed about the days long before anyone dated a rock. The second is a distinction, and this essay turns on it. There is a difference between what a text teaches and the questions it was never asked. A re-reading prompted by new knowledge is a retreat only if it imposes something foreign on the text. If it recovers what the text was doing all along, it is simply better reading.

The church has also learned to sort its disagreements by weight. Some things Genesis 1 teaches are first-order: that God made everything out of nothing, freely and on purpose, that what he made is good, and that human beings bear his image as nothing else in creation does. The Nicene Creed, in the form settled at Constantinople in 381, confesses the Father as maker of heaven and earth and of all things visible and invisible, and every Christian reading of Genesis kneels at that line. The age of the earth, the length of the days and the means by which God brought living things into being are second-order. Faithful churches differ on them without dividing the faith, the creeds never bound them, and this essay leaves them open. But the readings come second. Most of the shouting about Genesis is done by people who have stopped looking at the chapter, and the chapter is stranger and more carefully made than the shouting suggests.

## How Genesis 1 is built

The chapter opens with a sentence that is also a summary: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1, ESV). Then it names a problem. "The earth was without form and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters" (Genesis 1:2, ESV). The Hebrew pair behind "without form and void," *tohu wabohu*, describes a world not yet ordered and not yet inhabited. Everything that follows answers those two lacks in order.

The first three days deal with formlessness by separating: light from darkness, the waters above from the waters below, the sea from the dry land, which then brings forth plants. The next three deal with emptiness by filling what the first three made: lights for the day and night, fish and birds for the waters and sky, animals and then human beings for the land. Day one answers to day four, day two to day five, day three to day six, and the pairing reaches the smallest detail, since the third and sixth days are the only ones that hold two separate acts of creation each. Medieval theologians saw it; Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae*, sorted the days into a work of distinction and a work of adornment. Henri Blocher, a French evangelical theologian, built much of his reading in *In the Beginning* (1984) on the same two columns. This is not a pattern a reader has to strain to see. It is the chapter's architecture.

The chapter is also counted. Commentators since the Jewish scholar Umberto Cassuto have observed, and Gordon Wenham's *Genesis 1-15* (1987) sets out, that the opening verse has seven Hebrew words and the second has fourteen, and the sevens keep coming. God sees that what he has made is good seven times, and the seventh time it is "very good." The formula "And God said" appears ten times, which is why the Mishnah, in the tractate *Avot*, could speak of the world as created by ten utterances. The verb *bara*, "create," which in its basic form takes no subject in the Old Testament but God, marks three turning points in the week: the heavens and the earth at the beginning, the great sea creatures on the fifth day, and human beings on the sixth, where it appears three times in a single verse. A text built this way is neither careless folklore nor a laboratory report. Laboratory reports don't count their words in sevens.

And the counting leads somewhere. Day six, with the making of humanity, is the height of God's work, but it is not the end of the story. The seventh day is. "So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it God rested from all his work that he had done in creation" (Genesis 2:3, ESV). Every other day closes with evening and morning. The seventh does not. The whole architecture bends toward a day God blesses and sanctifies, which means the chapter was shaped so that a people could live inside it, their weeks copying the week of their Maker. Whatever else Genesis 1 is, it is a text for worship. Its climax is not the appearance of man but the holiness of time.

Then, at Genesis 2:4, the book begins again with the heading, "these are the generations of," that Genesis uses to open each major section. Genesis 1 surveys the cosmos from above and calls God *Elohim*. Genesis 2 comes down to the ground and calls him by his covenant name, the LORD God. "Then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature" (Genesis 2:7, ESV). The order differs too. In chapter one, plants and animals precede humanity, and male and female are made together. In chapter two, the man is formed before any plant of the field has sprung up, the animals are brought to him to name, and the woman is built last from his side.

Readers who hold to ordinary days answer, fairly, that Genesis 2 is a close-up of the sixth day rather than a rival timeline. Meredith Kline, a Reformed Old Testament scholar at Westminster Theological Seminary, pressed 2:5 the other way in his 1958 article "Because It Had Not Rained." The reason given for the missing plants is ordinary: "for the LORD God had not caused it to rain on the land, and there was no man to work the ground" (Genesis 2:5, ESV). Kline argued that this assumes God was working through ordinary providence while he created, plants waiting on rain, which sits poorly with a week in which everything happens in hours. Neither reading can pretend the two chapters were written to be laid over each other as one chronological list.

## The world that first heard it

In 1872 George Smith, a self-taught Assyriologist working through tablets in the British Museum that had come out of the ruins of Nineveh, found a Babylonian flood story. In *The Chaldean Account of Genesis* (1876) he published fragments of a Mesopotamian creation account from the same library, known by its opening words, *Enuma Elish*, "When on high." The Bible's first pages turned out to have neighbors.

Genesis 1 reads differently once you've heard that story. First there are two primeval waters, Apsu and Tiamat, and from their mingling the gods are born. The generations of gods quarrel, and the quarrel turns to war. Tiamat raises an army of monsters, and the young god Marduk, promised kingship over the gods, kills her, splits her carcass in two, and props up one half as the sky to hold back her waters. Then, to relieve the gods of their labor, humanity is made from the blood of Tiamat's slain general, so that the gods can rest while human beings toil.

In 1895 Hermann Gunkel argued in *Schöpfung und Chaos* that Genesis 1 had absorbed this kind of chaos-battle myth, and that *tehom*, "the deep" of Genesis 1:2, echoed the name Tiamat. The linguistic link is still debated, and the case for direct borrowing has not held up well. But Gunkel was right that the two texts share a world: waters above and below with a firm expanse between, watery chaos giving way to order, a deity at rest at the end.

What makes Genesis 1 remarkable is everything it refuses. Its God has no parents and no birth story. There is no war. The deep is simply water, lying passive under the hovering Spirit, and it does whatever it is told. The creatures that other ancient peoples feared as dragons of chaos appear on the fifth day as more creatures: "So God created the great sea creatures and every living creature that moves" (Genesis 1:21, ESV). The word for those sea creatures, *tanninim*, names the monsters of Canaanite myth, and Genesis gives them the verb *bara* and calls them good. The chapter declines even to name the sun and moon, which its neighbors worshiped, and calls them lamps set in the sky to mark days and seasons. For a people surrounded by temples to the sun, it was a declaration of war on every other god, delivered in a voice that never rises.

The sharpest contrast falls on humanity. In the Babylonian story, human beings are made out of a dead god's blood to do the gods' drudgery. In Genesis they are made by a word and given a crown: "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness. And let them have dominion" (Genesis 1:26, ESV). J. Richard Middleton, in *The Liberating Image* (2005), showed how this sounded in its world. In Egypt and Mesopotamia a king could be called the image of a god, the living statue through which the god's rule was made present. Genesis takes that royal title and gives it to every human being, male and female alike.

Even the rest is reversed. In *Enuma Elish* the gods rest because humans labor. In Genesis God rests and then gives Israel a commandment of rest that reaches the lowest household servant: "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (Deuteronomy 5:14, ESV). The Babylonian gods hoarded their Sabbath. Israel's God shares his.

Here the distinction between what a text teaches and what it was never asked earns its keep. Genesis 1 was asked questions by its world, and it answered every one. Is there one maker or many? Is the sea a monster? Are the sun and stars to be served? Is a human being a slave of the gods or a royal image? Who rests, and who works for whom? What nobody in that world was asking was how long the stars had been burning or by what biological mechanism the land brought forth its creatures. To demand that Genesis answer those questions in the idiom of a modern textbook, and then to treat its silence as either a scientific claim or a scientific failure, is to hand an ancient text an exam it never sat for.

## Six ways the church reads the days

Both sides of today's argument like to claim the fathers, and both are partly right. Augustine worked on *The Literal Meaning of Genesis* from about 401 to 415. By "literal" he meant an account of what really happened rather than allegory, and yet he concluded that God made all things at once, taking the six days as an ordered presentation of one creative act to minds that can only take things in sequence. He urged readers, on obscure questions where Scripture allows more than one reading without harm to the faith, not to rush into one position so firmly that they fall with it when better knowledge arrives (1.18.37). A page later (1.19.39) he warned that unbelievers often know a good deal about the earth and the heavens from reason and experience, and that when they hear a Christian talk nonsense about nature in Scripture's name, the real harm isn't the Christian's embarrassment. It is that outsiders conclude the biblical writers taught such things, and so dismiss those books when they speak of the resurrection.

That warning is sixteen centuries old, and it cuts. But honesty requires the rest of the record. Augustine was no believer in an ancient earth; in Book 12 of *The City of God* he reckoned from Scripture that fewer than six thousand years had passed since the first man. Basil of Caesarea, preaching his *Homilies on the Hexaemeron* in the 370s, took the days as ordinary days of twenty-four hours. Martin Luther, lecturing on Genesis from 1535, set Augustine aside for six real days, and the Westminster Confession of 1646 speaks of creation "in the space of six days." Young-earth readers are right that most Christians through most of history read the days as days. Their critics are right that the church never made one reading of them a test of faith, and that some of its greatest teachers read them otherwise long before Darwin.

**Young-earth creationism** holds that God created the universe in six ordinary days a few thousand years ago, and that the fossil record is largely the product of the global flood of Genesis 6 through 9. Its best-known voice is Ken Ham, whose ministry Answers in Genesis opened the Creation Museum in Kentucky in 2007. In *The Lie: Evolution* (1987) Ham argued that the question is finally one of authority: if the plain sense of Genesis can be revised whenever secular science changes its mind, nothing stops the same revision from reaching the empty tomb. He presses a point that deserves an answer, that a creation God called very good cannot have contained hundreds of millions of years of disease, predation and extinction before anyone sinned. The movement's most interesting scholar is Kurt Wise, who earned a Harvard doctorate in geology, studying paleontology under Stephen Jay Gould. In his contribution to *In Six Days*, a collection of essays by creationist scientists edited by John Ashton, Wise explained that his conviction rests on his reading of Scripture first, and that he would remain a young-earth creationist even if all the evidence turned against it; in *Faith, Form, and Time* (2002) he tried to build a model of earth history on that foundation. One can disagree and still honor his candor.

**The day-age view** reads each day as a long age. Hugh Ross, an astronomer who founded Reasons to Believe in 1986, is its leading advocate, and in *A Matter of Days* (2004) he argues that the Hebrew *yom* can mean an extended period, that the seventh day has no closing "evening and morning," and that Scripture treats God's rest as still open: "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9, ESV). If the seventh day is long, the others may be too. Ross holds that nature and Scripture are both God's revelation and cannot finally contradict each other, and he reads Genesis 1 as broadly matching the sequence science describes, while rejecting common descent in favor of direct creative acts across the ages. The pressure on his view comes from the text: plants on the third day before the sun on the fourth, and birds before land animals, fit awkwardly with the order the fossil record gives.

**The framework view** takes that awkwardness as a clue. Meredith Kline, beginning with his 1958 article, and Henri Blocher in *In the Beginning* (1984) argued that the six days are a literary frame. The two triads are arranged by topic rather than by clock, and the week is a picture of God as a workman, teaching Israel who made the world and why they keep the Sabbath. The light of the first day and the lights of the fourth describe one reality from two angles. The framework reader insists he reached this by attending to the text's own shape, not by consulting geologists. His critics reply that the chapter uses the ordinary Hebrew forms for narrating one event after another, and that Exodus grounds Israel's working week in God's.

**The cosmic temple reading** belongs to John Walton, who taught Old Testament for many years at Wheaton College, and whose *The Lost World of Genesis One* (2009) argues that in the ancient world to create something meant chiefly to give it a function in an ordered system, not to manufacture its matter. The first three days establish the functions of time, weather and food; the next three install the creatures who carry them out. On the seventh day the cosmos is inaugurated as God's temple and he takes up his rest, which in the ancient world meant taking up his throne. The prophets speak that language: "Thus says the LORD: 'Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool; what is the house that you would build for me, and what is the place of my rest?'" (Isaiah 66:1, ESV). Walton affirms that God made matter too; he holds only that Genesis 1 is not the text that says so. His critics doubt that ancient readers pulled what a thing is apart from what it is for as cleanly as the reading requires.

**The analogical days view** is Collins's own. In *Genesis 1-4* (2006) he argues that the days are God's workdays, analogous to ours but not identical to them, in the same way that God's "eyes" and "hand" are analogies. Scripture itself signals this when it says of God's seventh day that "he rested and was refreshed" (Exodus 31:17, ESV). God does not tire. The week tells the truth about God's work in a form fitted to creatures meant to imitate it, so the days are broadly sequential without being twenty-four hours long. Young-earth readers answer that analogy can be stretched to cover anything, and framework readers answer that Collins keeps more chronology than the text requires.

**Evolutionary creation** holds that God made living things, human bodies included, through the long process of common descent that biologists describe, a process he sustains and directs at every moment. The BioLogos Foundation, established by Francis Collins after he published *The Language of God* (2006), is its main institutional home. Its defenders note that Scripture already speaks this way about natural processes; the psalmist knew how children are conceived and still said to God, "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13, ESV). A natural process and a divine act are not rivals. Denis Lamoureux, who holds doctoral degrees in dentistry, theology and biology, gave the view its fullest theological statement in *Evolutionary Creation* (2008). He argues that the Holy Spirit accommodated revelation to the science of its day, so that the solid dome and the waters above are the ancient vessel carrying a true message about God. He counts young-earth and day-age readings alike as concordism, the attempt to make Scripture and science line up point for point.

That observation clarifies the whole table. Ham and Ross disagree sharply about the science, but they share the conviction that Genesis 1 speaks to physical chronology and must agree with the true account of it. Kline, Blocher, Walton, Lamoureux and, in part, Collins hold that the chapter's order is doing theological and liturgical work and was never meant to be checked against a geological column. That is the real line of division, and it concerns what kind of text Genesis is, not whether it is true. Everyone at this table believes Genesis 1 is the word of God. The physical evidence for an old universe and an old earth is massive and converges from many directions, and no church serves its young people by hiding that. But the argument between these readers is fought on the page.

## The question that is really about Adam

The age of the earth is the loud question. The hard one is Adam. Paul builds the logic of salvation on a man at the head of the race, and the passage where he does it most fully has to be read whole. "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (Romans 5:12, ESV). Paul breaks that sentence off unfinished, and before he takes it up again he calls Adam "a type of the one who was to come" (Romans 5:14, ESV). What follows is a comparison of two men and two acts, pressed clause by clause: "For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19, ESV). The passage is about the reign of sin and death and the greater reign of grace that overtakes it, and it ends there (Romans 5:21). But it moves by setting one man's act beside another's, and Paul says the same thing to the Corinthians: "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22, ESV). Luke traces Jesus' genealogy back through Seth to "the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38, ESV). The young-earth reader's question has real weight here: if there was no first man and no first sin, what exactly did the second Adam come to undo?

The pressure now comes from genetics as much as geology. Dennis Venema, a biologist, and Scot McKnight, a New Testament scholar, argued in *Adam and the Genome* (2017) that the genetic diversity of living humans indicates an ancestral population that never shrank to a single pair, but numbered in the thousands. *Four Views on the Historical Adam* (2013), edited by Matthew Barrett and Ardel Caneday, sets four evangelical answers side by side. Lamoureux argues there was no historical Adam: Genesis 2 and 3 carry true teaching about sin in the ancient science of their day, and the gospel rests on the reality of sin and the work of Christ rather than on ancient biology. Walton holds that Adam and Eve were real people in a real past, whom Genesis presents chiefly as archetypes, representing all humanity. William Barrick, writing for the young-earth view, defends a recently created first pair as the ancestors of everyone. Two pastors close the book on opposite sides, Philip Ryken holding that the faith cannot be understood without a real Adam and Gregory Boyd that it stands secure either way. C. John Collins, in *Did Adam and Eve Really Exist?* (2011), argues that the Bible's whole storyline, a good creation spoiled by human sin and set right by God, needs a real Adam and Eve at the headwaters of humanity, and he allows that if there were other humans, Adam may have been a chieftain whose fall carried those he represented.

The most surprising contribution came from S. Joshua Swamidass, a computational biologist, in *The Genealogical Adam and Eve* (2019). He distinguishes genetic ancestry, the stretches of DNA we inherit, from genealogical ancestry, the family tree. Genetic evidence speaks about populations. Genealogy spreads far faster. Swamidass argues that a couple specially created by God in the Near East within recent millennia could, through ordinary descent and intermarriage, have become genealogical ancestors of every human being alive within a few thousand years, even though they were not the only people on earth. Others lived outside the garden, sharing the long genetic history biologists describe, and Adam's line married into theirs. He doesn't claim the science proves this, only that it doesn't forbid it. Critics on several sides ask what to make of those outside the garden, and that question remains open.

Then there is death before the fall. Young-earth readers take Paul to mean that all death entered with sin, and they hear the whole creation in his later lament: "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now" (Romans 8:22, ESV). Others note that Romans 5 speaks of death spreading "to all men," and hold that Paul means human death. They add that Scripture praises predation as provision: "The young lions roar for their prey, seeking their food from God" (Psalm 104:21, ESV).

This essay doesn't choose a reading of the days, and it doesn't settle how Adam came to be or whether he stood alone. Nor does it close the historical Adam debate, which remains open among evangelicals who confess the same creeds. It can say what each position costs. The church's long reading of Genesis 3, and the plain movement of Paul's comparison, take the fall as something that happened: sin entering a world God had made good, not a description of how he made us. Collins, Walton and Barrick each hold a version of that, and Swamidass built his model to leave room for it. Lamoureux does not, and he argues that the reality of sin and the sufficiency of Christ stand without it, a reading of Paul that departs from how the church has usually understood him. The question the young-earth reader presses hardest remains live at every other seat: if death and struggle filled the world long before anyone sinned, what exactly did sin bring? The answer that Paul means human death has real force. Whether it reaches everything he says about a groaning creation is the part no reading has closed.

## The rest at the end of the week

Look at what everyone at the table confesses together. The world was made out of nothing by a God under no compulsion to make it: "By faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God, so that what is seen was not made out of things that are visible" (Hebrews 11:3, ESV). What he made is good, not a prison for spirits or the leftover of a divine war. Human beings bear his image, every one of them, which is why no one may be used as a thing. And sin is real and universal, not a flaw in the Maker's work but a human turning away from him, and only God can undo it. These are first-order, and they are what Genesis was written to teach. The rest is the church's honest argument about what the text was never asked.

Each side of that argument has its own temptation, and I know both pulls. One reader is so jealous for the plain sense that he makes a date for the rocks a condition of fellowship, and so demands of the seeker what Scripture never demanded. Another is so eager to be taken seriously by educated people that he keeps adjusting the text, until Adam dissolves into a symbol and the fall into a feeling and nothing in his faith could cost him a colleague's respect. Each dresses an idol in a virtue, calling a favorite reading faithfulness in one case and a need for approval honesty in the other. They deserve the same diagnosis, delivered with the same grief, and I am not outside either of them.

There is one more thing the chapter's shape says. Its week ends in rest, and its seventh day has no evening. Long afterward the Word through whom "all things were made" (John 1:3, ESV) lay in a tomb cut into a garden through a Sabbath, and "on the first day of the week" (John 20:1, ESV) a woman came in the dark and found the stone moved. She mistook the risen Christ for the gardener. She wasn't entirely wrong. The new creation began where the first one went wrong, in a garden, at the start of a week, with dust that had once been told, "for you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19, ESV), standing up alive.

For years my complaint against Genesis was about mechanism, and it turned out to be the wrong complaint. The chapter was never withholding an answer about process. It was giving one about ownership, about what a human being is for, and about where rest is found, and I wasn't willing to hear it. The earth may be as old as the starlight says. What Genesis leaves with every reader is older than the rocks: whether we will take our rest from the one who made us, or keep laboring, like the servants of Babylon's gods, as though everything depended on us.
