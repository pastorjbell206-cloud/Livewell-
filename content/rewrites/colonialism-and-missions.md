## The charge at full strength

On mission Sunday the story is usually told in one color. Someone crossed an ocean, learned a hard language, buried a child in foreign ground and stayed anyway, and now a people who had never heard the name of Jesus sing to him. The slides show smiling faces, the offering goes around, and the congregation goes home moved. I have stood in front of my own church and told that version. Every sentence of it can be true and the whole of it still dishonest, because of what it leaves out.

What it leaves out is why the word "missionary" sounds, in much of the world and to many of the skeptics this writing most wants to reach, like another word for conquest. A saying has circulated across Africa for generations, attributed to several people and securely to none: when the missionaries came, they had the Bible and we had the land; they taught us to close our eyes and pray, and when we opened them, we had the Bible and they had the land. In Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958) the church arrives in Igboland alongside the colonial court and prison, and Achebe is fair enough to make one missionary patient and curious and his successor rigid and contemptuous. But the last page belongs to the district commissioner, who decides a great man's ruin might make a paragraph in his planned book, *The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger*. Achebe's charge isn't that every missionary was cruel. It's that kindness and conquest came ashore in the same boat, and the people on the beach had no way to sort the cargo.

That charge deserves its strongest form before any reply. It says that Christian mission from the fifteenth century to the twentieth was one of empire's instruments. The same ships carried soldiers and friars, the same treasuries paid governors and bishops, and the same assumptions about who was civilized governed both the seizure of land and the baptism of its people. Even missionaries who opposed particular abuses asked colonized peoples to accept the religion of their conquerors, and often made the surrender of names, languages, dress and memory the price of admission. Whatever the gospel is in itself, this is what it was in history.

Most of that is true, and the church should say so without clearing its throat. But it isn't the whole record, and what it leaves out is why a religion carried by empire did not die when the empires did. The honest history turns on a distinction both versions miss. There is a difference between Christianity *extended*, as the religion of a Christian realm pushing out its borders, and Christianity *translated*, as a message rendered into another people's language. Extension moves by territory; it plants a flag, and the faith arrives as a condition of belonging to the conquering order. Translation moves by words; it learns the other people's speech, puts Scripture into it, and discovers that the faith, once it lives in those words, no longer belongs to the people who brought it. For four centuries the two were tangled together, often inside a single life. The untangling is the work this history demands of us.

## The cross under the crown

In 1513 a Spanish jurist, Juan López de Palacios Rubios, drafted the Requerimiento, the Requirement, a text conquistadors had to read aloud before making war on the peoples of the Americas. It explained that God had given authority over all peoples to Saint Peter and his successors, that a pope had granted these lands to the monarchs of Castile, and that the hearers must therefore acknowledge the church and the crown. If they refused, it promised war, the enslavement of their wives and children and the seizure of their goods, and it declared in advance that the deaths to follow would be their own fault. It was read in Spanish to people who spoke none, sometimes to villages already emptied by fear. Bartolomé de las Casas later wrote that he couldn't decide whether to laugh or weep at it.

The Requerimiento matters because it is so candid. It wasn't an abuse of the system. It was the system's theology written down: the gospel as a legal title, the pope as a grantor of continents, conversion as a clause in a surrender. (The papal bulls later called the Doctrine of Discovery belong to the same world and need their own essay.) Through papal grants of the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, the crowns of Portugal and Spain received the patronage of the church in the lands they claimed, the Portuguese *padroado* and the Castilian *patronato real*, which Julius II confirmed in 1508. The king nominated the bishops, collected the tithes, licensed the missionaries, paid their passage and built their churches.

It was sincerely meant as a gift, since Rome lacked the ships and money to reach the world and the crowns had both. But a missionary who sails on the king's ship under the king's license, answering to the king's bishop, is not a free witness, whatever his holiness. The faith had been made a department of state, and no amount of individual heroism undoes that, because the fault lay less in the missionary's heart than in the hand that paid him. The same system did send friars who learned Nahuatl and Quechua, wrote the first grammars of those languages, and in the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún's Florentine Codex, compiled with Nahua collaborators, recorded a culture with a care nobody else was taking. That work ran against the grain of the system, from inside it, and on the question of power it usually lost.

Rome itself came to see the cost. In 1622 Gregory XV founded the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, the *Propaganda Fide*, partly to recover the direction of missions from the Iberian crowns. In 1659 its instructions to the first vicars apostolic bound for East Asia told them, in substance, not to press the peoples they served to change their rites, customs and manners unless these were plainly contrary to religion and morals, and asked what could be more absurd than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China. Bring them the faith, not your countries. That Rome named the error more clearly than it practiced the cure is the story of every age of the church, ours included.

## Christianity, commerce and civilization

Protestants like to think that story is a Catholic one. It isn't. Britain's nineteenth-century empire produced its own version of the fusion, gentler in vocabulary. In December 1857 David Livingstone told an audience at Cambridge that he was returning to Africa to open a path for commerce and Christianity. The formula later attached to his name, Christianity, commerce and civilization, was not cynical. Livingstone had seen the slave trade in the African interior at close range and believed honest trade would starve it. The historian Brian Stanley, in *The Bible and the Flag* (1990), shows that Protestant missions and British imperialism were never simple allies: missionaries could oppose colonial policy as well as endorse it, and their first loyalty ran to another kingdom. Yet the formula made the gospel one of three goods in a single package, and the other two were European. A package like that can be opened by anyone who wants what is inside.

The Congo shows how. Around the Berlin Conference of 1884 and 1885, the European powers recognized King Leopold II of Belgium as sovereign of a vast territory in central Africa, largely on his promise to suppress the slave trade and bring civilization to the interior. He built a private extraction state instead. When demand for rubber rose in the 1890s, his army enforced village quotas with hostage-taking, burning and mutilation; soldiers cut off hands to account for their bullets. Adam Hochschild, in *King Leopold's Ghost* (1998), counts the population loss in millions, though historians still dispute the figures. His book also records a fact that cuts both ways. Leopold courted the missions, and many, especially those that depended on the state's favor, kept quiet. Yet some of the regime's most damaging witnesses were missionaries. William Sheppard, an African American Presbyterian from Virginia, investigated a raid in 1899 and reported severed hands being dried over a fire; he was later tried for libel in a colonial court and acquitted. The English missionary Alice Seeley Harris photographed a man named Nsala beside the hand and foot of his small daughter, and her pictures fed the reform campaign that forced Belgium to take the colony from Leopold in 1908. The mission field produced both the silence and the evidence.

North America belongs to the same ledger, and there the churches were not bystanders. Richard Henry Pratt founded the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in 1879 and in an 1892 speech summed up his method as killing the Indian in a person to save the man. In Canada, from the 1880s until the last school closed in 1996, more than 150,000 First Nations, Métis and Inuit children were sent to residential schools funded by the federal government and run largely by the Roman Catholic, Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian churches and, after 1925, the United Church. Children were taken from their families and punished for speaking their own languages, and many met hunger, disease and abuse. Thousands died. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, in its final report of 2015, called the system cultural genocide. In July 2022 Pope Francis went to Maskwacis, Alberta, and asked forgiveness for the evil so many Christians had done to Indigenous peoples.

The people who ran those schools mostly believed they were saving children. Their sincerity did not reduce the harm. It powered it, because a theology that could not tell conversion from assimilation could do terrible things with a clear conscience and pray over them. And here the history stops being about them. The assumption beneath "Christianity, commerce and civilization" is that the carrier's culture is the normal form of the faith and every other culture a problem to be solved on the way there. We still carry it, every time we can't imagine faithful worship without our instruments and our sort of sermon, or measure a church overseas by how closely it resembles ours. The residential schools were that assumption given dormitories and a government contract. We don't get to grieve them from a safe distance.

## The witnesses the charge leaves out

Las Casas is best remembered for Valladolid, where in 1550 and 1551 he argued against Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda that the peoples of the Americas were fully human and could not be conquered for their own good, a debate told elsewhere on this site. For mission, an earlier work matters more. In the 1530s he wrote a Latin treatise known in English as *The Only Way*, and its argument is in its title. There is one way to draw people to the true faith, the way Christ used: persuading the understanding and gently attracting the will, with no force, no threat and no conquest going before. A gospel preached after a war isn't really preached, because it arrives as the religion of the men who killed your family. A Dominican friar and later a bishop, Las Casas argued at length from Scripture, the church fathers and Aquinas. The Gospels had already given the verdict on the other way, on the night Peter drew a sword in Gethsemane:

> "Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword." (Matthew 26:52, ESV)

The one time an apostle tried to defend the kingdom with steel, Jesus stopped him and, Luke adds, healed the wound. A mission that marches behind soldiers has to explain that night away.

William Carey, an English Baptist shoemaker turned pastor, published *An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens* in 1792 and sailed for Bengal the next year. The claim that missions simply followed the flag breaks on his story, because the flag didn't want him. The East India Company feared missionaries would unsettle the populations its trade depended on, and Carey stayed in the country by managing an indigo works until he and his colleagues settled in 1800 at Serampore, a Danish enclave outside the Company's reach. British India wasn't formally opened to missionaries until the charter renewal of 1813. From there Carey and his partners translated Scripture, in whole or in part, into dozens of the subcontinent's languages and campaigned for years against *sati*, the burning of widows, which the Bengal government outlawed in 1829. He arrived less as the empire's chaplain than as its irritant.

In 2012 that pattern became a finding in political science. The sociologist Robert Woodberry published "The Missionary Roots of Liberal Democracy" in the *American Political Science Review*. He found that places across the non-Western world where conversion-minded Protestant missionaries had worked are, on average, more likely to have stable democracies today, and he traced the connection through what those missionaries built: mass literacy, printing and newspapers, voluntary associations, and public campaigns against colonial abuses. The effect attached to missionaries independent of the state, supported by churches at home, rather than to clergy paid by colonial governments. The ones the empire didn't pay were free to criticize it, and many did. Other scholars have questioned how far a statistical association across so many countries can show cause, and whether missionaries simply went where conditions were already favorable, a problem Woodberry himself worked to address; the thesis should be held as a strong argument rather than a closed case. But see what it does to the charge. It doesn't deny the complicity. It suggests that the missions' most lasting public gifts came where the faith stood furthest from the state.

The deepest reply came from Lamin Sanneh, who was born in the Gambia and raised a Muslim, became a Christian, and taught at Harvard and then Yale. In *Translating the Message* (1989) he argued that the defining act of Christian mission is translation, with consequences no missionary could control. Christianity, unlike Islam with its Arabic Qur'an, has no sacred language; from the start the words of an Aramaic-speaking Jesus reached the church in Greek. So a missionary who rendered the Bible into Yoruba or Kikuyu or Bengali conceded, whether he meant to or not, that this language was a fit vessel for the word of God. The grammars missionaries made for unwritten languages became tools of cultural renewal and, in time, of the nationalist movements that sent the colonial powers home. The colonizers wanted to bring Africa into Europe. The translators, often the same people, put a Bible into African hands in African words, and Africans read it for themselves. The vernacular Bible carried a verdict on colonialism the missionaries themselves had not yet reached.

Sanneh had Pentecost on his side, where the apostles were given the crowd's languages and pilgrims from Parthia, Egypt and Rome asked the question that stands over every translated Bible since:

> "And how is it that we hear, each of us in his own native language?" (Acts 2:8, ESV)

Pentecost is the Requerimiento turned inside out. One reads a demand in the conqueror's tongue to people who can't understand it; the other gives the gospel away in the hearer's tongue.

## Whose religion is it now?

If Christianity had been nothing more than the chaplaincy of empire, it should have left with the administrators. It didn't. Sanneh made the point in *Whose Religion Is Christianity?* (2003): across much of Africa the faith grew fastest after independence, in churches led by Africans, worshiping in African languages. The historian Andrew Walls, who taught in West Africa before building the study of world Christianity at Aberdeen and Edinburgh, explained why. In *The Missionary Movement in Christian History* (1996) he argued that Christian expansion has always been serial rather than progressive. Jerusalem gave way to the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, that world to northern Europe, and Europe and North America held the center for a few centuries; then in the twentieth century, the same century that dismantled the European empires, its center of gravity moved south. By its close, as Walls and Philip Jenkins in *The Next Christendom* (2002) both documented, most of the world's Christians lived outside Europe and North America.

The European missionary of 1880 thought he was carrying the faith from its home to its frontier, when he was carrying it from one temporary home to the next. Far from colonial residue, the growth that followed is the faith doing what it has always done: crossing a border and becoming native on the other side.

Walls gave the church two principles for holding this. The first he called the indigenizing principle. God accepts us as we are, and "as we are" includes our language, family ties and the whole social world that made us, so the gospel is always making itself at home in each culture it enters. The second he called the pilgrim principle. God takes us as we are in order to make us what we are not, so the gospel also unsettles every culture, and no people is ever entirely at home in this world. Colonial mission broke the tension in both directions. It denied the indigenizing principle to others, demanding that Africans and Indians and Cree become pilgrims from everything their cultures had given them, and it forgot the pilgrim principle for itself, treating European habits as the kingdom's native dress. The sin was not that missionaries asked people to change. The gospel asks everyone to change. The sin was that they exempted themselves.

The first church faced this exact question. When Gentiles began believing, some Jewish believers insisted they be circumcised and keep the law of Moses, in effect becoming culturally Jewish to belong to the Jewish Messiah. Those were no European customs. They were marks God himself had given Israel. Yet the council at Jerusalem, around the year 49, ruled that they would not be required, and James gave the judgment "that we should not trouble those of the Gentiles who turn to God" (Acts 15:19, ESV). Walls drew from it a distinction between a proselyte, who adopts another people's way of life, and a convert, who turns his own way of life toward Christ. If the apostles refused to bind Gentiles to customs God had commanded, no later church had standing to bind converts to the customs of Lisbon or London.

None of this means the colonial patterns vanished with the colonies. Money still flows mostly from north to south, and authority tends to follow the money. A pastor in Accra or Manila can find that his judgment counts for less than the budget of the church that funds him. The churches of the global South are now most of the church, and poverty and persecution have taught them things comfortable congregations haven't learned. If a colonial habit survives in Western mission, it is the reflex that assumes we are still the ones who teach.

## The trip and the photograph

For most American Christians, this history comes closest not through a papal bull but through a church van headed for the airport. A team flies to Guatemala or Haiti, spends a week building, painting or running a children's program, and comes home with photographs and testimonies. The testimonies are usually about the team: how their eyes were opened, how much they have, how joyful people seemed with so little. I am not outside this. I have been on these trips, felt the satisfaction of having done something, and used the photographs.

The case against the trip begins with arithmetic. A team's airfare could employ local workers for a long time, and the wall it puts up in five days often took weeks of local preparation and could have been built better by local builders who needed the wages. Steve Corbett and Brian Fikkert, in *When Helping Hurts* (2009), gave that intuition a framework. They distinguish relief, the urgent aid that stops the bleeding after a disaster, from development, the long work of people becoming more able to shape their own lives, with rehabilitation between them. Their central warning is that North American churches habitually deliver relief into situations that call for development, and that doing so teaches dependence. Drawing on Bryant Myers, they describe poverty as the breakdown of four relationships, with God, self, others and creation, which means the materially rich are poor too. The characteristic poverty of the comfortable is a god complex, the quiet belief that we are the ones who fix. When that meets the shame of material poverty, each confirms the other's brokenness, and both go home poorer than the photographs suggest.

Robert Lupton reached the same place from decades of community development in inner-city Atlanta. In *Toxic Charity* (2011) he argued that one-way giving, right in a genuine emergency, turns corrosive when it becomes a permanent relationship. His guiding rule, in paraphrase, is never to do for people what they have, or could have, the capacity to do for themselves. A neighborhood perpetually visited by helpers learns who acts and who is acted upon, and the lesson outlasts the paint. That is the colonial pattern in miniature.

The defenders of short-term mission deserve the same care. Many churches in the global South ask for visitors, and a congregation that hosts guests is exercising hospitality, which is its own dignity and agency; to cancel the trip in the name of their dignity without asking them is a new paternalism in an old coat. Many lifelong partnerships and missionary callings began with one week somewhere. And the New Testament itself has cross-cultural money. Paul spent years gathering an offering from Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem, carried by delegates the giving churches chose so the gift could be trusted. Rather than rich helpers rescuing poor recipients, he describes an exchange:

> "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness." (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV)

And in Romans he names which way the deeper debt runs:

> "For they were pleased to do it, and indeed they owe it to them. For if the Gentiles have come to share in their spiritual blessings, they ought also to be of service to them in material blessings." (Romans 15:27, ESV)

The givers were the debtors. That reverses the whole grammar of the mission photograph, in which the visitor is benefactor and the visited are beneficiaries.

The trip is not the core problem. The direction of the story is. Tourism, however devout, makes the visitor the subject and the community the setting. Mission makes the receiving church the agent and the visitor a guest who arrives with resources but without the answers. In practice that means going when invited and under local leadership, paying local hands wherever the work is labor, coming to learn at least as much as to help, and staying in the relationship for years after the photographs fade. Paul's own pattern was long presence, eighteen months in Corinth and two years in Ephesus. A useful test is whether the partner church, asked privately, would describe the trip the way we describe it on Sunday. If it wouldn't, we have been telling our story with their faces in it.

## The book that judged its carriers

At the end of the canon John is shown where all mission is going, and one detail in it is a verdict on the residential school:

> "After this I looked, and behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands," (Revelation 7:9, ESV)

The languages are still there. The redeemed are not melted into one tongue before the throne; Cree and Yoruba and Bengali survive into the age to come, washed and gathered but not erased. A school that beat a child for speaking her grandmother's language was not preparing her for heaven. It was preparing her for a heaven God never described. The Requerimiento dreamed of one crown and one language. The Lamb gathers every nation in its own.

That is also why grief is not enough. Churches have apologized, some early, some late, some while records stayed sealed and graves unmarked. Repentance that means anything looks like the prophets' kind: truth told in full, records opened, burial grounds searched and honored, suppressed languages funded and learned, and a willingness to let Indigenous Christians and their neighbors say what repair requires. It is the only confession Scripture recognizes as real.

It also comes with a reversal. Missionaries from Korea, Nigeria and Brazil now serve in countries that once sent missionaries to theirs. Christians from churches our grandparents evangelized look at the American church and see what we can't: a faith fused to a nation, a gospel packaged with a way of life, the old error of extension over translation alive in our own sanctuaries. The question colonial mission never learned to ask is now ours. Can we sit under the preaching of someone whose people we once presumed to civilize, and be taught? Paul's description of the faithful messenger belongs to them now as surely as to the ones we sent:

> "For what we proclaim is not ourselves, but Jesus Christ as Lord, with ourselves as your servants for Jesus' sake." (2 Corinthians 4:5, ESV)

Go back, finally, to the saying: they had the Bible and we had the land, and when we opened our eyes we had the Bible and they had the land. It is an indictment, and a true one. The land was not given back, and in much of the world it still hasn't been; that account remains open, and a church that forgets it hasn't repented. But notice what its tellers did with what they were left holding. They kept the book. They read it in their own languages, found in it a God who heard the cry of slaves in Egypt and a Messiah executed by the most powerful empire of his age, and turned it against the ones who had carried it to them. Every mission that marched behind a flag carried in its baggage the story of a man an empire crucified. Sooner or later that story judges the flag.

It has been judging ours for some time. The book that outlived the empires is now read most widely by the peoples the empires tried to remake, and it comes back to the West in accents our grandparents tried to correct, asking whether we still believe what we sent. We were trusted with a message that was never our possession, and we wrapped it in our flags and our trade and our schoolrooms, and it slipped the wrapping. What remains to be seen is whether the churches that sent it can bear to hear it preached back to them.
