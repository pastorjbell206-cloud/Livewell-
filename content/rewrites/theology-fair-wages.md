## The paycheck that comes up short

Most people who have been shorted on a paycheck know it before they can prove it. The hours on the stub don't match the hours on the floor. The overtime turned into "comp time" nobody was ever allowed to take. The manager asked everyone to clock out and finish closing, just this once, and then it was every night. You tell yourself it was a mistake, and sometimes it was. You also know that asking about it could cost you the schedule, and the rent is due either way.

Most households sit in more than one chair on this question. We are employees, counting on the deposit. We are employers, sometimes of a whole payroll and sometimes of one person: the sitter, the man who cleans the gutters, the woman who cleans the house on Thursdays. And we are customers, paying prices that are somebody's wage, usually somebody we'll never meet. The Bible speaks to all three chairs with a directness that ought to embarrass a church that has mostly kept quiet.

I'm inside that quiet. I have read James 5 and preached a general sermon on financial anxiety instead of the specific sin the text names. I have turned the harvesters' cry into a picture of the soul's longing for God, because a picture asks less of the people in the room. I have sat through church budget meetings where the numbers came out even because of the lines with people attached, a custodian's hours, a nursery worker's rate, and I raised my hand for those budgets. None of this is written from the safe side of the ledger.

The argument here turns on a distinction the American conversation keeps collapsing. There is the wage that is *owed*: what was agreed and earned. And there is the wage that is *just*: whether a full week of honest work ought to be able to buy a life. Withholding the first is theft, and Scripture says so without a hedge. The second is harder, and faithful Christians argue it in good faith. The church's failure has run in two directions at once. It has let the difficulty of the second question excuse its silence about the first, and it has let a legal, agreed wage pass for a just one without ever asking whether the agreement was free.

## The wage before the sun goes down

Start where Scripture starts, in the law. Leviticus 19 is the chapter that gives us "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18, ESV), and five verses earlier it shows what that love looks like on a payday: "You shall not oppress your neighbor or rob him. The wages of a hired worker shall not remain with you all night until the morning" (Leviticus 19:13, ESV). The verb translated "oppress" is *ashaq*, a word for extortion, for getting by pressure what you couldn't get by right. The law files a held wage beside the man who takes your purse, not among the bookkeeping lapses.

Deuteronomy sharpens the command and gives its reason.

"You shall not oppress a hired worker who is poor and needy, whether he is one of your brothers or one of the sojourners who are in your land within your towns" (Deuteronomy 24:14, ESV).

"You shall give him his wages on the same day, before the sun sets (for he is poor and counts on it), lest he cry against you to the LORD, and you be guilty of sin" (Deuteronomy 24:15, ESV).

The hired worker, the *sakir*, was a day laborer in a society where security meant land, and he had none. The command covers the foreigner as fully as the Israelite; there is no citizenship test on a day's pay. And the parenthesis is the theology of the whole passage. "He counts on it" renders a Hebrew idiom that says, more literally, that he lifts up his soul to it. His life is pinned to that wage. Today's work is tonight's supper, and a delay that would merely annoy a man with savings is, for him, the difference between a meal and none.

Read the verses in their chapter and the pattern becomes plain. Deuteronomy 24 is a run of laws about the means of life of the poor. A lender may not take a family's millstone as a pledge, because that would be taking a life in pledge (24:6). If he takes a poor man's cloak as security, he must give it back by sundown so the man has something to sleep in (24:12–13). The widow's garment may not be taken at all (24:17), and the edges of the harvest are left for the sojourner, the fatherless and the widow (24:19–22). The same sunset governs the cloak and the wage. Again and again the law puts a clock on the powerful, because the powerful can always wait and the poor can't.

Then comes the reason under all the reasons: "but you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt and the LORD your God redeemed you from there; therefore I command you to do this" (Deuteronomy 24:18, ESV). Israel's founding story is a labor story. Pharaoh's economy ran on work extracted without wages, and when the slaves were slow he took away their straw and kept the quota of bricks (Exodus 5). The exodus began with a cry: "Their cry for rescue from slavery came up to God" (Exodus 2:23, ESV). When Deuteronomy warns that a cheated worker will "cry against you to the LORD," it echoes the exodus. The worker's complaint doesn't go to a personnel office. It goes to the court that once broke Pharaoh.

Notice also what the law does not do. It sets no shekel figure. It sets a time, a protection and a witness. That will matter later, because it is the strongest card in the market case. The Torah shaped the floor under the poor by other means, through gleaning, the release of debts and the return of land (the subject of [a separate essay on the Jubilee](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded)), and its wage law is chiefly about paying what is owed, in full and on time, to people who can't afford to wait.

## A palace, a courtroom and a field

The prophets didn't invent this concern. They remembered it, and they aimed it upward. Jeremiah turned it on a king. Jehoiakim, son of the reforming king Josiah, came to the throne of Judah around 609 BC as a vassal, and he taxed the land to pay tribute to Pharaoh Neco (2 Kings 23:35). He also built himself a palace, and Jeremiah saw how.

"Woe to him who builds his house by unrighteousness, and his upper rooms by injustice, who makes his neighbor serve him for nothing and does not give him his wages" (Jeremiah 22:13, ESV).

The phrase "for nothing" is *hinnam*, gratuitously, without pay. The palace was paneled in cedar and painted in vermilion (22:14), and Jeremiah refuses to be impressed by any of it. He asks the king whether cedar is what makes a man a king, and then he points him to his own father, who ate and drank like any man and did justice. "He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 22:16, ESV). Knowing God, in Jeremiah's mouth, is not first a feeling or a doctrine held in the head. It's a way of treating people who can't make you treat them well, and a king who builds with unpaid labor has shown that he doesn't know the God whose name he rules in.

Malachi, writing to a small and disillusioned community after the exile, hears the people complain, "Where is the God of justice?" (Malachi 2:17, ESV). God answers that he is coming, and that they may not like it when he arrives.

"Then I will draw near to you for judgment. I will be a swift witness against the sorcerers, against the adulterers, against those who swear falsely, against those who oppress the hired worker in his wages, the widow and the fatherless, against those who thrust aside the sojourner, and do not fear me, says the LORD of hosts" (Malachi 3:5, ESV).

The company on that list is the point. The church has usually treated sorcery and adultery and perjury as serious sins, and treated the payroll as a matter of business judgment. Malachi puts them in one sentence and makes God himself the witness for the prosecution. A community that asks where God's justice has gone had better be ready for it to begin with the wages.

James carries the whole tradition into the New Testament. His letter went to congregations that were mostly poor; he asks them, "Are not the rich the ones who oppress you, and the ones who drag you into court?" (James 2:6, ESV). In chapter 5 he turns to address the rich directly, in the voice of an Old Testament oracle, over the heads of the congregation: "Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you" (James 5:1, ESV). Then comes the charge.

"Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:4, ESV).

Three details carry the weight. The charge is fraud, the old charge of Leviticus and Malachi, and its nearest neighbor in the Gospels is the command Jesus added when he recited the commandments to a rich young man, one that isn't among the ten: "Do not defraud" (Mark 10:19, ESV). The wages themselves cry out, as if the money sitting in the landowner's account had a voice. And the one who hears is the Lord of *Sabaoth*, the Lord of armies, a title that appears only twice in the New Testament. James could have said the Lord hears. He chose the name that means the Lord can do something about it. Then, and only then, he turns to the defrauded: "Be patient, therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord" (James 5:7, ESV). That is not a counsel of passivity. It's the patience of people who know the case has been filed with a judge who doesn't lose files.

The New Testament also says the positive thing plainly. Jesus sent out his messengers with the proverb "the laborer deserves his wages" (Luke 10:7, ESV), and Paul cites the same line as Scripture when he tells a church to pay the people who serve it (1 Timothy 5:18). The wage isn't a favor the employer grants. It's owed.

## The vineyard that argues with both sides

Every side of the wage debate eventually reaches for the parable of the laborers in the vineyard, and it cuts every hand that grabs it. Read it in its place first. It comes right after the rich young man walks away and Peter asks, "See, we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?" (Matthew 19:27, ESV). It is bracketed by the same saying, "the last will be first, and the first last" (Matthew 20:16, ESV; compare 19:30). It is a parable about the kingdom and about grace, told to disciples who had begun to keep score. [The essay on vocation](/writing/what-is-vocation-work-as-calling) follows that first meaning: the kingdom runs on gift, and gift offends the wage earner in us.

But Jesus built the parable out of a real labor market, and the details are exact. A landowner goes to the marketplace at dawn, where day laborers stood waiting to be hired. "After agreeing with the laborers for a denarius a day, he sent them into his vineyard" (Matthew 20:2, ESV). A denarius was the ordinary day's pay for such work. He goes back at nine, at noon, at three, and tells each new crew, "whatever is right I will give you" (Matthew 20:4, ESV). At the eleventh hour he finds men still standing there and asks why. "They said to him, 'Because no one has hired us'" (Matthew 20:7, ESV). At evening he tells his foreman to pay everyone, and he pays them the same.

The market's defender reads this and hears his own convictions spoken by Jesus. The first workers struck a bargain freely and received exactly what they agreed to. When they complain, the owner answers, "Friend, I am doing you no wrong. Did you not agree with me for a denarius?" (Matthew 20:13, ESV). Then he asserts a property right in so many words: "Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me? Or do you begrudge my generosity?" (Matthew 20:15, ESV). That reading is faithful to those lines; they're in the text.

The living-wage advocate reads the same parable and hears something else. The owner goes out again and again for men nobody would hire. He pays the last as much as the first, which means he pays by what a household needs to eat that day rather than by the hours on the clock. He promises "whatever is right," not whatever the market will bear. And he pays at evening, before sundown, as Deuteronomy commands. That reading isn't a distortion either.

Both readers are right about what they see, and both are wrong to make the parable a policy. What survives both readings is the shape of the owner. He keeps the contract to the letter; nobody is shorted, nobody waits until morning. And then he goes beyond the contract, freely, toward the men the market had passed over. Justice is the floor he never falls below, and generosity is what he builds on it. The only person in the story who sins is the worker who thinks another man's gift is his loss.

The parable won't settle the minimum wage. It does settle what kind of employer a disciple of Jesus is supposed to be: one whose agreements can be trusted absolutely, and whose freedom with what belongs to him bends toward the last man standing in the square.

## How the church learned to talk about wages, and then went quiet

The industrial age made the *sakir* the majority. Most people who once lived off a plot of land now lived off a wage, and the churches had to decide whether the old texts still applied. In May 1891 Leo XIII answered with *Rerum Novarum*. He defended private property against the socialists without apology, and in the same letter he drew the distinction this whole essay depends on. Worker and employer may agree freely on wages, he wrote, but beneath any bargain lies an older demand of natural justice: a wage must be enough to support a "frugal and well-behaved wage-earner." If a worker accepts harsher terms out of necessity, or for fear of something worse, because no employer will offer better, Leo judged him a victim of force and injustice rather than a free party to a bargain. Leo also named defrauding a worker of his wage as a crime that cries to heaven, and he cited James to say so. Consent, in other words, is necessary for a just wage, and it isn't sufficient.

Six months later, in November 1891, Abraham Kuyper opened the first Christian Social Congress in Amsterdam with the address published as *The Problem of Poverty*. He told Dutch Calvinists that the misery of the industrial poor was a question Christians had no right to leave to the socialists, and that where rich and poor stand opposed, Jesus stands with the poor. His doctrine of sphere sovereignty gave labor and enterprise their own God-given integrity and left the state a limited office of protecting the weak within each sphere from the strong. It's worth adding what happened when Kuyper governed. As prime minister, his cabinet answered the Dutch railway strikes of 1903 with laws that made such strikes a criminal offense. One of the most serious Protestant social thinkers of his generation found it easier to speak for the poor than to govern alongside them. That isn't a reason to dismiss him. It's a warning to everyone who admires him.

In America the argument took its most careful form in the work of a Minnesota priest. John A. Ryan's *A Living Wage* (1906), written as his doctoral dissertation at the Catholic University of America, argued that God gave the earth for the sustenance of all, and that a worker whose only access to that bounty is his wage has a claim of justice, not charity, to a wage that supports a decent life. Ryan also argued that the state could rightly enforce the claim. Protestants were saying something close; the Federal Council of Churches adopted a social creed in 1908 that called for a living wage as the minimum in every industry. In 1919 Ryan drafted the Catholic bishops' *Program of Social Reconstruction*, which proposed minimum-wage laws among other reforms. The Supreme Court struck down a minimum wage for women in *Adkins v. Children's Hospital* (1923), reversed itself in *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish* (1937), and in 1938 the Fair Labor Standards Act set a federal minimum of twenty-five cents an hour and required overtime pay. An argument Christians had pressed from Leviticus and from Leo had become, in part, a statute.

Then much of the church, especially American Protestantism, went quiet. Part of the reason was success; the argument seemed won. A larger part was the partition that settled over American evangelicalism in the twentieth century, which filed salvation, prayer and personal morality under "spiritual" and put wages under "politics." On that partition Deuteronomy 24 becomes an ancient regulation, James 5 becomes a warning about greed in the heart, and the prophets become devotional writers whose economics expired with their kings. [A companion essay on money and wealth](/writing/what-the-bible-actually-says-about-money-and-wealth) deals with the heart's love of money. This one has to deal with the payroll, because the texts do.

The payroll needs naming. The federal minimum wage has stood at $7.25 an hour since July 2009, the longest stretch without an increase since the law was passed, though many states set their own higher floors. And the theft is not marginal. In 2008 a team led by Annette Bernhardt surveyed 4,387 low-wage workers in Chicago, Los Angeles and New York; their report, *Broken Laws, Unprotected Workers* (2009), found that 26 percent had been paid less than the minimum wage in the previous week, and that 76 percent of those who had worked more than forty hours weren't paid the overtime the law required. In 2017 David Cooper and Teresa Kroeger of the Economic Policy Institute estimated that minimum-wage violations alone cost about 2.4 million workers in the ten most populous states some $8 billion a year. The forms are ordinary: work before clocking in or after clocking out, breaks that are docked but not taken, workers misclassified as managers or independent contractors to avoid overtime, tips skimmed, illegal deductions for uniforms and equipment. They fall hardest on the workers least able to complain, which is exactly the worker Deuteronomy had in mind.

## The market case, and the order of the question

The case against treating wage levels as a matter for the pulpit deserves its strongest voice, because serious Christians hold it and its evidence is real. It goes like this. Scripture condemns withholding the agreed wage, and it never names a wage level; the Torah set a clock, not a number. A wage is a price, and as Friedrich Hayek argued in "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), prices carry knowledge that no planner possesses. Fix the price of labor above what a worker's hour produces for the business, and fewer hours will be bought, starting with the least experienced workers. David Neumark and William Wascher, reviewing decades of research in *Minimum Wages* (2008), concluded that the weight of the evidence shows job losses among the least skilled. When the Congressional Budget Office studied a $15 federal minimum in 2019, it estimated that about 1.3 million people would rise out of poverty and, in its median estimate, about 1.3 million would lose work, with a likely range running from about zero to 3.7 million. And a voluntary job is a real good. Deirdre McCloskey argues in *Bourgeois Dignity* (2010) that the modern escape from mass poverty came when societies began to honor commerce and the people who trade, hire and build. The man who offers a job is doing his neighbor good. Most employers pay honestly, many small-business owners feel every payroll in their stomachs, and wage law is complex enough that a decent person can misclassify a worker in good faith. Calling every such dispute "theft" wins the argument with a word when it should be won with facts.

An honest employer would sign that, and parts of it should be granted outright. The economics is contested. David Card and Alan Krueger's 1994 study of fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania found no job losses after New Jersey raised its minimum, and economists have argued over that finding ever since. A preacher can't settle the dispute by quoting Amos. The advocacy side of the church has its own sin to grieve, too: a habit of treating every mandate as free and every employer as Jehoiakim, which is party loyalty dressed up as prophecy.

So order the question before arguing it. First-order, binding on every Christian conscience: pay what you owe, in full and on time; defrauding a worker is sin; the worker's cry reaches God; and no economy is exempt from his judgment. Second-order, where faithful Christians divide: what the law should require, whether minimum wages, wage subsidies like the Earned Income Tax Credit (enacted in 1975), stronger enforcement, collective bargaining, or some mix. Third-order: the dollar figure and the schedule. Someone who doubts a particular increase on the employment evidence remains a brother in good standing. Someone who has never let James 5 near his own payroll or his own shopping hasn't started.

The essay's conclusion is this. The market case, granted all its evidence, assumes a bargain between parties who can each walk away, and that assumption often fails. Adam Smith saw it in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): employers are few and combine easily, he observed, and in a dispute a master can live for a year or two on his stock while many workers couldn't last a week without wages. Smith also held that a wage must at least keep the worker alive and generally enough more to raise a family. Joan Robinson gave the problem a name in *The Economics of Imperfect Competition* (1933): monopsony, a market with few buyers of labor, where wages can sit below what the work is worth. That is Leo's distinction again. A wage agreed under necessity is agreed, and it may still be unjust. It's also why Deuteronomy put the day laborer's pay under law rather than under the landowner's mood.

The strongest objection this conclusion hasn't fully answered is the one the market case presses hardest. If a mandated floor prices the least skilled worker out of his first job, his living wage has become no wage at all. The essay has no clean answer to that, only the conviction that the question belongs to Christians on both sides of it, and that neither side gets to stop asking it.

## Three chairs, and the sun going down

What follows is moral counsel, not legal or financial advice. Wage law differs by state and by job, and anyone facing a real dispute should talk to the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division, a state labor office or a legal aid attorney.

If you're the worker, keep your own record of your hours. Federal law forbids retaliating against someone for filing a wage complaint, and asking for what you've earned isn't disloyalty. Scripture treats the worker's cry as legitimate enough for God to hear it. And the command runs both ways: "Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men" (Colossians 3:23, ESV).

If you're the employer, even of one sitter or one lawn crew, pay what you agreed, when you agreed, including what the law requires. The cash price that's cheaper because somebody's overtime or taxes went unpaid is the old sin at a new rate. "Masters, treat your bondservants justly and fairly, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1, ESV). A church is an employer too. A church that preaches James 5 at the economy and pays its own custodian the least the market allows has turned the text into a weapon for use on other people.

If you're the customer, remember that a price is somebody's wage. Federal law still lets restaurants pay tipped workers a cash wage of $2.13 an hour, a figure unchanged since 1991, so long as tips bring them up to the minimum. The tip is part of someone's pay. Nobody can audit the whole economy, but a Christian can refuse the willful ignorance that enjoys the palace without asking who built it.

None of that is a program, and none of it saves anyone. When Jesus called Zacchaeus down from the tree, grace came first; he hadn't repaid anyone yet. What grace produced was restitution. "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8, ESV). Repentance about money has the shape of a refund. It doesn't bypass the ledger. It opens it and pays.

And there is one more worker in the Gospels hired at the eleventh hour. A thief was crucified beside Jesus, with nothing to offer and no time left to earn anything. "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43, ESV). He was dead before sundown, his legs broken so the bodies could come down before the Sabbath (John 19:31–32). The last man hired in the vineyard was paid the whole day's wage before dark, and it was paid for by the man on the middle cross.

That is the grace we live on, all of us who sit at these tables. It puts us back under Deuteronomy's clock, and gladly. Somewhere tonight someone is lifting up his soul to a paycheck that may not come in full, and his cry is already on its way to the Lord of hosts. The sun is going down on all three of our chairs.
