## The Question Parents Are Actually Asking

Most parents who type "when should my child get a smartphone" into a search bar already suspect the answer is later than the one they're about to give. The pressure comes from every side. The school sends homework through an app, the coach runs the team through a group chat, and the other sixth graders all seem to have one, a fact your child reports with the precision of a lawyer entering evidence. You want to protect without smothering, and you have a nagging sense that the thing you're being asked to hand over isn't quite what it appears to be. It looks like a telephone. It doesn't behave like one.

I should admit where I stand. I've lectured my sons about screens with my own phone within reach, and I'm writing this with one in my pocket. Any parent who comes to this subject as someone who has it handled hasn't looked closely at his own evenings. The device that is shaping our children is shaping us, and in most homes it got to the adults first. Whatever this essay says about children, it says about us before it says about them.

The public argument has mostly been conducted in the vocabulary of screen time: how many hours, which apps, what age. That vocabulary isn't useless, but it quietly assumes the phone is a tool, something a child picks up for a purpose and sets down when the purpose is served. The smartphone is closer to a place. It has its own weather and its own crowd, and it was built by some of the most capable engineers alive to be more compelling than whatever room your child is standing in. When we talk about limiting screen time as if we were rationing dessert, we miss what we're actually deciding, which is how many hours a day our children will live in a different world from the one we're raising them in.

The whole question turns on a distinction. There is a *content* problem with phones, and Christian parents have mostly been good at seeing it: pornography, violence, predators, cruelty. But there is also a *formation* problem, and it would remain if every harmful image on earth were filtered out. The phone doesn't only deliver things to a child. It trains the child who holds it: what he attends to, how long he can attend, what he wants, how he knows whether he matters. The first problem calls for filters. The second calls for something older and harder, which Scripture describes, strikingly, in the language of the eye.

## What Haidt Gets Right, and What His Critics Want Us to See

The most influential account of what happened to children and phones is Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024), and it deserves to be stated at its strongest before anyone argues with it. Haidt, a social psychologist at New York University, argues that between roughly 2010 and 2015 childhood was rewired. He calls it the great rewiring. A *play-based childhood*, in which children spent long unsupervised hours together in the physical world, taking small risks, settling their own disputes and learning to read faces, gave way to a *phone-based childhood*, in which social life migrated onto devices that were always present and never finished. The timing is the heart of his case. The iPhone arrived in 2007, the App Store in 2008, the "like" button and the retweet around 2009, and the front-facing camera around 2010. By the middle of the decade, Haidt argues, adolescents across the English-speaking world were living through their phones, and measures of adolescent anxiety, depression and self-harm turned sharply upward at nearly the same moment, earliest and most steeply among girls.

He names four foundational harms: social deprivation, sleep deprivation, attention fragmentation and addiction. He has a sharp phrase for the parenting mistake of the era: we overprotected children in the real world and underprotected them in the virtual one. We stopped letting a ten-year-old walk to the store alone, and we let him carry the internet to bed. His remedy is four norms: no smartphones before high school, only basic phones; no social media before sixteen; phone-free schools; and far more independence, free play and responsibility in the physical world. And because no family can hold those norms alone without isolating its child, he frames the whole thing as a collective action problem that parents have to solve together.

The case has serious critics, and a Christian who wants to tell the truth has to listen to them. The most prominent is the developmental psychologist Candice Odgers, whose review of the book in *Nature* in March 2024 argued that its central causal claim runs well ahead of the evidence. Researchers, she pointed out, herself among them, have searched for the large effects Haidt describes and found mostly small, mixed or null associations. The arrow may point the other way, since struggling teenagers may simply spend more time online. And a single compelling villain, she warned, could distract families from causes with stronger evidence behind them. The critics also have history on their side: the novel, the comic book and the television were each blamed for ruining the young, and the alarm was often louder than the harm.

That should chasten anyone who writes about this with too much certainty. The honest word for the causal question is *unsettled*, and I won't give you statistics I can't stand behind. But notice what the critics mostly dispute and what they don't. They dispute how much of the rise in adolescent distress the phone explains. They rarely dispute that children are sleeping less, that their attention is interrupted constantly, or that the platforms are designed to hold the eye as long as possible. Sean Parker, Facebook's first president, said publicly in 2017 that the product was built to consume as much of its users' time and attention as possible by exploiting a vulnerability in human psychology. You don't need settled epidemiology to take a designer at his word about what he designed.

The Christian parent doesn't need to win the argument between Haidt and Odgers in order to act. How much of a mental-health crisis one technology has caused is a question for researchers, and it may take decades to answer. What a child's attention is *for*, and who should be allowed to train it, is a question Scripture answered long before there was a study to cite. Haidt got the symptom right, and his critics are right that it has more than one cause. Neither side, on its own terms, can say why attention matters in the first place.

## Every Medium Is a Teacher

The critics are right that fear of new media is old, but the long view cuts in a direction they don't always expect. Neil Postman opened *Technopoly* (1992) with the story Plato tells in the *Phaedrus*, where the god Theuth offers King Thamus the gift of writing and the king refuses it, warning that it will give his people the appearance of wisdom without the substance and make them forgetful. Thamus was wrong that writing would do only harm. He was right that it would change what memory is. Every technology gives something and takes something, and the question is never whether a medium is good in the abstract but what it does to the people who live inside it.

Postman spent his career asking that question. In *The Disappearance of Childhood* (1982) he argued that childhood as a protected stage of life is a social arrangement, not a biological constant, and a fairly recent one. It took its modern shape after the printing press of the 1450s, because a literate culture created a world of adult knowledge that children entered gradually, as they learned to read. The book, with its difficulty, was a gate. Television, which requires no literacy, opened the gate and showed everything to everyone at once, and Postman predicted children who know too much too soon and adults who never quite grow up. The smartphone put his box in every child's pocket, took the family out of the room and added a crowd that talks back. In *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985) he contrasted the two great dystopias of the century. Orwell feared a world where truth would be forbidden; Huxley feared a world where truth would drown in distraction and no one would need to ban books because no one would want to read them. Postman thought Huxley had seen further. We would be undone not by what we hate but by what we love.

Behind Postman stood Marshall McLuhan, whose *Understanding Media* (1964) gave the century its most quoted sentence about technology: "the medium is the message." McLuhan meant that the deepest effect of any medium is not the content it carries but the change of pace, scale and pattern it introduces into human life. Content, he suggested, is like the meat a burglar throws to the watchdog while he goes about his real business. A parent who asks only "what is my child watching?" is watching the meat. The deeper question is what hours of swiping, refreshing and waiting for a response are doing to the shape of a child's mind, whatever happens to be on the screen.

Jacques Ellul pressed further. In *The Technological Society* (1954) he described *technique*, the pursuit in every field of the one most efficient method, and warned that it becomes autonomous: once efficiency is the only question anyone asks, the question of what it's *for* drops out, and people adapt themselves to their methods rather than the reverse. A platform measured by engagement is Ellul's technique in pure form. Time on the device serves nothing beyond itself; it is the end, optimized by people who are very good at their jobs, and your child's attention is the raw material.

The Christian account of formation names what these critics can see but can't quite explain. James K. A. Smith argues in *You Are What You Love* (2016) that we're shaped less by the ideas we hold than by the habits we practice, which he calls liturgies. The repeated rituals of a culture train our loves beneath the level of argument, so that we come to want what our practices taught us to want. Smith wrote about the shopping mall, but the phone is the most intensive liturgy ever devised: hundreds of repetitions a day, begun in childhood, rewarded at unpredictable intervals. The psalmist said it more bluntly of the idols: "Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (Psalm 115:8, ESV). We become what we give our attention to.

And what does the phone make? The deepest harm is not any single image but a contradiction the device teaches without a word. It tells a child he is the center of a world arranged to serve his appetite, and at the same moment that his worth is decided by a crowd that doesn't love him. He scrolls a feed built for his pleasure, then posts something and waits, heart in his throat, for the verdict of people who will forget him by morning. The child is sovereign and worthless in the same five minutes. No filter touches that, because the problem isn't in the content. It's in the form.

## The Parent Who Needs the Phone

The other side of the kitchen table deserves its strongest hearing, because many parents who hand their children smartphones at eleven aren't careless. They're afraid, and some of their fears are reasonable.

The first case is safety. A parent wants to reach her child and know where he is. Many children move between two households, and the phone is the thread that lets a mother know her son got to his father's house. Parents who remember Parkland in 2018 and Uvalde in 2022 aren't being hysterical when they want a child able to call from a locked classroom. For many families the phone isn't a toy. It's how they keep watch.

The second case is belonging, and it's harder to answer. A middle schooler's social life now happens in group chats, and the child who isn't in the chat doesn't hear about the plans or understand the jokes. A parent who delays the phone may be cutting her child off from his friends, and a lonely child isn't a safe child. For some children the case is stronger still: the kid in a rural county with no one nearby who shares his interests, the child with a disability for whom the physical world is hard to reach, the teenager for whom an online friend was a lifeline in a bad year. Odgers's worry that panic could make parents neglect these children's real needs is no academic quibble.

The third case is preparation, and it may be the strongest. A child who never touches a smartphone at home will get one the day he leaves, with no practice using it well and no parent nearby when he uses it badly. Better, this parent argues, to learn under supervision at thirteen than alone at eighteen. We don't keep children out of cars until they're twenty-five. We teach them to drive.

Each of these can be honored without conceding that the answer is a smartphone in a sixth grader's hand. Nearly everything the safety case requires can be met by a basic phone or a watch that calls and texts, without the entire internet and a global audience coming along. Haidt's distinction matters here: the case for *a* phone is not a case for a *smartphone*, and the case for a smartphone is not a case for social media. The belonging case is real, which is exactly why Haidt treats it as a collective problem and not a private one. The parent who delays alone does pay a price, and she shouldn't be told it's imaginary. The answer is to stop delaying alone, to find the three or four other families in the class who want the same thing and agree together. Churches gather families with shared convictions every week and are unusually well placed to do this, and it says something about us that so few have tried.

The preparation case wins a real concession. Total abstinence followed by sudden, unsupervised access at eighteen is a postponed crash. The better image is apprenticeship: access that grows as the child shows he can carry it, starting with the least powerful devices and the most public uses, under a parent who is actually paying attention. The driving analogy is right, and it cuts both ways. We teach teenagers to drive, but we don't hand a twelve-year-old the keys and wish him well.

## The Eye Is the Lamp

If the formation problem is real, its heart is attention, and Scripture says more about attention than the modern church usually hears.

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus says: "The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light, but if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light in you is darkness, how great is the darkness!" (Matthew 6:22–23, ESV). It's easy to fire this at a teenager as a verse about bad images, but its setting makes it deeper. It sits between a warning about treasure ("For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also," Matthew 6:21, ESV) and the claim that no one can serve two masters (Matthew 6:24). The word translated *healthy* means single or undivided, and in Jewish idiom the *bad* eye was the grasping, envious eye; Jesus uses the same idiom when the vineyard owner asks the grumbling workers whether they begrudge his generosity (Matthew 20:15). The healthy eye is undivided, fixed on one master, and free enough from comparison to be generous. Read in context, the saying is nearly a diagnosis of the feed: an eye pulled in a hundred directions an hour, trained to compare, never at rest.

Proverbs 4 makes the same point as a father's instruction to his son, and the father remembers his own father teaching him the same way (Proverbs 4:3–4). At the center of the chapter: "Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life" (Proverbs 4:23, ESV), and two verses later, "Let your eyes look directly forward, and your gaze be straight before you" (Proverbs 4:25, ESV). The heart is guarded partly through the eyes, and the teaching is handed down as a thing a parent owes a child. I grew up without a father, and a boy in that position can reach adulthood without anyone teaching him where to put his eyes. That absence isn't only a matter of fathers who leave. A father can sit at the same table looking down at a glowing screen, and his son can learn the same lesson.

Psalm 101 is a king's vow about his own household, and before it says a word about anyone else it binds the king: "I will walk with integrity of heart within my house; I will not set before my eyes anything that is worthless" (Psalm 101:2–3, ESV). David doesn't begin with rules for the servants. He begins with his own eyes. There's no Christian way to talk to children about phones that skips this step, because children read the vow in our faces long before they hear it in our words, and a rule that binds them and not us is a hypocrisy they can see straight through.

Paul, writing from prison, gives the Philippians a sentence usually read as a pleasant thought: "Finally, brothers, whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things" (Philippians 4:8, ESV). Its context is anxiety. Two verses earlier he wrote, "do not be anxious about anything" (Philippians 4:6, ESV), and his remedy is prayer followed by a deliberate direction of attention; the verb means to reckon with something, to dwell on it until it has weight. An anxious generation is, among other things, one whose attention has been taken from it, and Paul assumes attention can be trained and given to what deserves it.

No modern writer saw this more clearly than Simone Weil. In her 1942 essay "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God," she argued that the real purpose of schoolwork, even of a geometry problem a student never solves, is to train the faculty of attention, because attention is the substance of prayer. It is also the substance of love for a neighbor: the rare ability to turn toward someone who is suffering, ask what he is going through, and actually wait for the answer. If Weil is right, a technology that fractures attention, whatever conveniences it offers, is eroding the very capacity by which a person prays and loves. A child raised inside the feed may become an adult who can't do the things a deep life is made of, not because he chose against them but because the capacity was never trained.

None of this is new. Augustine, in his *Confessions* around the year 400, tells of his friend Alypius, dragged to the gladiatorial games in Rome, who shut his eyes against the spectacle, opened them at the crowd's roar, and went home in love with the blood. Later Augustine confesses that even a lizard catching flies on his wall could pull his mind from serious thought. He called it the lust of the eyes, and he would have understood the phone in a minute: a lizard on the wall that never stops catching flies.

## Sabbath and a Better Room

If the problem is formation, the answer can't be subtraction alone. The phone feeds real hungers, for connection, for novelty, for a place to be somebody, and a parent who removes it and offers nothing will be resented, fairly, for making her child's life smaller and calling it discipline. The phone has to be displaced by something with more weight.

Andy Crouch's *The Tech-Wise Family* (2017) is the most practical Christian book on this, and its central image is the hearth. Crouch argues that the center of the home should hold things that reward skill and effort, books, instruments, a table, rather than screens that reward passivity, and he proposes family commitments to protect that center: no screens before double digits; devices go to bed before the people do, outside the bedrooms; car rides kept for conversation; and one hour a day, one day a week and one week a year with the devices off. The commitments bind the whole household, adults included, and Crouch is candid that the adults usually find them hardest.

Behind that rhythm stands the oldest technology of rest the human race was given. Deuteronomy grounds the Sabbath in deliverance: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. Therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15, ESV). Walter Brueggemann argues in *Sabbath as Resistance* (2014) that the Sabbath was Israel's refusal of Pharaoh's economy, where the brick quotas never stopped and no one was ever finished. The feed is Pharaoh's economy for attention. It has no last page and no seventh day, and it never tells anyone he has done enough. Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Sabbath* (1951), called the day a palace in time, built of hours set apart rather than stone. A family that keeps a day without screens is building that palace and inviting its children to live in it, telling them without a lecture that their worth isn't measured by what they produce or how many people respond. "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27, ESV). Children will receive it as a gift only if the day is full of something better than what was taken away.

The shape of that better room isn't complicated, though it is costly. It's a table where the family eats with the devices in another room. It's real work, chores that matter and eventually a job that pays. It's friendship in the physical world, which parents now have to arrange on purpose, and the independence Haidt pleads for: the walk to the store, the afternoon with no adult hovering. It's time outdoors in amounts that feel almost unreasonable. And it's a faith practiced visibly at home. Moses told Israel's parents to talk of God's words "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:7, ESV). That describes a household whose attention is available to its children all day long. It's hard to talk by the way when everyone in the car is looking down.

Two cautions belong here. Some children are struggling with more than a phone. If your child shows signs of depression or anxiety that won't lift, talk to your pediatrician or a licensed counselor; this essay isn't medical advice. If a child speaks of wanting to die or of hurting himself, call or text the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline right away. And the parent who has already given the phone and now regrets it hasn't lost. Access can be narrowed as well as widened, and a parent who says, "I decided this too quickly, and I'm going to change it, starting with my own habits," is teaching her child something worth more than any rule.

## Jesus, Looking at Him, Loved Him

The hardest sentence in this essay isn't about children. We are the first generation of parents who can sit with our children for an entire evening and never once look at them. Sherry Turkle's *Alone Together* (2011) described a culture that expects more from its devices and less from one another, where connection offers the feeling of company without its demands, and some of the young people she interviewed spoke of competing with their parents' phones. I've done what those parents did. I've handed a child a screen to buy myself twenty minutes of quiet, and the quiet was real, and so was the cost. The phone is the path of least resistance for the tired parent as much as for the restless child. Grace covers the evenings we lose to it, but grace was never meant to be a strategy.

So the question comes back to the adults, where Psalm 101 put it from the start. What our children most need from us is our attention, the thing the feed is built to take from us before it takes it from them. The crowd behind the screen offers a child a counterfeit of being seen: the flicker of a count, the verdict of strangers. What he needs is to be looked at by someone who loves him and isn't going anywhere.

When a man ran up and knelt before Jesus to ask what he must do to inherit eternal life, Mark records a small detail before the hard answer: "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him" (Mark 10:21, ESV). The look came first. Before the command that sent the man away sorrowful, the Lord gave him the full weight of his gaze. That is the gospel, finally: a God who does not glance at us between other things, who looked at a world that had turned its eyes everywhere except toward him, and came, and kept looking all the way to a cross. Every distracted evening of ours is forgiven there. None of them is excused.

The question in the search bar was when to give the phone, and it has a real answer, which for most children is later than the culture says. But the deeper question is what our children will see when they look up from whatever they're holding. If they find us looking back, they'll learn that the gaze of the crowd isn't the gaze that decides who they are. If they find us looking down, the phone will teach them the rest. They've been watching where our eyes go for longer than they've owned a device, and whatever they've learned from it so far, the lesson is still being taught, tonight, at our own tables.
