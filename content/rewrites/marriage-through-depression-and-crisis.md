"My spouse is depressed" is usually typed late, after the house has gone quiet. The person you married went to bed at seven, or hasn't left the bedroom in days, or answers every question with one flat word. Some who type that sentence are asking how to help. Some are asking, more quietly, how much longer they can keep doing this. Most are asking both, and feel ashamed of the second question the moment it forms.

Two things need to be said first. If your husband or wife has talked about wanting to die, about being a burden, or about everyone being better off without them, take it seriously tonight. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour, whether the call is for your spouse or for you. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 and don't leave them alone; outside the US, call your local emergency number. And this is a pastor's essay, not medical advice. Depression is a medical condition as well as a spiritual burden, and the people who can name it and treat it are physicians and licensed mental health professionals. Nothing here replaces them, and nothing here should delay reaching them.

The argument that follows is simple to state. The church has often confused kinds of sorrow that its own older tradition learned to tell apart, and it has answered all of them with explanations, when Scripture's first answers are care for the body, presence for the soul, and a language of lament that lets a person speak to God from the bottom of the pit. And we have nearly forgotten the spouse who stays well, who carries the house, and who is quietly going under.

### What the illness does to a marriage

Depression isn't sadness, or not only sadness. The person you love goes flat. The things that used to reach them, a joke, a child's news, your hand on their back, don't seem to land. Sleep goes wrong. Small decisions become impossible. Sometimes it looks less like grief than like irritability, a short fuse and a closed door, and the well spouse spends months thinking the problem is the marriage. Very often there's no clear cause, which may be the cruelest part, because the well spouse keeps searching for the thing to fix and there's frequently nothing there that fixing would cure.

A marriage runs on reciprocity: two people give and receive, and the giving is renewed by what comes back. Depression suspends that exchange, sometimes for a long time, and not because your spouse has chosen to withhold anything. The illness has taken the capacity to receive as well as to give. It also lies. It tells the person inside it that they are a burden, that they have always been like this and always will be, that the love around them isn't real or isn't meant for them. None of that is true, and it feels truer than anything they have ever known. So the first work of the well spouse is to refuse the illness's account of the person you married, and to keep telling them the truth they can't feel, knowing it may not land, because someday the fog may thin and they'll need to have heard it.

The second work is where the church has sometimes done real harm. Get help, and don't let anyone shame your spouse for receiving it. What you're seeing may be depression, or grief, or another medical problem, or a medication's side effect, and sorting that out is a doctor's work, not yours and not a pastor's. Treatment may include counseling, medication, or both, as the professionals judge. The idea that a faithful Christian ought to be able to pray their way out of a major depression isn't only mistaken; it can be dangerous, because it keeps sick people from care. We don't tell a diabetic that insulin shows a lack of faith. The brain is an organ of the body God made, and sometimes it gets sick. Treating it is stewardship, not unbelief. The spouse who gently insists on treatment, makes the appointment, and sits in the waiting room is doing one of the most loving things a marriage allows. Pray, by all means, for the doctor and the patience to wait. Prayer and medicine have never been rivals, and those of us who taught otherwise owe the sick an apology.

### Three sorrows the church learned to tell apart

In the late fourth century Evagrius of Pontus, a monk in the Egyptian desert, described in his *Praktikos* the thoughts that assailed the men who had gone there to pray. Two of his eight matter here. One is *lypē*, sadness, which he ties to loss and frustrated desire. The other is *akēdia*, acedia, which he calls the noonday demon after the Greek text of Psalm 91:6. Acedia makes the day feel endless, sends the monk to the window again and again, fills him with contempt for his cell and his brothers, and persuades him that he'd serve God better and be happier somewhere else. Its remedy is to stay, to keep to the cell and the work and the prayers until the temptation passes. By the time Gregory the Great set out his list of capital sins in the late sixth century, acedia had been folded into sadness, and later medieval teaching slowly flattened it into sloth.

That flattening did damage. Once the heaviness of the soul was filed under a sin, it became easy to treat every dark mood as a failure of will, and the melancholy person was told, in effect, to repent of being sick. But the tradition also produced its own correction. In 1621 an Oxford clergyman named Robert Burton published *The Anatomy of Melancholy* under the pen name Democritus Junior, a sprawling survey of everything then known about the dark mood, written by a man who suffered it. Burton treated melancholy as a disorder of body and soul together, defended a minister's writing on what was then a medical subject, and argued that physician and divine should work side by side rather than guard their territory. He closed the whole enormous book by counseling his reader not to be solitary and not to be idle. That's still decent advice for a household in the dark.

The nineteenth century's most famous preacher knew the same darkness. Charles Haddon Spurgeon was twenty-two when, on 19 October 1856, someone shouted "Fire" during his service at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall in London; seven people died in the panic, and Spurgeon was shaken for weeks. The darkness recurred through his life. In "The Minister's Fainting Fits," a lecture collected in *Lectures to My Students* (1875), he told young preachers that seasons of deep depression come over most of us, that the body has its part in them, and that they are no proof God has abandoned a man. His marriage carried sickness on both sides: Susannah Spurgeon was largely confined to home by illness for many years, and Charles's own ailments and depressions sent him to Menton, in the south of France, to recover. The greatest Baptist preacher of his century didn't pray his way out of his body. He preached, and rested, and grieved, and went south, and came back.

A secular witness belongs here too. The novelist William Styron fell into a severe depression in 1985 and wrote about it in *Darkness Visible* (1990), taking his title from Milton. He complained that "depression" is far too mild a word for what it names, a small clinical term for something he lived as a storm in the mind. He insisted that the sufferer must be told again and again, against his own conviction, that the illness will pass. He closes with Dante's line about climbing out of hell to see the stars again. Styron got the symptom right and told it more honestly than many sermons have. What his account can't supply is a reason the sufferer is worth more than his usefulness, or a hope that reaches past recovery. Andrew Solomon's *The Noonday Demon* (2001) went back to the desert monks for its title, as if the modern account of the illness needed the old word to find language equal to it.

So here is the distinction the church at its best has drawn, and that Kathleen Norris sharpened in *Acedia & Me* (2008) when she insisted that acedia and clinical depression are not the same thing. Grief is sorrow with an object; it mourns a real loss and is comforted, slowly, by truth and company. Acedia is a temptation of the will, a weariness with the good one has been given and a restless conviction that life lies elsewhere; it is resisted by staying. Depression is an illness of body and mind that may have no object at all, and it is treated by medicine alongside the care of the soul. The pious error has been to treat all three as the second, a sin to be repented of. The therapeutic error, which is ours as surely as the pious one was our grandparents', is to treat all three as the third, so that nothing is ever a temptation and nothing is ever asked of anyone. Both are ways of not looking closely at the person in front of us.

There's an uncomfortable turn in that old distinction. In a marriage darkened by depression, the one most exposed to acedia may not be the sick spouse at all. It may be the well one, who is not ill but exhausted, who looks at the house and the silent person in it and begins to feel, with a force that seems like insight, that life must be somewhere else. That feeling isn't a sin to be ashamed of. It's a temptation to be recognized, and we'll return to it.

### What Job's friends got right for a week

The Bible's longest meditation on suffering contains both the best and the worst pastoral care ever recorded, and they come from the same three men.

When Job has lost his children, his wealth and his health, his friends come. "They made an appointment together to come to show him sympathy and comfort him. And when they saw him from a distance, they did not recognize him. And they raised their voices and wept, and they tore their robes and sprinkled dust on their heads toward heaven. And they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great" (Job 2:11–13, ESV). Every spouse of a depressed person knows the phrase in the middle: they did not recognize him. And for seven days the friends did exactly the right thing. They came, they grieved, and they said nothing.

Then Job spoke, and for thirty-odd chapters they explained. Much of their theology was true in the abstract. But they applied it to a man in anguish in order to protect their picture of how the world works, and Job named them: "miserable comforters are you all" (Job 16:2, ESV). At the end God gives his own verdict to Eliphaz: "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7, ESV). The man who argued with God and demanded a hearing spoke rightly. The men who defended God with explanations were rebuked.

That's the pattern of bad comfort, and we have practiced it often. We reach for a verse because silence beside suffering is hard to bear, and the explanation relieves our anxiety more than theirs. The depressed spouse hears it as an accusation, because that's usually what it is: if you trusted more, you wouldn't be like this. The seven silent days are the better model. The first gift a well spouse can give isn't an answer. It's staying in the room.

And Scripture gives the one in the dark a language the bad comforters never taught. Walter Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms* (1984), sorted the Psalter into psalms of orientation, which celebrate a world in order; psalms of disorientation, which cry out when that world has fallen apart; and psalms of new orientation, which sing of a surprising restoration. A church that sings only the first kind, he argued, is practicing a form of denial. Lament isn't a lapse of faith. It's faith telling the truth.

Psalms 42 and 43, probably once a single poem, show the pattern. "Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls; all your breakers and your waves have gone over me" (Psalm 42:7, ESV). Three times comes the refrain: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God" (Psalm 42:11, ESV). The psalmist is preaching to his own soul, refusing to let the downcast self have the last word. There are seasons when a depressed person can't say that refrain. There are seasons when a husband or wife says it for them, quietly, on their behalf, which is a form of intercession older than the church.

Psalm 88 goes further, and its willingness to go further is a mercy. It's the one psalm that ends in darkness. It opens with the only confession of faith it will make, "O LORD, God of my salvation, I cry out day and night before you" (Psalm 88:1, ESV), and then it descends: "O LORD, why do you cast my soul away? Why do you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 88:14, ESV). It closes with no turn and no dawn: "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18, ESV). God put that prayer in the canon. Scripture makes room for a believer whose last word, for now, is darkness, and who is still addressing it to the God of their salvation. The address is the faith. A person who can only pray Psalm 88 is still praying.

Kathryn Greene-McCreight, an Episcopal priest and theologian, wrote of her own severe mood disorder in *Darkness Is My Only Companion* (2006), a title that echoes that psalm's last line. She describes seasons when she couldn't pray and had to be carried by the prayers of others, and she receives psychiatric treatment as a gift within the Christian life rather than a betrayal of it.

### Sleep and bread before theology

If Job gives the pattern of bad comfort, Elijah gives the pattern of God's care, and the order of that care is the lesson.

Elijah had just seen fire fall on Mount Carmel when Jezebel threatened his life, and he ran. At Beersheba he "left his servant there" (1 Kings 19:3, ESV), which is to say he made himself alone. He went on into the wilderness and "came and sat down under a broom tree. And he asked that he might die, saying, 'It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers'" (1 Kings 19:4, ESV).

God didn't begin by correcting his theology. He let him sleep. Then an angel touched him and said, "Arise and eat" (1 Kings 19:5, ESV), and there was bread baked on hot stones and a jar of water. Elijah ate and lay down again. The angel came a second time, touched him again, and told him to eat because the road ahead was too much for him. Only after the second meal did Elijah go on to Horeb, where God asked, "What are you doing here, Elijah?" (1 Kings 19:9, ESV), and came to him not in wind or earthquake or fire but in "the sound of a low whisper" (1 Kings 19:12, ESV). Only then did God correct Elijah's belief that he alone was left, telling him of seven thousand in Israel who had not bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18). And God gave him a companion, Elisha, so he would not go on alone.

Sleep and bread, twice; then the conversation, the correction and the friend. God met Elijah's wish to die with care rather than argument, and the first care was for his body. In an ordinary house that order looks unglamorous. It means keeping meals coming even when they go half eaten, protecting sleep, making room for a short walk. It means helping with appointments without taking over a grown adult's life. It means postponing large decisions about jobs, houses, or the marriage itself until the sick spouse is well enough to make them, and lowering the bar for what the household has to accomplish this month. And it means watching. Warning signs of suicide can include talking about wanting to die or about being a burden, pulling away from everyone, giving things away or putting affairs in order, sharp changes in drinking or drug use, and sometimes a sudden, unexplained calm after a long darkness. If you see signs like these, don't wait to be sure. Call or text 988 in the US for guidance, tell your spouse's doctor, and if there's immediate danger, call 911. Mainstream suicide-prevention guidance, including the National Institute of Mental Health's, holds that asking someone directly whether they're thinking about suicide doesn't put the idea in their head, and it may be the question they needed someone to ask.

Now turn the story toward the one doing all this. Elijah's first mistake was leaving his servant behind, and the well spouse is in real danger of doing the same. Caring for a depressed husband or wife is among the heaviest loads a person carries, and it's often carried in secret, out of loyalty and embarrassment and a wish to protect the sick spouse's dignity. Your suffering is real too, even though your spouse is the one who is sick. Getting your own support isn't betrayal. It means a counselor of your own, a doctor if your own sleep and health are failing, one or two friends who know the truth, and a church that can bring meals and pray without gossiping. Organizations such as the National Alliance on Mental Illness offer education and support specifically for family members. Burton's last counsel is yours as much as the sufferer's: don't be solitary. And the angel's instruction to eat and sleep is yours as well.

### The case for leaving, stated fairly

Some readers have waited through all of this to see whether the essay will be honest about them. Their case isn't weak, and it goes something like this. I didn't sign up for this. The person I married is gone, and it's been years, not months. I've given everything and nothing comes back. I'm losing my own health. The children are growing up with one parent absent in the next room and the other running on fumes, and they're learning that this is what marriage is. My spouse has refused treatment, or tried it halfway and quit, and I can't make a grown adult get well. The church's old insistence on staying no matter what trapped people, often women, in lives that crushed them. Surely God doesn't require me to disappear into someone else's illness for the rest of my life.

Much of that is true, and one part of it must be said without hedging. Illness doesn't excuse abuse, and no reading of the marriage vow requires anyone to endure it. If depression or any other crisis has come with violence, threats, or danger to you or your children, the first obligation is safety. In the US the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. Separating for safety isn't abandoning the covenant; it may be the only way to protect the people the covenant was meant to protect.

But set danger aside and take the far more common case, where no one is being harmed and one spouse is sick and the other is exhausted. Here the vow has something to say, and its history matters. When Thomas Cranmer's *Book of Common Prayer* gave English speakers their wedding service in 1549, the couple promised to have and to hold one another "from this day forward, for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death us depart." Every pair in that sentence imagines the worse half arriving. No one promises "in sickness and in health" on the assumption that sickness won't come. The vow wasn't written for the wedding. It was written for this.

Depression and crisis are seasons far more often than they are life sentences, and treatment helps many people. The spouse who leaves in the depth of the dark often leaves before a dawn they couldn't see coming, and carries the leaving for a long time. Leaving isn't always sin; there are situations, untreated and dangerous and abusive, where staying isn't faithfulness but destruction, and no one is required to be destroyed. But illness in itself is what the vow was made to hold. Before you decide the person you married is gone, make sure you've fought for them the way you'd want to be fought for if you were the one in the dark. Someday you may be.

The strongest objection this essay can't fully answer is the spouse who refuses all treatment for years, who won't see a doctor or talk to anyone, and leaves the other to carry a household alone with no end in sight. The vow binds you to the person, not to the demands of their illness, and love may require steps they won't welcome, such as involving their family and their doctor. Some pastors and counselors would go further, to insisting on treatment as a condition of the marriage's ordinary life, or even to a season of separation aimed at healing rather than ending; faithful counselors differ on that. Where it leads is for a wise pastor and a good counselor to help you discern, not a decision for midnight. On the grounds for divorce itself, faithful churches differ; it's a second-order question on which Christians can disagree and remain brothers and sisters. What this essay won't do is tell an exhausted person that their exhaustion is sin, or that the vow expires when it becomes costly.

Here the word from the desert returns, gently. Some of what the exhausted spouse feels is plain depletion, cured by rest and help. Some may be the noonday demon, the restless certainty that peace lies anywhere but here. At two in the morning you can't always tell which, which is why the decision shouldn't be made at two in the morning, or alone.

### A promise written for the dark

The vow is where a marriage stops running on feeling and starts running on structure, and in a season like this the structure is the whole point. When one of the two people holding a marriage up can't feel anything at all, a marriage built only on feeling has nothing left to stand on. The promise carries the weight the emotions used to carry. One of the few mercies hidden in a dark season is finding out that it can, that the vow was made of something stronger than your mood or theirs.

There's a harder grace underneath. To keep loving a person who can't love you back for a long time is about as close as most of us come to the way God loves us. "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). He loved us when we had nothing to give back and couldn't even reach for him. The well spouse in a dark season is being shaped, often against their wishes, into that kind of love, and what comes out the other side isn't the eager love of the wedding day. It's quieter and more costly, a love tested in an emptied room that did not leave.

That grace is no argument against getting help, or for pretending it's easy. On the night before he died, Jesus took his three closest friends into Gethsemane and told them, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me" (Matthew 26:38, ESV). The Son of God asked for company in his anguish, and his friends fell asleep. There's no human darkness he hasn't entered from the inside and no failure of the watchers he hasn't known. "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15, ESV).

So to the one in the dark, if you can read this at all: you are not a burden, whatever the illness is telling you. Your worth doesn't depend on your usefulness, and the love around you isn't a debt you've failed to pay. Stay. Get the help. If you're thinking about ending your life, call or text 988 now, in the US, or tell someone in the house tonight. Let the people who love you carry you through the part where you can't carry yourself; that's what the carrying is for. If the fog thins, and for many people it does, you may find that what was given to you in the dark was real the whole time, and that the marriage held while you couldn't. That won't be your failure. It will be the covenant doing what it was made to do.

And to the one keeping watch. Psalm 30 says that "weeping may tarry for the night, but joy comes with the morning" (Psalm 30:5, ESV). It doesn't say how long the night is, and some nights in a marriage last years. It promises a morning, not a short night. The Gospels add a detail worth carrying out of this room: on the first Easter, "Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early, while it was still dark" (John 20:1, ESV). The resurrection was first discovered by a grieving woman who went out before there was light to see by, expecting nothing but a body to mourn. She kept faith in the dark with a love that had nowhere left to go, and the dark was where she learned it hadn't been wasted.

No one can tell you when your spouse will be well, or promise you that the psalm your house is praying this year will be 30 and not 88. What can be said is that you aren't keeping this watch alone, that you were never meant to, and that the God to whom Psalm 88 was addressed is still the God of your salvation, and of theirs, in whatever part of the night you are living through now.
