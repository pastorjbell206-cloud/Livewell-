It is easy to read the history of biblical interpretation from the balcony, where the errors look obvious. The slaveholder quoting Noah, the cardinals ruling on whether the earth moves, the Baptist farmer in upstate New York who worked out from the book of Daniel that Christ would return around 1843: we look down on them with a mixture of pity and relief, and the relief gives us away. Without quite saying so, we assume the parade of confident misreadings ended around our grandparents' time, and that we are the first generation to read the Bible with clean eyes.

Jesus met that assumption in the temple courts, and he did not treat it gently. "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you build the tombs of the prophets and decorate the monuments of the righteous," he said (Matthew 23:29), "saying, 'If we had lived in the days of our fathers, we would not have taken part with them in shedding the blood of the prophets'" (Matthew 23:30). The men he addressed knew their Scriptures better than almost anyone alive, and they were sure that honoring the murdered prophets proved they had outgrown their ancestors' blindness. Jesus read the monument building the other way: "Thus you witness against yourselves that you are sons of those who murdered the prophets" (Matthew 23:31). The confidence that they would have done better was itself the family resemblance. Within days the temple's leaders would hand their Messiah to the Romans, having read their Bible toward a different kind of king, and his own disciples, reading the same Bible, would scatter.

That sentence, "If we had lived in the days of our fathers," is the one every generation of Christians says, and it is the one that ought to frighten us. American evangelicals are certain, and so am I. I also know that somewhere inside that certainty is a reading Christians a century from now will find as strange as I find the antebellum sermons, and I can't tell you which one it is. If I could see it, it would not be a blind spot.

Every generation of the church has misread the Bible in ways it could not see from within, and the misreadings were rarely the work of cynics. They were the sincere, consensus readings of intelligent believers whose interests, fears and common sense had quietly decided what the text must mean before the reading began. We are not the exception. But a distinction keeps this from sliding into something worse: humility about our readings is not skepticism about the text. The first is one of the oldest Christian postures there is; the second is its counterfeit. Humility says I may be wrong about what this passage means. Skepticism says there is nothing in the passage to be right about. The historical record supports the first and refutes the second, because you cannot call a reading a mistake unless the text meant something else.

## The misreadings we can see now

Start with the misreading nearly everyone now recognizes. After the flood, Noah wakes from drunkenness, learns what his son Ham has done, and pronounces a curse: "Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants shall he be to his brothers" (Genesis 9:25). For generations of American slaveholders and their preachers, that verse was a charter. It was read as God's sentence on Africa, fixed in the blood and marked on the skin. The essay on [how to read the Bible in context](/writing/how-to-read-the-bible-without-making-it-say-what-you-want) shows how that reading collapses under ordinary attention to the passage, beginning with the fact that the curse names Canaan, not Ham, and says nothing of Africa or of skin.

David Goldenberg showed in *The Curse of Ham* (2003) that the link between Noah's curse and dark skin is nowhere in Genesis. It was supplied by later interpreters, many centuries after the text, and then handed down until it felt like part of the text itself. Stephen Haynes traced in *Noah's Curse* (2002) how the reading became a pillar of American proslavery argument, repeated by men who were educated, pious and entirely sure they were submitting to Scripture. They were not stupid, and most were not lying. The reading served their economy and their place in the social order so completely that they could not see the service. A misreading rarely feels like a choice. It feels like the plain sense.

Notice, too, how the error was exposed. It was refuted not by abandoning Genesis but by reading it more carefully: by noticing whose name the curse actually names, by reading the table of nations in the next chapter, by hearing the whole canon's witness that every human being bears the image of God. The text corrected its readers, a detail that will matter when we come to the opposite error.

The second misreading needs to be told accurately, because the popular version is a cartoon. Galileo was not tortured or thrown into a dungeon, and the affair was no clean contest between faith and reason. The essay on [faith and science](/writing/faith-and-science) gives the fuller history; what belongs here is the failure of reading at its center. In an April 1615 letter to the Carmelite Paolo Antonio Foscarini, Cardinal Robert Bellarmine granted that a true demonstration of the earth's motion would oblige the church to explain the contrary-seeming passages with great care and to say it had misunderstood them rather than call the demonstration false. He did not believe such a demonstration existed, and until one appeared he held that the fathers' reading must stand. The principle was right, and it came from the most formidable theologian in Rome. Yet in 1616 the sun-centered cosmos was pronounced contrary to Scripture, and in 1633 Galileo was made to abjure and was confined to house arrest for the rest of his life. Pope John Paul II acknowledged the error in 1992.

What went wrong was not that churchmen believed the Bible but that they could no longer tell the Bible apart from a picture of the heavens that the best learning of two thousand years had taught them. Aristotle and Ptolemy had so thoroughly furnished the educated mind that the psalmist's unmoving earth looked like astronomy rather than praise. The misreading was not eccentric. It was the consensus, and that is exactly why it was invisible. Protestants have no room to feel superior. We keep consensus pictures of our own, and they feel to us exactly as Ptolemy's heavens felt to Rome, like the plain sense of the page.

## When a nation read itself into the text

The third misreading is harder for Americans to see, because it is ours and it was hopeful. In 1742, in *Some Thoughts Concerning the Present Revival of Religion in New England*, Jonathan Edwards speculated that the great outpouring of the latter days, the glory the prophets foretold, would probably begin in America. Edwards was no crank; his hope grew from real revival and a serious reading of the prophets. But he had given American Protestants a thought they would never quite put down: that the kingdom of God and the career of their own land might be moving in step.

Ernest Lee Tuveson followed that thought through the next century in *Redeemer Nation* (1968). Postmillennial hope, the expectation that the gospel would gradually win the world and usher in a thousand years of peace before Christ's return, became in American hands a story about America. The republic was the instrument, and its spread was the kingdom's spread. The hope did real good. It powered missions, schools, temperance and, in the North, abolition. But it also made it very hard for a nation to hear the prophets as spoken to itself. In 1845 an essay in the *Democratic Review* arguing for the annexation of Texas, usually credited to its editor John L. O'Sullivan, gave the idea its lasting name when it spoke of America's manifest destiny to spread across the continent Providence had allotted it. The essay wasn't theology, but it drew on a theology already in the air, and the continent it meant was not empty. The essay on [the Doctrine of Discovery](/writing/doctrine-of-discovery) tells what that reading of Providence cost the nations already living there.

Then the war came, and the same Bible was carried into both camps. In February 1862 the *Atlantic Monthly* printed Julia Ward Howe's hymn, whose first line, "Mine eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord," cast the Union armies as the instrument of the Lord who treads the winepress of Isaiah 63 and Revelation 14. Mark Noll's *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis* (2006) shows a Protestant nation that trusted any honest reader, bringing common sense to the plain words of Scripture, to reach the same conclusions, and then found that honest readers had not. The quarrel over slavery exposed a method with no way to settle a quarrel between sincere readers of the same book. Noll observes, with a historian's dry grief, that in the end the meaning of the Bible on slavery was settled less by theologians than by Grant and Sherman.

Abraham Lincoln saw it more clearly than most of the clergy. In his Second Inaugural Address, on March 4, 1865, he said of North and South, "Both read the same Bible and pray to the same God, and each invokes His aid against the other." He did not pretend the causes were morally equal; he called it strange that anyone would ask God's help in wringing bread from other men's sweat. But he refused the easier comfort of declaring his own side God's instrument. "The prayers of both could not be answered; that of neither has been answered fully. The Almighty has His own purposes." That is a wartime president declining to make the Bible his side's weapon, and it rebukes more sermons than we would like to count.

Here the history turns toward us. Most of us read it as heirs of the side that won, tempted to conclude that the abolitionists read correctly and we are their children, so we must read correctly too. But the same generation that read the whole canon against slavery read manifest destiny out of Providence with hardly a pause. The Christian reader in 1850 could be right about one thing and blind about another at the same time, and he could not tell which was which from inside. Neither can we.

## Dates, dollars and the plain sense

William Miller deserves a fairer hearing than he usually gets. A farmer and War of 1812 veteran from Low Hampton, New York, he was a deist who came to faith in 1816 and then worked through the Bible with a concordance, determined to let it explain itself. From the 2,300 evenings and mornings of Daniel 8:14, read by the day-for-a-year principle that most Protestant interpreters of prophecy then accepted, he concluded that Christ would return around 1843. He was slow to preach it and reluctant to name a day. Once he began lecturing in the 1830s and the Boston minister Joshua Himes took up the cause, the message spread through ordinary Baptist, Methodist and Congregational churches. The historians in *The Disappointed* (1987), edited by Ronald Numbers and Jonathan Butler, treat the Millerites not as a lunatic fringe but as ordinary American Protestants who took a common method to its conclusion.

The date moved. It was finally fixed, by others in the movement, at October 22, 1844, and Miller accepted it only in the last weeks. The day passed. Many believers who had settled their accounts and said their goodbyes went home to what they called the Great Disappointment. Miller himself confessed plainly that he had been wrong about the time, and he died in 1849 still hoping for the Lord's coming and no longer claiming to know its hour. Some of his followers kept the date and reinterpreted what had happened on it, and new movements grew from that decision.

What makes Miller instructive is that his sincerity and his method were never the problem. The problem was that a careful reader, using a respected method, reached a conclusion that Jesus had forbidden in advance: "But concerning that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father only" (Matthew 24:36). Before the ascension Jesus said it again: "It is not for you to know times or seasons that the Father has fixed by his own authority" (Acts 1:7). The plainest words in the canon on the subject were overruled by a calculation, because the calculation felt like faithfulness. The pattern did not end in 1844. Hal Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth* (1970) taught millions to read the evening news as prophecy fulfilled, and Harold Camping announced the end of the world for May 21, 2011. Each generation of date setters had watched the last one fail and was sure it was different.

The prosperity reading follows the same logic at a different address. Deuteronomy 28 lists the blessings and curses of the covenant, addressed to Israel as a people in the land God gave them, and the prosperity teacher reads them as private contracts between God and the individual giver. The essay on [the prosperity gospel](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel) makes the full case; what it shares with the date setters is a hermeneutic of control: the Bible read as a mechanism that, rightly operated, yields a guaranteed result, whether a date or a deposit. Both flatter the reader's desire to secure what God has kept in his own hand, and both do it with a Bible open on the table.

It would be comfortable to leave these errors to televangelists and prophecy charts. But most of us want a Bible that gives us a hold on the future, or at least on our anxiety about it, and we read toward that want without noticing. Miller wanted Christ's appearing, which is a holy desire, and it still bent his reading. The question is not whether we have desires when we open the book. It is whether we know what they are.

## Why no generation can see its own lens

Why can we see Miller's error and not our own? The German philosopher Hans-Georg Gadamer gave the most searching answer in *Truth and Method* (1960). The Enlightenment, he argued, carried a prejudice of its own, a prejudice against prejudice itself. It imagined an ideal reader who comes to a text from nowhere, every inherited judgment scrubbed off. Gadamer thought that ideal impossible and confused. Every reader stands inside what he called a horizon, the range of vision available from a particular place in history, formed by language, tradition and the long afterlife of the very texts we are reading. Our pre-judgments are not simply obstacles to understanding; they are its conditions, since without some prior sense of what a text is about we could not begin to read it.

That does not make every pre-judgment innocent. Gadamer's point was that some of our prejudices open the text and some close it, and that the reader usually cannot tell which is which by introspection, because they all feel like common sense. Understanding happens, he argued, when the reader's horizon meets the horizon of the text and is enlarged by it, what he called a fusion of horizons. The reader does not leave his place behind; he lets the text question it. And Gadamer made an observation that explains the balcony view with uncomfortable precision. Distance in time, he argued, is not only an obstacle to understanding but a help, because time sifts out the prejudices that belonged merely to a moment. The misreadings of 1844 have had nearly two centuries to separate from the text. Ours have had none.

C. S. Lewis reached the same place by a more personal road. In *Surprised by Joy* (1955) he confessed that before his conversion he had been guilty of what he called chronological snobbery, the uncritical assumption that the thinking of one's own age is correct and that whatever has gone out of date has been refuted for that reason alone. His friend Owen Barfield broke the habit by asking whether an older view had ever actually been answered or had only stopped being fashionable. Lewis's companion argument, his 1944 introduction to Athanasius's *On the Incarnation*, is told in the essay on [the church fathers](/writing/church-fathers-not-boring). Its thesis belongs here in one sentence: every age sees some truths especially well and is blind to its own characteristic mistakes, and the old books help us precisely because their authors made different mistakes from ours.

Scripture had named the mechanism long before either of them. "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). The prophet is describing not a failure of information but a heart that deceives its owner, the only kind of deception that passes for sincerity. The psalmist, having praised the law as perfect and sweeter than honey, turns at once to pray about the reader rather than the text: "Who can discern his errors? Declare me innocent from hidden faults" (Psalm 19:12). The faults are hidden from the one who has them, and a church that believes in sin has less excuse than anyone for believing its own readings exempt.

So the source of misreading is not mainly a lack of scholarship. The slaveholding South had learned divines. Rome had Bellarmine. Miller had a concordance and years of study. The failure lay deeper, in what Jesus said to the scholars of his own day: "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me" (John 5:39). He went on: "yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (John 5:40). They were not ignorant of the text. They had mastered it so thoroughly that they had come to own it, and a text you own can no longer surprise you. When the one it pointed toward arrived, he looked like a threat to their reading of it.

## Our blind spots, right and left

If the pattern holds, our misreadings will be the ones that feel most like common sense to our own tribe. The Christian nationalist reads Israel's covenant as though America were its party, and hears the command "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities" (Romans 13:1) most clearly when his side holds office. He tends to read quickly past a law Israel was given for exactly the people his politics finds most inconvenient: "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 19:34). The progressive Christian has made "Judge not, that you be not judged" (Matthew 7:1) nearly the whole of Jesus' ethics, though the paragraph it opens ends with a command to judge rightly once the log is gone: "You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye" (Matthew 7:5). He often treats the Bible's justice texts as timeless and its sexual ethic as culturally bound, and he rarely asks who authorized the sorting. Each keeps the passages that indict the other tribe and softens the ones that indict his own, the oldest habit there is of hearing the prophet's accusation as aimed at the neighbor.

Richard Hays wrote *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* (1996) as a discipline against that habit. He asked readers to hear each New Testament witness in its own voice before synthesizing, proposed three focal images to hold its ethics together, community, cross and new creation, and then tested the method on hard cases. The results pleased neither camp. He concluded that the New Testament calls the church to renounce violence, which unsettles much of the American right, and he held the church's historic sexual ethic, which unsettles much of the left. One more fact belongs in the record. In *The Widening of God's Mercy* (2024), written with his son Christopher, Hays revised his earlier conclusion on same-sex relationships. The change is not cited here as evidence for either side of that question, which is argued on its own terms in [the essay on the Christian sexual ethic](/writing/christian-sexual-ethic). It illustrates the difficulty this essay is about, and the difficulty cuts both ways: of the 1996 conclusion and the 2024 one alike, a reader can ask whether the text or the moment did the persuading, and from inside a generation the two feel identical.

Philip Jenkins supplied a check from outside the West in *The New Faces of Christianity* (2006). Christianity's center has moved to Africa, Asia and Latin America, and Jenkins showed that believers there often read the Bible with an immediacy Western readers have lost, because its poverty, persecution, spirits and exile describe their lives. Their readings tend to be more radical than the American right on wealth and power, and more traditional than the American left on sexuality and the supernatural. Neither Western camp can claim them. That is exactly why we need them.

## If everyone misreads, does every reading count?

The opposite error deserves its strongest statement. If every generation has misread the Bible, the argument runs, then no reading has authority, and the honest course is to admit that the text means what each community makes of it. Some who say this are skeptics; others are Christians who watched the church defend slavery with chapter and verse. The conservative answer is usually fear: a text that can mean anything can command nothing, and a church that cannot say a reading is wrong cannot tell the slaveholder he was wrong either. Both are right about something. The skeptic is right that confidence has been catastrophically misplaced. The conservative is right that relativism destroys the one thing that ever corrected the misreadings.

For that is what the record actually shows. The curse of Ham was not refuted by deciding Genesis meant nothing; it was refuted by reading Genesis more carefully. The date setters were not answered by a new theory of meaning but by Matthew 24:36. Every correction in this essay came from the text pressing back against its readers. Gadamer, for all his attention to the reader's horizon, was no relativist here: he insisted that a text makes a claim on us, and that understanding means letting that claim put our own assumptions in question. A reading that can never be wrong is a reading that can never be corrected, and a Bible that cannot correct us has stopped being Scripture and become a mirror.

So the remedy is not less confidence in the text but more company in reading it. Alan Jacobs makes the case for company across time in *Breaking Bread with the Dead* (2020). Borrowing a phrase from Thomas Pynchon, he urges readers to increase their temporal bandwidth, to live with a longer stretch of past and future in view, and he argues that reading the dead, flaws and all, gives us what he calls personal density, a self less easily blown about by the present moment. The dead are neither our masters nor our inferiors. They are people to argue with, and they disagree with us in exactly the places we cannot see.

The church has read this way at its best. Around 180, Irenaeus of Lyons compared the Gnostic teachers to someone who takes a mosaic of a king, pries loose the jewels and rearranges them into a dog or a fox, then insists it is the same work because the stones are real. The church's answer was the rule of faith, the shape of the whole story later confessed in the creeds (told more fully in [the faith once delivered](/writing/the-faith-once-delivered)), which lets the reader see when the stones have been rearranged. The creeds do not replace Scripture. They are the church's long memory of how the pieces fit.

And we read with those unlike us. Peter, an apostle who had walked with Jesus, needed a vision and a Gentile household before he could say, "Truly I understand that God shows no partiality" (Acts 10:34). Years later he still needed Paul, who reports, "But when Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned" (Galatians 2:11). If Peter needed correcting by a reader from outside the Twelve, so do we. In practice that means old books between the new ones, the creeds as boundary, the global church as conversation partner, the enslaved hearers who understood Exodus better than their masters, and one question asked of every passage that troubles us: what would I have to give up if this means what it appears to mean? If the answer is something I am not willing to give up, I have found my lens.

## The tombs we are building

The worst misreading in the Bible's own story happened at its center. The people who knew the Scriptures best, his own disciples among them, read them toward a king who would not suffer, and when he came they could not see him. Paul says it without softening: "None of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (1 Corinthians 2:8). Yet from the cross came mercy for exactly those readers: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). Weeks later Peter, who had himself denied Jesus three times, stood in the temple, charged the crowd with denying the Holy and Righteous One, and then said, "And now, brothers, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers" (Acts 3:17), and then called them to repent. The ignorance was real, and it did not excuse them. It made forgiveness possible, and it made repentance necessary.

We are building our own monuments now, in the essays and sermons that shake their heads over 1844 and 1861, and every one of them risks saying what the scribes said: had we lived then, we would not have joined in. Someone will read our sermons the way we read the antebellum pulpit. They will find what we preached with confidence and did not see, and wonder how people who loved the Bible so much missed what was on the page. We could not explain it if we were there.

You are not the exception. Neither am I. What remains is to open the book tomorrow as a reader who expects to be corrected, in company with the dead and the distant and the people we would rather not read beside, praying the psalmist's prayer about faults we cannot see. The misreading we most need to repent of is the one we cannot yet name. We will carry it until the day we see face to face, and we can only ask to be forgiven for it before we know what it is.
