Ask a room of American Protestants to trace their faith backward and most will make a jump without noticing it, from the apostles in Acts to Luther in 1517. The centuries in between feel like somebody else's property, a Catholic attic full of relics or a lecture hall where bearded men argued over Greek words. The skeptic tells the same story from the other side. Christianity, he has heard, was more or less invented by Constantine, when an emperor needed a religion to hold his empire together and the bishops obliged.

Both versions skip the same people. The church fathers are the pastors, bishops, teachers and martyrs of the centuries after the apostles, and they were neither marble saints nor imperial functionaries. Ignatius wrote his letters under military guard on his way to die. Perpetua kept a prison diary while nursing her son. John Chrysostom died on a forced march into exile because he wouldn't stop preaching against the rich. We made them boring by reducing them to footnotes, and then we stopped reading them.

This essay turns on a distinction the word "father" already carries. A father is not an oracle. You don't consult him to settle every question, and you aren't bound to agree with him about everything. But you received your name from him, and you can't understand yourself without knowing him, including the places where he failed. The fathers are witnesses, not judges; Scripture judges them and us. Held that way, they are the family we were given, and a family you have never met still shapes you.

## Who counts as a church father

The title comes from the New Testament. Writing to a quarrelsome church he had planted, Paul said, "For though you have countless guides in Christ, you do not have many fathers. For I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (1 Corinthians 4:15, ESV). A father, in that sense, is someone who has begotten others in the faith by handing it on, and by the fourth century the bishops who had defended the faith at the great councils were simply called "the fathers."

The strongest objection comes from Jesus: "And call no man your father on earth, for you have one Father, who is in heaven" (Matthew 23:9, ESV). In context the saying is aimed at scribes and Pharisees who loved titles and the best seats while binding burdens on others. Jesus forbids a spiritual caste that puts a human teacher where only God belongs. Paul could still call himself a father because he meant something else, not a rank that commands obedience but a relationship of begetting and care. The objection stands as a permanent warning whenever a father is quoted to end an argument Scripture has left open. For a reader raised without a father, the word isn't neutral. It names a gift some people learn to value by its absence.

Catholic scholarship eventually settled on four marks, which Johannes Quasten sets out at the start of his *Patrology* (1950): orthodoxy of doctrine, holiness of life, ecclesiastical approval and antiquity. The marks explain some surprising omissions. Tertullian of Carthage, who gave Latin theology the word *trinitas* early in the third century, is usually classed as an "ecclesiastical writer" rather than a father, because in later life he sided with the rigorist Montanists. Origen of Alexandria, the most learned biblical scholar of the early church, falls outside the category because some of his speculations were condemned three centuries after his death. The line isn't a verdict on genius. It marks where the church was willing to vouch for a teacher's whole life and teaching.

The fathers fall into rough families. The earliest are grouped under a seventeenth-century label, the Apostolic Fathers: Clement of Rome, whose letter to the Corinthians dates to around 96; Ignatius of Antioch; Polycarp of Smyrna, martyred around 155; and the anonymous authors of the *Didache*, a short church manual, and the *Epistle to Diognetus*. The golden age of the fourth and fifth centuries divides by language, the Greek fathers of the East (Athanasius, the three Cappadocians, Chrysostom) and the Latin fathers of the West (Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine and, at the end of the sixth century, Gregory the Great). By convention the age closes with Isidore of Seville in the West, who died in 636, and John of Damascus in the East, a century later.

Around 434 Vincent, a monk of Lérins off the coast of Gaul, proposed the most famous test for what the fathers held in common. In his *Commonitorium* he defined the catholic faith as *quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus creditum est*, what has been believed everywhere, always and by all. The essay on [the faith once delivered](/writing/the-faith-once-delivered) takes up Vincent's rule and its limits. What matters here is its instinct. Vincent wasn't asking which father was cleverest but where the voices agreed, because no single father, however holy, was the faith. A chorus is harder to bend than a soloist.

## Before the empire: Ignatius on the road and Perpetua in prison

If Constantine invented Christianity, somebody forgot to tell the Christians who died for it generations before he was born.

Early in the second century, traditionally around 107 to 110 in the reign of Trajan (some scholars date the letters later), the bishop of Antioch in Syria was arrested and sent to Rome to die in the arena. Ignatius travelled overland across Asia Minor under a guard of ten soldiers he called leopards, because they grew crueler the more kindly they were treated. He wrote seven surviving letters on the way, four from Smyrna, where Polycarp was bishop, and three from Troas. They were written in haste by a condemned man.

They aren't what a modern reader expects. Ignatius is fierce against teachers who said Christ only seemed to suffer, and his reply is his own chains: if Jesus only appeared to suffer, he asks the Trallians, why is Ignatius in bonds? He is the first surviving writer to call the church "catholic," telling the Smyrnaeans that wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the catholic church. He calls the bread of the Lord's Supper the medicine of immortality, and he insists that the churches stay united around bishop, elders and deacons. In his letter to the Romans he begs them not to rescue him, calling himself God's wheat, to be ground by the teeth of beasts into the pure bread of Christ.

Those of us in the Free Church traditions should let those letters be uncomfortable. Ignatius's church doesn't look like ours, and we can't wave off his sacramental language and his ordered ministry as late corruption, since they were written within a lifetime of the apostles. How much of that order is apostolic and how much is a pastor's response to a church being torn apart is a real second-order question that faithful Christians answer differently. What no reader can do is make Ignatius a functionary of a state church. There was no state church, only a road to Rome.

On 7 March 203, a young woman named Vibia Perpetua died in the amphitheater at Carthage. *The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity* is nearly unique in the ancient world, because its central portion is her own first-person account of her imprisonment, which the editor says she wrote in her own hand. It is among the earliest surviving writings by a Christian woman.

Perpetua was about twenty-two, well born, educated and nursing an infant son. She was arrested while still preparing for baptism, with others including a pregnant slave named Felicity. Her pagan father came to the prison again and again to beg her to save herself. Once she pointed to a water jug and asked whether it could be called anything other than what it was. When he said no, she answered that neither could she be called anything but what she was, a Christian. Felicity gave birth in prison a few days before the games, and when a guard mocked her cries and asked how she would face the beasts, she replied, in the editor's report, that now she suffered alone, but then another would be in her who would suffer for her, because she would suffer for him. In the arena Perpetua guided the trembling hand of a young gladiator to her own throat.

The fathers themselves warned against seeking martyrdom for its own sake, and these stories are easy to sentimentalize. But they settle a historical question. The faith Constantine tolerated in 313 had been carried for nearly three centuries by people with nothing to gain, many of them slaves, women and provincials, whose whole offense was confessing that Jesus is Lord. Hebrews, written to believers who had already endured public abuse and the plundering of their property, tells them what company they keep: "Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us" (Hebrews 12:1, ESV). The Greek word for witness is *martys*. The church learned what it meant in the arena.

## At the birth of Christendom: Athanasius, the Cappadocians and Macrina

In 313 the persecutions ended and a different danger began. The fourth-century fathers lived through the church's passage from hunted to favored, and their hardest struggles were with Christian emperors, not pagan ones. The essay on [what Christendom was and what comes after it](/writing/christendom-is-ending) tells the larger story; the fathers show what it cost.

Athanasius of Alexandria is the emblem. He attended the Council of Nicaea in 325 as a young deacon, and as bishop of Alexandria from 328 until 373 he refused to let its confession, that the Son is of one being with the Father, be softened into something an emperor found more convenient. He was driven from his city five times, under Constantine, Constantius, Julian and Valens, and spent about seventeen years in exile, once hidden among the monks of the Egyptian desert. The later Latin tag *Athanasius contra mundum*, Athanasius against the world, isn't unfair. Why the word *homoousios* was worth it belongs to [the faith once delivered](/writing/the-faith-once-delivered). What deserves notice here is his short book *On the Incarnation*, written when he was young, though its exact date is debated. Humanity, he argues, made in God's image, had turned toward nothing and was sliding back into it, and a defaced portrait can only be restored if the subject sits for it again. So the Word took a body of his own, died the death that was owed, and rose, breaking death's hold from within. In Athanasius's own summary, loosely rendered, he became human so that we might be made partakers of God. In 367, in his thirty-ninth festal letter, the same bishop listed the twenty-seven books of our New Testament, the earliest surviving list to match it exactly. The man our Bibles owe something to spent years of his life on the run.

The Cappadocian fathers, Basil of Caesarea, his friend Gregory of Nazianzus and his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa, did the theological work in the 360s and 370s that let the Council of Constantinople in 381 confess the Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life. Their story can't be told honestly without the brothers' older sister. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Macrina*, written around 380, tells how she turned the family estate in Pontus into an ascetic community, persuaded their widowed mother to live alongside her former servants as equals, and raised the youngest brother, Peter. When Basil came home from his studies in Athens swollen with pride in his eloquence, Gregory says, it was Macrina who drew him toward a life of prayer. In *On the Soul and the Resurrection* Gregory sets a dialogue at her deathbed, where she answers his grief and his doubts about the resurrection, and he calls her simply the teacher. One of the most profound theologians of the Christian East wanted the church to know he had learned from his sister.

Their theology didn't float above the street. During the Cappadocian famine of the late 360s, Basil preached on the rich fool who planned bigger barns, and his argument hasn't lost its force: the bread you hold back belongs to the hungry, the unused coat in your closet to the naked, the shoes rotting in your house to the barefoot. Then he built. Outside Caesarea he founded a complex for the poor, the sick and travelers, with physicians attached, which Gregory of Nazianzus in his funeral oration for Basil called a new city. Historians often count it among the first hospitals. Gregory of Nyssa went further than almost anyone in the ancient world. Preaching on the verse in Ecclesiastes where the Preacher boasts of the slaves he acquired, he condemned slavery itself, asking what price could buy a being made in the image of God. Most of the church took well over a thousand years to follow him.

The desert mothers belong here too, *ammas* such as Syncletica, Sarah and Theodora; the essay on [the desert fathers and mothers](/writing/why-people-fled-to-the-desert) tells their story. With Macrina they are a reminder that the faith was handed on in kitchens, cells and hermitages as well as in council chambers.

## Chrysostom and Augustine: preachers under pressure

Robert Louis Wilken argues in *The Spirit of Early Christian Thought* (2003) that early Christian thought can't be separated from the worship, Scripture and preaching of the church, because its great thinkers were pastors first. John of Antioch is the proof. Born around 349 and trained under Libanius, the most celebrated pagan orator of his age, he spent years as an ascetic in the hills, returned to be ordained, and from 386 to 397 preached through Genesis, Matthew, John and much of Paul to working people who sometimes applauded mid-sermon. A century and more after his death the church named him Chrysostom, the golden-mouthed.

His great theme was the poor. In his sermons on the rich man and Lazarus he argued that failing to share with the poor is a kind of theft, because what we hold beyond our need was never ours. Preaching on Matthew, he told a congregation proud of its church furnishings that there was no honor in dressing Christ's table in silk while neglecting Christ outside, cold and naked in the person of the beggar. In 398 he was made archbishop of Constantinople and carried the same preaching into the capital. He cut the bishop's household luxuries, funded hospitals and preached against the court's excess in terms the empress Eudoxia took personally. His enemies deposed him at a packed synod in 403. Recalled, he was exiled again in 404 to Cucusus in the mountains of Armenia, and in 407 ordered to a harsher place on the Black Sea. His guards marched him through heat and rain until he collapsed at Comana in Pontus. His last words, by the ancient account, were glory to God for all things.

Augustine, born in 354 at Thagaste in North Africa, is the father Western Christians think they know, and most of us know only half of him. The *Confessions*, written around 397, tells of a gifted rhetor who spent about nine years among the Manichees, lived some fifteen years with a woman he never names and by whom he had a son, Adeodatus, and climbed from Carthage to Rome to Milan, where he heard Ambrose preach. In a Milan garden in 386, in anguish over a will he couldn't make obey him, he heard a child chanting *tolle lege*, take up and read, opened Paul at Romans 13, and found the long war in him over. The book's opening prayer has stayed in the church's mouth ever since: *fecisti nos ad te, et inquietum est cor nostrum donec requiescat in te*, you have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.

Most readers miss the bishop who came after the convert. Ordained at Hippo against his wishes in 391, Augustine spent nearly forty years pastoring an ordinary port town. After Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in 410 and pagans blamed the abandoned gods, he began *The City of God* in 413 and finished it in 426: two loves have made two cities, the earthly built on love of self and the heavenly on love of God, and no earthly empire, not even a Christian one, is the city of God. Peter Brown's *Augustine of Hippo* (1967) traces how Augustine left behind the confident talk of "Christian times" that had followed the emperors' conversion. Against Pelagius he argued that the fallen will is not merely weak but bound, so that grace does not assist a healthy will but heals a broken one; the Council of Ephesus condemned the Pelagian party in 431. He died in August 430 with the Vandals besieging Hippo. His biographer Possidius says he had the penitential psalms fixed to the wall beside his bed and wept as he read them.

Chrysostom's doctrine of the poor cost him his see and his life. Augustine's two cities were written for a church that had watched Rome burn and had to learn quickly that its hope had never been Rome. "Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God. Consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith" (Hebrews 13:7, ESV). The outcome of their lives is part of what they teach.

## Why Protestants went back to them

Many Protestants assume the fathers belong to Rome. The Reformers would have been baffled. Luther was an Augustinian friar, and in the 1545 preface to his Latin writings he recalled reading Augustine's *On the Spirit and the Letter* after his breakthrough on the righteousness of God and finding, beyond his hope, that Augustine had read Paul the same way. He held that councils could err, but he fought with the fathers in his hands. Anthony Lane's *John Calvin: Student of the Church Fathers* (1999) shows Calvin quoting them constantly, Augustine above all, sometimes as allies and sometimes as opponents, and always as witnesses under Scripture rather than masters over it.

Later Protestant theologians gave this a precise form. Scripture is the *norma normans*, the norm that norms; the creeds and the fathers are *norma normata*, norms that are themselves normed. The fathers are real authorities, but derived ones, heard and honored and corrected by Scripture where they depart from it. A son who tests his father's counsel against God's word is still a son. One who refuses to hear his father at all has only made himself an orphan.

American evangelicalism largely lost that balance, and [the faith once delivered](/writing/the-faith-once-delivered) traces how "no creed but the Bible" left many churches with a Bible and no memory. Recovery came from an unexpected direction. Thomas Oden, a Methodist theologian at Drew University, had spent his early career chasing each new movement in modern theology. In his memoir *A Change of Heart* (2014) he tells how his colleague Will Herberg, a Jewish scholar and former Marxist, told him he would remain theologically uneducated until he read the great Christian writers of the past, the fathers among them. Oden took the rebuke. *Agenda for Theology* (1979) announced what he later called paleo-orthodoxy, a theology that sought no novelty, only the consensus of the early church under Scripture, and he spent decades putting the fathers in ordinary hands through the *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture*. D. H. Williams made the case to evangelicals in *Retrieving the Tradition and Renewing Evangelicalism* (1999): the fathers are the common inheritance of every church that confesses the Nicene faith, and a Protestantism cut off from them loses the means to read its own Bible well.

C. S. Lewis said it most memorably. In 1944 he introduced a new translation of Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* with an argument for old books. Every age, he wrote, sees some truths well and is blind to its own characteristic mistakes, which no one notices because everyone shares them. Old books help because their authors made different mistakes, obvious to us and so harmless, while their strengths expose errors we can't see. He urged keeping "the clean sea breeze of the centuries" blowing through our minds, and advised reading an old book between every two new ones. In *Surprised by Joy* (1955) he named the disease: chronological snobbery, the assumption that whatever has gone out of date is discredited for that reason alone.

The cure works in both directions. The progressive who thinks the fathers too primitive to teach justice hasn't read Basil on the barns, and the conservative who thinks them founders of a Christian civilization hasn't read Augustine on the two cities. The fathers embarrass both of us.

## Where the fathers failed

A father is not an oracle, and some of these fathers were terribly wrong. The skeptic who says so is not being unfair.

The worst is Chrysostom's. In 386 and 387 the same golden mouth that defended the poor preached eight sermons at Antioch known as *Adversus Judaeos*, Against the Jews. Robert Wilken's *John Chrysostom and the Jews* (1983) supplies the context: the target was Chrysostom's own congregation, Christians who attended synagogue festivals and kept the Jewish fasts, and he used the brutal conventions of ancient invective against a confident Jewish community, not a frightened minority. The context explains the sermons. It does not redeem them. They call the synagogue a brothel and a den of robbers, and later centuries, with the context long gone, used them against Jews who had never heard of Antioch. The essay on [what Christians owe Judaism](/writing/what-christians-can-learn-from-judaism) follows that line. Paul had warned Gentile believers at Rome against exactly this arrogance toward the olive tree they had been grafted into: "do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you" (Romans 11:18, ESV). Chrysostom preached through Romans verse by verse. The warning was in his Bible, and he didn't hear it.

The fathers' views of women are a second failure, less violent and more pervasive. Tertullian told the women of Carthage in *On the Apparel of Women* that each of them was Eve, and called woman "the devil's gateway." Augustine, reading Genesis, couldn't see why the woman was made as a helper if not for childbearing, since for company, he thought, another man would have served better. Yet the tradition kept undercutting its worst voices. The churches read Perpetua's diary aloud, and Augustine preached on her feast day. Gregory of Nyssa called his sister the teacher. Chrysostom marveled that Junia was counted among the apostles, and the essay on [women in ministry](/writing/women-in-ministry) weighs what that does and doesn't settle. Over them all stands the text: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, ESV). What it means for church office is a second-order question on which faithful Christians differ. It cannot mean that a woman is the devil's gateway.

Augustine adds one more. After years of preferring persuasion, he came to defend imperial force against the Donatists, a rival North African church, reading the parable's command to compel guests to come in as a warrant for coercion. He believed he was rescuing people. His argument became a charter for centuries of persecution by Christian rulers, and the father who taught that no earthly city is the city of God handed the earthly city a tool it would use in God's name.

These failures don't disqualify the fathers from being fathers. They disqualify every Christian from being an oracle, which was the Reformers' point, and they turn the question back on us. Each of these men was blind where his age was blind and his Bible should have corrected him. We have no reason to think ourselves the first generation exempt, and those of us who preach every Sunday least of all.

## A church before Christendom and at its birth

For fifteen centuries Western Christians read the fathers from inside a Christian civilization and were tempted to treat them as its founders. As that civilization ends, the fathers become readable again, because they stood on both sides of its beginning. Ignatius and Perpetua knew a church with no standing at all. Athanasius, the Cappadocians, Chrysostom and Augustine watched the empire embrace the church and learned what the embrace could cost. We are living through the same hinge in reverse, and we need both kinds of witness.

The earlier fathers show that a church without cultural advantage is not a church without power. The clearest testimony comes from an enemy. When the emperor Julian, raised Christian and turned pagan, tried in the 360s to revive the old religion, he wrote to one of his high priests complaining that the Galileans supported not only their own poor but the pagan poor as well, and he told his priests to imitate them. The later fathers show the opposite danger from inside. A Christian emperor could exile a bishop as easily as a pagan one, and a court full of baptized people could march a preacher to his death. The church the empire loved was not safer. It had traded one set of dangers for another that was harder to see because it flattered.

Hebrews ends its roll call of the faithful, from Abel to those sawn in two, with a sentence that changes how every name in this essay should be heard. "And all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised" (Hebrews 11:39, ESV). The reason follows: "since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect" (Hebrews 11:40, ESV). The ancient faithful are not finished, and we have not moved beyond them. They are waiting, and they and we will be made perfect together in the resurrection none of them has yet received.

Jesus told the Sadducees, who denied the resurrection, that the God of Abraham "is not God of the dead, but of the living, for all live to him" (Luke 20:38, ESV). If that is true, then Ignatius and Perpetua, Macrina and Augustine, and Chrysostom with his golden mouth and his terrible sermons are not dead authors in a library. They are family, alive to God, waiting for the church to finish the race they ran. They are waiting for us to confess the faith they kept and to repent of the sins they couldn't see, sins we are very likely committing in forms of our own. And someone will one day read what we did with the inheritance, the way we now read them, hoping to find that we heard what was in our Bibles, and not yet knowing whether we did.
