Jesus was a Jew. Almost every Christian would agree with the sentence, and very few of us let it change how we read, preach or pray. He was circumcised on the eighth day and presented at the temple, as the law of Moses required (Luke 2:21–24). He prayed in synagogues, kept the feasts and taught in the idiom of Israel's Scriptures, the only Scriptures he had. The woman who pushed through the crowd to touch him reached for the fringe of his garment (Matthew 9:20), most likely the tassel the law told every Israelite to wear (Numbers 15:38). Asked which commandment came first, he answered with the confession observant Jews still say morning and evening: "Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Mark 12:29, ESV). He died under a sign that called him the King of the Jews.

I preach every week from a book written almost entirely by Jews, and it's easy to forget that, and I do. The church forgot it early. Within a few generations Christianity had begun to define itself against Judaism: the synagogue became the foil, the Torah the old dispensation, and the Jewish people the ones who had rejected their Messiah. From John Chrysostom to Martin Luther that charge fed centuries of expulsion, forced conversion and ghetto walls, and it became part of the soil in which the Holocaust grew.

Recovering the Jewish roots of the faith is therefore not a matter of interfaith manners. It's a question about who Christians are. Paul had an image for it, and it's a humbling one: the gentile church is a wild olive shoot grafted into a tree it didn't plant, living on sap it didn't produce. The whole argument that follows turns on the difference between a graft and a replacement. A replacement can forget what came before it. A graft that forgets its root dies.

## A Jewish Messiah and a Jewish Church

The first Christians did not think they had joined a new religion. They thought the God of Abraham had kept his word. Paul describes himself, years after his conversion, as "circumcised on the eighth day, of the people of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee" (Philippians 3:5, ESV). Decades later the elders of the Jerusalem church told Paul that thousands of Jews had believed and added, with no sense of contradiction, "They are all zealous for the law" (Acts 21:20, ESV).

The question the first generation fought over tells you whose house it was. It was never whether a Jew could follow Jesus and remain a Jew. It was whether a gentile could follow him without first becoming one, and the council recorded in Acts 15, around AD 49, decided that gentile believers need not be circumcised. Nobody debates the terms of admission to a house that belongs to the guest.

Since Geza Vermes's *Jesus the Jew* (1973), scholarship has spent half a century putting Jesus back into that house. Amy-Jill Levine, a Jewish scholar of the New Testament, pressed the point home to the church in *The Misunderstood Jew* (2006). She showed how routinely Christian sermons paint a dark Judaism behind Jesus (cold legalism, purity codes that shunned women, leaders indifferent to the poor) so that he can shine against it. The portrait is false. His arguments with the Pharisees were arguments inside a family that argued constantly, and on questions like the resurrection he stood closer to them than to anyone else in the room. Those of us who preach have painted that dark background more often than we'd admit, and taught congregations to despise the people Jesus belonged to while singing about him.

Paul's olive tree in Romans 11:17–24 was written against exactly that temptation. The Roman church was by then largely gentile, and many scholars think its Jewish members had only recently returned after the emperor Claudius expelled Jews from the city around 49 (Acts 18:2). Paul has spent two chapters grieving that most of his own people have not believed, and gentile Christians were drawing the obvious conclusion: God is finished with Israel. Paul's answer is blunt: "I ask, then, has God rejected his people? By no means! For I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin" (Romans 11:1, ESV). Then he warns the grafted wild shoot about the sin it's most likely to commit: "do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you" (Romans 11:18, ESV).

The image is stranger than it looks. The ordinary practice was to graft a cultivated branch onto hardy wild stock, and Paul admits the gentiles were grafted in "contrary to nature" (Romans 11:24, ESV). The absurdity is the point, and the point is grace: the gentile believer is in the tree by a gift that makes no agricultural sense. Paul closes with a sentence that has troubled every tidy Christian theology of Israel since: "For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29, ESV). He writes it about Jews he has just called enemies of the gospel for the gentiles' sake and, in the same breath, "beloved for the sake of their forefathers" (Romans 11:28, ESV). God has not taken back what he gave them. The church lives inside a promise that was someone else's first.

## Marcion and the Scriptures the Church Would Not Give Up

The first great test of that root came from Marcion, a wealthy shipowner from Pontus who was expelled from the church in Rome around 144. Marcion couldn't reconcile the Creator who gave the law, judged and commanded war with the Father of Jesus Christ, so he concluded that they were two different gods, threw out the Old Testament, and kept an edited Luke and ten letters of Paul trimmed of what tied Jesus to Israel's God.

The church called this heresy, and the reason still matters. Irenaeus of Lyons in *Against Heresies* (around 180) and Tertullian in *Against Marcion* (begun around 207) saw that Marcion had cut creation off from redemption. If the God who saves is not the God who made the world, then the body, history and the people of Israel are not things God is redeeming but things he is rescuing us from. By keeping Israel's Scriptures as its own, the church confessed that the one who raised Jesus from the dead is the one who brought Israel out of Egypt: two testaments, one God, one story, which the essay on [why the Bible is one story](/writing/why-the-bible-is-one-story-not-66-books) follows through.

Marcion lost the argument and has never stopped winning converts. The liberal historian Adolf von Harnack, in *Marcion: The Gospel of the Alien God* (1921), argued that the church was right to reject Marcion in the second century, but that for Protestants to keep the Old Testament as canonical Scripture in the modern era was a sign of religious paralysis. The idea did not stay in the seminar room. At a rally of the pro-Nazi German Christians in Berlin's Sportpalast in November 1933, a speaker demanded that the church free itself from the Old Testament, and in 1939 Protestant theologians founded an institute at Eisenach to remove Jewish influence from German church life. Susannah Heschel's *The Aryan Jesus* (2008) documents how it produced a de-Judaized New Testament and taught that Jesus had been an Aryan fighting the Jews. A church that cuts off its root doesn't become more spiritual. It becomes available.

Our own Marcionism is quieter and comes from every side. It sounds like the offhand remark that the God of the Old Testament is wrathful and the God of Jesus is love, as if Jesus had never quoted Deuteronomy or wept over Jerusalem like Jeremiah. On one end of the church it looks like a progressive faith that keeps Jesus the teacher and quietly retires the God who judges; on the other, like an evangelical faith that mines the Old Testament for prophecies of Christ and never hears its law, its prophets' demand for justice or its Psalms' cries. Both treat the Scriptures Jesus prayed as scaffolding to be taken down once the building is up. Preachers aren't exempt; anyone who preaches through the Bible knows the pull to hurry past Leviticus.

## The Teaching of Contempt

The skeptic who says the church's record with the Jewish people counts against Christianity is not being unfair. He is reading the record. The French Jewish historian Jules Isaac, whose wife and daughter were murdered at Auschwitz, spent the years after the war tracing what he called the teaching of contempt, the settled habit of Christian preaching and law that taught believers to regard Jews as a cursed and blind people. His *Jesus and Israel* (1948) made the case from the Gospels themselves.

It began early and in the family. The *Epistle of Barnabas*, written around 130, argued that the covenant belonged to Christians alone and that Israel had lost it at Sinai when Moses broke the tablets. Melito of Sardis, preaching on Passover later that century, accused Israel of murdering God. When the Council of Nicaea in 325 fixed Easter apart from the Jewish calendar, Constantine's letter to the churches explained the decision by telling them to have nothing in common with the Jews.

In 386 and 387 John Chrysostom delivered eight homilies at Antioch known as *Adversus Judaeos*, Against the Jews. Robert Wilken's *John Chrysostom and the Jews* (1983) shows that his target was his own congregation, Christians who went to the synagogue on feast days and kept the Jewish fasts, and that he was using the harsh conventions of ancient invective against a confident rival, not a frightened minority. None of that redeems the words. In the first homily he called the synagogue a brothel and a den of robbers. The context explains why he spoke that way. It can't undo what later centuries did with the text once the context was gone.

Augustine gave the West a gentler doctrine that did its own damage. In *The City of God* (18.46), reading Psalm 59, he taught that God had preserved the Jews scattered among the nations as witnesses who carried the Scriptures and showed the cost of rejecting Christ. Paula Fredriksen's *Augustine and the Jews* (2008) shows that this doctrine protected Jewish life in medieval Christendom. It also fixed Jews in a role: to survive, but humbled, as living exhibits. The dates run on from there. The Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 required Jews to wear distinguishing dress. England expelled its Jews in 1290 and Spain in 1492. And in 1543 Luther, who had recovered justification by grace alone, published *On the Jews and Their Lies*, urging rulers to burn synagogues; the essay on [the Reformation](/writing/how-the-reformation-started-and-why-it-still-matters) tells that part of the story.

Historians rightly distinguish Christian anti-Judaism, a theological hostility that baptism could always end, from modern racial antisemitism, which no baptism could cure. But the distinction is not an alibi. Centuries of preaching had taught ordinary Christians to see Jews as a people under a curse, and the racial theory found that ground already cleared. About six million Jews were murdered. Even the church's resistance showed the scar. The Barmen Declaration of 1934, the Confessing Church's great refusal of Nazi claims, never mentioned the Jews, and Karl Barth, its chief drafter, later said he counted himself guilty for not having made the Jewish question central to the church struggle. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's April 1933 essay "The Church and the Jewish Question" argued that the church might have not only to bandage the victims under the state's wheel but to jam the wheel itself. It was one of the bravest things written in Germany that year, and it still repeated the old claim that Israel bore a curse for the cross. If the best of that generation carried the teaching of contempt, the rest of us should assume we carry some of it.

The church did begin to repent, slowly. In 1947 Christians and Jews meeting at Seelisberg in Switzerland, Isaac among them, drafted ten points for purging that teaching. In June 1960 Isaac met Pope John XXIII and pressed the case on him, and the pope set in motion the work that became a conciliar declaration. On 28 October 1965 the Second Vatican Council promulgated *Nostra Aetate*. Its fourth section, drawing on Romans 11, says the church draws nourishment from the root of the good olive tree; that the Jews remain dear to God for the sake of their fathers, since God does not take back his gifts or his call; that the death of Christ cannot be charged against all Jews then living or against Jews today; and that the church deplores hatred and persecution of Jews by anyone at any time. The declarations came late, after the graves. They were still repentance.

## Was Judaism a Religion of Earning? Torah as Gift

Behind much of the contempt sat a picture Christians were sure was biblical: Judaism as a religion of earning, in which anxious people climbed toward God by keeping rules, and Christianity as the religion of grace that set them free. Protestants inherited the picture from Luther, who read Paul's opponents through his own struggle with late medieval penance, so that the Pharisees became mirrors of the indulgence sellers.

The picture doesn't survive a reading of the Scriptures Jesus prayed. The law was given to people God had already rescued, and the Ten Commandments begin with grace: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exodus 20:2, ESV). The longest psalm is a love song to the law: "Oh how I love your law! It is my meditation all the day" (Psalm 119:97, ESV). Nobody writes that about a ladder he is desperately climbing. In Jewish practice to this day the study of Torah is itself worship, and the festival of Simchat Torah ends the yearly cycle of readings with dancing. A people doesn't dance with a burden.

Twentieth-century scholarship made the church face this. Krister Stendahl, a Swedish Lutheran scholar who later became Bishop of Stockholm, argued in "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (1963) that the West had read Paul through Augustine's and Luther's troubled consciences, when Paul seems to have had a remarkably robust one; he could describe his former life as "as to righteousness under the law, blameless" (Philippians 3:6, ESV). Paul's burning question was how gentiles come to share in Israel's promises. E. P. Sanders, in *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977), surveyed the Jewish literature of the period and named its pattern covenantal nomism: Israel was in the covenant by God's gracious election, and obedience was the way to stay in it, with atonement provided for failure. James D. G. Dunn named the resulting movement in "The New Perspective on Paul" (1983), arguing that the "works of the law" Paul resisted were chiefly the markers that divided Jew from gentile: circumcision, food laws and Sabbath.

The critics deserve to be heard at full strength, because they are not defending the old caricature. *Justification and Variegated Nomism*, edited by D. A. Carson, Peter O'Brien and Mark Seifrid (volume one, 2001), argued that Second Temple Judaism was more varied than Sanders allowed, and that some of its texts do weigh deeds for final judgment in ways Paul could fairly resist. Others note that Paul's logic in Romans 4 reaches past boundary markers to a God who "justifies the ungodly" (Romans 4:5, ESV). John Barclay's *Paul and the Gift* (2015) moved the argument forward. Grace, Barclay shows, was everywhere in the Judaism of Paul's day. The question was what kind of grace. Many Jewish writers held that God gives to fitting recipients, as any wise giver would. Paul insisted that in Christ God gives to the unfitting, the ungodly and the enemy.

That debate is second-order and still open, and faithful scholars stand on both sides of it. But most on both sides would now grant that the old sermon setting a legalistic Judaism against a gracious Christianity was a caricature. It told Christians something false about their neighbors and about their own Bible, whose law was gift before it was demand. Paul could ask whether faith overthrows the law and answer, "By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law" (Romans 3:31, ESV).

## Memory, Argument and Rest

If the church can hear Torah as gift, it can learn from the people who kept it, and three of their practices press where American Christianity is thinnest.

The first is memory. The Passover command anticipated a child's question: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?'" (Exodus 12:26, ESV). The answer in Deuteronomy is spoken in the first person plural by people who were never in Egypt: "We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt" (Deuteronomy 6:21, ESV). The seder as Jews keep it took shape after the temple fell; the Mishnah, compiled around 200, already sets out its order, from the child's questions to a telling that moves from shame to praise, and teaches that in every generation a person is to regard himself as though he had come out of Egypt. Memory in this grammar isn't nostalgia. It's participation.

Christians have this grammar and rarely notice where it came from. At a Passover meal, by the account of the first three Gospels, Jesus took bread and said, "Do this in remembrance of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24, ESV). He meant the Passover kind of remembrance, which places the one remembering inside the event. A church that treats the Lord's Supper as a mental exercise in recalling a fact has forgotten the table it was born at. That doesn't mean churches should stage seders of their own; many Jews find that an appropriation of their feast with its meaning changed, and they have a point. It means learning what remembrance is from the people who taught it to Jesus's disciples, and accepting a Jewish neighbor's invitation as a guest before trying to host.

The second is argument with God. Abraham bargains over Sodom and asks, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25, ESV). Jacob wrestles through the night and is renamed: "Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel, for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28, ESV). Job protests chapter after chapter, and at the end God says the friends who defended him glibly have not spoken rightly, as Job did (Job 42:7). The Talmud (Eruvin 13b) records that after years of dispute between the schools of Hillel and Shammai a heavenly voice declared both to be the words of the living God, and that the law followed Hillel because his school was gracious and humble and taught its opponents' view before its own. A tradition that rewards the side that states the other side first has something to teach a church that can't bear a question. In Elie Wiesel's play *The Trial of God* (1979), set after a massacre of Jews in 1649, the one character who defends God smoothly turns out to be Satan. The essay on [lament](/writing/lament-the-prayer-the-church-forgot) traces how Scripture turns such protest into prayer.

The third is rest. Every Friday at sundown observant Jewish households light candles, bless wine and bread, and enter a day in which work stops until nightfall on Saturday. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Sabbath* (1951) taught a generation of Christians to see the day as holiness in time rather than space, and the essay on [the Sabbath](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it) takes up his argument. For most of two thousand years Jews kept this day while Christians argued about it, and a people scattered among empires survived partly by refusing, one day in seven, to belong to anyone but God.

## The Disagreement That Remains

Honesty cuts both ways. Christians who learn to love the root are sometimes tempted to blur the disagreement, as though nothing but misunderstanding stood between church and synagogue. That would be a new kind of disrespect. Jews and Christians disagree about Jesus, and the disagreement is not small.

The Jewish objection deserves its strongest form. In the prophets the Messiah's work is public and historical: the exiles gathered, Israel restored and a world at peace in which "they shall beat their swords into plowshares" (Isaiah 2:4, ESV). Moses Maimonides, in the laws of kings in his *Mishneh Torah* (completed around 1180), set out the tests a claimant must meet and judged that Jesus had not met them, though he allowed that Christianity and Islam, by spreading Scripture and the commandments among the nations, were preparing the world for the true redemption. Look at the world, the objection says. The swords are still swords, and the people who announced the Messiah spent centuries persecuting Jews in his name. *Dabru Emet* (2000), a statement by a large group of Jewish scholars and rabbis, affirmed that Jews and Christians worship the same God, judged that Nazism was not a Christian phenomenon but could not have taken hold without the long history of Christian anti-Judaism, and said the difference between the two communities will not be settled until God redeems the whole world.

The Christian answer is not that the objection sees nothing. The world is not yet redeemed. The Christian claim is that the Messiah came first in the way of the cross, that God raised him as "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV), and that the rest of the harvest is still to come (see [the already and the not yet](/writing/already-and-not-yet-living-between-two-worlds)). Paul knew how it would sound: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). That Jesus is the Christ, crucified and bodily raised, is first-order, a confession the church can't soften without ceasing to be the church, and Jewish partners in dialogue have often said they don't want Christians to pretend otherwise.

The harder question is what that confession means for Israel. Supersessionism holds, in its strongest form, that the church has not so much replaced Israel as fulfilled it. Its defenders argue from across the New Testament. "For all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Corinthians 1:20, ESV). "And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (Galatians 3:29, ESV). "In speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete" (Hebrews 8:13, ESV). On this view there is one people of God gathered around Israel's Messiah, and a covenant path for the Jewish people apart from Jesus would deny that he is Israel's Messiah at all. Many who hold it prefer the word fulfillment and reject antisemitism without reserve, and many read Romans 11:26 as promising a future turning of the Jewish people to their Messiah, so that Israel has a future on their view, in Christ. That is the case its defenders would sign.

Its critics answer from the same Bible. R. Kendall Soulen, in *The God of Israel and Christian Theology* (1996), distinguished three forms of the teaching: economic, in which Israel's role simply ends when Christ comes; punitive, in which God rejects Israel for rejecting Christ; and structural, in which the church tells the biblical story from creation and fall straight to Christ and leaves Israel out of the plot. Even gentle fulfillment theology, Soulen argued, slides toward the structural form and loses sight of the God who chose Israel. Karl Barth, writing *Church Dogmatics* II/2 (1942) as the deportations were under way, argued that the one elect community of God exists in two forms, Israel and the church, and that Israel's election has never been revoked. And Paul expects a future for his people that the church does not simply absorb: "And in this way all Israel will be saved" (Romans 11:26, ESV). What that means is fiercely debated. That it points to God's faithfulness to Israel is hard to deny.

Some go further than Barth. Two-covenant theology holds that the Jewish people stand in a living covenant with God through Torah, while gentiles come to the same God through Christ, so that the church owes Israel no mission at all. The German Jewish philosopher Franz Rosenzweig gave the idea its classic form in a 1913 letter to Rudolf Ehrenberg: he granted that no one comes to the Father except through Christ, and added that the case is different for a people already with the Father. Among Christian scholars, Lloyd Gaston's *Paul and the Torah* (1987) and John Gager's *Reinventing Paul* (2000) read Paul as the apostle to the gentiles whose arguments against the law concern gentiles only. Its defenders note that Romans 11 never names Christ, and that "all Israel will be saved" may describe God's own act rather than the church's project. Its critics answer that it leaves the first apostles, Jews who preached Jesus as Israel's Messiah in Jerusalem before anywhere else, with no gospel for their own people: "Let all the house of Israel therefore know for certain that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified" (Acts 2:36, ESV).

The shape of Israel's continuing place in God's purpose is a second-order question. Catholic teaching since 1965, Reformed covenant theology, dispensationalism and Messianic Jews who confess Jesus and remain Jews all answer it differently, and all stand on the creedal floor. This essay lands where Paul's warning to the Roman gentiles points. It rejects supersessionism in its replacement and punitive forms, the claim that God has revoked his covenant with the Jewish people and handed it to the church. It takes seriously the possibility that God's covenant with Israel remains living, because Paul says the gifts and the calling are irrevocable. It holds the confession of Jesus as Israel's Messiah and Lord of all without apology, and with the humility fitting a community that spent most of two thousand years getting its relationship with its root catastrophically wrong. That means meeting Jewish neighbors as family rather than targets, and hearing Jewish self-understanding on its own terms.

The strongest objection to that landing is Paul, and it doesn't go away. The apostle who wrote Romans 11:29 also wrote, "Brothers, my heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved" (Romans 10:1, ESV), and called the gospel the power of God for salvation "to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16, ESV). A Christianity that has stopped hoping and speaking about Jesus with Jewish people seems to have parted company with Paul. A Christianity that treats them as a mission field repeats, in Jewish ears, centuries of forced sermons and forced baptisms, and they have reason to hear it that way. Paul did not resolve the tension in an argument. After three chapters of anguish he broke into praise: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!" (Romans 11:33, ESV). The church has usually wanted a policy where Paul offered a doxology.

Pilate had a sign written for the cross: "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" (John 19:19, ESV). Every Christian who kneels before that cross kneels before a Jew whose title was nailed above his head, and for most of our history we hung contempt on the people that sign named. The risen Christ did not shed his people when he rose. At the end of the Bible he still names himself by his family: "I am the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star" (Revelation 22:16, ESV). His kin are still among us, reading the Scriptures he read, keeping the Sabbath he kept, waiting for the redemption we say has begun. Repentance for the teaching of contempt is not a statement a denomination issues once. It's a way of reading Leviticus, of preaching the Pharisees and of speaking about the synagogue down the street. Whether we can confess their Messiah without contempt for his family is the question the church failed for most of its history. It is still ours to answer.
