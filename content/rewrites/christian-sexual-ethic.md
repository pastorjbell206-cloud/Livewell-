### The question behind the question

The question rarely arrives as a question. It arrives as a person who has already decided whether you are safe.

Gay men and women who grew up in church tell a version of the same story often enough that anyone who has listened will recognize it. They sat in the pews for years, sang, served, and listened to how the room talked about people like them when it assumed no one like them was present: the jokes that weren't quite jokes, the certainty that cost the certain nothing. They learned what we would do with the truth long before they risked telling it to us. When many of them finally spoke, they were no longer asking whether they could stay. They were explaining why they had gone.

That has to be said before anything else, or the rest of this is a man arranging arguments over a wound he refuses to look at. The church has been cruel here, and not in the abstract: in rooms I have stood in, from pulpits I have shared, in a tone I have used myself before I knew who was listening. We singled out one sin and made it *the* sin, the one a person could be known by and defined by and kept at the door for, while the gossip and the greed and the divorces in the third pew got a nod and a casserole. If you are gay and grew up in the church, you already know this, and you don't need me to perform sorrow about it. You need me not to lie. The cruelty was real, it was ours, and a great deal of what has been said in defense of the historic teaching has been the ugliest thing in the room while claiming to be the holiest.

The record is long. England's Buggery Act of 1533 made sodomy a capital crime, and for centuries Christian nations wrote their moral theology into criminal law with little sense that the gospel might object to the gallows. In the 1980s, while AIDS was killing young men by the tens of thousands, some of the best-known preachers in America called the epidemic the judgment of God, and too many of the rest of us said nothing. For decades a network of ministries promised gay Christians that enough prayer and effort would make them straight; in 2013 Exodus International, the largest of them, closed, and its president, Alan Chambers, publicly apologized for the harm the promise had done. The Centers for Disease Control's Youth Risk Behavior Survey has found repeatedly that lesbian, gay and bisexual high school students seriously consider and attempt suicide at far higher rates than their straight classmates. The church did not cause all of that, but it has not been the refuge it was meant to be. If you are carrying that weight tonight, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, and tell a real person. Your life is a gift the rest of us were meant to guard, and no argument in this essay outranks it.

Now the word itself. The psychologist George Weinberg popularized *homophobia* in *Society and the Healthy Homosexual* (1972), and he meant something specific: the dread of being in close quarters with gay people, a fear aimed at persons. The definition separates two questions the public argument blurs. Have Christians treated gay people with dread and contempt? Many have. Is the historic Christian teaching about sex itself a form of that dread, or could a person hold it without contempt anywhere in his heart? Those questions come apart. Contempt for persons must be repented of whether or not the teaching stands, and a false teaching held with great tenderness would still be false. Naming the cruelty doesn't by itself settle what the church should teach, and pretending it does is its own kind of dodge.

Serious, Scripture-loving Christians disagree here, some holding the historic teaching as a boundary of the faith and others treating the question as a difference between brothers and sisters. This essay will say where it lands, but only after both cases have been set down in the form their best defenders would sign, because a verdict handed over before the other side has been felt is not a conviction. It is a reflex.

### Why the old ethic now sounds like hatred

To many readers under forty, the charge of homophobia doesn't feel like an argument. It feels like a description, and that feeling has a history.

The word *homosexual* was coined in 1869 by the Hungarian writer Karl-Maria Kertbeny, and Michel Foucault argued in *The History of Sexuality* (1976) that where older law saw an act, the new psychiatry saw a kind of person, a claim scholars still dispute. Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) traces the deeper current beneath it: expressive individualism, the conviction, carried from the Romantics into the whole culture after the 1960s, that each person has a unique way of being human, discovered by looking within, and that to be pressed into a pattern handed down from outside is to be wronged.

Carl Trueman's *The Rise and Triumph of the Modern Self* (2020) follows that thread from Rousseau to Freud, who made sexual desire central to what a person is, and on to the New Left, which made sexual liberation a political cause. Few people read Rousseau, but on Trueman's account nearly everyone now lives inside the picture of the self he helped draw. Once the self is understood as its inner desires, and sexual desire as the deepest of them, any moral limit on sexual acts can only be heard as a sentence passed on the person. In 1973 the American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from its manual of mental disorders; in 2015 *Obergefell v. Hodges* made same-sex marriage the law of the land. The Christian ethic did not change its content in those years. The self that heard it did.

Some of that change was a real gain. The church often treated same-sex attraction as a whim or a rebellion that could be dropped with enough resolve, and people were crushed under that falsehood. The modern self was right that desire runs deep, arrives unchosen, and cannot be argued away. The Christian faith confesses a Lord who "in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15), and so it has always known that temptation is not guilt, and that a desire can be real without being obeyed.

And the church gave up much of its sexual ethic long before this debate reached it. The Anglican bishops at the Lambeth Conference of 1930 cautiously permitted contraception within marriage, and most Protestants followed with little theological reflection. California's Family Law Act of 1969 introduced no-fault divorce, the rest of the country followed, and evangelical churches absorbed the new divorce culture with barely a sermon of resistance. We made our peace with all of it, and then discovered the full force of our convictions at the one point where they cost the majority in our pews nothing. The skeptic who suspects prejudice dressed as principle has noticed something true about us. The question is whether it is true about the teaching.

### A vision of the body, not a list of prohibitions

The historic Christian ethic begins with a story about what bodies are for, and every prohibition in it is a shadow cast by that story.

"So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). Human beings bear the image of God, and they are made in two kinds, different and fitted to one another. In the next chapter God says, "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him" (Genesis 2:18). Loneliness is the first thing God calls not good, before sin has entered the world. Then the woman is brought to the man, and the narrator draws the conclusion the rest of the Bible leans on: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24).

Asked about divorce, Jesus went back behind Moses: "Have you not read that he who created them from the beginning made them male and female, and said, 'Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh'? So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matthew 19:4–6). Jesus treats the male and female pattern of Genesis as the given shape of marriage. And when his disciples said it would be better not to marry at all, he answered with a saying the modern church has nearly forgotten: "For there are eunuchs who have been so from birth, and there are eunuchs who have been made eunuchs by men, and there are eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 19:12). In the same breath that grounds marriage in creation, Jesus honors the unmarried life, its unchosen forms alongside its chosen one.

In 1 Corinthians 6, writing to a church that thought freedom in Christ made the body morally irrelevant, Paul answers with a claim of ownership: "The body is not meant for sexual immorality, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (1 Corinthians 6:13). "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (6:19–20). Just before this, Paul lists those who will not inherit the kingdom: the sexually immoral, adulterers and "men who practice homosexuality," but also thieves, the greedy, drunkards, revilers and swindlers. Then comes the sentence the passage turns on: "And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11). The church in Corinth stood inside that list, washed, and nowhere outside it pointing in.

In Ephesians Paul quotes Genesis 2:24 and adds, "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Marriage, on this reading, is a living sign of two who are genuinely other made one without being made the same, the way Christ and his people are other and one. And signs point past themselves. Jesus said that "in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage" (Matthew 22:30). For most of its history the church honored celibacy so highly that it risked despising marriage. The Reformation corrected that after Luther married Katharina von Bora in 1525, and then Protestants overcorrected, until in much of American evangelicalism an unmarried adult is a problem awaiting a solution. If singleness is a waiting room, the historic ethic sentences gay Christians to wait forever. If it is a vocation that lives ahead of the resurrection, the ethic is asking something else.

Put together, the ethic says this: sexual union belongs to the covenant of a man and a woman in marriage, and everything outside that covenant falls under the same word, whether it is fornication, adultery, pornography, the lust a married man entertains, or same-sex sexual relationships. It also says that no one's desires, straight or gay, are the deepest truth about them, which offends every modern person equally. It asks the same thing of everyone, which is why the charge of singling out fails as a description of the teaching even as it succeeds as a description of the church.

Kyle Harper's *From Shame to Sin* (2013) shows how strange this ethic looked when it first appeared. Roman sexual morality, Harper argues, was organized around status and shame. A freeborn wife was guarded so that her children would be legitimate heirs; a male citizen could use slaves and prostitutes without disgrace, so long as his partners were beneath him in rank and he kept the dominant role. Justin Martyr's *First Apology* (around 155) protested that exposed infants were gathered up and raised for prostitution. Into that world the church brought a morality built on the free will of every person before God, and it called sexual sin by the same name whoever committed it and to whomever. Its "no" fell hardest on the powerful: the master who took his slave girl, the citizen at the brothel. Roman morals judged same-sex relations by who held which role; the church forbade them outright, as it forbade the master's use of the slave. Whatever else one concludes, the historic ethic began as a despised minority's vision pressed against the pleasures of the most powerful men in Rome.

### Romans 1 and the trap in chapter 2

Romans 1 is the passage most often thrown and least often read. Paul states his subject first: "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth" (Romans 1:18). The root is the suppression of what can be known about God, and the pivot is an exchange: "they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever! Amen" (1:25). Only then does Paul write, "For their women exchanged natural relations for those that are contrary to nature; and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women and were consumed with passion for one another" (1:26–27).

Robert Gagnon, in *The Bible and Homosexual Practice* (2001), and Richard Hays, in *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* (1996), both notice that Paul is echoing the creation account. The image, the birds and animals and creeping things of 1:23 come from Genesis 1, and the unusual words Paul uses for "women" and "men" are the words for "female" and "male" in the Greek of Genesis 1:27. On their reading, same-sex relations are one picture of the larger exchange, humanity turning from the Creator's pattern to its own.

But Paul keeps writing, and the church has often stopped reading. The list that follows is long and ordinary: "They are gossips, slanderers, haters of God, insolent, haughty, boastful, inventors of evil, disobedient to parents" (1:29–30). Then Paul turns on the person who has been nodding along: "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things" (Romans 2:1). Hays reads the whole movement as a rhetorical trap. Paul lets his hearer enjoy the indictment of pagan vice and then springs the door, because the argument is headed for the conclusion that "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (3:23), and for the righteousness of God given through faith in Christ.

Two misreadings follow from ignoring the trap. The first reads 1:26–27 and skips 2:1, setting gay people apart as a special class of sinner; a church that does that has become the very man Paul addresses, and the reader who came to Romans 1 for ammunition is the reader Paul was aiming at. The second lets 2:1 swallow the chapter before it, as though the turn on the judge meant the things Paul named no longer counted. The turn widens the diagnosis to everyone and cancels none of it. What Romans 1 refuses is a ranking, and it refuses to let the one reading it stand anywhere but under the same mercy.

### The affirming case at full strength

The affirming case deserves better than the cartoon it usually gets. Its most serious advocates love the book they are arguing about.

Matthew Vines, in *God and the Gay Christian* (2014), writes as an evangelical with a high view of Scripture. The world Paul knew, he argues, had no concept of a fixed, lifelong orientation toward one's own sex, and the same-sex behavior it saw most often was the older man and the boy, the master and the slave, and the excess of men who had tired of their wives. Desire "consumed with passion" describes lust boiling over its banks, not two people building a faithful life. Even "nature" may carry less than it seems, since Paul can also write, "Does not nature itself teach you that if a man wears long hair it is a disgrace for him" (1 Corinthians 11:14), where nature plainly means something close to settled custom. And one of the two Greek words the ESV renders together as "men who practice homosexuality" is so rare in surviving literature that its exact edges are disputed.

Vines presses a second argument that is harder to wave off. The New Testament treats celibacy as a gift: "I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another" (1 Corinthians 7:7). For those without it, Paul counsels marriage, "For it is better to marry than to be aflame with passion" (7:9). Mandatory lifelong celibacy for every gay person, Vines argues, turns a gift Paul says not everyone has into a law. And the teaching bears fruit that can be seen. "Thus you will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:20), Jesus said, and the affirming Christian asks the church to look: at the despair and the hiding and the young people driven from their families, and then at the gay believer faithful to one person for decades, whose life shows patience, kindness and self-control. If the tree is bad, where is the bad fruit?

James Brownson's *Bible, Gender, Sexuality* (2013) comes at the question as a Reformed New Testament scholar asking about the moral logic beneath the texts. When the man sees the woman, he says, "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (Genesis 2:23), and Brownson argues that the emphasis falls on shared nature and kinship rather than anatomical difference; "one flesh" names the forming of a new family bond. He reads Romans 1 through the ancient concerns that shaped it: lust and excess, honor and shame, and a hierarchy that thought it degrading for a man to take a woman's role. If those are what Paul condemned, the texts do not address a committed, mutual, lifelong same-sex union, a thing the ancient world did not have in view.

Beneath all of it runs the argument from history. The church once quoted chapter and verse for slavery, whole libraries of it, and it was wrong, and it took the witness of the excluded to make it see. What if we are standing in that place again, defending with a handful of verses a cruelty our grandchildren won't believe we couldn't see? That argument has moral seriousness. Refuse to feel it and you have not understood it.

### The traditional reply, and the debt it carries

The traditional case gets the same refusal to caricature. Its first reply is that the argument begins in Genesis and the words of Jesus, and the prohibitions are the edge of that pattern rather than its center. Gagnon's second reply is historical. The ancient world did imagine lasting, even inborn, same-sex desire, as Aristophanes' speech in Plato's *Symposium* shows with its myth of divided humans seeking their other halves, some of them male and male. Romans 1:26 condemns women's same-sex relations, which cannot be explained as pederasty. And the rare word in 1 Corinthians 6 appears to have been formed from the Greek translation of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13, which forbid a man lying with a male without reference to age, rank or exploitation. On this reading Paul was repeating the sexual ethic he had inherited from Israel's Scripture, without exception clauses.

To the argument from celibacy, the tradition answers that the church has never promised anyone a sexual life. It asks chastity of the single man who wanted to marry and never did, of the widow who buried the only person she ever touched, of the woman whose husband left through no fault of hers. Either a faithful life without sex is livable for all of them, or the church has been lying to them for two thousand years. Jesus lived it whole and unmarried.

Hays's chapter is the reply at its most humane. He opened it with a friend, a gay Christian named Gary who died of AIDS, and wrote as a man who had loved someone the argument concerned. He concluded that the New Testament's witness against same-sex practice was consistent and that the church could not bless it, and he insisted that the church had no warrant to treat this sin as worse than others or to make gay people bear a stigma the greedy and the self-righteous never carry. Honesty requires one more fact. In *The Widening of God's Mercy* (2024), written with his son Christopher, Richard Hays said he had come to a different conclusion, reading the canon's long story of God enlarging his people as leading toward affirmation. The 1996 exegesis stands or falls on its merits, but a reader deserves to know its author changed his mind.

Put the two voices in the same room and spare neither. The traditionalist says: you have made experience the judge of Scripture, so tell me what it cannot revise. How do you know that what you call the Spirit is not the age you happen to live in, wearing the Spirit's coat? The affirming Christian answers: you found the one prohibition that costs straight people nothing and made it the wall, then told the gay teenager it was about your theology of the body when it was about your comfort. Your doctrine may be right. Your fruit has been rot. Neither has a clean answer to the other.

I am not above that scene. When I line up the arguments I can feel my own hands wanting the version that would cost me least, and I don't fully trust the wanting, on either side. The temptation on the traditional side is to love being right more than I love the person the rightness lands on. The temptation on the affirming side is to love being kind more than I love telling the truth. Both are in me. Neither is the Holy Spirit.

This essay lands with the historic teaching, the one the church has held across every culture for two thousand years: that God made us male and female, that the covenant of marriage is the one place he set apart for sexual union, and that a same-sex sexual relationship falls outside that design, however much love is in it. People inside this debate call the affirming view Side A and this one Side B, and I hold it the way Side B holds it. Same-sex attraction is not a sin to be hated out of a person. The call is the call to chastity that lands on every unmarried believer, the widow and the single man alongside the gay Christian, and it is not a lesser life. Whether this is a boundary of the faith or a difference between brothers is itself part of what faithful Christians have not agreed, and I won't pretend from a keyboard to have settled for you what the church has not settled for itself. Nor will I stand over your particular life and hand down a ruling on how you should carry a desire you did not choose.

Wesley Hill has lived inside that teaching and written about it without self-pity. In *Washed and Waiting* (2010) he describes himself as gay and celibate, takes his title from Paul's "you were washed" and from the Christian's waiting for "the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23), and does not pretend the waiting is easy. His *Spiritual Friendship* (2015) makes the claim the tradition most needs to hear: if the church asks gay believers to forgo marriage, it owes them committed friendship and a real place at the table. Jesus promised those who leave home and family for his sake "a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions" (Mark 10:30), and God promised the eunuchs who hold fast his covenant "a monument and a name better than sons and daughters" (Isaiah 56:5). The celibate gay believers I have known are not second-class saints. Some of them are the truest disciples in the room.

I won't pretend that costs nothing, and the cost is the objection I have not fully answered. I am asking a gay believer for a lifelong chastity I was never asked to bear, and the church that asks it has mostly failed to build the friendship and belonging that would make such a life anything but lonely. If we require the cross and withhold the community, we have preached a burden and kept back the body that was supposed to help carry it, and the fault is ours. I hold the historic teaching. I hold it with a debt I have not paid.

### What we will be asked

If you are gay and have read this far, past everything the church has done to make sure you wouldn't, you have shown more willingness to sit in the hard room than most of the people who talk loudest about you have ever shown you. You are not an issue. You never were. You are welcome to argue with every line, and if you finish unconvinced, I hope you can at least say the teaching was stated without contempt.

The story the historic ethic tells does not end at the prohibition. Every marriage on earth, the best and the worst, is a sign pointing at a wedding none of them is. Scripture closes with the church as a bride and a voice saying, "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9), and at that table the married and the widowed, the celibate gay believer and the straight woman who never found anyone, sit as equals, none of them shut out of the only union that lasts. The whole church groans for the redemption of the body. The ones who groan most honestly are often the ones who have been told least often that they belong.

I keep coming back to the pews in those stories, to years of voices and none of the lives behind them, and to how a church can get its doctrine into every sermon and miss the person in the room. Whatever it cost to land where this essay has landed, I am sure of one thing about the day I stand before God to give an account of the people I was given to pastor. He is not going to ask me first whether I was Side A or Side B. He is going to ask me whether anyone sitting in front of me was afraid to tell me the truth, and why. I do not yet have an answer that will hold.
