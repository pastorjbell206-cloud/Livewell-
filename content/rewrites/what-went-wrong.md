Nobody needs to be persuaded that something has gone wrong with the world. The argument has always been about what. Some say the trouble is ignorance, and education will cure it. Some say it is bad systems, and better laws will set the people inside them right. The most popular answer has always been other people: the other party, the other class, the other tribe. Each holds a piece of the truth, and none explains why the wrongness survives every reform. We educate ourselves and invent more sophisticated cruelties. We rebuild the system and watch the new one rot. We defeat our enemies and find their appetites alive in ourselves. G. K. Chesterton wrote a book called *What's Wrong with the World* (1910), and a story still circulates that when a newspaper put that question to famous writers, he answered in two words: "I am." No copy of the letter has ever turned up, and it is probably a legend. It survives because it names what the other answers leave out.

The Bible's word for what went wrong is *sin*, and it is the one word the modern world has most successfully retired. It lives on in dessert menus and in the mouths of partisans describing the other side, which is to say it survives as a joke or as a weapon. What Scripture means by it is stranger and more searching than either. Sin in the Bible is a deed, but it is also a condition and a power: a love that has turned away from God and curved back on itself, and in doing so has torn the fabric of a good world. Everything turns on one distinction. A doctrine can manufacture guilt in order to control people, or it can disclose a guilt already there in order to lift it. The first is a machine, and the church has sometimes run it. The second is a diagnosis, and a true diagnosis is the beginning of mercy, because it includes the one giving it. The line between the world as it is and the world as it should be doesn't run between me and my enemies. It runs through me.

## Three Hebrew words and a Greek one

The Old Testament has no single word for sin. It has a cluster, and three of its words travel together so often that they read like a formula. When the LORD passes before Moses on Sinai and proclaims his name, he describes himself as "forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exodus 34:7). When David finally breaks after the prophet Nathan's visit, he reaches for all three in the opening lines of the fifty-first psalm:

> Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love; according to your abundant mercy blot out my transgressions. (Psalm 51:1)

"Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin!" (Psalm 51:2). Transgression, iniquity, sin: in Hebrew *pesha'*, *'awon* and *hatta't*. Each names a different face of one wrong.

The last is the most common, and it comes with a famous picture. Its verb describes the left-handed slingers of Benjamin, every one of whom "could sling a stone at a hair and not miss" (Judges 20:16), and from that verse generations of preachers have taught that sin means missing the mark. The New Testament's Greek word, *hamartia*, gets the same treatment; Aristotle's *Poetics* (c. 335 BC) used it for the error of judgment that brings down a tragic hero who is not wicked, only mistaken. The picture is true as far as it goes, but it doesn't go far. James Barr's *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961) took apart the habit of reading theology out of word roots, as though a word's origin were a secret definition hidden under every use. Words mean what their uses make them mean. An archer who misses the target isn't guilty of anything, and if sin were only a miss it would be a mistake, which is exactly what our age would like it to be. In the law of Moses, *hatta't* names a moral offense against God, and the same word names the sacrifice that deals with it. Its first appearance in the Bible is not an arrow at all. God warns Cain, before he kills his brother, that "sin is crouching at the door" (Genesis 4:7), an animal waiting to spring.

*Pesha'* is a political word, what a vassal does to his overlord: "After the death of Ahab, Moab rebelled against Israel" (2 Kings 1:1). The prophets carried it from the world of treaties into Israel's covenant with God, and Isaiah puts it inside a family: "Children have I reared and brought up, but they have rebelled against me" (Isaiah 1:2). Sin in this sense is a breach of loyalty against someone. That is why David, whose crimes had a woman and her murdered husband as their victims, could still pray, "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight" (Psalm 51:4). He wasn't pretending Bathsheba and Uriah hadn't been wronged. He was confessing that the wrong done to them was also done to the God who made them.

*'Awon*, usually rendered iniquity, is often linked to a root meaning to bend or twist, though the lexicons disagree and the etymology shouldn't carry much weight. Its range is not in doubt. It covers the twisted act, the guilt it leaves, and the punishment that follows. When Cain is sentenced he cries, "My punishment is greater than I can bear" (Genesis 4:13), and the word for punishment is *'awon*. Hebrew doesn't neatly separate the deed from the weight it leaves on the doer, which is why Isaiah can say of the suffering servant that "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6). How that exchange works is the subject of [why Jesus had to die](/writing/why-did-jesus-have-to-die).

The New Testament adds *parabasis*, stepping over a known line, and *anomia*, lawlessness, and John joins them bluntly: "Everyone who makes a practice of sinning also practices lawlessness; sin is lawlessness" (1 John 3:4). Taken together, the vocabulary refuses to let sin be only one thing. Cornelius Plantinga Jr., in *Not the Way It's Supposed to Be: A Breviary of Sin* (1995), gathered it all under *shalom*, which means far more than the absence of conflict. Shalom, as Plantinga describes it, is God, humanity and all creation woven together in justice, fulfillment and delight. Sin is the culpable vandalism of it. He keeps a careful distinction: an earthquake is evil, but no one is to blame for it, and sin is the evil for which someone is answerable, finally to a personal God. The legal picture of sin as a broken rule is true, but it is the smaller truth. The rules were fences around a garden. Sin is what happens to the garden.

## A power that reigns, and a love that curves inward

Ask most Christians what sin is and they give a list: lying, lust, cruelty, theft. Scripture names those, but the list answers a smaller question than the one Paul asks in Romans. Watch his grammar in chapters 5 through 7. *Hamartia* there is usually singular, carries the article, and is the subject of the verb. Sin "came into the world through one man, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12). Sin "reigned in death" (Romans 5:21). Sin takes a good commandment and turns it into a weapon: "For sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me" (Romans 7:11). Paul is describing a tyrant, the crouching animal Cain was warned about, and his command has the shape of resistance to an occupier: "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, to make you obey its passions" (Romans 6:12).

Then comes the chapter every honest reader recognizes: "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing" (Romans 7:19). Readers have argued for sixteen centuries about who this "I" is. Augustine first took it as a man under the law before grace and later changed his mind and read it as the Christian still at war with himself; many modern scholars take it as a figure for Israel, or humanity, under the law in Adam. On the point that matters here, every reading agrees. The person in Romans 7 does not lack information. He knows the law is holy and the commandment "holy and righteous and good" (Romans 7:12) and he wants to keep it, and knowing and wanting are not enough. "Now if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me" (Romans 7:20). That is not an excuse, since the chapter ends in anguish rather than self-acquittal. It is a description of bondage. The man who preaches against anger and loses his temper with his children on the drive home from church isn't short of a sermon on anger. Something in him runs deeper than what he knows.

Augustine spent his life describing that something, and his answer was love. In the second book of the *Confessions* (c. 397-400) he tells how, at sixteen, he and his friends stole pears they didn't want and threw most of them to pigs. What he loved, he came to see, was the stealing itself, and in the end his own undoing. The story is small on purpose. Sin, for Augustine, is rarely a love of evil things, since everything God made is good. It is a disorder in how we love good things. In *The City of God* (413-426) he defined virtue briefly as rightly ordered love (15.22), and he told the whole human story as a tale of two loves: love of self reaching to contempt of God, and love of God reaching to contempt of self (14.28). A man who loves his work more than his wife, or his nation more than his neighbor, may love nothing bad in itself. He has put the loves in the wrong order, and that is enough to ruin a family or a country. Plantinga calls sin a parasite for the same reason. It creates nothing. It can only spoil what God made, which is why it so rarely looks like itself. Pride borrows the face of self-respect, and cruelty often borrows the face of justice.

Martin Luther, lecturing on Romans in 1515 and 1516, gave Augustine's diagnosis the image that stuck. Fallen human nature, he said, is *incurvatus in se*, curved in on itself, bent so far inward that it turns the gifts of God, and even God, into instruments for its own ends. The image is frightening because it describes religious people at least as well as irreligious ones. A curved heart can pray, preach and serve, and feed itself on all three. I have to be careful here, because this is the sentence I'd most like to aim at someone else. The pride that drives much of what I do doesn't leave because I'm a pastor. It learns to dress as pastoral dedication or theological seriousness, and it is recognizable to anyone paying attention, including me when I'm honest.

The image has had a serious critic. In 1960 Valerie Saiving published "The Human Situation: A Feminine View," arguing that theologians who defined sin chiefly as pride were generalizing from men's experience, and that for many women the characteristic temptation was the opposite, the loss of a self dissolved into other people's demands. She caught something the preaching of pride has often missed: the person who never takes up the life God gave her is not thereby humble. But the older picture has room for her point. If sin is love in the wrong order, it can take the shape of self-exaltation or of self-erasure, and both can stay curved inward, measuring everything by what it does to me. The question was never how much we love ourselves. It is what we have put in the place of God. Jeremiah's verdict covers every version: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). Nor does the curved heart stay inside the skin. It builds laws, markets and habits in its own shape, and those go on bending the people born into them, which is the subject of [what the Bible says about systemic sin](/writing/individual-sin-and-systemic-sin-explained).

## Original sin: Augustine, Pelagius and the East

If sin is a condition and not only a choice, where does the condition come from? The argument deserves to be told first from the side of the man who lost it. Pelagius, a British monk teaching in Rome around 400, was appalled by baptized Christians living slack lives and blaming the weakness of the flesh. His *Letter to Demetrias* (c. 413) argues that God, who made human nature and knows what it can do, does not command the impossible. If God commands holiness, we can be holy. In Pelagius's teaching, Adam harmed his descendants by bad example, which we follow by choice and habit, not by a damaged nature passed down. Grace was real for Pelagius, but it came as the law, the teaching and example of Christ, and the forgiveness of past sins, offered to a will free to take them. His strongest point still has force, and every modern objection descends from it: responsibility seems to require ability. If I was born with a guilt I never chose, how is God just to hold it against me?

Augustine answered in a series of treatises beginning with *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants* (412), and the Council of Carthage condemned the Pelagian teaching in 418, a condemnation the Council of Ephesus confirmed in 431. He read Romans 5:12 in a Latin text that said all sinned "in whom," that is, in Adam, and took it to mean the race was somehow present in the first man's act. Most modern commentators take the Greek phrase *eph' ho* to mean "because," and on that phrase his translation misled him. But his case never rested on one phrase. David's confession, "Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me" (Psalm 51:5), locates the problem before any deed. Paul says all of us were once "by nature children of wrath" (Ephesians 2:3). And Paul's parallel between Adam and Christ runs both ways: "For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). The fallen will still chooses, but from a bent center, so that fallen humanity is, in the Latin tag later theologians used to sum him up, *non posse non peccare*, unable not to sin.

The Eastern churches read the same texts another way, and their reading deserves its own voice. The Greek fathers speak far less of inherited guilt and far more of inherited mortality and corruption. John Meyendorff, in *Byzantine Theology* (1974), argued that Romans 5:12 can be read to say that death spread to all, and because of death all sinned: we inherit Adam's mortality, and mortality breeds the fear that breeds sin. Hebrews speaks of Christ coming to "deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Hebrews 2:15). Orthodox writers often say ancestral sin rather than original sin to mark the difference. The East is not Pelagian. It confesses universal bondage and the absolute need of grace. What it declines is the claim that a newborn stands guilty for an act he did not commit.

Modern objections sharpen the older ones. Immanuel Kant, in *Religion within the Bounds of Mere Reason* (1793), startled his Enlightenment readers by affirming a radical evil in human nature, a universal propensity to put self-love above the moral law. Yet he judged the picture of that evil as an inheritance from our first parents the least fitting way to represent it, since guilt is the most personal thing there is. Questions about human origins are real too, and they are weighed in [how Christians read Genesis 1](/writing/how-to-read-genesis-one) rather than re-argued here.

Sort the question by order before settling it. What the church binds, and what Pelagius was condemned for denying, is first-order: "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23), no one is saved by unaided power, and grace goes first. How the condition passes down, whether we inherit guilt or corruption or both, whether Adam is our representative head or we were somehow in him: these are second-order, and the Roman, Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed and Wesleyan traditions have not agreed on them. The neighboring quarrel over grace and the will is told in [predestination and free will](/writing/predestination-and-free-will).

This essay lands with Augustine against Pelagius. Sin is a condition before it is a choice. We don't originate it, and yet we own it, because every generation without exception has consented to it and made it its own. Reinhold Niebuhr pressed the paradox in *The Nature and Destiny of Man* (1941): sin is universal, and in that sense inevitable, yet never necessary. No one sins by fate, and everyone recognizes the sin as his own. The strongest objection is Kant's, and it has not been fully answered. How can I answer for a bent I didn't choose? The tradition's best reply is solidarity. The human race is one family, bound together in its ruin as it can be bound together in its rescue, and the chapter that says we were made sinners in one man says we are made righteous in another. That reply goes a long way without dissolving the mystery.

## How sin became sickness, mistake and wound

In 1973 a psychiatrist asked the question preachers had stopped asking. Karl Menninger, co-founder of the Menninger Clinic in Topeka, published *Whatever Became of Sin?* and argued that the word had not been refuted so much as redistributed. Some of it went to the courts and became crime. Much of it went to medicine and became illness, or dissolved into a collective irresponsibility in which everyone is implicated and no one is to blame. His point was that a person who is only sick can be treated, while a person who has sinned can repent, and repentance is a form of hope. Philip Rieff had described the same shift in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966): the religious person of older cultures knew he needed saving, and the new psychological person knows only that he needs to feel well. Chesterton had seen it coming. In the second chapter of *Orthodoxy* (1908) he remarked that some modern theologians were disputing original sin, the one part of Christian theology, he said, that can really be proved. The evidence walks past us in the street and looks back at us from the mirror.

Some of the redistribution was right, and the church has to say so first. Depression is not a lack of faith, and addiction has a biology as well as a moral history. Christians were told for generations that their illness was spiritual failure, and pastors deepened their suffering by calling it sin. If you carry that weight, read [why depression is not a lack of faith](/writing/mental-health-and-the-church-beyond-pray-about-it); this essay is not medical advice, and a doctor belongs in that story alongside a pastor. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

What we need is four categories held apart. A mistake is an error of means. A sickness befalls us. A wound is done to us. A sin is a wrong love we have consented to and must answer for. Call a sickness a sin and you crush a sufferer under guilt that isn't his. Call a sin a sickness and you take from the sinner both the dignity of responsibility and the hope of pardon.

Our culture did not stop moralizing when it stopped saying sin. It kept the accusation and discarded the absolution, so there is a verdict for everything and a pardon for nothing, and the verdict always falls on someone else. The right locates sin in the other side's morals and the left in the other side's structures, and both leave the accuser outside the indictment. Alan Jacobs, in *Original Sin: A Cultural History* (2008), argues that for all its misuses the doctrine has a leveling power. If everyone is implicated, no class can set itself up as the pure, and a confessed common ruin becomes a kind of solidarity. Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn learned the same thing in the Soviet camps and wrote in *The Gulag Archipelago* (1973) that the line dividing good and evil runs not between states or classes or parties but through every human heart. He found it running through his own.

## The strongest objection: a machine for making guilt

The sharpest case against the doctrine isn't that it is false but that it is useful to the wrong people. Friedrich Nietzsche made it brilliantly in *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887). The German word for guilt, *Schuld*, is also the word for debt, and he argued that moral guilt grew out of the old relation of creditor and debtor. Then he told a story about the priest. Suffering people want someone to blame, and the priest turns their resentment back on themselves, renames their misery as sin, and binds them to himself as the only physician for a disease he has named. Sigmund Freud, in *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), added that the sense of guilt is the central problem of civilization and that we pay for progress in happiness lost to it. Together they make religion a cosmic creditor selling relief from an anxiety it created.

The charge is not only theory. Children have been told they were worms before they were told they were loved. Teenagers have been shamed over their bodies until they couldn't pray without self-loathing. Preachers have thundered at the sins outside the building while protecting the sins on the platform, and abusers have used "we are all sinners" to erase the difference between the one who harmed and the one harmed. Many who left the faith didn't find the doctrine of sin too hard to believe. They watched it used as a lever. If that is your story, [religious trauma is real](/writing/religious-trauma-is-real). If you are being harmed at home, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233, and no doctrine of sin ever requires anyone to stay under the blow.

So concede what Nietzsche saw, and then look at what the machine needs to run. It needs guilt that never ends, a class of people exempt from the diagnosis, and an accusation always aimed at someone else. Scripture breaks all three. Nathan comes to King David, who has taken another man's wife and arranged his death, and tells him of a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb. David pronounces sentence, and the prophet turns it: "Nathan said to David, 'You are the man!'" (2 Samuel 12:7). The king who judged at a safe distance finds the sin in his own chest. Confession and answer then arrive in a single verse: "David said to Nathan, 'I have sinned against the LORD.' And Nathan said to David, 'The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die'" (2 Samuel 12:13). Uriah is still dead, and the consequences run on for chapters, but the guilt is disclosed in order to be removed, not kept. Paul, having confessed the war of Romans 7, opens the next chapter with the sentence the machine can never say: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). And no one is exempt. A preacher who believes the doctrine cannot stand outside it, and a church that uses it only on outsiders has stopped believing it. Nietzsche's priest is precisely the figure the doctrine forbids. The machine runs on a counterfeit, with the self-implication cut out and the pardon held back, and only a race curved in on itself could take a teaching of universal need and free pardon and make it an instrument of control. The abuse confirms the diagnosis.

## What the diagnosis leaves in our hands

The shape of the problem decides the shape of the cure. If sin were ignorance, education would mend it; if it were only behavior, better habits and firmer accountability would do. Those aren't worthless, but compliance alone tends to produce people skilled at hiding what they are. A disorder of love has to be met at the root, which is why the New Testament speaks of new birth and new creation rather than self-improvement, and why real change comes slowly and by grace, as [how people actually change](/writing/how-do-you-actually-change) describes.

We cannot stop grading the world. We rage at injustice and grieve betrayal with a fierceness that makes no sense unless things were meant to be otherwise, and that ache is the memory of shalom. It is also the instrument we use to judge everyone but ourselves. I know the asymmetry from the inside. I can diagnose a colleague's pride quickly and be slow to notice the same thing in me. A fracture that ran neatly outside us would be easy to see. This one is hard to see because it runs through the faculty we use to look.

Jesus told of two men praying in the temple. One thanked God he was not like other people, and named them. The other stood far off, beat his breast, and prayed, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13). It was the second who went home justified. The first had a doctrine of sin and applied it to everyone but himself. The second didn't call himself a mistake or a casualty of his circumstances, though he may have been both. He said the word our age has retired, about himself, and it was the word that sent him home forgiven.

The world did not break the way a machine breaks. It broke the way a covenant breaks, through loyalty betrayed and love turned away, and a broken covenant can be restored, at cost, by the one who was wronged. The Christian claim is that God paid that cost himself, in the Son who carried the iniquity of us all to a cross and came out of the grave on the third day without it.

Writing the claim down settles nothing. What it leaves is Paul's cry, aimed at no one but himself: "Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" (Romans 7:24). The world has a great many accusers and very few confessors. Every argument about what went wrong ends at a door in our own chest, and the question left standing is whether we will say the word about ourselves before we say it about anyone else.
