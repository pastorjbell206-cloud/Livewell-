The question usually arrives at a kitchen table, and it rarely arrives as theory. A mother has found burnt foil in her son's room. A wife has counted the pills in the medicine cabinet for the third time this month. A twelve-year-old has learned to read her father's evening from the way his truck door closes in the driveway. Sooner or later someone in that house asks the question the church has been asked for two hundred years: is this a sin or a disease? Underneath it sit two older questions, one about blame and one about hope. If it's a sin, then he chose it, he can stop, and the family's anger is fair. If it's a disease, then he didn't choose it, nobody is allowed to be angry, and everyone waits on a cure. Held alone, each answer leaves the house with less than it needs.

A practical word first, because some who read this are nowhere near a safe distance. If someone in front of you is unresponsive or not breathing, call 911. Naloxone, which can reverse an opioid overdose, has been available without a prescription in the United States since the FDA approved an over-the-counter nasal spray in 2023, and a household that loves someone who uses opioids should keep it where anyone can find it. SAMHSA's National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, is free, confidential and open around the clock; it can point you toward treatment and support where you live, whether you are the one using or the one lying awake. If you or someone you love is thinking about suicide, call or text 988. Nothing here is medical advice. Decisions about detox, medication and treatment belong with a physician or an addiction specialist who knows the person. And nothing here is written to shame anyone. Shame is one of the fuels addiction runs on, and the church has supplied more than its share.

The argument that follows is that the sin-or-disease question is badly formed, and that our answers to it have never been only a matter of theology or medicine. Scripture has a third word, older than both of ours and more exact: bondage. A slave is really bound, and a slave still answers for what he does inside the chains. The distinction this essay turns on is between the causes that explain a behavior and the guilt that attaches to it, which in the Bible's own grammar is the difference between sin as something we do and sin as a power we live under. Christians have tended to remember the first and forget the second; the medical account rediscovered the second in secular dress and lost the first. And because this country has answered the blame question differently depending on who was addicted and who was making money, addiction is also a question of justice, one the church has to judge with a single instrument whichever political direction the evidence points.

## How a vice became a disease

The oldest descriptions of addiction don't use either of our words. The sages of Proverbs drew the drinker with a precision that anticipates the clinic by more than two millennia: the woe, the quarrels, the wounds without cause, the red eyes, and then the line that every family of an addicted person will recognize.

> "They struck me," you will say, "but I was not hurt; they beat me, but I did not feel it. When shall I awake? I must have another drink." (Proverbs 23:35, ESV)

The damage goes unfelt, the harm is narrated away, and the first thought on waking is the next drink. The sage calls it folly, but he describes it as something that happens to a person as well as something a person does. Aristotle, working the same problem from the other side in Book VII of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, asked how a man can know the better and do the worse, the condition he called *akrasia*, weakness of will. His answer was that such a man has his knowledge the way a sleeper or a drunk has it: possessed, but not in use.

The modern argument begins in Philadelphia. In 1784 Benjamin Rush, physician and signer of the Declaration of Independence, published a pamphlet on spirituous liquors that he revised for decades, later editions carrying the title *An Inquiry into the Effects of Ardent Spirits upon the Human Body and Mind*, and in it he came to describe habitual drunkenness as a disease, progressive, marked by a loss of control, and curable only by total abstinence. The historian Harry Gene Levine argued in "The Discovery of Addiction" (1978) that this was a real turning point. Colonial Americans had condemned drunkenness freely, but they assumed that people drank because they wanted to and could stop if they chose. The idea that desire itself could become a master was, in Levine's account, a discovery of the late eighteenth century, and the temperance movement built on it. For the temperance preachers the enslaving power lived in the bottle, and the logic ran straight to the Eighteenth Amendment, ratified in 1919 and repealed in 1933.

After repeal the problem moved. It was no longer alcohol that enslaved, since most people plainly drank without ruin; it was a minority of drinkers with some special vulnerability. That relocation made room for two things that arrived almost together: Alcoholics Anonymous in 1935, and the scientific program associated with E. M. Jellinek at the Yale Center of Alcohol Studies. Jellinek's *The Disease Concept of Alcoholism* (1960) is more careful than its reputation. He sorted problem drinking into several types and argued that only some of them, those marked by loss of control or an inability to abstain, deserved the name disease. The American Medical Association had classed alcoholism as an illness in 1956, and by 1960 the disease concept had become the dominant public account.

The account's modern form came from neuroscience. In 1997 Alan Leshner, then director of the National Institute on Drug Abuse, published a short essay in *Science* titled "Addiction Is a Brain Disease, and It Matters." His successor, Nora Volkow, who has led NIDA since 2003, developed the model with George Koob and A. Thomas McLellan in a 2016 review in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. At its strongest, the model holds that repeated use of an addictive substance alters three systems in the brain: the reward circuitry, which learns to prize the drug and to find ordinary pleasures dull; the stress systems, which make the absence of the drug feel like an emergency; and the prefrontal regions responsible for weighing consequences and restraining impulse. The person is left wanting the drug more, enjoying it less, suffering without it, and less able to stop.

The disease model deserves its due before anyone argues with it, because it did real good. It moved the drunk from the jail cell toward the hospital. It gave families a way to speak about a husband or a daughter that wasn't contempt. It made room for medicine, and since Vincent Dole and Marie Nyswander reported on methadone maintenance in 1965, medications for opioid addiction have been associated in a large body of research with fewer overdose deaths. It also carried a truth the church should have said first: a man who believes he is simply bad often concludes he is past help, and a man told he is sick may finally be willing to be treated. Those who defend the model are not excusing anyone. Many of them have spent their lives keeping people alive long enough to change.

## What the critics of the disease model see

The strongest criticism of the disease model comes from inside psychology and neuroscience, not from preachers, and it deserves the same fair hearing.

Gene Heyman, a psychologist who taught at Harvard, made the case in *Addiction: A Disorder of Choice* (2009). Drawing on national surveys of the general population rather than clinic patients, he argued that most people who at some point meet the criteria for addiction eventually stop, the majority by their early thirties, and most of them without formal treatment. The picture of addiction as a lifelong, relapsing illness was drawn from the minority who never quit and who sought treatment. Heyman leaned too on Lee Robins's study of American soldiers who used heroin in Vietnam: most of those who had been addicted overseas did not return to addiction once they were home, which is not how a disease of the brain would be expected to behave. Addiction, he argued, responds to consequences, incentives, costs and circumstances, the things that govern choice, and it thrives when a person evaluates each decision on its own, where the drug usually wins, instead of choosing a pattern of life as a whole, where it usually loses. Heyman's argument was that addiction is choice gone badly wrong, not the absence of choice.

Marc Lewis came at the question from a different door. A developmental neuroscientist who was himself addicted to drugs as a young man, he argued in *The Biology of Desire* (2015) that the brain changes the disease model points to are real but are not evidence of disease. They are what learning looks like. The same circuits that narrow around a drug narrow around a beloved person, a vocation or a faith; the brain is built to be reshaped by what we repeatedly pursue, especially what we pursue to relieve pain. Addiction, in Lewis's account, is deep learning of a narrow desire, usually rooted in the emotional history of the person, and recovery is further development rather than the remission of an illness. He worried that the disease label teaches people they are helpless before their own neurons.

Christian critics pressed a related point in theological terms. Edward Welch, a counselor with the Christian Counseling and Educational Foundation, argued in *Addictions: A Banquet in the Grave* (2001) that addiction is at root a disorder of worship. Welch didn't deny the body's part, but he insisted that the heart of addiction is a heart that has given itself to something other than God, and that a purely medical vocabulary quietly removes the category the Bible uses to name it. Kent Dunnington, a philosopher, took the most careful path in *Addiction and Virtue* (2011). He argued that both models are too thin, and that the older tradition of Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas already had the right concept: habit, a settled disposition formed by our own acts that comes to operate beneath our deliberation. A habit is ours, since we built it, and it governs us, since we can't simply decide it away. Dunnington read addiction as a disordered habit that carries a religious hunger, a counterfeit of the devotion only God should receive.

Set the two sides next to each other. The disease model is right that the addicted brain is changed and that willpower alone almost never frees anyone. The critics are right that those changes are the kind desire and repetition always produce, that people are more than the patients of their neurons, and that choice never entirely leaves the room. What neither side can say easily is the thing both are describing: something chosen that has become unchosen. That is only a paradox for a culture that has split the person into a body that suffers things and a will that decides things. Scripture never made that split, and so it never had to choose between the two words. The family at the table assumes that a disease can't be a sin and a sin can't be a disease. The Christian tradition at its best never accepted that. The most common human condition is the one where the thing destroying me is both mine and stronger than me.

## The divided will: Augustine and the wretched man of Romans 7

No one has described that condition better than Augustine, and he did it from inside it. In the eighth book of the *Confessions*, written around 397, he tells of the summer of 386 in Milan, when he was thirty-one, persuaded of the truth of the faith and unable to live it. His bondage was sexual rather than chemical, but the anatomy is the same. He had once prayed for chastity and self-control and added, in effect, not yet. Now he found two wills in himself, an old one hardened by long use and a new one too weak to master it, and he traced how the chain was forged: a perverse will gave birth to desire, desire indulged became habit, and habit unresisted became necessity. The mind commands the hand and is obeyed at once, he observed; the mind commands itself and is refused. What makes the passage so honest is that Augustine never uses the chain as an excuse. He forged it link by link, he says, and he could not break it. Both sentences are true, and he refuses to give up either one.

He was freed in a garden, weeping, when he heard a child's voice chanting "take up and read," opened Paul's letter to the Romans, and read words that name drunkenness among the works of the night and end with a command.

> But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires. (Romans 13:14, ESV)

He didn't describe that moment as a triumph of his will but as a gift his will had been unable to give itself. He read his experience through Romans 7, and Christians still reach for that chapter when they talk about addiction. It has to be read in its setting, because it isn't a clinical case study. Paul is arguing about the law of God, which is holy and good, and asking why it can't make anyone good. He answers by describing a man who agrees with the law and cannot keep it.

> For I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate. (Romans 7:15, ESV)

> For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing. (Romans 7:19, ESV)

Who this "I" is has divided faithful readers for centuries: Paul before his conversion, Israel under the law, or the believer still at war with the flesh. Augustine himself changed his mind, reading it early of the man under law and later of the mature apostle. It is a question on which the church can leave room. Either way, Paul's language for sin in this chapter is more than a list of acts. Sin "dwells within me" (Romans 7:17, ESV). It is a power that makes war and takes prisoners.

> but I see in my members another law waging war against the law of my mind and making me captive to the law of sin that dwells in my members. (Romans 7:23, ESV)

This is the distinction the sin-or-disease question lacks. In Scripture sin is both a deed a person is answerable for and a power a person is held by, and Paul speaks of both without apology. The man of Romans 7 is captive, and he still calls himself wretched; he doesn't blame the power and he doesn't pretend he can master it. And Paul's point is that the law, more rules and more resolve, can't free him. The law can describe the chain precisely. It can't cut it.

> "Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" (Romans 7:24, ESV)

The question is who, not what: not which program, which method, which stronger decision. The answer comes as thanksgiving to God through Jesus Christ and then, at the opening of the next chapter, as the sentence the whole chapter has been waiting for: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV). No condemnation and a new power at work within, the Spirit, are exactly what neither moralism nor medicine can supply on its own.

The psychiatrist Gerald May saw how far this reaches. In *Addiction and Grace* (1988) he argued that addiction isn't the peculiar disorder of a few. Everyone is addicted, he wrote, in the sense that everyone has attached desire to things that were never meant to carry it: approval, work, control, food, security, the esteem of the right people. May believed that only grace frees desire, and that grace doesn't bypass the struggle but meets us in it. That puts every reader inside the diagnosis, the writer first. We who have looked at the addicted with pity from the pew have usually been attached to things our culture rewards instead of punishes, and the reward kept us from noticing the chain. Addiction is sin's captivity made visible, and the rest of us are simply better at hiding ours.

## What the Twelve Steps learned from the church

The most famous response to addiction of the twentieth century was built from Christian parts. Bill Wilson, a New York stockbroker ruined by drink, was visited late in 1934 by an old drinking friend, Ebby Thacher, who had gotten sober through the Oxford Group, an evangelical movement led by the Lutheran minister Frank Buchman that practiced surrender to God, confession of sin to another person, restitution to those harmed and daily guidance in prayer. Wilson had a spiritual experience at Towns Hospital that December, and in May 1935, tempted in Akron, Ohio, he sought out another drinker and was introduced to a surgeon, Dr. Robert Smith. Dr. Bob's last drink, in June 1935, is counted as the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous. The Episcopal rector Samuel Shoemaker of Calvary Church in New York, a leader of the Oxford Group in America, shaped Wilson's thinking enough that Wilson credited him as a primary source of the Steps, which were published in the book *Alcoholics Anonymous* in 1939.

Look at the Steps and the church's own furniture is everywhere. The admission of powerlessness is Romans 7. The turning of the will and life over to God is conversion. The fearless moral inventory is the old examination of conscience. Admitting one's wrongs to God, to oneself and to another human being is what James commanded: "Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16, ESV). The amends are Zacchaeus restoring fourfold. The historian Ernest Kurtz argued in *Not-God* (1979) that AA's deepest insight is the confession of limitation, that the alcoholic's first freedom is admitting he is not God. Even AA's framing of the illness held both truths at once: the alcoholic is powerless over the drink, and he is responsible for listing his wrongs and repairing them.

AA also parted company with the Oxford Group within a few years and spoke of God "as we understood Him," so that Catholics, agnostics and the church-wounded could sit in the same room. Christians have complained that this makes the Higher Power too vague, and the complaint has force; a power without a name can't forgive anyone. But the fairer judgment falls on us. The church had practiced confession, restitution and the fellowship of sinners for centuries, and then kept its sanctuaries for people who looked fine and sent its drunks to the basement on Tuesday nights. AA didn't steal the church's practices. We gave them away because we were embarrassed by the people who needed them most.

## Who profits, and who goes to prison

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported more than 106,000 drug overdose deaths in the United States in 2021, most involving opioids, and most of those involving synthetic opioids such as fentanyl. The CDC has described the epidemic in waves: prescription opioids from the 1990s, heroin from around 2010, illicit fentanyl from around 2013. The first wave was not an accident. In 2007 an affiliate of Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty in federal court to a felony for misbranding OxyContin, having promoted it as less addictive and less subject to abuse than other opioids, and three company executives pleaded guilty to misdemeanors; the penalties exceeded six hundred million dollars. In 2020 Purdue Pharma itself pleaded guilty to three federal felonies, including conspiracy to defraud the United States and conspiracies to violate the federal anti-kickback law. In June 2024, in *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma*, the Supreme Court rejected the bankruptcy plan that would have shielded the Sackler family, who owned the company, from opioid lawsuits without the consent of those bringing them.

Anne Case and Angus Deaton gave the wider pattern a name in *Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism* (2020): rising deaths from drugs, alcohol and suicide among Americans without a four-year degree, where good work had vanished and marriage, unions and churches had thinned out with it. Their account indicts both the market that hollowed out those towns and the cultural collapse that left people with nothing to hold. The psychologist Bruce Alexander had argued in *The Globalization of Addiction* (2008) that addiction flourishes wherever people are dislocated from belonging and purpose. James saw the same connection between the powerful and the crushed: "Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:4, ESV).

Then there is the prison. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 set a mandatory five-year sentence for five grams of crack cocaine and for five hundred grams of powder, a hundred-to-one disparity that fell hardest on Black neighborhoods; the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 cut it to eighteen-to-one, not to parity. Michelle Alexander argued in *The New Jim Crow* (2010) that the drug war functioned as a system of racial control. James Forman Jr. complicated that account in *Locking Up Our Own* (2017), showing that many Black leaders in Washington demanded tough enforcement because their own neighborhoods were being destroyed; the 1986 law passed with broad support from both parties. Grief, not only malice, built the machine. But it is hard to set the crack years beside the opioid years and miss how much more often the whiter, more rural epidemic was met with the language of treatment.

The instrument has to cut both ways. The characteristic failure of the right has been moralism: preaching responsibility to the targeted, trusting prison to heal what it mostly hardens, and naming the dealer on the corner more readily than the company in the boardroom. The characteristic failure of the left has been a compassion that sometimes stops believing in freedom. Oregon's Measure 110, passed in 2020, decriminalized possession of small amounts of drugs; in 2024 the state's legislature restored criminal penalties. Its defenders argue, with some justice, that an arrest and a record drive people away from the help they need, and that the promised treatment money arrived too slowly. Still, harm reduction that keeps a person alive while he moves toward freedom is mercy, and harm reduction that stops expecting freedom is abandonment with kinder manners. And states run by both parties now draw revenue from lotteries, cannabis and, since the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in *Murphy v. NCAA*, sports betting, and some of that money comes from citizens who can no longer stop. Meanwhile treatment stays scarce: SAMHSA's annual national survey has found year after year that most Americans who meet the criteria for a substance use disorder receive no treatment for it. Ezekiel's charge against Israel's shepherds fits every party and every pulpit: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4, ESV). I have preached surrender to the addicted far more often than I have named what was done to their towns. Surrender was in my vocabulary, and predation was not.

## A parent, a spouse, a child

All of this comes home to the kitchen table. The parent of an addicted son or daughter lives between two terrors: that love will enable the addiction, and that firmness will end in a funeral. Jesus told of a father in that place who let the younger son go and didn't follow him into the far country with money. But he watched the road. "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20, ESV). Love that refuses to fund the addiction and refuses to close the door is the shape of the Father's own heart, and it is agonizing to hold. Parents shouldn't hold it alone. Al-Anon, founded in 1951 by Lois Wilson and Anne B., exists because families of the addicted need their own recovery, and a church can be that kind of company.

The spouse carries a covenant and, often, a danger. Addiction can bring rage, debt, betrayal and violence into a house, and no vow requires anyone to endure abuse. If you are being hurt, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233. Separating for safety betrays nothing. Forgiveness can be given freely; trust is rebuilt slowly, by truth told over time, and the one who broke it doesn't get to set the schedule.

The child needs to hear what family recovery groups have said for decades: you didn't cause it, you can't control it, and you can't cure it. Children in addicted homes often become small adults, caretakers and peacemakers and invisible ones, and the church's job is to have at least one grown-up who notices, asks, and doesn't go away.

The Gospels record a detail that belongs here. At the cross they offered Jesus wine mixed with myrrh, "but he did not take it" (Mark 15:23, ESV). The point isn't that relief from pain is sin; medicine is a mercy. It is that the Son of God went into the worst of human suffering without numbing himself, so that those who can't bear their pain without numbing might not be left alone in it. He carried the guilt that is ours and broke the power that holds us, which is why the servant of Isaiah is described the way he is: "a bruised reed he will not break, and a faintly burning wick he will not quench; he will faithfully bring forth justice" (Isaiah 42:3, ESV). Mercy for the bruised and justice for the wronged come from the same hands.

So the question at the table doesn't resolve into sin or disease. It becomes a question about the rest of us. The person bound by addiction needs to hear two sentences from the same mouth: your sin can be forgiven, and God saw what was done to you. Whether there is a church anywhere near that kitchen able to say both, and to mean both, and to stay long enough to prove it, is not settled by this essay. It is settled one household at a time, by people who know they are captives too.
