In the late summer of 2020 a photograph traveled around the country of two girls sitting on the pavement outside a fast-food restaurant in Salinas, California, laptops balanced on their knees, borrowing the restaurant's wireless signal so they could attend school. Their classrooms had closed that spring, like nearly every classroom in America, and school had moved into the home on the assumption that the home had a connection. Theirs didn't. The picture moved people because it was so plain. Two children were doing their homework in the only place it could be done.

The picture also let us off lightly, and that's worth saying at the start. It invited us to feel the divide as a piece of pandemic bad luck, a gap that opened when the schools closed and would close again when they reopened. The schools reopened. The gap stayed, because it was never a pandemic event. The pandemic only turned the lights on in a room the country had been living beside for a quarter of a century.

The digital divide is the gap between people who can reliably reach the internet, with a connection fast enough, a device fit for the task and the skill to use both, and people who can't. It's usually argued about as telecommunications policy or as a line in a federal budget, and it is both. It's also something older: the question the Torah put to every field in Israel at harvest: what does a productive system owe the people at its edge? The answer Leviticus gives cuts against the market's champions and the program's champions alike, and it lands on the church before it ever lands on Congress.

## Who is still on the far side of the connection

Start with what the evidence can bear. The Pew Research Center has surveyed Americans about the internet since 2000, and its findings over two decades describe real progress resting on a stubborn floor. Most adults are online now, and the smartphone has become close to universal. But the floor sits where you'd expect. In 2021 Pew found that only a little over half of adults in households earning under $30,000 a year had broadband at home, against more than nine in ten in households earning $75,000 or more, and that about a quarter of lower-income adults reached the internet only through a phone. Rural adults were less likely than suburban adults to have home broadband that year. And about a quarter of Americans aged sixty-five and older told Pew in 2021 that they never went online at all.

The children had been telling us for years. In 2018 Pew reported that nearly one in five American teenagers said they were at least sometimes unable to finish homework because they lacked a reliable computer or connection, and the share was higher among teens in lower-income homes. Jessica Rosenworcel, then a member of the Federal Communications Commission and later its chair, had been calling this the homework gap long before 2020 made it a national story. When the schools closed, Pew found that more than half of lower-income parents expected their children to face at least one digital obstacle to schoolwork: no computer at home, no dependable connection, or assignments done on a phone.

For most of that time the government's own picture of the problem was blurry. The FCC counted a whole census block as served if a provider could serve a single location in it, so a rural block with one connected farmhouse and thirty unconnected homes appeared on the map as covered. Critics in both parties said the maps overstated access, and Congress ordered better ones in the Broadband DATA Act of 2020; the new national map, built address by address, arrived in late 2022. In March 2024 the Commission also raised its benchmark for what counts as broadband, from the 25 megabits down and 3 up it had set in 2015 to 100 down and 20 up. Each of those decisions was technical. Each one changed who counted as left out.

The thinnest service in the country has long been on tribal lands, where the FCC's own deployment reports have for years found access well below the national rate, and where distance, terrain and a long history of federal neglect compound one another. Congress acknowledged as much when it set aside money specifically for tribal broadband in December 2020 and again in the infrastructure law of 2021. Americans with disabilities meet the divide twice. Pew's surveys have repeatedly found them less likely than other adults to own a computer or to have broadband at home, and those who are connected still meet forms that won't work with a screen reader, videos without captions and sites built for a hand that can steady a mouse. Older adults meet it at the bank whose branch has closed and at the doctor's office, where the appointment is now a video call and the test results sit behind a portal that wants a password reset.

The divide isn't only a matter of wires, and the scholarship has been careful here. In 2002 the sociologist Eszter Hargittai argued, in a paper whose title named the idea, that a second-level digital divide lay beneath the first: among people who had access, there were wide differences in the skill to use it well. The Dutch scholar Jan van Dijk built that insight into a fuller account in *The Digital Divide* (2020), where physical access is only one stage in a sequence that runs from motivation through material access to skills and finally to the uses people actually make of the connection. The distinction keeps the argument honest. A cable to the house is necessary. It isn't sufficient, and a policy that counts cables will declare victory in homes where nobody can fill out the form.

What changed over those twenty-five years wasn't that the internet grew more pleasant. It's that institutions moved behind it. Job applications, school assignments, unemployment claims, benefit renewals, court notices and medical records now assume the connection by default, and the paper door beside the digital one has quietly narrowed or closed. Virginia Eubanks, in *Automating Inequality* (2018), tells how Indiana's 2006 contract to automate its welfare eligibility system, moving casework into call centers and online processes, cut people off benefits for missing a step in a procedure many of them couldn't follow, until the state canceled the contract in 2009. Nobody set out to punish the poor. The system simply assumed a connected person, literate in forms, and treated everyone else as a failure to comply. A household without a connection now isn't cut off from entertainment. It's cut off from the channels through which a society hands out its opportunities.

## The republic has wired the edge before

None of this is new, and that's the first thing the history teaches. Every generation of Americans has met a network that became the channel of common life, and every generation has had to decide whether that network would reach the people at the far end of the road.

The first was the mail. Richard John's *Spreading the News* (1995) shows how the Post Office Act of 1792 made the postal system the most extensive institution of the early republic, pushing routes into the interior and carrying newspapers at subsidized rates, so that a farmer far from any city could learn what Congress had done. John's point is that this was a deliberate political choice about who would be able to take part in the life of the nation, and not a byproduct of commerce. A century later the same choice was made again. Before Rural Free Delivery began in 1896, farm families had to travel to a post office to collect their mail; afterward, the mail came to them. The telephone followed a similar path. Theodore Vail, who returned to lead AT&T in 1907, made universal service part of the company's slogan, and Tim Wu's *The Master Switch* (2010) traces how the Bell system came to accept regulation in exchange for monopoly. The Communications Act of 1934 then wrote into federal law the aim of making wire and radio service available, so far as possible, to all the people of the United States.

The sharpest precedent is electricity. When the Rural Electrification Administration was created by executive order in 1935, the private utilities had largely declined to string lines into the countryside, reasoning with perfect commercial logic that too few customers per mile of wire would never repay the poles. Robert Caro's *The Path to Power* (1982) gives the Texas Hill Country's version of that decision in a chapter called "The Sad Irons," where women hauled water from the well and pressed clothes with irons heated on a wood stove, years after women in town had stopped doing either. Caro shows the young Lyndon Johnson pressing Washington until the cooperative lines finally reached the Hill Country at the end of the decade. The detail worth noticing is the instrument. The chief means by which rural America was electrified was neither the unaided market nor a federal power company. It was a federal loan, authorized by the Rural Electrification Act of 1936, to a local cooperative its own members owned. In 1949 Congress extended the same program to rural telephones.

The modern chapters follow the pattern. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 wrote universal service into the statute, and out of it came the Universal Service Fund and the E-Rate program that still helps connect schools and libraries. The Lifeline program, begun in 1985 to help low-income households afford a telephone, was extended to broadband in 2016. Susan Crawford argued in *Captive Audience* (2013) that high-speed internet had become basic infrastructure, as electricity had been, and that a market dominated by a few large providers had little reason to serve thin or poor markets well.

Then came the largest effort yet. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, signed on November 15, 2021, set aside about $65 billion for broadband, including roughly $42.45 billion for a construction program called BEAD and $14.2 billion for the Affordable Connectivity Program. The ACP, which replaced a smaller pandemic benefit, took up to $30 a month off an internet bill for eligible households, and up to $75 on tribal lands. More than 23 million households enrolled. The money was finite, and it ran out. The program stopped taking new enrollments in February 2024; a bipartisan bill to extend it was introduced that winter and never passed; and when the money ran out, April 2024 was the last full month of the discount and the program formally ended on June 1. BEAD, meanwhile, spent its first years on maps, plans and approvals, with construction barely begun when the administration changed in January 2025. In June 2025 the Commerce Department rewrote the program's rules, dropping its preference for fiber and opening it more widely to satellite and other technologies.

Across two centuries the pattern holds. When a network becomes the channel of common life, the far end of the road either gets reached or falls out of that life. The argument has been about how, and who pays.

## What the gleaning law asked of a landowner

Israel's answer to the question of the edge was written into its harvest. It sits in the nineteenth chapter of Leviticus, and the setting matters before the verses do. The chapter opens with the charge that governs everything after it, "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2, ESV), and then it does something modern readers find disorienting. It moves without a pause from worship to wages, from Sabbath to scales, from idols to the way a man treats the deaf. Holiness in Leviticus 19 isn't a private interior state. It's a way of running a farm, paying a worker and keeping a market honest, and the gleaning law stands at the center of that economy.

> "When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not reap your field right up to its edge, neither shall you gather the gleanings after your harvest." (Leviticus 19:9, ESV)

> "And you shall not strip your vineyard bare, neither shall you gather the fallen grapes of your vineyard. You shall leave them for the poor and for the sojourner: I am the LORD your God." (Leviticus 19:10, ESV)

The Hebrew word for the edge, *pe'ah*, means the corner or border of the field, and the law makes that border a place the owner's efficiency may not reach. Three things about the command bear directly on the question of access, and each one draws a distinction the modern debate tends to blur.

The first is that the edge is left, not delivered. The owner isn't told to harvest everything and then hand out a portion at his gate to the petitioners he approves of. He's told to restrain his own harvest and leave the portion standing in the field, where the poor come and gather it with their own hands. The rabbis who later codified the practice in the Mishnah's tractate *Peah*, named for the corner-portion, noticed the point and guarded it. They listed the corner among the things that have no fixed measure (Peah 1:1), set a minimum of a sixtieth of the crop (1:2), and counted an owner who let one poor person glean while turning another away as one who robs the poor (5:6). The gleaning was placed deliberately beyond the owner's favor. That's the difference between access and a handout. A handout depends on the giver's continuing goodwill and leaves the receiver waiting at the door. Access leaves a place open at the edge of the system and trusts the poor to come and work it.

The second is that the law is addressed to the owner as an obligation before God. It isn't framed chiefly as the poor man's claim against the king. The refrain that closes the command, "I am the LORD your God," locates the demand in the owner's conscience and the owner's worship. Nicholas Wolterstorff argues in *Justice: Rights and Wrongs* (2008) that duties and rights are two sides of one relationship, so that when God obligates the landowner, the gleaner has something genuinely due to her, and he's right. But notice where the Torah places the weight. It speaks first to the one who holds the field. Deuteronomy restates the law and gives its reason:

> "When you reap your harvest in your field and forget a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it. It shall be for the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow, that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands." (Deuteronomy 24:19, ESV)

> "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt; therefore I command you to do this." (Deuteronomy 24:22, ESV)

The owner leaves the edge because he remembers being on the far side of every edge. Israel's economics began in a brickyard.

The third is that the gleaning law doesn't stand alone in the chapter. A few verses later comes a command that reads, in a century of screen readers and captioned video, with unexpected force: "You shall not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:14, ESV). And near the chapter's end: "You shall stand up before the gray head and honor the face of an old man, and you shall fear your God: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:32, ESV). The same passage that tells the owner to leave the edge of his field tells every Israelite not to build obstacles in the path of those who can't see them, and to honor the old rather than pass them by. The poor, the stranger, the disabled and the aged all appear in Leviticus 19 as persons the holy community must keep inside its common life, a thread traced through the canon in [the essay on the widow, the orphan and the stranger](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger).

The book of Ruth shows the law at work, and the whole of that story, with its redeemer and its field and its city gate, is traced in [the essay on the Jubilee](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded). What matters here is a detail that's easy to pass over. Ruth goes out to glean, and she works. She gathers in the field until evening and threshes what she has gathered, and the book reckons her day's labor in barley (Ruth 2:17). Gleaning was an open door into work for people shut out of land ownership, and it assumed the gleaner's strength. The field has changed form. The strip of barley at the border of a field in Bethlehem is now a connection, a device and the skill to use them, since those stand between a person and the work, the schooling and the public life of a community. The edge of the field has moved. The obligation hasn't.

## The case against, at its strongest

The argument against treating this as a matter of justice deserves its full weight, because much of it is true, and a church that can't state it fairly has no business answering it.

It begins with the market's record. The price of moving data has fallen for decades, and private capital, not sermons, built most of the networks Americans now use. The smartphone put the internet into the pockets of millions who never had a home connection, and low-orbit satellite service now reaches remote homes that no fiber plan would have reached for a decade. Then there is the government's record, which is mixed at best. The roughly $7.2 billion the 2009 stimulus put into broadband produced uneven results. The FCC's 2020 rural broadband auction ended with the Commission, in 2022, rejecting some of its largest awards after concluding the winners couldn't deliver what they'd promised. BEAD took years to connect anyone. And critics of the ACP, among them the FCC commissioner Brendan Carr, argued that much of its money went to households that already had service before the subsidy arrived. F. A. Hayek's essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945) puts the principle behind all of this: the knowledge needed to allocate resources well is scattered among countless people on the ground, and no central planner can gather it. An office in Washington can't know which hollow needs a fiber line and which needs a satellite dish.

The language of rights draws its own objection. Vint Cerf, one of the designers of the internet's basic protocols, argued in *The New York Times* in 2012 that internet access is not a human right. Technology, he wrote in substance, enables rights; it isn't itself one. Human rights are reserved for what a person needs to live a healthy and meaningful life, freedom from torture and freedom of conscience among them, and he noted that the horse was once essential for making a living without anyone calling it a right. Inflate every useful good into a right and the word loses the force the gravest rights depend on.

The sharpest version of the case goes further still. Perhaps the poor need less screen time, not more. Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) argues that the smartphone-based childhood has harmed the young, and Nellie Bowles reported in *The New York Times* in 2018 that affluent parents in Silicon Valley were restricting their own children's screens while poorer children spent more time on theirs. Handing every poor household a faster feed of the thing the wealthy are paying to keep away from their children is a strange picture of justice. The site's essay on [digital discipleship](/writing/digital-discipleship) takes up that evidence and its critics, and it belongs in this argument.

A serious conservative would sign every sentence of that, and so would many progressives who have watched their own programs stall. It rules out treating any program as gospel, and it rules out equating connection with flourishing. But it leaves something unanswered.

Cerf's distinction is correct, and the gleaning law doesn't need the word he rejects. Leviticus doesn't declare barley a human right. It lays an obligation on the owner of the field, and the obligation holds whatever we call the gleaner's portion. Amartya Sen's *Development as Freedom* (1999) supplies the frame the rights debate lacks. Sen treats poverty as the deprivation of capabilities, the real freedom to do and be what a person has reason to value, including the freedom to take part in the life of the community without shame. He leans on Adam Smith, who argued in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) that necessities include whatever the custom of a country makes it indecent for respectable people, even the poorest, to be without, and who used the linen shirt of a day laborer as his example. The horse was never necessary in that sense, because no institution required one to apply for work. The connection is, because the institutions themselves have made it the door. The child outside the restaurant wasn't there for entertainment. She was there because the school had moved inside the wire.

Hayek's point, taken seriously, doesn't favor leaving the edge empty. It favors reaching it through those closest to it. Pius XI's encyclical *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931) named the principle of subsidiarity: a larger body shouldn't absorb what a smaller one can do, and the very word comes from the Latin for help, because the larger body exists to support the smaller. The electric cooperatives of the 1930s are the principle in wire and pole, and many of them are stringing fiber now. And the screen-harm argument, honest as it is, concerns what flows through the pipe. The affluent parent who limits her child's screen still has the connection and chooses how to use it. The poor child with a phone and no computer gets the feed without the tools.

There is an order to this disagreement, and it helps to name it. What binds every Christian is first-order: God claims the edge of the field, and a church that shrugs while its neighbors are shut out of the systems through which opportunity now flows has abandoned something Leviticus treats as holiness. Whether that obligation is best met by subsidized private networks, public or cooperative ones, satellites or fiber, a renewed ACP or none, is second-order, and faithful Christians will disagree. A congregation should be very slow to preach a broadband bill.

The failure isn't holding a view on the second-order questions. It's the two ways of hiding behind them, and they deserve the same measure. On the right, the program's genuine waste becomes an alibi for looking away, and the market is praised as though it hadn't declined the countryside in 1935. On the left, advocacy for the program stands in for love of neighbors we've never met, and a $42 billion plan that took years to lay its first line is defended as though the plan were the point. Both keep the neighbor abstract. Both let us feel we've answered the child outside the restaurant without learning her name, and most of us have taken shelter in one or the other.

## The edge is also where the danger is

Ruth's story carries a warning the access debate usually forgets. Boaz orders his young men not to touch her (Ruth 2:9), and Naomi tells Ruth to stay with his young women, "lest in another field you be assaulted" (Ruth 2:22, ESV). The edge of the field was an exposed place, and access without protection wasn't mercy. The same is true now. The connection that carries the benefits portal also carries the scammer, and the FBI's yearly reports on elder fraud count Americans over sixty losing billions of dollars to schemes that arrive by email, text and pop-up window. A child handed a device with no one beside her receives the harvesters of attention along with the homework. Justice at the digital edge asks for both halves of Ruth 2: the edge left open, and someone standing guard in the field.

That's where congregations and households come in, and it's work no policy fight has to settle first. Most churches, ours among them, moved their own life online without asking who fell silently out of it: the sign-up that exists only as a link, the notice sent only by email, the code on the bulletin that assumes a phone. For years I didn't ask. A church can begin by keeping a paper door open beside every digital one. It can partner with the public library instead of competing with it. Eric Klinenberg's *Palaces for the People* (2018) describes libraries as social infrastructure, among the last places anyone may enter and stay without buying anything, and many already teach the digital skills a church would struggle to staff; what they often lack is volunteers and rooms. A church can run patient hours for older members, one person at a time, including how to recognize a fraud. It can open a homework room with dependable wireless and a working printer, help families find out whether they qualify for Lifeline or other programs (the current rules are the program's to state, not ours), and make its own website usable by someone who can't see the screen. A household can wipe an old laptop for a neighbor, or sit beside a grandparent at the patient portal until she no longer needs the help. These are small. They're also the gleaning law in a form Leviticus would recognize.

## Outside the gate

The letter to the Hebrews was written to believers tempted to retreat inside familiar walls, and it tells them where their Lord was found.

> "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood." (Hebrews 13:12, ESV)

> "Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured." (Hebrews 13:13, ESV)

The cross stood at the edge, where the city put what it didn't want to see. The Son of God didn't wait at the center for the excluded to find their way in. He went out to where they were, and he calls his people to follow him there. The gleaning law was a rehearsal for that movement, with its restraint, its open edge and its memory of Egypt. The gospel fulfills it, and it judges us by it.

The mechanisms will change again. Satellites will replace some of the fiber, something will replace the satellites, and a new set of institutions will move behind a new door and assume everyone holds the key. The measure won't change. The question the Torah asked of a field in Bethlehem is the one God still asks of every church: not how smoothly we serve people who already have everything, but what reaches the people at the margin. Somewhere near your building tonight, someone is sitting wherever the signal is, or has quietly stopped trying. The church doesn't need a telecommunications policy to go and find her. It needs first to have noticed that she was gone.
