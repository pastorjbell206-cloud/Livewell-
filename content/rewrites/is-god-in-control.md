Somebody says it at the funeral. Somebody says it in the hallway outside the oncology ward, or on the phone the night a marriage finally breaks: *God is in control.* It's meant as comfort, and it often lands like a slap, because the person hearing it is staring at a piece of reality that doesn't look governed by anyone, least of all by someone good. So a great many people quietly file the sovereignty of God under things Christians say without meaning, a reflex for moments that should have been met with silence.

I've heard that sentence land wrong, and I've probably said it when I should have said nothing. I also came to faith out of atheism, and part of what kept me out was a version of "God is in control" so glib, so untouched by the way the world actually goes, that rejecting it felt like the only honest option. Some skeptics reading this are still rejecting that version. They're right to. It was never the doctrine.

The church's word for what it confesses is *providence*, and it is older and stranger than the slogan. The slogan implies that if you could only see the plan you'd applaud it, and that your grief is a failure of sight. The doctrine claims something at once smaller and much larger: that no event, including the worst one in your life, has slipped out of God's hand or broken free of his purpose, and that none of it has thereby been declared good. John Calvin, supposedly the coldest voice on this subject, conceded in 1559 that events as we meet them look like chance, because the order behind them is hidden in God's counsel. It doesn't feel like control because providence was never a feeling. It's a confession, usually made in the dark, about what is true beneath what is seen.

Why a good God permits evil at all is the subject of the site's essay on [suffering](/writing/if-god-is-good-why-suffering). The question here is narrower and closer to the bone. ## What the church means by providence

The word comes from the Latin *providentia*, a seeing ahead, but the church has never meant by it mere foreknowledge. It has meant God's continuing, active care for everything he made. The Nicene Creed calls the Father *Pantokrator*, the Almighty, literally the ruler of all, and over the centuries theologians sorted what that holding involves into three parts. Louis Berkhof's *Systematic Theology* (1938), a textbook generations of Reformed pastors learned from, names them preservation, concurrence and government, a division far older than his book.

Preservation means the world doesn't run on its own momentum. It's held in being moment by moment. "And he is before all things, and in him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:17, ESV).

Concurrence is the hardest part and the most important. God works in and through the real actions of creatures without replacing them. Thomas Aquinas gave the church its standard grammar here in the *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274): God is the first cause, and creatures are true second causes. The rain really waters the field, the surgeon really heals, the drunk driver really chooses. God's agency isn't one more cause competing on the same level, shoving the creature aside wherever he acts. It's the ground every creaturely cause stands on. "The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps" (Proverbs 16:9, ESV). Both clauses are fully true, and neither shrinks to make room for the other.

Government means God directs all of it toward his ends. Jesus pressed the claim down to the smallest scale he could find: "Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? And not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father" (Matthew 10:29, ESV). Notice what that verse doesn't say. The sparrow still falls. Jesus isn't promising his disciples that nothing will fall on them; the same chapter warns them of flogging, betrayal and death. He's promising that no falling happens apart from the Father.

One of the doctrine's most careful statements is the Heidelberg Catechism, drafted largely by Zacharias Ursinus for Elector Frederick III of the Palatinate in 1563. Its twenty-seventh answer, in paraphrase, calls providence God's almighty and ever-present power, by which he upholds heaven and earth and every creature as though by his own hand, and so rules them that leaf and blade, rain and drought, fruitful years and lean ones, health and sickness, riches and poverty all come to us not by chance but from a Father's hand. The twenty-eighth asks what good it does us to know this. The answer isn't an explanation. It's a posture: patience when things go against us, thankfulness when they go well, and confidence for the future that nothing will separate us from the Father's love.

The catechism never calls drought or sickness good in themselves, and it offers no reason for any particular one. The Westminster Confession (1646), whose chapter on providence the English Particular Baptists took over almost word for word in the Second London Confession of 1689, adds the guardrails the slogan forgets: God ordinarily governs through second causes, and he is neither the author nor the approver of sin. Both halves have to stand, or the doctrine collapses into one of two cartoons, a puppeteer who scripts every atrocity or an absent landlord whose name is on the deed and who is never in the building. Most people who say they've stopped believing God is in control have stopped believing one of those cartoons, and they were right to. Nobody offered them the third thing.

## Joseph, Paul and the verses the slogan borrowed

Two texts carry most of the slogan's weight, and both have been worn smooth by use. The first comes at the end of Genesis. Joseph was thrown into a pit by his brothers at seventeen, sold into slavery in Egypt, falsely accused by his master's wife, and forgotten in prison. When the brothers finally stand before him, their father dead, afraid he'll take his revenge at last, he says: "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today" (Genesis 50:20, ESV).

Joseph doesn't say the evil was secretly good, and he doesn't let his brothers off the hook: *you meant evil.* Two intentions ran through the same events, theirs and God's, and God's governed the outcome without cleansing theirs. Notice the sentence just before it: "Do not fear, for am I in the place of God?" (Genesis 50:19, ESV). The man who confesses providence most clearly in the Old Testament does it while refusing to play God, and he says it nearly forty years after the pit. He said it himself, at the end, about his own suffering, to the people who had caused it, as the ground for forgiving them. It's a confession a sufferer may make over his own life when God has given him the sight to make it. It isn't a sentence a bystander gets to hand to someone still in the pit.

The second text is Romans 8:28, the most quoted and most abused sentence Paul wrote: "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28, ESV). Lifted out, it sounds like cheerfulness. In place, it sits at the center of a chapter about groaning. Creation groans, believers groan, and just before the promise even prayer breaks down: "Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26, ESV). Romans 8:28 is spoken to people who don't know what to pray. Paul never tells them to stop groaning. He tells them the groaning isn't the last word.

Then he defines the good, and it isn't comfort, recovery or success. "For those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, in order that he might be the firstborn among many brothers" (Romans 8:29, ESV). The good is likeness to Christ, which in this chapter means sharing his sufferings on the way to sharing his glory. And at the end of the argument Paul names what his "all things" include: "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword?" (Romans 8:35, ESV). He answers with a line from Psalm 44, a lament of the faithful in defeat: "As it is written, 'For your sake we are being killed all the day long; we are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered'" (Romans 8:36, ESV). The man who wrote Romans 8:28 was later beaten, imprisoned and, by the church's early testimony, executed in Rome. His "all things" includes the sword.

So the promise isn't that the cancer is good, or that you'll see the good this side of death. It's the one the chapter closes on: "For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38–39, ESV). That's what the Heidelberg Catechism was reaching for in its twenty-eighth answer. In Paul's hands, providence isn't finally about control in the sense a manager means it. It's about inseparability.

The clearest case of all is the cross. Peter, preaching in Jerusalem weeks after the crucifixion, holds both claims in a single breath: "this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23, ESV). God didn't do the evil, and he wasn't absent from it either. Every Christian account of providence has to be measured against Golgotha, because there God showed what he does with the evil he permits: he takes it into himself and turns it toward resurrection.

## A prisoner in Pavia and a city on All Saints' Day

The question in this essay's title wasn't invented by the modern world. Around 524 a Roman senator named Boethius sat in a cell near Pavia waiting to be executed. He had risen high in the service of the Ostrogothic king Theodoric and had fallen on a charge of treason he insisted was false. There he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy*, one of the most copied books of the Middle Ages, and it's built on the complaint of the funeral: if God governs the world, why do the wicked flourish while the innocent wait for the executioner?

Boethius imagines Philosophy herself visiting his cell. Her first work is to show him that he had been trusting Fortune, whose nature is to turn her wheel; to complain that she turned it is to complain that she is Fortune. Then, in the fourth book, she draws a distinction the church has used ever since. Providence is the divine plan itself, whole and simple in the mind of God. Fate is that same plan unfolded in time, through the moving, tangled order of things. From inside the unfolding we see only fate, and fate looks like chance or cruelty. Providence can't be seen from within the sequence. It can only be trusted from within it. In the fifth book Philosophy adds that God doesn't know the world by watching time pass but holds all of it present at once, in eternity, and that his seeing no more forces what he sees than our watching a man walk forces him to walk. Aquinas later gave the thought its best-known picture in the *Summa*: a traveler on a road sees only those near him, while someone on a height sees the whole road at once. The book gives him no reprieve. It gives him a way of seeing that doesn't depend on the verdict, which is precisely what the slogan counterfeits.

Twelve centuries later the question returned at the scale of a city. On the morning of 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day, with Lisbon's churches full for the feast, one of the strongest earthquakes in European history struck the city. A tsunami followed within the hour, and fires burned for days. Many thousands died, and the event broke two readings of providence at once.

The first was the preacher's. The Jesuit Gabriel Malagrida, among others, told the survivors that the earthquake was God's judgment on the city's sins. The second was the philosopher's. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz had argued in his *Theodicy* (1710) that a wise and good God must have made the best of all possible worlds, and Alexander Pope had set the mood to verse in *An Essay on Man* (1733–1734): "Whatever is, is right." Voltaire answered both in his *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster* (1756), subtitled as an examination of the axiom that all is well. He asked, in substance, whether Lisbon had been more wicked than London or Paris, and how anyone could look at the crushed bodies and call this the best possible arrangement. Susan Neiman, in *Evil in Modern Thought* (2002), argues that Lisbon became for the eighteenth century what Auschwitz became for the twentieth, a single name for the whole problem, and that after it the West slowly learned to treat natural disaster and human wickedness as different kinds of evil.

Still, Voltaire's scorn landed on something real. The preacher and the optimist both claimed to know too much; one read God's verdict off the rubble and the other read God's excellence off it. Long before Lisbon, Jesus had refused both moves. Told about Galileans killed by Pilate, he raised another disaster himself: "Or those eighteen on whom the tower in Siloam fell and killed them: do you think that they were worse offenders than all the others who lived in Jerusalem?" (Luke 13:4, ESV). He answered his own question: "No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all likewise perish" (Luke 13:5, ESV). He wouldn't read the collapse as a verdict on the dead, and he wouldn't call it good. He turned it into a summons to the living. A sound doctrine of providence does the same: it confesses that nothing falls outside God's rule, and it refuses, on Christ's own authority, to announce why this tower, why these eighteen, why your child. Voltaire was right that the optimists were wrong. He wasn't right that the church had nothing else to say.

## Why providence isn't fate

A fear always rides in behind this doctrine. If God governs everything, then my effort, my choices and my prayers are decorations on a script already written, and the honest response is to go limp. That's fatalism, and providence often gets the blame for it. Serious traditions have taught something close to it, and they deserve a fair hearing first.

The Stoics of Greece and Rome had a providence of their own. The cosmos was shot through with divine reason, the *logos*, and every event followed from every other in an unbroken chain; the wise man's task was to want what happens. Epictetus, the freed slave turned teacher, whose *Enchiridion* is the handbook his student Arrian compiled from his teaching, opens it by dividing the world into what is up to us and what isn't, and he counsels his students not to demand that events go as they wish, but to wish them to go as they do. There is real dignity in this, and it has steadied people in prisons and on battlefields. But Stoic providence has no face. It's the reason of the world, not a Father who hears; you can consent to it, but you can't pray to it. Calvin, anticipating the charge that his teaching was Stoic fate, rejected it in the *Institutes*: the Christian confesses not a chain of causes bound up in nature but a free and personal God who rules by his own counsel.

Islam confesses divine decree with a seriousness Christians ought to respect. Belief in God's *qadar*, his decree of good and evil alike, is among the articles of Muslim faith, and the popular word *kismet*, one's allotted portion, carries that conviction into ordinary speech. It's a caricature to call Islam simply fatalist; Muslim scholars have argued about decree and human responsibility for more than a thousand years, and a saying attributed to the Prophet, preserved in al-Tirmidhi's collection, tells a man to tie his camel and then trust God. But one strand of classical Islamic theology, the Ash'ari school, pressed divine agency further than Christian providence goes. Its most famous voice, al-Ghazali, in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (1095), argued that when fire touches cotton it isn't the fire that burns it; God creates the burning on the occasion of the contact. The doctrine of concurrence insists on the opposite: the fire really burns, because God gives creatures real causal power and works through it. And the deepest difference here isn't how much God decrees. It's who the One decreeing has shown himself to be. Christians confess that the God who governs the world entered it as a man and suffered under its evils.

Modern determinism is fate with the gods removed. In 1814 Pierre-Simon Laplace imagined an intelligence that knew every force and every particle at one instant, a mind for which the future would lie open like the past; Robert Sapolsky's *Determined* (2023) argues in that line that every choice is the product of biology and circumstance we never chose. Here the chain is complete and nobody holds it. It has the Stoic's necessity without the Stoic's consolation.

Against all three, providence says something close to the opposite of fatalism. Fatalism says the end is fixed whatever you do, so why act. Providence says God ordains the ends and the means together, and your action is one of the means. Calvin, who gets blamed for everything, made the point himself: the God who set the bounds of our life also entrusted us with its care, supplied the means to preserve it and gave us the foresight to see dangers coming, so providence is never an excuse for carelessness. The farmer who believes God gives the harvest plants anyway, because planting is how God gives harvests. Prayer is one of those means, neither a lever on a reluctant God nor theater before a finished script; whether God changes his mind in answer to it is taken up in [a separate essay](/writing/does-god-change-his-mind).

The Bible's best picture of this is a book that never names God. In Esther the Jews of Persia face annihilation by royal decree, and nothing in the story looks governed. There's a beauty contest, a grudge, a lot cast to choose the day of slaughter, and a king who can't sleep: "On that night the king could not sleep" (Esther 6:1, ESV). The reader is meant to feel the hand without ever seeing it. When Mordecai presses Esther to risk her life before the king, his argument isn't certainty: "And who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (Esther 4:14, ESV). *Who knows.* That's what providence sounds like from inside it. It doesn't show Esther the plan. It tells her the plan is real enough to risk her life on, and then it sends her in.

## Where faithful Christians part ways

Everything said so far is common ground among historic Christians. How divine governance relates to human freedom is not. It's a second-order question, one that divides faithful churches without dividing the faith, and each side deserves its strongest voice.

The Reformed tradition, following Augustine and Calvin, holds what is sometimes called meticulous providence. The Westminster Confession teaches that God ordained whatever comes to pass, and in the same breath denies that this makes him the author of sin or does violence to the wills of his creatures. Nothing, not even a sinful choice, happens outside God's decree, though God stands differently toward good and evil, working the one, and ordering and bounding the other rather than merely permitting it. Its strongest text is Ephesians, where God "works all things according to the counsel of his will" (Ephesians 1:11, ESV). Its pastoral gift is the one Calvin prized: nothing in your life is unsupervised.

The Arminian tradition answers that this ties God to evil in a way no qualification can finally repair. Jacobus Arminius, in his *Declaration of Sentiments* (1608), insisted that he affirmed God's preservation and government of all things as firmly as anyone, and that his quarrel was with accounts of the divine decree that, as he saw it, made God the author of sin. On this view God grants creatures real freedom, foreknows its use, and governs by permitting, overruling and bending it toward his ends, as he did with Joseph's brothers. Its strongest texts are the many in which God pleads, grieves and invites. Its pastoral gift is just as real: God never wanted the evil done to you.

This essay doesn't settle that argument, and its case doesn't depend on settling it. The Calvinist and the Arminian both stand beside Joseph and say *you meant evil, but God meant it for good.* Both confess the cross as wicked and planned, and both pray. The strongest objection to the Reformed view, that it can't show how God ordains without authoring, is one its best defenders admit they hold as mystery. The strongest objection to the Arminian view, that permitting a foreseen evil you could prevent is still a kind of choosing, is one its best defenders feel as well. The fuller case for each is in the essay on [predestination and free will](/writing/predestination-and-free-will).

## The sentence that wounds and the prayers that don't

Kate Bowler, a historian at Duke Divinity School whose first book traced the American prosperity gospel, was diagnosed with stage IV colon cancer in 2015, at thirty-five, with a young son at home. *Everything Happens for a Reason: And Other Lies I've Loved* (2018) names that sentence as one of the lies, and it closes with a short list of things never to say to people in terrible times. She writes as a believer. Her point, as I read it, is that the explanation usually serves the one offering it: it packs the terrible thing into a lesson, lets the speaker feel the world is orderly again, and leaves the sufferer alone with an answer she never asked for. If everything happens for a reason, there's a reason for this, and the reason might be me.

That's where the slogan and the doctrine part company. The slogan claims there's a reason you'd accept if you could see it. Providence claims that God rules, and keeps its silence about why this, why now, why you. We hand people the explanation anyway, and I probably have, because silence feels like failure. When we do, we repeat the mistake of Job's friends, who said many true things about God's justice and were wrong about Job, and whom God rebuked at the end for not speaking rightly of him.

The Bible's alternative is lament, and it happens inside providence rather than against it. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1, ESV). The psalmist doesn't say God is absent. He says God is hiding, a complaint only someone who believes God is there can make. Four verses later, with nothing changed that we're told of: "But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation" (Psalm 13:5, ESV). The "but" doesn't cancel the "how long." (The essay on [lament](/writing/lament-the-prayer-the-church-forgot) walks through the form.)

Psalm 73 is providence's own crisis, told from inside. Asaph nearly lost his footing over our exact question, the prosperity of the wicked. What turns him isn't an argument: "But when I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task, until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end" (Psalm 73:16–17, ESV). He goes to worship with the question still open, and he's given not the reason but a presence: "Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand" (Psalm 73:23, ESV). In the Psalms, complaint is how trust speaks when it can't see. So what should a Christian say at the funeral? Much less than we usually do. Say you're sorry, admit you don't know, and stay. Later, perhaps much later, the grieving may find the confession on their own lips, as Joseph did. It's theirs to say first.

## A Friday that didn't look governed

The world really doesn't look governed much of the time, and the church has never honestly claimed otherwise. The doctrine was never the claim that you'd feel held. It's the claim that you are held when you don't. We should own what we've done to it: used it to hurry grief along, to excuse our passivity and call it trust, to baptize whatever outcome our side preferred, as if an election or a market were a verdict from heaven. That temptation runs in every political direction, and the way back is repentance, which here mostly means saying less and staying longer.

In the end the doctrine rests on a Friday. By every appearance available to the people near the cross, that afternoon proved nobody good was in control. The innocent man was dying, his friends had scattered, and he cried out in the words of a psalm: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46, ESV). Peter would later call it the definite plan of God. No one said that on Friday. On Friday it looked exactly like what it also was, the murder of the Son of God by people who meant evil. Providence was there, all of it, and it didn't look like anything.

So I won't tell you it feels like God is in control. Often it doesn't, and a faith that depends on the feeling won't survive the first waiting room. What the church has confessed, from a cell in Pavia to the rubble of Lisbon, is that nothing happening to you has escaped God, none of it has been called good by him, and none of it can separate you from his love. You're allowed to say *how long*, and to carry the question into the sanctuary unanswered. The tomb outside Jerusalem didn't feel like providence on Saturday either. It was sealed and guarded and silent, and the women who went to it at dawn on Sunday carried spices for a body, because that was all they knew to do. They were wrong about what they would find. Most days the hope has to be carried like that, in the dark, toward a place that still looks like an ending.
