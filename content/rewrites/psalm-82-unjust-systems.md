Most Christians meet Psalm 82 secondhand. Some meet it in an online lecture promising a hidden biblical world of rebel gods. Some meet it on religious television, where a preacher tells a studio audience that believers are little gods. Some meet it in a footnote to John 10, where Jesus quotes its sixth verse to men who have already picked up stones. Very few have heard it preached whole. It's eight verses long, and it opens in a courtroom unlike any other in Scripture: God rises in an assembly of beings the text calls gods, charges them with ruining the poor, and sentences them to death.

The strangeness is worth keeping, because a psalm this odd refuses the categories we carry into it. Readers have argued for two thousand years about who the gods are, but every side agrees on one thing. Whoever stands in that council, the charge against them is the same, and it's specific: they were given authority and used it for the wicked instead of the weak. The psalm's deepest question isn't the identity of the defendants. It's what authority is for.

"You are gods" is the language of office, not of privilege. It names a trust, and a trust can be forfeited. Heard as a compliment, the verse becomes flattery and eventually heresy. Heard as a commission, it judges everyone who has held power over someone weaker, including the parent, the pastor, the employer, the voter and the one writing this. I grew up without a father, and I can't read the word "fatherless" in this psalm as a category. It describes a child with no one to speak for him when decisions are made, which is exactly the child the gods in Psalm 82 were charged to protect.

## A courtroom in heaven

The psalm, one of the twelve headed with the name of Asaph, sits inside what scholars call the Elohistic Psalter, Psalms 42 to 83, where the general word for God, *elohim*, often appears where the rest of the Psalter would use the covenant name, the LORD. That matters in the first verse, because the word shows up twice with two different meanings. "God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment" (Psalm 82:1, ESV). The first *elohim* is Israel's God. The second is plural and refers to the beings around him.

*Elohim* is plural in form, and when it refers to Israel's God it takes singular verbs, as it does here. But the word was never a species name reserved for the one Creator. It named a rank, an inhabitant of the unseen realm. When the medium at Endor sees the dead prophet Samuel rising, she tells Saul, "I see a god coming up out of the earth" (1 Samuel 28:13), and the word is *elohim*. Michael Heiser argued in *The Unseen Realm* (2015) that much confusion about this psalm comes from treating *elohim* as if it meant "the uncreated God" every time. It tells you where a being belongs, not what it is. Only one *elohim* is Creator. Many can be called *elohim*.

The phrase the ESV renders "divine council" is, in Hebrew, *'adat-'el*, the assembly of God, or of El, and that second rendering opens a window onto the psalm's world. In 1928 a farmer's plow broke into an ancient tomb near the Syrian coast, and from 1929 French excavators under Claude Schaeffer uncovered the city of Ugarit and its archive of clay tablets. The texts, from roughly the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries before Christ, describe an assembly of the gods presided over by the high god El, with lesser deities called the sons of El gathered around him. E. Theodore Mullen's *The Assembly of the Gods* (1980) set these texts beside the Hebrew Bible and showed how well Israel's poets knew this picture of heaven as a royal court. The prophet Micaiah sees the LORD "sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing beside him" (1 Kings 22:19). Psalm 89 praises God as incomparable in "the assembly of the holy ones" (Psalm 89:5-7).

Israel borrowed the picture in order to break it. In the Canaanite council the gods sit, deliberate and bargain. In Psalm 82 God stands. The verb behind "has taken his place" means to take a stand, the posture of a judge rising to give sentence, and he does it "in the midst of" the gods. Mark S. Smith, in *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), reads the psalm as a hinge in Israel's religious history, a scene in which the other gods are stripped of standing and condemned to mortality. Some historians of religion suggest that in an older layer of Israel's faith the "Most High" and the God who judges were separate figures, but the psalm as Israel sang it leaves no room for that: the one who stands in verse 1 is the one called on to rule every nation in verse 8. Whatever the council was, it is now a courtroom, and there is only one judge in it.

Then God speaks, and the first words are a question with years behind it: "How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked?" (Psalm 82:2). The verdict has been a long time coming.

## The charge and the sentence

The phrase "show partiality" is, in Hebrew, to lift up the faces of the wicked, the courtroom idiom for favoritism. Picture a defendant with his head bowed, and a judge who reaches down and lifts his chin because of who he is. Israel's law forbade it in both directions: "You shall do no injustice in court. You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor" (Leviticus 19:15). The psalm is not asking judges to tilt the scales toward the poor. It is charging them with the tilt that power produces on its own. The scales are never level by default. The wicked arrive with their faces already lifted, with money, counsel, standing and friends at the gate, and a judge only has to do nothing for the verdict to go their way.

So the command that follows corrects a slope rather than creating one. "Give justice to the weak and the fatherless; maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute" (Psalm 82:3). "Rescue the weak and the needy; deliver them from the hand of the wicked" (Psalm 82:4). Five words for the vulnerable fill two verses: the weak (*dal*, literally the thin, the one with nothing to spare), the fatherless, the afflicted, the destitute and the needy. The verbs change as the lines go on. The first pair belongs to the courtroom, to judge rightly and to declare in the right. The second belongs to the rescuer, and its object is someone held in a grip, "the hand of the wicked." A judge who ruled fairly on every case brought to him would still fall short, because the people in that grip often never reach court.

None of this was new. Deuteronomy had already called Israel's God "God of gods and Lord of lords," who "is not partial and takes no bribe" and "executes justice for the fatherless and the widow" (Deuteronomy 10:17-18). The gods of Psalm 82 are measured against the one who stands among them. How the law and the prophets built this triad of widow, orphan and stranger into Israel's economy is traced in [What Does the Bible Say About Widows, Orphans and Strangers?](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger). What Psalm 82 adds is the scale. The standard God applied to village elders at the town gate, he applies here to the powers set over nations.

Then the voice turns, and the gods are spoken of rather than to: "They have neither knowledge nor understanding, they walk about in darkness; all the foundations of the earth are shaken" (Psalm 82:5). A few interpreters take "they" to mean the oppressed, stumbling without help, but the more natural reading is that the judges are the ones in the dark. Their ignorance is moral rather than informational, and Jeremiah defines the knowledge in question when he contrasts King Josiah with his grasping son: "He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 22:16). To know God, in the Hebrew Bible, is to do what he does. Judges who don't defend the poor don't know God, however much they know about him, and the earth's foundations shake under them. That last clause is the psalm's largest claim, and the end of this essay returns to it.

The sentence comes next, and it begins with a reminder of what they were given. "I said, 'You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you'" (Psalm 82:6). The title was never self-awarded. God conferred it, and "sons of the Most High" names a real rank and a real likeness, the family resemblance of those who were to govern as God governs. The next line turns on it: "nevertheless, like men you shall die, and fall like any prince" (Psalm 82:7). The Hebrew behind "like men" is *ke'adam*, like Adam, like humanity. The ones who held the highest title in the psalm will die the most ordinary death in the world, and "like any prince" means like one of the officials whose power they were supposed to supervise. The punishment fits the crime with a terrible precision. They would not protect the mortal and the weak, so they will share their mortality without their protector.

The title was real, and it was forfeited. It never described what the gods were in themselves, only what they were trusted with, and once the trust was betrayed the title became the indictment.

## Who are the gods? Three readings

Three answers have run through Jewish and Christian interpretation, and each deserves to be heard in the form its best defenders would give it.

The first reading takes the words at face value: the gods are divine beings, members of the heavenly council, set over the nations and condemned for ruling them unjustly. Its defenders point first to the vocabulary: "divine council," plural *elohim* and "sons of the Most High" all belong to Israel's language for the heavenly court, the language behind "the sons of God" in Job. They point next to the logic of the sentence. Telling human judges that they will die like men is hardly a sentence at all, since they were always going to die. The threat only has teeth if the defendants were not mortal to begin with. And they point to the last verse, where God is called to "inherit all the nations," which reads naturally against the Song of Moses, where, on the text the ESV follows, the nations were divided among heavenly overseers while Israel remained the LORD's own portion (Deuteronomy 32:8-9). The textual question behind that verse, and the "prince of Persia" in Daniel 10, are discussed in [What Does the Bible Actually Say About Angels and Demons?](/writing/angels-demons-and-the-unseen). Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* is the most thorough recent case for this reading, and it has old company. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the text scholars call 11QMelchizedek applies the psalm's opening line to a heavenly deliverer who judges Belial and the spirits aligned with him. At least some Jews before Jesus heard this psalm as a story about the unseen world.

The second reading holds that the gods are human rulers and judges, called *elohim* because they judge in God's place. Its defenders point to the psalm's content, which is entirely about courtrooms: unjust verdicts, partiality, the rights of the poor. They point to places where *elohim* seems to refer to the authorities who heard cases. In Exodus 21:6 and 22:8-9, where a dispute is brought "to God," the King James Version translates the word "judges," and the ESV notes the possibility. When King Jehoshaphat appointed judges in Judah, he charged them, "Consider what you do, for you judge not for man but for the LORD. He is with you in giving judgment" (2 Chronicles 19:6). To the objection that mortals can't be sentenced to mortality, they answer that the irony is the point: the title was the highest God could give, the death is the commonest, and holding his office will not exempt anyone from Adam's end. Much rabbinic and medieval Jewish interpretation read the psalm this way, and so did the Reformers. In 1530 Martin Luther published an exposition of Psalm 82 addressed to the princes of his day, arguing that God calls rulers gods because they hold his office, and that the psalm both honors that office and teaches preachers they may rebuke the rulers who abuse it. John Calvin, in his *Commentary on the Book of Psalms* (1557), read the gods as magistrates who bear the title because God has clothed them with his authority, and in the final edition of the *Institutes* (1559) he cited the verse to show that civil office is a calling from God and must answer to him. The strength of this reading is that it never lets the psalm float away from us. Somebody reading it tonight is a judge.

The third reading is the least known. A strand of rabbinic tradition hears "I said, 'You are gods'" as spoken to Israel at Sinai, when the people received the Torah, and "like men you shall die" as the sentence passed after the golden calf. The Babylonian Talmud (Avodah Zarah 5a) preserves the idea that Israel accepted the Torah in order to be freed from the angel of death, and that the calf undid it. Jerome Neyrey argued in the *Journal of Biblical Literature* (1989) that this tradition illuminates the phrase Jesus uses in John 10, "those to whom the word of God came." Its defenders note how exactly that phrase fits a people who had just received the Torah, and how naturally "like Adam you shall die" fits a nation that, like Adam, was given a command and fell almost at once. On this reading the psalm is about a people lifted to holiness by the word and brought low by their own idolatry.

Less rides on the choice than the heat of the argument online suggests. This is a third-order question. The creeds say nothing about it, and faithful scholars who hold the same confession disagree. The essay lands with the first reading: the psalm, read on its own terms, describes divine beings entrusted with the nations who betrayed their charge. The strongest objection is the one John 10 raises. "Those to whom the word of God came" sounds like human beings who heard God speak, and defenders of the divine-council reading have answers to that, but none yet offered removes the difficulty entirely. A reader who concludes that the psalm is about corrupt magistrates, or about Israel at Sinai, has surrendered nothing essential and is a brother in the argument.

The three readings also converge more than their defenders sometimes admit. Scripture doesn't imagine the heavenly and earthly powers as sealed off from each other. "On that day the LORD will punish the host of heaven, in heaven, and the kings of the earth, on the earth" (Isaiah 24:21). Whatever rules the nations from above rules them through human hands, and the charge in Psalm 82 lands on both. On every reading, the ones called gods were trusted with the weak, and on every reading they failed.

## Why Jesus quoted Psalm 82 in John 10

The scene in John is set with unusual care. "At that time the Feast of Dedication took place at Jerusalem. It was winter" (John 10:22). The feast, known now as Hanukkah, remembered the rededication of the temple in 164 BC, after Judas Maccabeus retook Jerusalem from the Seleucid king Antiochus IV, who had set up pagan worship at the altar. Jesus is walking in the temple courts during the festival of the consecrated altar, and he has just described himself as the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, set against thieves and hired hands who flee when the wolf comes (John 10:11-13). That picture of rulers as shepherds comes from Ezekiel, where God indicts Israel's leaders in terms that sound very like Psalm 82: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up" (Ezekiel 34:4). Before Jesus ever quotes the psalm, he has been speaking its subject.

The crowd presses him to say plainly whether he is the Christ. He tells them, "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30), and they pick up stones. Challenged, they explain, "It is not for a good work that we are going to stone you but for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God" (John 10:33). "Jesus answered them, 'Is it not written in your Law, "I said, you are gods"?'" (John 10:34). "Your Law" here means Israel's Scripture as a whole. Then the argument: "If he called them gods to whom the word of God came" (John 10:35), and, in a parenthesis, "Scripture cannot be broken," then "do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, 'You are blaspheming,' because I said, 'I am the Son of God'?" (John 10:36).

This is an argument from the lesser to the greater, a form the rabbis called *qal wahomer*, light and heavy. If Scripture, which cannot be set aside, applies the word *elohim* to those who received God's word, then the bare use of divine language cannot by itself be blasphemy. And if it can be said of them, how much more of the one the Father consecrated and sent. The verb "consecrated" is the language of dedication, spoken at the feast of a consecrated altar. Jesus presents himself as the one God has set apart and sent.

It matters just as much what the argument does not claim. Jesus is not making a modest claim, as if he were a god only in the psalm's sense. The next thing he says is, "the Father is in me and I am in the Father" (John 10:38), and the crowd tries again to arrest him (John 10:39). They understood that he had retracted nothing. Nor is he saying that human beings in general are gods. The contrast in his own sentence runs the other way: they are those to whom the word of God came, and he is the one the Father sent into the world. John has told his readers from the first verse which of those Jesus is. "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). The word came to the ones Psalm 82 addresses. The Word himself stands in the temple courts at Hanukkah, and the men holding stones are the ones on trial.

That is the irony of the scene. The psalm opens with God standing among the gods to judge them. In John 10 God stands among Israel's rulers in the flesh, and they sit in judgment on him, shepherds of the kind Ezekiel indicted, protecting their own position. Within a few months the council would decide it was better for one man to die than for the nation to be lost (John 11:50). The judged became the judges, and the judge let himself be judged.

## "Little gods": the misuse of verse 6

The psalm's sixth verse has had a second life on religious television. Teachers in the Word of Faith movement, which D. R. McConnell traced in *A Different Gospel* (1988) from E. W. Kenyon through Kenneth Hagin, have told believers that they are little gods, sharing God's own kind of life and able to speak their circumstances into being as God spoke the world. Hank Hanegraaff documented the teaching from the movement's broadcasts and books in *Christianity in Crisis* (1993). Its favorite texts are Psalm 82:6 and John 10:34, where, its teachers say, the Bible states it outright and Jesus repeats it.

It deserves a fair hearing before an answer. Its defenders begin with Genesis, where human beings are made in God's image and given dominion over the earth (Genesis 1:26-28). They add Peter's promise that believers become "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). They are protesting a Christianity that leaves believers passive and resigned, and many who hear them are poor, sick or powerless. To someone told all his life that he matters to no one, hearing that he bears God's authority can feel like the first dignity anyone ever gave him. Nor is the vocabulary simply invented. Irenaeus, late in the second century, read "you are gods" as a word about those who receive adoption from God, and Athanasius wrote in *On the Incarnation* that the Word became human so that we might be made divine. The Eastern churches call this theosis. The Word of Faith teachers can claim that they are only saying what the fathers said.

They are not, and the difference can be stated precisely. The fathers spoke of human beings sharing God's life by grace, through union with Christ, while remaining creatures. What God is by nature, we come to share by gift, and the line between Creator and creature is never crossed. Peter's own sentence shows what the sharing is for: "so that through them you may become partakers of the divine nature, having escaped from the corruption that is in the world because of sinful desire" (2 Peter 1:4). It is about holiness and freedom from corruption, not about commanding health and wealth into existence. And John 10, as we saw, argues from the lesser to the greater about the Son. It says nothing about the nature of believers.

The deepest answer is in the psalm itself. Verse 6 is not a promise. It is the first half of a death sentence, and the second half follows at once: "nevertheless, like men you shall die." Quoting the title without the sentence turns an indictment into a compliment. Worse, the psalm tells us exactly what the title was for. The gods were given rank so that the weak, the fatherless and the needy would be defended, and they were condemned for turning their authority toward the wicked and toward themselves. A teaching that takes "you are gods" as a license for personal prosperity, and so often funds itself from the gifts of the poorest people in the room, has not escaped the sin of Psalm 82. It has repeated it. The wider history of that teaching is in [What Is the Prosperity Gospel, and Is It Biblical?](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel).

The rest of us should not feel too safe. The instinct to hear authority as status rather than trust is not confined to television. It lives in the pulpit that becomes a platform, the board that protects the institution before the people it exists to serve, the parent who confuses control with care. Any Christian who holds power over anyone weaker, and most of us do, has been tempted to hear "you are gods" as flattery. The psalm will not let us keep it.

## When the foundations shake

Now return to the clause left hanging in verse 5: "all the foundations of the earth are shaken." The psalm links two things modern people keep apart, the justice done in a courtroom and the stability of the world. Elsewhere God says, "When the earth totters, and all its inhabitants, it is I who keep steady its pillars" (Psalm 75:3), and the Psalms twice name what his throne rests on: "Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne" (Psalm 89:14; compare Psalm 97:2). Hans Heinrich Schmid argued in *Gerechtigkeit als Weltordnung* (1968) that across the ancient Near East righteousness was understood as the order of the world itself, so that a corrupt verdict disturbed more than one household. Israel shared that conviction and grounded it in a person. Justice for the weak is not an ornament on a stable world. It is part of what holds the world up, because it is the character of the one who holds it.

This is not only poetry. Where courts can be bought, contracts mean nothing, and where the powerful learn that no one will call them to account, trust drains out of every institution at once, the church included.

The psalm measures every political tribe with the same rod, and none of us passes. Those on the right who fear that talk of unjust systems will excuse personal sin are right that each defendant here is personally guilty and that the law forbids tilting the scales toward the poor. But the charge is also about how power is arranged and for whom it works, and a church that won't ask that question has chosen not to read its own Scripture. Those on the left who hear an endorsement of their program should notice that the psalm condemns the powers themselves, not only the other side's use of them, and gives the defenseless no party. The child not yet born and the family fleeing a failed state both have a claim on the psalm's question, and the question is whose hand holds the weak. The theological case that biblical justice is neither a partisan platform nor a private sentiment is made in [Is Biblical Justice Political?](/writing/justice-not-political-theological).

Then the psalm ends with a prayer. "Arise, O God, judge the earth; for you shall inherit all the nations!" (Psalm 82:8). The one who stood to judge in verse 1 is asked to rise and rule what the gods mismanaged. The church has long heard that prayer answered in Christ. God had said to his anointed, "Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage" (Psalm 2:8), and after the resurrection Jesus told his disciples, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (Matthew 28:18). Paul says that at the cross God "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (Colossians 2:15). The gods of Psalm 82 were sentenced to die like men. The Son of God did die like a man, at the hands of rulers who judged unjustly, and he rose. The nations are inherited by the one who let the powers do their worst to him.

That is why the prayer is dangerous to pray. Christians will one day judge the world, Paul says (1 Corinthians 6:2), which means we are a council already, trusted with smaller judgments now. Peter warns that "it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (1 Peter 4:17). When we ask God to arise and judge the earth, we are asking him to begin in the rooms where we hold the power: at the family table, in the church budget meeting, in the office where someone's hours are set, in the voting booth. The psalm leaves every reader holding the question it put to the gods, whose hand the weak are in, in the rooms where we have authority this week. No one else can answer it for us. The judge who asks it has already stood in the dock for us, and that is the only reason we can bear to hear it asked.
