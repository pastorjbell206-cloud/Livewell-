The paper comes in an envelope, or it comes as a sentence at the kitchen table, or it comes as the slow understanding that the person across the bed left the marriage years before either of you said the word out loud. However it arrives, it doesn't feel like a doctrine. It feels like a death.

The people who type this question into a search box are rarely doing theology for sport. Some still wear the ring in a house gone quiet and want to know whether leaving would be a sin. Some are a decade past it, married again, with a shame that walks into church a step ahead of them. Some were left, some did the leaving, and some can no longer tell which is true. And some are skeptics who want to know whether Christians believe anything here at all or simply adjust to whatever the culture has already decided.

One thing belongs at the top, before any argument, because some who read this are reading it from inside danger. No Christian view of divorce, including the strictest one, requires anyone to stay in a home where they are being beaten, threatened, sexually coerced, or controlled. The traditions disagree about what separation means for the marriage bond and whether a new marriage may follow. They do not disagree that a person may get safe. If that is where you are, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 at any hour, and if you are in immediate danger, call 911. The essay returns to this near the end.

The claim this essay has to earn is that the Bible's teaching on divorce is harder than the culture wants and more merciful than the church has often been. Jesus, asked whether a man could send away his wife "for any cause," pulled the law of Moses back toward the garden and in the same breath named an exception. Paul, facing a case Jesus hadn't addressed, named another. Faithful Christians have read those exceptions differently for most of the church's history, and an honest answer has to begin by saying what kind of disagreement that is.

### What kind of question this is

Start with what isn't in dispute, because nearly everything true here begins there. Marriage is good, and it is older than any culture that has tried to amend it. When Jesus was asked about divorce, he didn't begin with Moses. He reached past Moses to the beginning, to the man and woman God made and joined, and concluded: "So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matthew 19:6, ESV). Paul would later say that the one flesh points beyond itself to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32). That's why the vow is never a convenience and its breaking is never small. Every tradition described below confesses as much. The argument that follows is among people who all hold onto it.

The dividing line is narrower than the noise suggests. Are there grounds on which a Christian may rightly end a marriage? If so, may a divorced Christian marry again while the former spouse is living? Those questions have divided sincere believers from one another without dividing any of them from Christ. The creeds don't mention them, and nothing about the Trinity, the person of Christ, the cross, or the resurrection hangs on the answer. They are second-order questions, serious enough to shape a church's discipline and a family's future, and open enough that a brother who answers them differently is still a brother. The Eastern Orthodox churches have long permitted a second marriage, with a penitential rite, as mercy toward human weakness. The Roman Catholic Church does not. Most churches of the Reformation permit it on defined grounds. All of them recite the Nicene Creed, and none of them thinks divorce is morally neutral or the divorced beyond grace.

### What Moses allowed and what Malachi saw

The foundational passage in the Old Testament is not a command to divorce. It's case law, one long conditional sentence with its only command at the very end:

> When a man takes a wife and marries her, if then she finds no favor in his eyes because he has found some indecency in her, and he writes her a certificate of divorce and puts it in her hand and sends her out of his house, and if she goes and becomes another man's wife, and the latter man hates her and writes her a certificate of divorce and puts it in her hand and sends her out of his house, or if the latter man dies, who took her to be his wife, then her former husband, who sent her away, may not take her again to be his wife, after she has been defiled, for that is an abomination before the LORD. (Deuteronomy 24:1–4, ESV)

Moses assumes divorce was already happening. He doesn't invent it or bless it. He regulates it, and the regulation cuts in the woman's favor. A wife simply driven out of the house was left in limbo, neither married nor free. The written certificate settled her status, and when the rabbis set down their law in the Mishnah around AD 200, the tractate on divorce, Gittin, treated its essential words as a declaration that she was free to marry any man. The prohibition at the end restrains the husband too. He can't send a wife away and reclaim her at his pleasure, like property.

The Law also knew that a marriage could be broken by neglect. Regulating the marriage of a slave-wife, Exodus says that if her husband takes another wife, "he shall not diminish her food, her clothing, or her marital rights. And if he does not do these three things for her, she shall go out for nothing, without payment of money" (Exodus 21:10–11, ESV). The rabbis reasoned that if the least protected wife in Israel had those rights, every wife did. That small passage will matter later.

Malachi, preaching to Judah in the century after the return from exile, is where the Old Testament speaks most fiercely. The men of Judah were weeping at the altar and asking why God no longer accepted their offerings, and the prophet told them:

> Because the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant. Did he not make them one, with a portion of the Spirit in their union? And what was the one God seeking? Godly offspring. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and let none of you be faithless to the wife of your youth. "For the man who does not love his wife but divorces her, says the LORD, the God of Israel, covers his garment with violence, says the LORD of hosts. So guard yourselves in your spirit, and do not be faithless." (Malachi 2:14–16, ESV)

Verse 16 is one of the hardest lines in the Hebrew Bible to translate. The King James Version and the older NIV made God the subject: the LORD "hateth putting away," or "I hate divorce." The ESV makes the husband the subject, the man who does not love his wife but divorces her. Both renderings are defensible, and God's anger survives either one. But the ESV shows where the anger lands: on men discarding the wives of their youth (while, it seems, marrying "the daughter of a foreign god," 2:11), a treachery God calls violence. "I hate divorce" has too often been heard as a sentence against the divorced. The passage is a sentence against the one who breaks faith, spoken on behalf of the one he broke it against.

### What Jesus was asked

Matthew preserves the exact wording of the question put to Jesus: "Is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause?" (Matthew 19:3, ESV). It sounds general. It wasn't. "Any cause" sat at the center of a live dispute between the two great Pharisaic schools of the generation before him.

The Mishnah records the argument. The school of Shammai read the "some indecency" of Deuteronomy 24:1 narrowly: a man could divorce his wife only for unchastity. The school of Hillel read the Hebrew phrase, literally "a matter of indecency," as two grounds, indecency and "a matter," so that a man could divorce his wife for anything, even for spoiling a dish. By the first century, on Instone-Brewer's reading of the evidence, the Hillelite view had largely won in practice; Josephus, retelling the law of Moses in the 90s, paraphrased it as permission to divorce for any cause whatever. David Instone-Brewer argues in *Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible* (2002) that "any cause" had become a technical term for this easy, no-fault divorce available to any Jewish husband. The Pharisees were asking Jesus which school he belonged to.

He refused the terms. He went behind Deuteronomy to Genesis, to "male and female" and "the two shall become one flesh," and concluded that God, not man, had done the joining. When they pressed him ("Why then did Moses command one to give a certificate of divorce and to send her away?"), he corrected their verb: "Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:7–8, ESV). They said *command*. He said *allowed*. Moses' law restrained sin; it did not describe God's design, and Jesus judged the entire debate from the design. Then came the sentence the controversy has turned on ever since: "And I say to you: whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery" (Matthew 19:9, ESV).

The disciples heard how hard that was: "If such is the case of a man with his wife, it is better not to marry" (Matthew 19:10, ESV). Jesus had taken away an exit the men of his generation assumed was theirs by right, as he had in the Sermon on the Mount: "everyone who divorces his wife, except on the ground of sexual immorality, makes her commit adultery, and whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery" (Matthew 5:32, ESV).

Mark's account has no exception at all. In the house, Jesus tells the disciples, "Whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her, and if she divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery" (Mark 10:11–12, ESV). *Against her* was startling: in Jewish law a man committed adultery against another man's marriage, never against his own wife, and Jesus makes the discarded wife the injured party. Luke's single verse is equally unqualified (Luke 16:18).

So the same Jesus speaks absolutely in Mark and Luke and with an exception in Matthew. Everything the church has argued since flows from how those two facts are fitted together.

### Paul and the deserted believer

Paul faced a problem Jesus hadn't ruled on: in Corinth, converts found themselves married to spouses who still worshiped other gods. First he repeats the Lord's teaching: "To the married I give this charge (not I, but the Lord): the wife should not separate from her husband (but if she does, she should remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband), and the husband should not divorce his wife" (1 Corinthians 7:10–11, ESV). Then he turns to the new case: "To the rest I say (I, not the Lord) that if any brother has a wife who is an unbeliever, and she consents to live with him, he should not divorce her" (1 Corinthians 7:12, ESV).

"I, not the Lord" isn't Paul lowering his authority. It's Paul marking that he is applying Christ's teaching to a situation Christ's recorded words didn't cover. Faith in Christ is no reason to discard an unbelieving spouse, who is even "made holy" through the believing one (7:14). But then: "if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved. God has called you to peace" (1 Corinthians 7:15, ESV).

What "not enslaved" releases the believer from is the second fault line of the debate. The permanence reading says it frees the deserted believer from the duty to hold the marriage together, but not to marry again, since Paul has just said a separated wife should "remain unmarried or else be reconciled." The Reformation reading says that readers who knew divorce as the certificate that made a woman free to marry would have heard "not enslaved" as exactly that freedom. The permanence reading has verse 11. The Reformation reading has the plain force of verse 15. Each has to explain the other's verse.

### Four answers, each in its strongest voice

Take the permanence view first, at full strength, because the last fifty years have made it easy to sneer at, and it doesn't deserve the sneer. It holds that what God joins, no one on earth has standing to pull apart, including the two people inside it, and that a separated Christian may not remarry while the former spouse lives. It can claim early witnesses. *The Shepherd of Hermas*, in the second century, told a husband who put away an adulterous wife to remain unmarried so that she could repent and return. Gordon Wenham and William Heth argued in *Jesus and Divorce* (1984) that the church of the first centuries overwhelmingly refused remarriage after divorce, and that this near-consensus, among people who read the Gospels in their own language and culture, is the best guide to what Jesus meant; Matthew's exception, on their reading, permits separation but not a new marriage. Others read it as unfaithfulness during betrothal, since Matthew alone tells of Joseph, who "resolved to divorce her quietly" (Matthew 1:19, ESV). The permanence Christian looks at a culture that ends a marriage more easily than it breaks a lease and says that somebody has to testify with an entire life that the promise meant what it said. That isn't cruelty. It is guarding what a covenant is, at personal cost.

The Roman Catholic view shares that conviction and grounds it sacramentally. Augustine, in *On the Good of Marriage* (c. 401), named three goods of marriage: offspring, fidelity, and the *sacramentum*, a bond that persists even when spouses separate. The Council of Trent, at its twenty-fourth session in November 1563, anathematized anyone who said the church errs in teaching that adultery does not dissolve the bond and that neither spouse, not even the innocent one, may remarry while the other lives. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1992) holds that a ratified and consummated marriage between the baptized cannot be dissolved by any human power, and that the divorced and civilly remarried may not receive Communion while that situation continues. What the church grants instead is a declaration of nullity, a judgment that a true marriage never came into being because something essential, such as free consent, was missing at the start. Critics call it divorce by another name; Catholics answer that ending a marriage and finding that one never existed are different things. Catholic law also applies Paul's "not enslaved": under the Pauline privilege, a marriage between two unbaptized people can be dissolved in favor of the faith when one converts and the other departs.

The Reformation view, often called the Erasmian view, takes its name from a man who stayed Catholic. Erasmus, in the annotations to his Greek New Testament (the long note on divorce, attached to 1 Corinthians 7:39, appeared in the 1519 edition), argued from charity that the church should allow divorce with freedom to remarry for adultery and, he suggested, other grave causes. The Reformers took it up. Luther, in *The Estate of Marriage* (1522), allowed divorce for adultery and for a spouse's stubborn refusal of the marriage bed, and let the innocent party marry again. The Westminster Confession of 1646 gave the position its classic English form: "In the case of adultery after marriage, it is lawful for the innocent party to sue out a divorce, and, after the divorce, to marry another, as if the offending party were dead" (24.5). Then it guards the door, warning that human corruption "is apt to study arguments unduly to put asunder those whom God hath joined together in marriage," and insisting that "nothing but adultery, or such wilful desertion as can no way be remedied by the Church, or civil magistrate, is cause sufficient of dissolving the bond of marriage" (24.6). This is not the party of loose vows. It is the party that would not erase the exceptions Scripture wrote down. Craig Keener argues in *...And Marries Another* (1991) that the absolute sayings in Mark and Luke are the kind of sweeping principle Jewish teachers stated without listing the exceptions everyone knew, that Matthew makes one explicit, and that a divorce forbidding remarriage would have been unintelligible to hearers for whom the certificate's whole purpose was freedom to marry again.

The fourth view extends that logic, and Instone-Brewer is its most careful defender. He argues that when Jesus rejected "any cause" divorce, he rejected the Hillelite innovation, not the older grounds in Exodus 21 that both schools accepted and no one asked him about. Silence about grounds nobody disputed is not repeal. Paul, he notes, restates those obligations in Christian form: "The husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband" (1 Corinthians 7:3, ESV), and each spouse is concerned with "how to please" the other (7:33–34). If a covenant carries obligations, it can be broken by their persistent violation, and abuse is neglect in its most violent form. On this view, the abused or grossly neglected spouse is the victim of a broken covenant and is free. Its critics answer that it builds a great deal on what the Gospels don't say, and that once neglect is a ground, the category can widen until it resembles the "any cause" Jesus refused.

### What 1969 changed, and what it exposed in us

For most of Western history the church's debate ran alongside law that roughly shared its assumptions. In 1969 Governor Ronald Reagan signed California's Family Law Act, which took effect in 1970 and let a court dissolve a marriage for "irreconcilable differences" without either spouse proving fault. Within about fifteen years nearly every state had followed.

The reformers had real reasons. The fault system had produced a theater of perjury, couples staging evidence of adultery for courts that pretended to believe it, and forcing people to lie under oath was not protecting the covenant. But no-fault did something its authors didn't fully foresee. It made marriage the one contract in American law either party could end alone, at any time, without breach or penalty. John Witte Jr. traces in *From Sacrament to Contract* (1997) how Western marriage moved from sacrament to social estate to covenant and finally to private contract; by the time no-fault arrived, it had become something weaker than a contract. Barbara Dafoe Whitehead named the cultural shift in *The Divorce Culture* (1997): divorce had moved from a legal event marking a marriage's failure to what she called expressive divorce, the self's right to leave a relationship that no longer served it.

The social science cuts in more than one direction. Paul Amato and Alan Booth, following families for *A Generation at Risk* (1997), found that children whose parents ended high-conflict marriages often did better afterward, while children whose parents ended low-conflict marriages fared worse. The rigorist needs the first half of that finding. The rest of us need the second.

This is where the diagnosis turns inward, because the church didn't stand outside the divorce culture. We lived in it. Many congregations kept the language of permanence in their wedding liturgies while adopting the culture's practice, preaching against divorce in general and making exceptions for whoever was sitting in front of us. That is the sentimentalist's failure: the vow emptied until "till death" means "till it gets hard." The other failure has been far more cruel. Churches have sent women back to men who beat them, calling her staying obedience and her leaving rebellion. It doesn't look like holiness. It looks like protecting a doctrine with someone else's body. Malachi's word about violence was written against that kind of man, and we have sometimes aimed it at his victim.

The two failures share a root. The rigorist stopped feeling the person; the sentimentalist stopped feeling the promise. I have to say plainly that I have been the rigorist. I have reached for the rule because the rule was clean and the person in front of me was not, because a principle asks nothing of me and a suffering human being asks everything. When I've gotten this wrong, I've gotten it wrong in the direction of protecting my theology from somebody's pain, and that sin is available to the strict and the lenient alike.

The distinction the whole question turns on is between the person who files and the person who breaks. The law records who went to court. Scripture asks who broke faith. Sometimes they are the same person, and often they are not. The spouse who finally signs the papers after years of betrayal, desertion, or terror may be the one Malachi's God is defending, and the spouse who never filed anything may be the one who covered his garment with violence long before.

### Where this essay lands, and what it cannot settle

This essay reads the two exceptions Scripture names as real. Jesus could have said that whoever divorces and remarries commits adultery, and stopped. In Matthew he didn't. Paul could have told a deserted believer to wait out a marriage the other person had abandoned. He said instead that the believer is not enslaved. The essay won't erase either sentence to make the rule tidier. What those sentences permit next, separation only or a new marriage, is the question the traditions above divide over, and the essay doesn't settle it for anyone from here; that belongs with the reader's own church. It holds just as firmly that the church must never order an abused spouse back under the roof where the abuse happens. Whether abuse and grave neglect themselves dissolve the covenant, as Instone-Brewer argues, it treats as a serious question and leaves open. And it will not rule on anyone's particular marriage from a distance.

The strongest objection to reading the exceptions as freedom to remarry comes from the permanence side. Mark, Luke, and Paul in 1 Corinthians 7:10–11 give Jesus' teaching without exception; Matthew's exception appears in one Gospel only; and the Christians closest to the apostles, from Hermas to Augustine, largely read it as permitting separation without remarriage. If the Reformation reading is right, the early church misread its Lord. The best answer on the Reformation side is Keener's: to a first-century Jewish hearer, divorce simply was the freedom to remarry, so an exception to one was an exception to the other. That answer carries weight. It doesn't explain why so many early Christians heard it otherwise. Augustine himself conceded, in *On Faith and Works* (c. 413), that Scripture was obscure on whether a man who put away an adulterous wife could marry again, and that one who erred there erred pardonably. If Augustine could hold his view with that humility, so can we. A Christian who lands with the permanence view, with the Reformers, or further with Instone-Brewer, is still a brother or sister at the same table.

No view requires anyone to remain in danger. Separation for safety is allowed by every tradition described here; the disagreement concerns only what follows. If you are being hurt, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, where advocates can help you plan for safety, including how to leave safely. In immediate danger, call 911. If you are thinking of harming yourself, call or text 988. A real decision about a real marriage belongs in a room, with a pastor who knows your name and a wise counselor, and a lawyer where the law is involved. Nothing here is legal advice.

To the divorced reader, who has probably been condemned or flattered and deserves neither: if you broke your marriage, grace does not pretend you didn't. It names the sin and meets it at the cross, and repentance is the door, not the price of admission. If you were betrayed or abandoned, you are not guilty of another person's sin, and a church that treats you as though you were has failed you. If you have married again, Catholic and Protestant churches will tell you different things, and you should hear your own church honestly. But many who hold even the strictest Protestant view counsel remarried couples not to break a second covenant in penance for the first, and none of them would say God has filed you under a category his mercy has learned to skip.

The prophets were not afraid to describe God as a husband who was left. "I had sent her away with a decree of divorce," he says of faithless Israel (Jeremiah 3:8, ESV), and when the same book promises a new covenant, God describes the old one as "my covenant that they broke, though I was their husband" (Jeremiah 31:32, ESV). God knows the grief of a broken marriage from inside it, and his answer to betrayal was to keep faith himself, at the price of his Son. Hosea's God, the injured party, goes after the one who left: "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (Hosea 2:14, ESV).

Then there is the woman Jesus met at a well in Samaria. She had had five husbands, and the man she was living with was not her husband. John never tells us why, and in her world a woman could be divorced or widowed five times without having chosen any of it. Jesus didn't open with the count. He asked her for a drink. When he did name her history, he named all of it, and she went back to the town that knew her story saying, "He told me all that I ever did" (John 4:39, ESV). She was fully known and not sent away. That is what the church owes the divorced: honesty without contempt, and a welcome that doesn't depend on pretending.

Faithful Christians have stood over the same wreckage and disagreed, in the fear of God, about what love requires next. What none of them may do is make the broken covenant the last word about a person. Whatever your marriage became, you have not out-sinned the cross, and the God who was left by his own bride and went after her anyway has not stopped going after anyone. What that asks of you now, whether confession, patience, safety, or the long faithfulness of a second marriage, this page can't decide. You'll have to carry it into a room with people who can help you bear it, and into the presence of the one who already knows everything you ever did.
