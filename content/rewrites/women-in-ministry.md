The question almost never arrives as a debate topic. It arrives attached to a person. A woman in a Bible study turns out to be the clearest teacher in the room, and someone wonders aloud whether the men should be hearing her too. A daughter tells her parents she thinks God may be calling her to preach, and they don't know what to say. A church searches for a new pastor, and a member quietly notices that the most gifted person on the search committee could never have been a candidate. And somewhere outside the church a skeptic, who has heard that Christians won't let women lead, takes it as one more proof that the faith is an old arrangement for keeping power where it has always been.

Each of them deserves a better answer than they usually get. Too often one kind of church hands over a verse and a closed door, and another kind hands over a shrug at the verse and an open one. Both answers are shorter than the Bible, and people can tell. What follows states the two main Christian positions in the form their own defenders would sign, reads the passages they turn on in their setting, and tells the history plainly. It will not close the case, and the reason isn't timidity. It's the kind of question this is.

## Gifts everyone affirms, and the office in question

Start with what isn't in dispute, because it is far more than most people assume. Every serious participant in this argument begins with the first chapter of the Bible: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The image is given to both, and the charge to fill the earth and rule it is given to both in the next verse. Neither camp believes women are less intelligent, less spiritual or less fully human, and anyone who holds that view has left both camps behind.

Both also read the same New Testament, where women stand near the center of the story rather than at its edges. Luke, describing the company that traveled with Jesus through Galilee, names the Twelve and then, in the same breath, names the women who were with him and paid for the mission: "and Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod's household manager, and Susanna, and many others, who provided for them out of their means" (Luke 8:3, ESV). At the end of the Gospels, the women are the ones who stay at the cross when the men have scattered, and the ones who find the tomb empty, and the first to be sent to tell it. At Pentecost, Peter explains the noise in the streets by reading Joel, and the passage he reads is about daughters as much as sons: "even on my male servants and female servants in those days I will pour out my Spirit, and they shall prophesy" (Acts 2:18, ESV). Complementarians and egalitarians both affirm that the Spirit gives gifts to women, that women pray and prophesy, teach and evangelize, and that a church which wastes those gifts is disobeying God.

So the real question is narrower than it sounds, and the narrowness matters. It is not whether women may serve, speak or lead in general. It is whether the office of pastor, which the New Testament also calls elder and overseer, together with the authoritative teaching of the gathered church that belongs to that office, is open to women. Much of the heat in this argument comes from people who hear a claim about office as an insult to dignity, or who defend dignity as if it settled the question of office. Keeping the two apart doesn't answer the question, but it makes an honest answer possible.

The distinction also shows where the disagreement has to be sorted. Historic Christianity has always ranked its convictions. First-order doctrines are the ones the creeds bind: the Trinity, the full deity and humanity of Christ, his bodily resurrection, salvation by grace. Second-order questions divide faithful churches without dividing the faith: baptism, the Lord's Supper, church government. Third-order questions are genuinely open. The ordination of women belongs in the second category. The Nicene Creed and the definition of Chalcedon say nothing about who may hold the office of elder, and the people on both sides confess every article of them. Yet the question can't be pushed down to third-order either, because a congregation can't stay neutral in practice. Somebody is ordained or isn't. Somebody preaches on Sunday or doesn't. That is exactly why churches on different sides of it often end up in different denominations while still calling one another brothers and sisters.

The Catholic and Orthodox churches reserve the priesthood to men on sacramental grounds of their own. The argument here is the Protestant one, among people who agree that Scripture is the final authority and disagree about what it teaches, which is exactly what makes it painful.

## The complementarian case in its strongest voice

The caricature of complementarianism is a room of men guarding their turf. Its defenders don't recognize themselves in it, and a fair reader shouldn't either. The position's fullest modern statement came in two stages: the Danvers Statement, drafted in 1987 by the scholars and pastors who formed the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, and the long volume John Piper and Wayne Grudem edited from that work, *Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood* (1991). On the question of church office, its case has four steps.

It begins before the fall. In Genesis 2 the man is formed first, receives the command about the tree, and names the woman when she is brought to him. The woman is made as his partner: "Then the LORD God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him'" (Genesis 2:18, ESV). Raymond Ortlund Jr., in his chapter on Genesis 1 to 3 in that volume, argued that the passage presents equality and headship together, in a world with no sin in it yet, and that the curse of Genesis 3 doesn't create male leadership but corrupts it into domination on one side and resentment on the other. Headship, on this reading, is part of the goodness of creation, and the gospel restores it rather than abolishing it.

Then comes the text the whole argument leans on. Writing to Timothy about the order of the church's assembly, Paul says: "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; rather, she is to remain quiet" (1 Timothy 2:12, ESV). The next sentence supplies his reason: "For Adam was formed first, then Eve" (1 Timothy 2:13, ESV). Thomas Schreiner, in his long chapter in *Women in the Church* (1995), pressed the point that gives complementarians their confidence. Paul had many local reasons available to him if the problem were only local, and he reached for none of them. He reached back to Eden, to an order that predates Ephesus, the first century and the fall itself. Nothing in the passage says the women were uneducated or that the ban lifts once they learn. In the same volume Andreas Köstenberger studied how Greek joins two verbs with the word translated "or," and argued that the pairing requires both activities to be viewed alike, so that "teach" here means ordinary teaching, not false teaching, and "exercise authority" means ordinary authority, not abuse of it. H. Scott Baldwin surveyed the known ancient uses of the rare verb behind "exercise authority," *authentein*, which appears only here in the New Testament, and argued that they center on the idea of authority, so the word need not mean domineering.

Paul does the same thing in Corinth. "But I want you to understand that the head of every man is Christ, the head of a wife is her husband, and the head of Christ is God" (1 Corinthians 11:3, ESV). Schreiner's chapter on that passage in *Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood* argued that "head" carries the sense of authority (a meaning egalitarians contest), and that while the head covering was a cultural sign, the order it signified was not, since Paul again grounds it in creation: the woman came from the man and was made for him (1 Corinthians 11:8–9). When the same letter says women should keep silent in the churches, D. A. Carson, in his chapter in the same book, argued that Paul can't mean total silence, since three chapters earlier women pray and prophesy aloud. The silence, Carson proposed, concerns the weighing of prophecies that Paul has just described (1 Corinthians 14:29), an act of authoritative judgment in the assembly. This yields one of the most important complementarian distinctions. Prophecy, a Spirit-given word that the church then tests, is open to women. The authoritative teaching and governing that belong to the elder's office are not. The same distinction shapes how complementarians read Deborah: a prophetess raised in one of Israel's darkest seasons, who summoned Barak to command the army, and whose story counts his refusal to go without her as a loss of his honor (Judges 4:8–9).

The qualifications for that office point the same way. Paul describes the overseer as a man: "Therefore an overseer must be above reproach, the husband of one wife, sober-minded, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach" (1 Timothy 3:2, ESV). Complementarians add the example of Jesus, who broke convention with women constantly, taught them, defended them and sent them as the first witnesses of his resurrection, and still chose twelve men as apostles. A teacher that free with custom was not, they argue, bowing to it here. And they note that the teaching gift in women is given its own direction. Paul tells Titus that older women "are to teach what is good" (Titus 2:3, ESV), and Timothy's own faith came through his mother and grandmother (2 Timothy 1:5).

At its best the position speaks of headship as a burden. Its defenders insist that an elder will give account to God for the souls in his care (Hebrews 13:17), that the office is a weight to be carried, not a prize to be won, and that they have no authority to revise an order they didn't invent. They also carry a worry their critics should hear fairly: that a way of reading which dissolves 1 Timothy 2 into its local setting will not stop there, and that other plain texts will be relocated to the first century one by one.

The camp is not uniform, and its range is part of an honest description. At one end are churches that permit no woman to teach adult men in any setting. At the other are complementarians who restrict only the elder office and allow women to teach mixed classes, lead ministries and even preach on a Sunday when the elders invite and oversee it, on the reasoning that the authority belongs to the office rather than the act. Grudem himself has drawn up detailed lists sorting church activities by how much governing or teaching authority each carries, which tells you how much judgment the application requires. And in 2016 complementarians fell into a sharp dispute among themselves when Reformed theologians such as Carl Trueman and Liam Goligher argued that grounding male headship in an eternal subordination of the Son to the Father strayed from Nicene teaching on the Trinity. Many complementarians now rest the case on creation and the apostolic texts and leave the Trinitarian analogy aside.

## The egalitarian case in its strongest voice

The caricature of egalitarianism is a reader with an eraser, cutting out whatever the age finds embarrassing. Its best defenders don't recognize themselves in that either. Christians for Biblical Equality was founded in 1987, the same year as the Danvers Statement, by evangelicals who held a high view of Scripture and believed Scripture itself required their conclusion. Their fullest scholarly statement is *Discovering Biblical Equality* (2004), edited by Ronald Pierce and Rebecca Merrill Groothuis with Gordon Fee, and on church office its case runs from the garden to the new creation.

It also begins before the fall, but it reads the garden differently. The image and the commission of Genesis 1 go to both, without a word about one ruling the other. The word translated "helper" in Genesis 2, *'ezer*, carries no hint of subordination. The Old Testament most often uses it of God himself, as in the psalm: "My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (Psalm 121:2, ESV). The woman is a help "fit for him," corresponding to him, the one creature in the garden who is his match. Being formed first proves little, since the animals were formed before the man. The first time anyone rules over anyone in Genesis is in the curse of chapter 3, where it appears as a consequence of sin, and the egalitarian asks why the church should treat the curse as the pattern the gospel preserves.

Then the egalitarian walks through the women the Bible actually shows. "Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel at that time" (Judges 4:4, ESV). The people came to her for judgment, and she summoned Israel's general and told him what God required. The egalitarian sees nothing in the narrative that treats her as an anomaly to be corrected. When King Josiah's officials found the book of the law in the temple, the high priest among them went to Huldah the prophetess to inquire of the LORD concerning it (2 Kings 22:13–14), and the egalitarian reads her answer as an authoritative word on Scripture by a woman, sought by the priesthood. Joel promised a day when the Spirit would fall on daughters and female servants as well as sons, and Luke says that day came at Pentecost. After it, the pattern holds. Philip "had four unmarried daughters, who prophesied" (Acts 21:9, ESV). When the eloquent Apollos preached an incomplete gospel in Ephesus, a married couple corrected him, and the wife is often named first: "He began to speak boldly in the synagogue, but when Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:26, ESV). Paul calls two women in Philippi co-laborers "who have labored side by side with me in the gospel" (Philippians 4:3, ESV). And in Romans 16 he commends Phoebe as a *diakonos* of her church and greets Junia among the apostles, two texts that need a section of their own.

Then come Paul's restrictions, read against what Paul himself assumes. In 1 Corinthians he regulates how women pray and prophesy in the gathered church, and he never suggests they should stop: "but every wife who prays or prophesies with her head uncovered dishonors her head, since it is the same as if her head were shaven" (1 Corinthians 11:5, ESV). Whatever the command to silence in chapter 14 means, it can't contradict that. Gordon Fee, in his commentary *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* (1987), argued from the manuscripts that the two verses on silence were not part of Paul's original letter, since a group of ancient witnesses places them after verse 40 instead, the kind of wandering usually left by a marginal note later copied into the text. Philip Payne developed that textual case at length in *Man and Woman, One in Christ* (2009). Others, among them Craig Keener in *Paul, Women and Wives* (1992), accept the verses as Paul's and read them in light of the next line, which tells women who want to learn to ask at home. The problem, on Keener's reading, was disruptive questions from women who had been denied the education the men had received, and Paul's answer was to see that they got it.

That reading shapes the egalitarian account of 1 Timothy 2. The first command in the passage, "Let a woman learn quietly with all submissiveness" (1 Timothy 2:11, ESV), was itself a departure from custom, and the letter as a whole is a fight against false teaching that had found a hearing in the households of Ephesus, including among women Paul later describes as always learning and never arriving at the truth (2 Timothy 3:6–7). Keener and Payne read Adam's priority as an illustration fitted to that crisis. Eve had not heard the command from God directly, she was the one deceived, and the church in Ephesus had untaught women spreading deception. The rare verb *authentein* has also been rendered in different ways across the history of English Bibles. The King James Version of 1611 translated it "usurp authority," and the 2011 New International Version renders it "assume authority." Payne argues that Paul joins the two verbs into a single idea, forbidding a woman to take teaching authority on herself, not forbidding every woman to hold an office the church confers. The paragraph then closes with the promise that she "will be saved through childbearing" (1 Timothy 2:15, ESV), a verse scholars in both camps count among the hardest in Paul, which should keep every reader from treating the sentences around it as simple.

Finally, the egalitarian sets all of this under a sentence Paul wrote as a charter: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, ESV). The last pair breaks the pattern of "neither…nor" and borrows the wording of Genesis 1:27 in the Greek Old Testament, "male and female." Fee argued in *Discovering Biblical Equality* that this is not only a statement about who is saved but about the shape of the new creation, and that Paul showed it had social consequences when he confronted Peter over table fellowship in Antioch (Galatians 2:11–14). The egalitarian also has an answer to the complementarian worry about where this way of reading ends. The church already reads some apostolic instructions, those to slaves most obviously, as regulating an order the gospel would outgrow, and the question is whether there is a principled way to tell which. William Webb's *Slaves, Women and Homosexuals* (2001) argued that the canon itself shows a redemptive movement on slavery and on women that it does not show on sexual ethics, so the method, he contended, does not slide wherever the culture pushes.

This camp has its range too. Some egalitarians, Payne among them, argue that Paul forbade only a self-assumed teaching authority and never barred women from an office the church confers. Others, like Keener, grant that Paul did silence certain women in Ephesus and hold that the restriction answered a local lack of education and false teaching, and lapses where those are absent. Beyond both stands a position that says Paul was simply wrong, and the scholars of *Discovering Biblical Equality* reject that as firmly as any complementarian does. Their shared fear mirrors the other side's. A church that restricts what the Spirit has given, they believe, will waste its gifted women and teach its daughters that God's call is suspect because of their sex.

## Phoebe, Junia and the question each side can't quite answer

Romans 16 is a list of greetings most readers skim, and it holds two of the hardest texts in the debate. The first is Phoebe: "I commend to you our sister Phoebe, a servant of the church at Cenchreae" (Romans 16:1, ESV). The word behind "servant" is *diakonos*, the word Paul uses of himself and of Timothy, and the ESV notes "deaconess" as an alternative. Paul goes on to call her a patron of many, himself included, and most scholars think she carried the letter to Rome. Many complementarians readily grant that she was a deacon, and that women held that role in the early church, while arguing that the diaconate is an office of service, not of governing and teaching.

The second is harder: "Greet Andronicus and Junia, my kinsmen and my fellow prisoners. They are well known to the apostles, and they were in Christ before me" (Romans 16:7, ESV). Two questions hang on this verse. The first is whether Junia was a woman. Junia was a common Latin woman's name, and the masculine alternative, Junias, has not turned up in any ancient source. John Chrysostom, preaching on Romans in the late fourth century, marveled at the devotion of a woman counted worthy of the title of apostle. Eldon Jay Epp, in *Junia: The First Woman Apostle* (2005), traced how the masculine reading gained ground centuries later and held on in printed Greek editions well into the twentieth century before the standard text returned to the feminine. Today scholars on both sides largely agree she was a woman.

The second question divides them still. Does the Greek mean she was "well known to the apostles," as the ESV renders it, or "outstanding among the apostles," as the NIV does? Michael Burer and Daniel Wallace argued in *New Testament Studies* (2001) for the first reading from parallel Greek constructions, and Richard Bauckham in *Gospel Women* (2002), among others, contested their evidence. Even the inclusive reading may not settle the matter, since Paul can use "apostle" for a commissioned envoy of the churches (2 Corinthians 8:23), not one of the Twelve. Bauckham added a striking suggestion: that Junia may be the Latin name of Joanna, the woman of Luke 8:3 who followed Jesus in Galilee and stood at the empty tomb. If he is right, the woman Paul greeted among the apostles had been among the first to hear that the Lord was risen (Luke 24:10).

Now the hardest question for each side comes into focus. The complementarian asks the egalitarian why Paul, if the restriction was only for a crisis in Ephesus, grounded it in the order of creation instead of the crisis, and why the church for most of its history read it that way. The egalitarian asks the complementarian why, if that order forbids a woman to teach men with authority, God raised Deborah to judge Israel, sent the priests to Huldah for the meaning of the law, and let Priscilla correct Apollos, and why Paul greets Junia as he does. The egalitarian adds one more: complementarians themselves can't agree where the line falls, whether a woman may teach a mixed class or preach under the elders, and a text that plain shouldn't need so many lists. Neither side has fully answered the other. That is the honest state of the question, not a failure to find the answer.

This essay doesn't say where it lands, and that's deliberate. On a question the church has held open with its eyes open, it is more useful to hand the reader both positions at full strength than to add one more voice to a count.

## What the church has actually done

The history refuses to flatter either side. Around 112 the Roman governor Pliny the Younger wrote to the emperor Trajan that he had tortured two enslaved Christian women who were called *ministrae*, a title most historians take as the Latin for deacons. The Council of Chalcedon in 451 ruled that no woman should be ordained a deaconess before the age of forty, which assumes the office existed. Gregory of Nyssa wrote of his sister Macrina as the teacher who formed him. Yet the offices of presbyter and bishop were held by men, and around 200 Tertullian protested against women who claimed the right to teach and baptize. The early church honored women's gifts and restricted the governing office, and each side can find its own reflection there.

The revivals widened the room. In 1771 John Wesley wrote to Mary Bosanquet that her preaching could be justified by an extraordinary call. Charles Finney's revivals in the 1820s let women pray aloud in mixed meetings. Around 1819 Richard Allen of the African Methodist Episcopal Church recognized Jarena Lee as a preacher, and in 1853 Antoinette Brown was ordained by a Congregational church in New York. In 1859 Phoebe Palmer published *The Promise of the Father*, resting women's preaching on Joel and Pentecost, and Catherine Booth answered a critic of Palmer with *Female Ministry*.

Baptists have lived on both sides. The Separate Baptists of the Carolina backcountry, from whom much of Southern Baptist life descends, had women who prayed and exhorted in public in the 1750s and 1760s, Martha Stearns Marshall best remembered among them. After the Southern Baptist Convention formed in 1845, the room narrowed. When women from Arkansas were sent as messengers in 1885, the convention rewrote its constitution to say "brethren." Lottie Moon, appointed to China in 1873, spent nearly four decades evangelizing and teaching there, most of them years in which the convention at home seated only "brethren" as messengers. In 1964 a Baptist church in Durham, North Carolina, ordained Addie Davis, the first woman ordained in a Southern Baptist congregation. The convention's 1984 resolution opposed women's ordination to pastoral leadership, and in 2000 it added to the Baptist Faith and Message that "the office of pastor is limited to men as qualified by Scripture." In 2023 messengers upheld the removal of Saddleback Church, which had ordained women as pastors, and in 2024 an amendment writing the prohibition into the constitution fell short of the two-thirds it needed. Other Baptists, including the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship formed in 1991, ordain women. Faithful Baptists stand on both sides of this line.

## Two ways to fail the same women

Each position has a characteristic way of going wrong, and most of us who have spent our lives in churches have sat quietly in one room or the other while it happened. A complementarianism held badly stops being a doctrine of equal worth and becomes a habit of never listening to women, of treating a gifted teacher as a problem to be managed, of shrinking "distinct roles" until only the ranking is left. An egalitarianism held badly starts treating the hard texts as embarrassments, until Scripture no longer judges the culture and only reflects what the culture has already decided. The failures don't carry identical weight in every church or every era, and those who were silenced or harmed must stay in view. But both are the same sin at root, making something smaller than the Bible serve our own preference and calling it obedience. We who preach are not outside that indictment. The pulpit is where it is most easily committed.

The way home is not to pretend the question is simple. It is for each side to state the other's case as well as the other would, to name its own failures before its opponent's, and to honor the women who serve in the rooms their churches have given them, whether those rooms have pulpits or not. A thoughtful Christian on the other side should be able to read your position and feel understood rather than beaten, and should still be greeted as a brother or sister at the Lord's Table. A conviction that can survive only by mocking Paul, or by mocking the sister who reads him differently, has shrunk below the book it claims to defend.

Remember who first carried the news on which the whole church stands. On the first Easter morning, women came back from the tomb and told the apostles that Christ was risen, "but these words seemed to them an idle tale, and they did not believe them" (Luke 24:11, ESV). The men who would lead the church began by dismissing the women God sent to them. That doesn't settle who may hold the office of elder, and neither side should pretend it does. It does settle something about the posture every church owes the women in it, because the risen Lord entrusted women with the first announcement of his resurrection. Whatever your church decides about the office, it will answer for whether it heard them.
