There is a moment many congregations know. A family comes in late with a son whose body won't sit still and whose voice won't keep the hush, or a woman in a power chair finds that the only place for her is the aisle, and the room quietly decides where to put its eyes. Nobody says anything unkind. The warmth is real, and so is the wish that the moment would pass. Any honest pastor knows that room, and I am a pastor. Beneath the discomfort sits an assumption none of us would say out loud: that worship is performed with a working body and a quick mind, and that people who can't perform it are welcome, but as guests.

Families who live with disability usually read that room faster than it reads itself, and the question they bring to the Bible is sharper than the one most sermons answer. It isn't whether Christians should be kind to disabled people. Kindness costs little, and it can be withdrawn. It's whether the Bible's own texts have been teaching the church to look away all along: the priests kept from the altar, the disciples asking who sinned, the healings that seem to treat every impairment as a problem waiting to be fixed.

The argument about where human worth comes from, that the image of God is a status God gives before it is a capacity anyone exercises, is made in [What Does It Mean to Be Made in the Image of God?](/writing/what-it-means-to-bear-the-image-of-god) and in [What Makes Us Human in the Age of AI?](/writing/personhood-in-the-age-of-ai). This essay takes it as settled and asks the practical question that follows. If the disabled person bears the image whole, why has the church so often treated her as a problem to solve, a miracle waiting to happen, or an object of charity? The answer turns on two distinctions, between cure and healing and between inclusion and belonging.

## The Hard Texts, Read Honestly

Start with the passage that seems worst. In Leviticus 21 the Lord tells Moses that no descendant of Aaron who has a blemish may come near to offer the bread of his God, and the list includes blindness, lameness, a disfigured face and a hunched back. Readers with disabilities have found the church's unspoken policy written down here, and they aren't wrong to feel its weight. The text keeps certain bodies from a certain role. But the same paragraph that keeps the blemished priest from the altar goes on to feed him from it.

> He may eat the bread of his God, both of the most holy and of the holy things, (Leviticus 21:22)

He isn't expelled from the priesthood or declared unclean. He eats the most holy food with his brothers. What he may not do is approach the altar or go through to the veil, and the reason given concerns the sanctuary, not his standing (Leviticus 21:23). The rule governs priests, not the congregation, and it applies to sacrificial animals too (Leviticus 22:20). It is theology drawn in pictures: whoever approaches the holy God on the people's behalf must display a wholeness no one in Israel actually had. The rule is a sign pointing past itself. It doesn't say the blind Israelite is less of Israel.

Nor does the canon leave the sign where it stood. Hebrews calls the priest it pointed toward "holy, innocent, unstained" (Hebrews 7:26), and then that unblemished priest was marked on purpose, with pierced hands and a torn side, and his torn flesh became the way in:

> Therefore, brothers, since we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, (Hebrews 10:19)

> by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh, (Hebrews 10:20)

The priest of Leviticus 21 couldn't go through the veil because of his body. The priest of Hebrews 10 opened the veil through his.

Another text is the one parents of disabled children hear most often, usually from someone who means well.

> And his disciples asked him, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2)

> Jesus answered, "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him. (John 9:3)

The disciples assume that disability is an entry in a moral ledger, charged to the person or to the parents, and Jesus refuses both columns. Nor does he mean that God blinded the man to stage a miracle, as though a life were a prop for an afternoon. He turns the question from cause toward what God is doing now. And the chapter holds an irony that is easy to miss. The healing doesn't return the man to his community. His parents are too afraid to stand with him, and the authorities cast him out (John 9:22, 34), so Jesus goes and finds him (John 9:35). The cure gave the man his eyes. It didn't give him a people. Jesus did that afterward, in a second act of grace the church has mostly forgotten to imitate.

Behind all of this sits a harder claim, that God doesn't stand at a distance from disability. When Moses tries to refuse his calling because he is slow of speech, the Lord answers:

> Then the LORD said to him, "Who has made man's mouth? Who makes him mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Is it not I, the LORD? (Exodus 4:11)

Anyone who hands that verse to a grieving parent as an explanation has misused it. God doesn't explain himself. He claims the slow tongue as his own work, sends Moses anyway, and gives him Aaron to speak alongside him (Exodus 4:14–16). The man with the impairment isn't excused from the calling. He is given a companion for it.

## A Seat at the King's Table

The Old Testament's fullest story of a disabled man begins with a fall. Mephibosheth was five when news came that his father Jonathan and his grandfather Saul were dead. His nurse fled with him, and in her haste he fell and became lame (2 Samuel 4:4). New dynasties commonly cleared away the heirs of the old, and Saul's crippled grandson grew up out of sight, in another man's house in Lo-debar. Years later David asks a question no one expected a king to ask.

> And the king said, "Is there not still someone of the house of Saul, that I may show the kindness of God to him?" Ziba said to the king, "There is still a son of Jonathan; he is crippled in his feet." (2 Samuel 9:3)

Ziba names him by his feet. David names him by his father. The word for kindness is *hesed*, covenant love, and David is keeping a promise he made to Jonathan before Mephibosheth could earn anything (1 Samuel 20:14–15).

> And David said to him, "Do not fear, for I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan, and I will restore to you all the land of Saul your father, and you shall eat at my table always." (2 Samuel 9:7)

Mephibosheth, who has absorbed what everyone believed about him, calls himself a dead dog (2 Samuel 9:8). The narrator closes the chapter this way:

> So Mephibosheth lived in Jerusalem, for he ate always at the king's table. Now he was lame in both his feet. (2 Samuel 9:13)

The chapter's last word is about his feet. The table doesn't fix him. It seats him, on the strength of a covenant made for another's sake, which is the grammar of the gospel before the gospel had a name. And the story is honest enough to go on. When Absalom rebels, Ziba tells David that Mephibosheth stayed behind hoping for the throne, and David believes him, though Mephibosheth later explains that, being lame, he couldn't saddle a donkey to follow the king (2 Samuel 16:1–4; 19:26). Even at the king's table, the disabled man's word was the one doubted.

At a Pharisee's house, watching guests jockey for places of honor, Jesus throws that table's doors open:

> But when you give a feast, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind, (Luke 14:13)

They can't repay you, he says, and in a world where meals ran on reciprocity, that was the point. The repayment is left to the resurrection of the just (Luke 14:14). Then he tells of a banquet whose invited guests all send excuses, a field to see, oxen to try, a new wife, and of the master's response:

> So the servant came and reported these things to his master. Then the master of the house became angry and said to his servant, 'Go out quickly to the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor and crippled and blind and lame.' (Luke 14:21)

The list in verse 21 repeats the list in verse 13. What Jesus told the Pharisee to do, God does himself. The master's anger falls on the guests who had better things to do, and the people brought in from the streets aren't fed at the back door, the way charity is usually fed. They're seated, which was always the difference between a handout and a household. We tend to read the parable as the host, deciding whom to invite. Most of us are closer to the guests with the oxen, and if the gospel is true, every one of us came in from the streets and lanes with nothing to repay. A congregation that remembers this can't treat its disabled members as the objects of its hospitality. The chairs belong to the King.

## Cure, Healing, and the Wounds That Stayed

None of this makes the healing miracles an embarrassment. They stand at the center of the Gospels and announce what the prophets promised:

> Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; (Isaiah 35:5)

Jesus answered the imprisoned John the Baptist by pointing to the blind who were seeing and the lame who were walking (Luke 7:22), and any theology of disability that treats pain as simply one more way of being human has stopped listening to people in pain. But the miracles can be read in a way that does harm. If every disabled person is a miracle that hasn't happened yet, the man in the wheelchair is always an unfinished story. Too many disabled Christians have left prayer lines carrying the implication that the cure would have come if they'd believed harder, which is the prosperity gospel in its most intimate form.

The distinction that helps is between cure and healing. Cure removes an impairment. Healing restores a person to God and to God's people. In the Gospels the two usually arrive together, but Jesus attends to the second as carefully as the first. He touches the leper before the man is clean (Mark 1:41). He tells the paralyzed man his sins are forgiven before he tells him to walk (Mark 2:5). He finds the man from John 9 after the cure had cost him his synagogue. And the apostles didn't treat every affliction as a cure delayed. Paul prayed three times for his thorn to be taken, and it wasn't:

> But he said to me, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." (2 Corinthians 12:9)

A similar distinction runs through disability studies. The medical model locates disability in the body, as a deficit to treat or cure, and its strength is real: medicine is a gift, and pain is not an opinion. In 1976 a group of British disabled activists, the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation, distinguished impairment, the condition of a body, from disability, the exclusion society imposes on people with impairments, and the sociologist Michael Oliver set it out as the social model of disability in *Social Work with Disabled People* (1983). Its strength is real too. A wheelchair user at the bottom of a staircase is disabled less by his legs than by the staircase, which someone chose to build. Tom Shakespeare, a disabled scholar, argued in *Disability Rights and Wrongs* (2006) that the social model can't account for the pain and fatigue no ramp removes. Scripture holds both truths. Creation groans, and so do we:

> And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies. (Romans 8:23)

Some of what disables our members is an impairment we can only lament and bring to God. Much of it is our stairs.

The risen body of Christ settles more than we expect. Nancy Eiesland, a sociologist of religion born with a congenital bone defect, published *The Disabled God* in 1994. Her argument begins at Easter, where the risen Jesus identifies himself by his wounds:

> "See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me, and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have." (Luke 24:39)

A week later he says to the doubter:

> Then he said to Thomas, "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe." (John 20:27)

Eiesland's argument is that Christ's resurrected body is glorified and still marked, and that the marks belong to who he now is. When John is shown the throne of heaven, he sees "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6). The risen Lord didn't come back with the evidence removed.

That raises a question the creeds don't settle, and its order should be named first. The resurrection of the body is first-order; the confession binds it. What particular bodies will be like is third-order and genuinely open, and disabled Christians answer it differently. Joni Eareckson Tada, paralyzed in a diving accident in 1967, has written for decades of longing for a resurrection body free of paralysis, a hope that takes at its word the promise that "neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore" (Revelation 21:4), and no one has earned that hope more. Amos Yong, a Pentecostal theologian whose brother Mark has Down syndrome, asks in *Theology and Down Syndrome* (2007) whether a man whose whole way of knowing and loving was shaped by that condition would still be himself if it were simply deleted, and he argues that the resurrection will heal what wounds while keeping faith with the person God made. This essay leaves the question open, and readers may land on either side and remain brothers and sisters. What isn't open is this: the only resurrected body we have seen kept its wounds, and Scripture calls it glorious (Philippians 3:21).

## The History the Church Would Rather Not Read

The church's record isn't only failure. Around 369 Basil of Caesarea built a complex of care for the sick, the poor and lepers, an ancestor of the Christian hospital. Augustine, taking up in *The City of God* (16.8) whether people born with strikingly unusual bodies were fully human, answered that anyone born human descends from the one first man. In a world that exposed unwanted infants, those were not small convictions.

The darkest chapter was written in the language of progress. Francis Galton coined the word "eugenics" in 1883. Indiana passed the first compulsory sterilization law in 1907, and in 1927 the Supreme Court upheld Virginia's law in *Buck v. Bell* by eight votes to one, with Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. writing, "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." Paul Lombardo showed in *Three Generations, No Imbeciles* (2008) that the case against Carrie Buck rested on false claims and was argued by a defense lawyer quietly allied with the other side. Tens of thousands of Americans were sterilized under such laws. Christine Rosen's *Preaching Eugenics* (2004) shows that many respected pulpits were on the wrong side. Liberal Protestant ministers, along with some rabbis and priests, lent the movement their authority, and the American Eugenics Society ran sermon contests in the late 1920s. The clearest religious resistance came from the Catholic Church, whose encyclical *Casti Connubii* (1930) condemned state sterilization. Germany passed its own sterilization law in 1933, and from 1939 the regime's killing programs murdered disabled children and adults; the best known, later called Aktion T4, killed more than seventy thousand people by August 1941. That month Bishop Clemens von Galen preached against the killings in Münster, and the program was officially halted, though the murders went on by other means. The ideas that made them thinkable are traced in [What Makes Us Human in the Age of AI?](/writing/personhood-in-the-age-of-ai). None of us gets to read that history from a safe distance, and I don't either. The discomfort in our own sanctuaries grows from the same root, smaller and quieter.

It would be comfortable to call all this the sin of another century. When Congress passed the Americans with Disabilities Act in 1990, religious organizations were exempted from its public-accommodation requirements, and many of our churches have treated the exemption as permission rather than trust. We kept the stairs because no law made us remove them. The right and the left each carry a version of the old measure. One judges a life by what it produces. The other speaks of quality of life, and can slide from honoring a person's choices to ranking which lives are worth choosing. Both start from capacity, and the same people fall below both lines.

The hardest place this surfaces now is the obstetrician's office, and it needs more care than it usually gets. Since the early 2010s a blood test in the first trimester can screen for Down syndrome, and in some countries where screening is routine, far fewer children with Down syndrome are now born. Parents in that moment are rarely careless. They are frightened, often given a bleak picture in a hurried appointment, weighing their other children, their money and their own limits, and many who end a pregnancy grieve it for years. The sharpest critique of selective testing came not from a pulpit but from Adrienne Asch, a blind bioethicist who supported legal abortion. With Erik Parens, in "The Disability Rights Critique of Prenatal Genetic Testing" (1999), she set out what has come to be called the expressivist objection: that choosing against a pregnancy because of a single trait expresses a judgment about everyone who lives with that trait. (This is not medical advice. A family facing a diagnosis should hear from physicians and from families who live with the condition.) The church's question here is mostly about itself. A congregation has no standing to lecture a mother in a counselor's office unless it has already become a community that could help her carry what she fears. That case is made in [The Consistent Pro-Life Ethic](/writing/the-consistent-pro-life-ethic-womb-to-tomb), and the grief some parents carry afterward has [its own essay](/writing/grief-abortion-church).

## What L'Arche Taught, and What Its Founder Hid

A different witness grew up around shared kitchens. In 1964 Jean Vanier, a Canadian philosopher and former naval officer, bought a small house in the French village of Trosly-Breuil and invited two men with intellectual disabilities, Raphaël Simi and Philippe Seux, to leave their institution and live with him. He called the house L'Arche, the ark, and it grew into communities in dozens of countries where people with and without intellectual disabilities share homes, meals and prayer as one household.

Stanley Hauerwas became one of its most serious theological interpreters. In *Suffering Presence* (1986) he pressed on a nerve: a society determined to eliminate suffering can find itself eliminating those who suffer, and people with intellectual disabilities unsettle us because they expose the pretense that any of us is self-made. He and Vanier later wrote *Living Gently in a Violent World* (2008), arguing that communities formed around the weak are a sign to a world that worships strength. Henri Nouwen, who left Harvard and joined L'Arche Daybreak near Toronto in 1986, wrote in *Adam: God's Beloved* (1997) about Adam Arnett, a man who could not speak or dress himself, whom Nouwen came to call his friend and his teacher.

Then, in February 2020, L'Arche International published the findings of an independent inquiry it had commissioned. Vanier, who had died the year before, had engaged in manipulative sexual relationships with at least six adult women, none of them people with disabilities, between 1970 and 2005, typically in the context of spiritual accompaniment. The inquiry also found that he had known more than he had publicly admitted about the abuses of his own spiritual mentor, the priest Thomas Philippe. Those findings have to stand at their full weight, without hurrying to rescue the founder or the books that carry his name. The women he harmed come first, before the reputation of any movement. And the church should notice how the harm happened. A man renowned for teaching about weakness used the authority that teaching gave him against people who trusted him, and for decades the rest of us were glad to honor him as exactly the kind of saint we wanted.

Two more things can be said honestly. L'Arche commissioned the inquiry and published what it found, which is more than many Christian institutions have done when a founder fell, and the disabled members who made those homes what they were deserve no share of the shadow. And the able world should hear a warning about how it talks about disabled people even when it means to honor them. If Adam is mainly a lesson for Henri, he has become a means to someone else's growth, which is a subtler form of the old measure of usefulness. People with disabilities aren't angels sent to improve the rest of us. They are sinners and saints like everyone else, owed the ordinary dignity of being known as themselves.

John Swinton, a mental health and learning disability nurse before he became a theologian, argues in *Becoming Friends of Time* (2016) that modern life runs on a clock that prizes speed and productivity, and that people with profound intellectual disabilities or dementia live at a different pace, which churches often feel as an interruption. He calls for a gentle discipleship that keeps time with its slowest members. In an earlier article, "From Inclusion to Belonging" (2012), Swinton draws the distinction this essay has been approaching. Inclusion is access: the ramp, the large-print bulletin, the quiet room. To belong, he argues, you have to be missed. A congregation can include a man for years without noticing the Sunday he doesn't come.

## Three Households and One Table

Consider first the parent of a disabled child, often the most tired person in the building and the least likely to say so. She has usually been handed more theology than help: the assurance that God gives special children to special parents, which makes her exhaustion feel like ingratitude; the question, in the spirit of John 9, of whether there was sin in the family; a prayer for a cure spoken over her son as though he weren't in the room. Scripture gives her better company. It lets her grieve what she expected and didn't receive without that grief betraying the child she loves, because lament is the Bible's own language. And it shows her what she is owed. In Mark 2 four men dig through a roof to lower their paralyzed friend to Jesus.

> And when Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, "Son, your sins are forgiven." (Mark 2:5)

It was the faith of the ones carrying the mat. A congregation that wants to carry like that begins by learning a child's name and what calms him, by sitting with him so his parents can worship side by side or go to dinner, and by missing the family when they're gone. It keeps a way to help in plain view, too. Caregivers carry a strain that can turn into despair, and anyone who reaches that place can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour.

Consider next the disabled adult, who is weary of being a project. He wants to be a member of the church's body, with gifts to use and duties to keep, and Paul is on his side:

> On the contrary, the parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable, (1 Corinthians 12:22)

The word that matters is "seem." Paul isn't rescuing the weak by proving them useful after all. He is saying the body's judgment about which members matter is unreliable. A disabled man may be the one who should read Scripture aloud, teach a class or serve as a deacon, and where his body makes a task hard, Exodus 4 applies. God gave Moses a companion, not a release. Access, on this reading, is table-setting in a house that belongs to someone else.

Consider last the congregation, where the hardest work lies, because congregations rarely think they have a problem. We have a ramp. We say everyone is welcome. But inclusion can be complete on paper while belonging is absent in fact, and Swinton's test is simple enough to be frightening. If the family with the loud son stopped coming, how many weeks would pass before anyone called? If the woman in the power chair moved away, would anyone know what she was good at? Those questions implicate the pulpit before the pews, and they implicate me. A pastor can preach the image of God on Sunday and still plan the building, the calendar and the pace of the church's life around the people who move fastest.

Mephibosheth ate at the king's table always, and he was lame in both his feet. The chapter never says whether the other guests were comfortable. It says only where he sat and why: for the sake of another, by a promise made before he could earn it. That is where the rest of us sit too. Every Christian came to God's table from some Lo-debar, carried in, half convinced he was a dead dog, and seated for the sake of the Son. Our feet show under that table as well, whether or not anyone can see them.

On the evening of the first Easter, two disciples walking to Emmaus didn't recognize the stranger who had joined them until he sat at their table and broke the bread (Luke 24:30–31). Those were the hands he would show his friends in Jerusalem that same night, still marked. Whatever the resurrection will do to our bodies, it didn't hide his wounds, and the church that eats at his table is fed from wounded hands. Whether disabled people belong at that table was settled when the Host kept his scars. What remains unsettled is whether they would be missed if they stopped coming to ours. No building committee can answer that. Only a people can, one Sunday at a time, by noticing who isn't there.
