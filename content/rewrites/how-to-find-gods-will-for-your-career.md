It's a question I've heard in some form more times than I can count. A young man has two job offers and has prayed about both. What frightens him isn't the ordinary risk of a poor fit or a thin paycheck. It's the thought that God has already picked one of the two, that one option is right and every other one a mistake, and that his future hangs on reading a signal God hasn't yet chosen to send. The question under his question isn't how to be faithful but how to break a code.

That fear isn't foreign to me. I came to faith from atheism, and conversion doesn't automatically retire the habits of mind a person brings with him. As an atheist I'd assumed there was no one on the other end of the line. The new believer in me could swing to the opposite anxiety, where every coincidence might be a message and every decision an exam I could fail. Each is a kind of fear, and each leaves the whole burden on me.

Beneath the question sits an assumption most of us never examine: that God's will for my life is mainly concealed information, a map drawn in heaven and then hidden. For most of its history the church spoke of God's will in two senses. The anxious search for a career blueprint depends on a third sense that arrived much later. Once you see how that third sense is built, you can see why it breeds dread, and why the older distinctions and the apostles' own practice set a person free to choose.

## The Two Wills the Church Has Always Named

The oldest text on the question comes at the end of Moses's covenant speech on the plains of Moab. "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law" (Deuteronomy 29:29). The verse draws a line through the whole subject. What God hasn't disclosed belongs to him. What he has disclosed belongs to us, given so that we would do it. Israel isn't invited to pry into the first. It's handed the second and told to get to work.

Theologians later named the two sides of that line. Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* (begun around 1265), distinguished God's will of good pleasure (*voluntas beneplaciti*), his actual purpose for what comes to pass, from the will of sign (*voluntas signi*), the ways he makes his will known, such as commands and prohibitions. John Calvin, in the 1559 *Institutes*, insisted that God's will is one and simple in himself, though it appears manifold to us because we can't see how his hidden counsel and his revealed commands fit together. Later Reformed theology settled on the terms most readers meet today: the decretive will (the sovereign will, or will of decree) and the preceptive will (the moral will, or will of command).

The clearest place to see both at once is the end of Joseph's story. His brothers sold him into slavery, defying every command against hatred, kidnapping and deceit. Years later, standing over them in Egypt, Joseph could say: "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today" (Genesis 50:20). Their sin remained sin, and God's purpose went forward through it. The decree governed the whole story without excusing a single act inside it.

Consider what follows. The decretive will can't be missed, because it's simply whatever God brings to pass, and none of it waits on your ability to guess it. You'll learn it the way Joseph did, looking back. The preceptive will can be disobeyed, but it can't be missed through ignorance, because it has been published. Paul names it outright: "For this is the will of God, your sanctification" (1 Thessalonians 4:3). The prophet had already compressed it: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8). The moral will fills the Bible, and what it asks of you is a character before it is ever a position. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), speaking of the circumstances of worship and church government that are "common to human actions and societies," said they are to be ordered "by the light of nature, and Christian prudence, according to the general rules of the Word." The article's subject is the church's own life, but its logic reaches a career as well. Where God has spoken, obey; where he hasn't, judge well.

Set beside those two categories the one the young man is afraid of. Garry Friesen named it in 1980: the individual will, the belief that God has an ideal, detailed plan for each believer, one right career, spouse and city, which the believer must discover to stay in the center of God's will. Like the decree, it's hidden. Like the precept, it can be missed. It joins the secrecy of the one to the vulnerability of the other, and that combination is what makes it unbearable. You're asked to obey a command no one has told you and to fear getting wrong a thing only God knows. No wonder the young man can't sleep.

## Where the Blueprint Came From

Friesen called the view he was answering "the traditional view," and in the evangelical churches of his day it was. In the longer history of the church it's young. The Puritans cared intensely about providence, and John Flavel's *The Mystery of Providence* (1678) taught believers to trace God's hand in the particulars of their lives. But Flavel read providence backward, gathering mercies already given as grounds for gratitude and trust, far more than forward as instructions. Duty came from Scripture; providences were chiefly remembered, not decoded. The blueprint view reverses that direction, and the reversal has a history you can date.

Part of it is secular. In 1908 Frank Parsons, a Boston reformer, opened a Vocation Bureau for young people drifting into whatever work came to hand. He died that year, and *Choosing a Vocation* was published the next: understand yourself, understand the requirements of different lines of work, and reason carefully about how the two fit. His motive was generous: he wanted working-class young people to choose with open eyes instead of falling into a job at random. But the picture he left behind assumes that every person fits one occupation best, that the fit must be discovered, and that a poor fit is the chief danger. Christians absorbed that picture along with everyone else.

The other part grew inside the church. The holiness piety of the Keswick Convention (from 1875) called believers to a fully surrendered life, which raised the question of where exactly God wanted it spent, and the missionary movement made "Am I called to the field?" a rite of passage for serious young evangelicals. Then in 1965 Bill Bright's booklet *Have You Heard of the Four Spiritual Laws?* put a sentence into millions of conversations: "God loves you and offers a wonderful plan for your life." Bright meant God's saving purpose in Christ. A generation already trained to think of life as a match to be found heard a detailed plan with their name on it, and the next question was how to find it and what happens if you don't.

By 1980, when Friesen published *Decision Making and the Will of God*, the answer had hardened into a method: pray, wait for peace, watch for open and closed doors, put out a fleece, and don't move until the signs line up. Friesen's charge was that the method rested on a promise Scripture never makes. So the young man's fear isn't original to him. Two teachers who never met, a vocational science and a revival piety, both told him a single correct answer exists and the worst thing he could do is miss it.

The Reformers would have caught the irony. When Luther argued in 1520 that the farmer and the shoemaker stood before God on the same ground as the priest, he was tearing down a two-story Christianity in which only a spiritual elite had a real calling (the site's essay on [what vocation means](/writing/what-is-vocation-work-as-calling) tells that story). The blueprint view quietly rebuilds the second story. It makes the means God has actually given (his Word, a renewed mind, the counsel of his people, the judgment he has been forming in you all along) look like second-best substitutes for a private word reserved for the truly spiritual.

## The Case for the Blueprint, and the Case Against It

The view Friesen attacked deserves to be stated as its best defenders would state it, and having held it myself, I know its strength. God is sovereign over the smallest details of a life: "But even the hairs of your head are all numbered" (Matthew 10:30). A God that attentive surely has a purpose in where a person spends forty years of work, and Scripture shows him sending particular people to particular places. An angel sent Philip to a desert road toward Gaza (Acts 8:26). The Psalms promise more than general rules: "I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go; I will counsel you with my eye upon you" (Psalm 32:8). Surely such a God makes his path known, and the faithful response is to seek it until he does.

Henry Blackaby and Claude King gave that instinct its most influential modern form in *Experiencing God* (1990), and their case was relational: God is at work around you, speaking by the Holy Spirit through the Bible, prayer, circumstances and the church, and your task is to see where he is working and join him. Dallas Willard, in *In Search of Guidance* (1984, later revised as *Hearing God*), pressed from another angle: guidance belongs inside a conversational relationship with God. That's the strongest objection to what follows. A theology of decision that never expects God to say anything in particular can drift into practical deism, and many Christians in charismatic and contemplative traditions would testify that God has not gone quiet.

Friesen's answer, which he called the way of wisdom, is just as serious. He affirmed the sovereign and moral wills and denied only that Scripture teaches the individual blueprint. The specific guidance in Acts, he argued, came as unmistakable revelation, nothing like an inner impression, and the narratives describe what God did without promising to repeat it in every believer's decisions. His positive case runs in four steps. Where God commands, we must obey. Where there is no command, God gives freedom, and responsibility, to choose. Where there is no command, God gives wisdom to choose well. And when we have chosen what is moral and wise, we trust the sovereign God to work the details together for good.

Some of his readings hold up well. Psalm 32 is David's psalm of confession, and the next verse says what kind of instruction God means: "Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding, which must be curbed with bit and bridle, or it will not stay near you" (Psalm 32:9). The contrast is between a creature steered by a bit and a child given understanding.

Bruce Waltke sharpened the case in *Finding the Will of God: A Pagan Notion?* (1995). He noted that Israel had sanctioned means for a season, the Urim and Thummim and the lot, and the church cast lots once, to replace Judas (Acts 1:26). Then the Spirit was poured out at Pentecost, and the New Testament never records a lot again. Waltke's argument is that the Spirit took the lot's place by being given to the whole people of God, so that decisions would come from Scripture, a heart set on God, counsel, attention to providence and sound judgment. Kevin DeYoung pressed it on a younger audience in *Just Do Something* (2009), arguing that much of our anxious searching is really passivity, a fear of commitment dressed as spiritual caution.

Here the question has to be sorted by weight. The historic creeds bind the church to God's providence, not to a theory of guidance, and faithful Christians in the same pew hold both views. This essay lands here. God does guide specifically when he chooses, and Scripture shows him doing it. But he nowhere promises to do it in every decision or gives a technique for making him do it, and the blueprint view treats the rare case as the normal one. The strongest objection this conclusion hasn't fully answered is the one Acts keeps raising: the Spirit's interventions there come in the middle of ordinary travel plans, and no one can say in advance when he'll do it again. A believer who has known a leading like that is right to find the wisdom view a little too tidy.

## How the Apostles Actually Decided

The best evidence for how guidance works is the early church making a hard decision. Around the year 49 the apostles and elders met in Jerusalem over a question that could have split the church: must Gentile believers be circumcised and keep the law of Moses? Luke describes no vision and no lot. "And after there had been much debate, Peter stood up and said to them" (Acts 15:7). Peter told what God had done for Cornelius's household, Paul and Barnabas reported signs among the Gentiles, and James set it all beside the prophet Amos and proposed a judgment. Then the church wrote a letter: "For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to lay on you no greater burden than these requirements" (Acts 15:28).

"It has seemed good" is the language Greek cities used for their public decrees, the resolution a council reached after deliberation. The church put the Holy Spirit and itself in the same sentence without embarrassment, claiming that the Spirit had been at work inside the testimony, the argument, the Scripture read together and the agreement reached. Proverbs had always said wisdom looks like this: "Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed" (Proverbs 15:22).

The next chapter is the one the blueprint view reaches for. "And they went through the region of Phrygia and Galatia, having been forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia" (Acts 16:6). "And when they had come up to Mysia, they attempted to go into Bithynia, but the Spirit of Jesus did not allow them" (Acts 16:7). They went down to Troas, and in the night Paul saw a man of Macedonia urging him to come over and help them. "And when Paul had seen the vision, immediately we sought to go on into Macedonia, concluding that God had called us to preach the gospel to them" (Acts 16:10). Paul's company was already obeying a plain command, planning routes and trying doors, and the guidance came as a redirection of people in motion. Luke never says how the Spirit closed the way, so there's no technique to copy. And even after the vision they went on "concluding," a verb that means putting things together and drawing an inference. The vision gave their minds more to work with; it didn't replace them.

Romans 12 explains the mechanism. It follows eleven chapters on the mercy of God and opens by calling believers, in view of those mercies, to offer their bodies as a living sacrifice. Then: "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect" (Romans 12:2). God's will here is something a renewed mind learns to recognize, and Paul describes it by its moral quality, not its coordinates. The next verse turns that mind to the question of gifts, telling each believer to think of himself "with sober judgment" (Romans 12:3), and then comes one body with many members and different gifts. Discerning God's will happens inside a body, by a mind being remade, through a sober estimate of what you've actually been given.

James speaks directly to people making career plans. "Come now, you who say, 'Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a town and spend a year there and trade and make a profit'" (James 4:13). "yet you do not know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes" (James 4:14). "Instead you ought to say, 'If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that'" (James 4:15). He doesn't tell the merchants to wait for a sign. He tells them to stop planning as though tomorrow belonged to them. "If the Lord wills" confesses the decretive will, the purpose no one can read in advance. It isn't a code to crack before you move. It's the posture in which you move, holding your plans with an open hand.

## What Ignatius Knew About Desire

The wisdom view has a blind spot of its own. At its thinnest it treats a decision as a column of pros and cons and treats desire as noise. The Catholic tradition thought harder about desire. In 1521 a Basque soldier, Iñigo de Loyola, had his leg shattered by a cannonball at the siege of Pamplona. Convalescing at home with no romances of chivalry to hand, he read a life of Christ and a book of saints' lives. He noticed that daydreams of courtly glory delighted him while they lasted and left him dry afterward, while imagining himself following the saints left him glad, and the gladness stayed. That attention to the difference became the seed of the *Spiritual Exercises*, which received papal approval in 1548.

The Exercises open with the Principle and Foundation. Human beings are created to praise, reverence and serve God, and everything else is given to help them toward that end. So we should make ourselves indifferent to created things, not wanting health more than sickness, riches more than poverty, honor more than dishonor or a long life more than a short one. Indifference in Ignatius's sense is freedom from disordered attachment, the state in which you could actually hear an answer you didn't want. We want God's will provided it matches a preference we've already formed, and the fleece becomes a way of getting him to countersign it.

His rules for the discernment of spirits name movements every Christian has felt. Consolation is any increase of faith, hope and love, any inward joy that draws the soul toward God and leaves it at peace. Desolation is darkness and turmoil, a pull toward low things, restlessness, a sense of being cut off from God. Then comes a supremely practical rule: in a time of desolation, never make a change, but stay with the resolutions you made before the darkness came. Anyone who has drafted a resignation letter at midnight after the worst week of the year knows why. He also warned that the enemy can come as an angel of light, so even a sweet movement must be judged by where it leads.

When he turns to making a choice, which he calls an election, Ignatius insists it must concern things that are good or permissible; you don't discern whether to sin. He allows that God sometimes moves the will beyond doubt, as with Paul or Matthew, and that light can come through consolation and desolation. But for the tranquil time, when the soul uses its natural powers freely, he gives a patient method: weigh the advantages and disadvantages, then test them by imagination. What would I advise a stranger in my place whom I wished well? What will I wish I had chosen when I come to die? How would I want to have chosen when I stand before God? Ignatius had visions, and he still wrote careful instructions for choosing without one.

A Protestant reader will add Jeremiah's warning: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). Ignatius would agree, which is why his rules exist. Your desires are evidence to be tested, and the God who made you and is remaking you may be at work in what you find yourself wanting. Frederick Buechner compressed the conviction in *Wishful Thinking* (1973), in his entry on vocation: "The place God calls you to is the place where your deep gladness and the world's deep hunger meet." Buechner added a caution: work you love that meets no real need misses the mark, and so does work that meets a need while leaving you bored and miserable, since you probably aren't serving anyone well in it. Many people have fewer options than Buechner's sentence assumes, and for them the world's hunger is usually standing right in front of them.

## The Paralysis of Too Many Doors

The blueprint theology meets most of us amid nearly unlimited options, and the two together produce a particular paralysis. Barry Schwartz described its secular form in *The Paradox of Choice* (2004). He distinguished maximizers, who set out to find the best possible option, from satisficers, who look for one good enough by standards they've set and then stop. In a 2006 study of graduating college seniors, Sheena Iyengar, Rachael Wells and Schwartz found that the maximizers landed jobs with higher starting salaries yet were less satisfied with them, and more pessimistic, anxious and overwhelmed throughout the search.

Honesty requires a qualification. The broader claim that more options by themselves harm choosing is shakier; a 2010 meta-analysis by Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd found the average effect of choice overload across dozens of experiments close to zero, with wide variation between settings. The number of doors is only part of the problem. What matters more is what a person believes is riding on the choice, and the blueprint view makes every believer a maximizer by doctrine. The goal becomes the one best choice, the one God secretly made already, and anything less is a life in his second best.

Søren Kierkegaard, in *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), described anxiety as the dizziness of freedom, the vertigo of looking down into one's own possibilities. The blueprint view promises to cure it with a hidden answer and makes it worse, because now the answer exists and you might miss it. The Preacher knew where that leads: "He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap" (Ecclesiastes 11:4).

Beneath the paralysis there's usually something less respectable than humility. Part of it is a fear of limits. Accepting one offer means declining the other, just as a wedding vow renounces every other suitor. We want a choice with no cost, and we give that wish a devout name when it's really a request for insurance against regret. Part of it is the wish to be exceptional, and the hunt for a custom plan can be pride that has learned to pray. I have done this. I have wanted God to confirm I was special before I'd consent to be faithful. That's the sin hiding inside the search, and seeing it is already a mercy, because a sin can be confessed and forgiven, while a fear dressed as piety can only be managed.

Here the cross reaches the career question, because the self that insists on keeping every door open has to die. None of this is career or financial advice; weigh the practical questions with people who know your finances and obligations. If the worry has moved past indecision into anxiety that keeps you from sleeping, eating or functioning, it deserves a doctor's or counselor's care, not only a pastor's. If it has turned to despair, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

## Choosing Under the Cross

There's one scene in the Gospels where someone agonizes over the will of God, and it looks nothing like our agonizing. In Gethsemane Jesus wasn't unsure what his Father wanted. The anguish was in consenting to it, and Luke records the prayer that settled it: "Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). Usually our difficulty runs the same way. The will God has revealed is costly, and a hidden one we could hunt for indefinitely is easier to live with than a plain one we'd have to obey. It's possible to spend a year praying over which job to take while ignoring what he has said about the people at the job you have.

The cross is also where the two wills of God meet most sharply. At Pentecost Peter put both halves to the crowd in Jerusalem: "this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). The definite plan is the decree. The lawless men broke every command that mattered. The worst act in history couldn't derail God's purpose; he made it the means of the world's redemption, and on the third day raised the one they had killed. If that's the God under whose providence you choose, you can't wreck his plan by taking the wrong one of two honest jobs. "The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps" (Proverbs 16:9). That doesn't make your decisions trivial. It makes them bearable.

Within the will he has revealed, you're freer than the fear ever let you believe. When both offers leave room to become the person he has named, to serve your neighbor, work honestly and provide for those who depend on you, neither is the wrong door. Accept either after prayer and counsel, and trust him with the road you didn't walk. If he was at work in the choice in ways you couldn't see, you'll find out the way Joseph did, looking back.

The young man with two offers wants someone to tell him which one is right. A pastor can't, and shouldn't, because the thing that most needs settling isn't the job. Whichever door he walks through will close behind him, and years from now he'll likely see that the job was never the main thing God was doing. God was making a man who could be trusted with the people at whatever desk he ended up behind.

That part of God's will doesn't need to be found. It will be waiting on Monday morning, in the colleague who is hard to love, in the shortcut no one would notice, in the paycheck that has to stretch further than it should. It will be as plain there as it was in the garden, and it will cost you something. The secret things belong to the Lord. The revealed things are already yours, and they will ask more of you than any job ever could.
