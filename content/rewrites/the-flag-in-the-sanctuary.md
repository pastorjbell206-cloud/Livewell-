Walk into a great many American churches and you'll find it without looking: a flag on a staff near the front, sometimes on the platform itself, sometimes paired with a second flag across the pulpit. In most congregations nobody now living remembers voting to put it there. It has always been there, so it has become nearly invisible. The loyalties that shape us most are rarely the ones we chose. They are the ones we stopped seeing.

People do ask about it. A visitor asks why a church that confesses a kingdom not of this world keeps a national banner beside the pulpit; a longtime member asks what kind of Christians would move theirs to the foyer. One reflex hauls the flag out with a little contempt for the people who love it; the other treats every question about it as an insult to the dead. Neither asks how the flag got there, or what it has been teaching while nobody was watching.

The claim of this essay is that the flag in the sanctuary is the visible seam where two faiths meet: the Christian faith, and the American civil religion that borrows so much of its vocabulary that most of us have never noticed we attend both. That seam has a datable history and a cost the people who love both God and country are rarely asked to count. The line that matters runs between love and worship, and the Apostle Peter drew it in two short commands to a church of exiles: "Fear God. Honor the emperor" (1 Peter 2:17, ESV). I pastor an American church, and I write from inside that line, not above it.

### What the veteran and the family are right about

Begin with the man at the funeral, because he is the one this argument must not wound for nothing. An old soldier stands as his friend's casket passes, the honor guard folds the flag into its tight triangle and places it in a widow's hands, and his eyes fill. He is not committing idolatry. He is honoring a real sacrifice made for a country that has, for all its sins, protected the freedom to worship more steadily than most powers in history. The family with a folded flag on the mantel knows exactly who died for their sins and who died for their country, and they are right to be grateful. Every pastor who has buried a veteran knows that room, and knows that nothing in it is cheap.

Scripture does not ask that man to stop loving his country. When Jeremiah wrote to the Judeans carried off to Babylon in the deportation of 597 BC, he told them to build houses, plant gardens, and raise children there, and then this: "But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV). If God commanded exiles to seek the good of the capital that had marched them away, a Christian may certainly love the country that raised him. The tightness in the throat at a military funeral is love, aimed at a real good.

C. S. Lewis gave that love its most careful Christian account in *The Four Loves* (1960). He began his treatment of patriotism with plain love of home: this town and not another, these fields, this food, these ways of speaking. In its simple form, Lewis thought, that love is not aggressive; it mostly asks to be let alone, and it helps a man understand why another man loves his own place. Lewis then traced what can attach itself to that love, a sentimental reading of the nation's past and a settled belief in the nation's superiority, and he warned that once a people takes its cause to be simply God's cause, the love has turned demonic. The principle behind the whole book he borrowed from Denis de Rougemont: a natural love begins to be a demon the moment it begins to be a god.

G. K. Chesterton put the difference in a sentence few have improved. In *The Defendant* (1901) he wrote: "'My country, right or wrong,' is a thing that no patriot would think of saying except in a desperate case. It is like saying, 'My mother, drunk or sober.'" The son who loves his mother does not pretend she is sober; he sees her clearly, stays, and tells her the truth. That is the first distinction this essay rests on. Love can be corrected. Worship cannot. You can love your country and still tell it that it is wrong, and the love survives the correction and often deepens through it. The thing you worship cannot be corrected, because correction assumes a standard above it, and worship has put it at the top where no standard reaches.

The defender of the flag has one more point, his strongest. The warnings about nationalism, he says, always cut one way, and much of the suspicion of patriotism is really contempt for ordinary people and their ordinary loyalties. The contempt is real. Some churches have taken their flags down as a sneer rather than a confession, and a bare platform can serve pride as easily as a draped one can serve idolatry. Rootlessness is no virtue. The Bible's last pages do not dissolve the nations into a gray, placeless humanity; John sees the new Jerusalem receive them: "They will bring into it the glory and the honor of the nations" (Revelation 21:26, ESV). At the end the nations are judged, purified, and brought in as tribute rather than erased. The real question is where that love is placed, and what it is allowed to stand beside.

### How the flag got into the sanctuary

The fusion of cross and crown is older than America. In 312 Constantine is said to have ordered the Christian emblem painted on his soldiers' shields before the battle at the Milvian Bridge; in 313 the Edict of Milan ended the persecution of Christians; in 380 Theodosius made Nicene Christianity the religion of the empire. The church gained real goods, but it also acquired the empire's reflexes: its love of victory, its certainty that God rode with its armies, its habit of reading national success as divine approval. Every flag beside an American pulpit descends, whether anyone intends it or not, from those painted shields.

The American chapter is recent, and many of its dates arrived with a war. "In God We Trust" first appeared on a United States coin, the two-cent piece, in 1864, in the middle of the Civil War. The Pledge of Allegiance was written in 1892 by Francis Bellamy, a Baptist minister and a Christian socialist, for schoolchildren marking the four hundredth anniversary of Columbus's voyage, and it made no reference to God at all.

The church answered the nation's symbols with one of its own. In 1897, at a Sunday school rally in Brooklyn, a superintendent named Charles Overton proposed a flag for Christians, white with a blue canton and a red cross. Within a few years it had a pledge, written in deliberate imitation of the national one, in which the speaker pledged allegiance to the Christian flag and to the Savior whose kingdom it represents. The intention was devout. Yet notice the grammar. Even the church's counter-symbol took the nation's form, a banner and a spoken oath, and in many sanctuaries the two flags ended up side by side as a matched pair, the one arrangement that most confused what the Christian flag was meant to say.

The national flag itself came into many sanctuaries during the world wars, and it came in on grief. When the United States entered the First World War in 1917, Woodrow Wilson asked for war so that the world might be "made safe for democracy," and American pulpits, liberal and conservative alike, preached the cause as a crusade, as Ray Abrams documented in *Preachers Present Arms* (1933). Congregations hung service flags with a star for each member in uniform, and the national colors came in with them. In 1942, during the Second World War, Congress adopted the Flag Code. In its current form (4 U.S.C. § 7) it instructs that a flag displayed from a staff in a church should hold "the position of superior prominence," at the speaker's right. The same code makes one exception to the rule that no flag may fly above the national one: the church pennant, during services conducted by Navy chaplains at sea. A federal statute has thought harder about which banner goes highest in worship than most congregations have.

Then the words went in. On February 7, 1954, with President Eisenhower in the pew, the Presbyterian pastor George Docherty preached in Washington that the Pledge should acknowledge God, taking the phrase from Lincoln, who had spoken at Gettysburg of "this nation, under God." Eisenhower signed the change into law on Flag Day, June 14, 1954. In 1956 "In God We Trust" became the national motto, and it reached paper currency the next year. These were acts of Cold War statecraft, a nation marking itself off from atheist communism, by men who mostly believed in God. The historian Kevin Kruse argues in *One Nation Under God* (2015) that the push began earlier, in a 1930s and 1940s campaign by business leaders and allied ministers to present free enterprise and Christian faith as one American inheritance against the New Deal. Either way, the phrases many Christians assume date from the founding are younger than many of the people who recite them.

The answer, then, isn't sinister. Nobody decided it theologically. It came in during wars, carried by grief and gratitude and fear, and it stayed because moving it would have felt like betraying the dead. The history does not make the flag an idol. It shows that the church never asked the question, while the nation, in crisis after crisis, reached for God as a boundary marker, a way of saying which side we are on.

### Two faiths that use the same words

The fusion got its classic description from a sociologist. In 1967 Robert Bellah published "Civil Religion in America" in the journal *Daedalus*. He opened with John F. Kennedy's inaugural address of 1961, which invoked God and never once named Christ, and argued that alongside the churches there exists a well-institutionalized religion of the nation itself, with sacred texts in the founding documents, holy days in the Fourth of July and Memorial Day, and martyrs in the war dead. Its God, Bellah observed, is austere and generic, concerned far more with order, law, and right than with salvation and love. Bellah was clear that this religion is something other than Christianity: it borrows Christianity's words and answers to something else.

Bellah was not writing to attack it, and fairness requires saying why. At its best, he argued, civil religion held the nation under a standard higher than itself, and his great example was Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address of March 1865. With the war nearly won, Lincoln refused to tell the North that God was on its side. "Both read the same Bible and pray to the same God," he said of the two armies, "and each invokes His aid against the other." And then: "The Almighty has His own purposes." Lincoln used the language of civil religion to put his own nation under judgment, suggesting that the war's terrible cost might be God's verdict on the whole country's complicity in slavery. That prophetic possibility is real. Philip Gorski defends it in *American Covenant* (2017), distinguishing a prophetic civil religion, which measures the nation against a covenant and finds it wanting, from religious nationalism, which simply declares the nation God's own.

But Lincoln is rare, and Bellah himself lost hope. In 1975, after Vietnam and in the shadow of Watergate, he published *The Broken Covenant* and judged the American civil religion an empty and broken shell. What remained was the use Bellah had warned of in 1967: God invoked to bless what the nation had already decided to do. The God of the 1954 Pledge had little in common with the God of Amos, who told Israel, "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21, ESV). He was the God of the consensus, a chaplain to the republic, and a chaplain to power is the one role the prophets of Scripture refused.

This is why civil religion is so hard for Christians to see. It is not the opposite of our faith. It is close enough to be mistaken for it, using God's name and asking us to stand and place our hands over our hearts and feel something that rhymes with reverence. A child who notices that we stand, fall silent, and bare our heads before the flag much as we do before God has noticed something most adults stopped hearing.

### What the sociologists found, and what their critics say

Since Bellah, the fusion has been measured and given a harder name. In *Taking America Back for God* (2020) Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry define Christian nationalism as a cultural framework of myths, symbols, narratives, and values that idealizes and advocates a fusion of Christianity with American civic life, sorting Americans from those they call Ambassadors to those they call Rejecters. Their most important finding for the church: Christian nationalism is a different thing from Christian devotion. On a number of measures, once the nationalist framework is accounted for, Americans who pray, read Scripture, and attend worship often lean the other way on questions about outsiders. The fusion can grow alongside serious faith and pull against it. Philip Gorski and Samuel Perry pressed further in *The Flag and the Cross* (2022), arguing that the American version has long been bound up with race, a story about who counts as a real American.

The critics deserve a hearing in their strongest form. The political scientist Mark David Hall, in *Who's Afraid of Christian Nationalism?* (2024), argues that the survey measure sweeps ordinary religious conservatives into a sinister category. The scale asks, among other things, whether government should allow prayer in public schools and the display of religious symbols in public spaces, and a great many Christians who would never confuse their country with the kingdom of God answer yes to both. When a label built from such questions is attached to a mob, a grandmother who wants a nativity scene on the courthouse lawn has been placed in its company by a statistical maneuver. That is a real injustice, and many devout people now hear every warning about Christian nationalism as a political attack dressed in scholarship. Sometimes it is exactly that.

Yet the critics of the measurement have not made the thing measured disappear. Some Christians now claim the name; Stephen Wolfe's *The Case for Christian Nationalism* (2022) argued openly for it. And one of the sharpest critiques of the fusion came from the right. Paul D. Miller, an evangelical and a conservative, argued in *The Religion of American Greatness* (2022) that Christian nationalism is bad for the church and bad for the republic. The sensible conclusion is that a survey can blur the line between a devout citizen and an idolater, that the line is real all the same, and that no survey can draw it for you. It runs through the heart, and it is drawn by the answer to a single question: when your country is wrong, what do you do?

The same instrument must cut the other way, or it isn't the church's instrument at all. The fusion of cross and flag was not born on the political right. In 1917 many of the most ardent preachers of Wilson's war stood in the progressive pulpits of the mainline, confident that the kingdom of God was arriving through democratic progress, and Abrams's record spares them no more than anyone else. A congregation that takes down the national flag and hangs in its place, in its preaching if not on its walls, the banner of a political cause has not escaped civil religion; it has changed denominations within it. And there is a quieter inversion to be named with the same grief. The Christian who cannot speak of his country except with contempt, for whom America must be the worst nation on earth, has kept the nation at the center of his moral universe, only as the devil instead of the god.

Honesty also forbids a false balance. The flag beside the pulpit is most at home in churches like mine, conservative Protestant congregations, many of them Baptist, and Whitehead and Perry's data place the strongest embrace of the fusion among white evangelicals. The left has real idols. But this object in this room is chiefly our temptation, and I'd be a coward to pretend otherwise. I've watched it in myself. There have been seasons when I would have been more upset to hear my nation criticized than to hear my Lord ignored, when I grew hot at an insult to the flag and stayed cool at an insult to the cross. The thing you defend most fiercely is the thing you actually worship, whatever your mouth confesses on Sunday.

### Fear God, honor the emperor

Scripture begins with a people who were a nation before they had a country. Peter wrote to "elect exiles of the dispersion" (1 Peter 1:1, ESV), small congregations scattered across Asia Minor with no land, no army, and no standing, and then gave them Israel's title from Sinai: "But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9, ESV). Those are the words of Exodus 19:6, applied to a church drawn from many peoples. The church is a nation, the only one Scripture calls holy, and its citizens are scattered through every other.

Then Peter turns to the empire. "Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution, whether it be to the emperor as supreme, or to governors as sent by him to punish those who do evil and to praise those who do good" (1 Peter 2:13–14, ESV). Then he gathers it up: "Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor" (1 Peter 2:17, ESV). The verbs are doing the theology. Honor is given twice, once to everyone and once to the emperor, so the emperor receives the honor owed to any human being, no less and no more. Fear, the reverence that belongs to what is ultimate, is given once, and only to God. Peter wrote from the city he calls Babylon (1 Peter 5:13), where early tradition holds he died under Nero, and still he told the church to honor its emperor: honor without fear, loyalty without worship, even submission rendered "for the Lord's sake."

Paul makes the same move to a city that knew exactly what citizenship meant. Philippi was a Roman colony, as Luke notes (Acts 16:12), settled by veterans of Rome's civil wars. Paul used his own Roman citizenship there, protesting after a beating that he and Silas were uncondemned Roman citizens (Acts 16:37). Yet to that city of old soldiers and their descendants he wrote, "But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 3:20, ESV). He left their Roman citizenship intact and told them it was not final. A Christian may carry two passports. He cannot serve two ultimate loyalties, and the quiet work of civil religion is to keep him from ever noticing which one he has made supreme.

Scripture also shows what happens when the two collide. In Daniel 3 an empire raises a golden image and commands that when the music plays, everyone bows. Three Judeans who hold high office in Babylon and have served it faithfully refuse: "If this be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king. But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up" (Daniel 3:17–18, ESV). They drew the seam where it belongs, between the country they served and the God they worshiped. Jesus drew it in fewer words. Handed a denarius stamped with the emperor's face, he asked whose image it bore, and answered, "Therefore render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Matthew 22:21, ESV). We hear a division of territory. But the question was about images. The coin bears Caesar's image, so it goes back to Caesar. What bears God's image goes to God, and since Genesis 1:27 that has meant the human person.

Augustine wrote his answer while an empire fell. After Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in 410 and pagans blamed the Christian God, Augustine spent 413 to 426 writing *The City of God*. Its central image is two cities formed by two loves, the earthly by love of self reaching to contempt of God, the heavenly by love of God reaching to contempt of self (Book 14), tangled together in every nation and every heart until the end. He was no enemy of patriotism. In Book 5 he credited the old Roman patriots for loving their city above their comfort, and said God gave them the earthly glory they sought as their reward. In Book 19 he defined a people as a multitude bound together by agreement about the things it loves, which makes every nation finally a question about love, and he cited Jeremiah's letter to the exiles to explain why the pilgrim church prays for the peace of the city it passes through. Beneath it all lies his brief definition of virtue as rightly ordered love (Book 15). Sin, for Augustine, is most often a good thing loved out of its order. The nationalist loves his country out of order, above the kingdom, above the stranger his country fears, above the Christ who counted a welcome given to the stranger as a welcome given to himself (Matthew 25:35). The problem was never the size of the love. It was the place the love was put.

### What the room teaches

Architecture preaches. The eye learns from arrangement faster than the ear learns from speech, and a child raised where two banners flank the pulpit absorbs an equivalence long before anyone could explain it. The Flag Code asks that the national flag in a church hold the position of superior prominence. Paul says of the Son that he is "the head of the body, the church. He is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead, that in everything he might be preeminent" (Colossians 1:18, ESV). A congregation cannot give superior prominence to two things in the same room. It will decide, if only by its furniture, which claim it honors.

This is a local question, and a second-order one. Nothing in the creeds settles where a church hangs a flag, and anyone who sneers at the sober Christians on the other side has disqualified himself from the conversation. But the counsel of this essay is plain. Honor the nation in its place: in the prayers of the people every Lord's Day, offered "for kings and all who are in high positions" (1 Timothy 2:2, ESV); in a memorial for the congregation's own who served and died. Let the space where the Word is read, the table spread, and the waters of baptism given carry one glory. A church that moves its flag should move it slowly, with teaching first, with its veterans and Gold Star families at the table while it is decided, and never as a gesture against them. A church that leaves it standing owes its people the teaching that makes the seam visible, so the arrangement no longer preaches unopposed.

The skeptic who walks into a sanctuary and finds the national colors beside the cross concludes, reasonably, that Christianity is the chaplaincy of one tribe. We gave him the evidence. But the faith says the opposite about its own final hope. "After this I looked, and behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands, and crying out with a loud voice, 'Salvation belongs to our God who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!'" (Revelation 7:9–10, ESV). Every nation is there, its languages intact. What is missing are the banners. They hold palm branches instead, and they have one cry between them. Nothing they truly loved has been lost, and nothing they loved is being worshiped.

At the center of that vision stands a Lamb who was slain, and the cross is the one symbol in any sanctuary that refuses to share its place. When Jesus died on it, it was no religious object but the empire's instrument of execution, and over it Rome posted his crime: King of the Jews. When a church stands a national flag beside the cross, it places the emblem of a power beside the thing power used to kill the Son of God, and asks the congregation to feel one devotion toward both. The cross does not bless the flag. It judges every flag, gently, including the one we love, and waits to see whether the church will hear. Then the empire's victim walked out of a sealed and guarded tomb, and after that morning no nation can credibly claim to be the thing that saves. The resurrection is the permanent demotion of every empire, which is also, strangely, what frees us to love our own without fear. A country no longer asked to save us can finally be loved as what it is.

None of this resolves on a Sunday morning. The flag will still stand near the front of a great many churches this week, and a great many of us will stand near it without a thought, and I am one of them. The question it puts to us is the one the music put to three young men on the plain of Dura, and a coin put to the crowd in the temple court. The coin still goes back to Caesar. The music still plays. And the one thing no flag can ever be given, however dearly we love it, is the thing stamped with another image, which we carry into that room every week and must decide, again, whom we will render it to.
