Anyone who has spent enough Sundays in American churches has probably noticed the imbalance. The sermon series on sexuality comes around every few years, carefully built and sometimes genuinely courageous, and the denominational fights over marriage are fought to the end, at the cost of buildings and friendships. The sermon on greed, when it comes at all, tends to arrive a few weeks before the budget vote, and it is mostly about giving to the church. The skeptic puts it more sharply. The church, he says, cares a great deal about what happens in your bedroom and very little about what happens in your portfolio, and he suspects the reason is that the people with the portfolios pay the salaries.

Part of that accusation is unfair, and I'll come to the part that is. But the core of it is hard to answer, because the Bible will not let the two subjects be pulled apart. I write as a pastor who has preached on sexuality with more confidence and less anxiety than I have ever preached on wealth, so this is not an essay about other people's churches.

**The claim of this essay is that the church talks about sex more than money not because Scripture weighs them that way, but because the American church, on the right and on the left, divided a single biblical subject in two. The New Testament treats sexual immorality and covetousness as twin forms of one disordered desire, the grasping after more that turns a gift into a god. Twentieth-century Protestantism split into parties that each kept half of that sentence, and a consumer economy trained all of us to feel the one sin as a scandal and the other as success. The remedy is not to say less about sex. It is to hear the whole sentence again, beginning with the half that indicts the preacher.**

## What the Bible actually puts together

You will often hear that the Bible contains more than two thousand verses about money. The figure circulates without a clear source, and I won't lean on it. What matters more is the company these warnings keep. Read Scripture on money beside Scripture on sex, and what stands out is not that one outweighs the other. It is how often the writers refuse to separate them.

Begin where Israel's law ends. "You shall not covet your neighbor's house; you shall not covet your neighbor's wife, or his male servant, or his female servant, or his ox, or his donkey, or anything that is your neighbor's" (Exodus 20:17, ESV). Adultery and theft have already been forbidden. The tenth commandment reaches beneath the acts to the desire that produces them, and it puts the neighbor's wife and the neighbor's house under a single verb.

The prophets keep the two together, though they're usually quoted on only one. Amos is remembered as the great economic prophet, and Israel is condemned "because they sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals." But the indictment continues in the next breath: "those who trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth and turn aside the way of the afflicted; a father and his son go in to the same girl, so that my holy name is profaned" (Amos 2:6–7, ESV). Commentators differ over who the girl was, a servant in the household or a woman attached to a shrine, but on either reading she is someone without power, used by men who had it. Selling a debtor for the price of sandals and taking a girl who cannot refuse are the same act in different currency. Ezekiel supplies the case we have learned to read with one eye closed: "Behold, this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy" (Ezekiel 16:49, ESV). Genesis 19 and Jude 7 give Christians real reason to associate Sodom with sexual sin, and nobody has to choose between those texts and Ezekiel. What a faithful reader cannot do is quote the one for forty years and never mention the other.

Jesus sharpens all of it. Asked to settle an inheritance dispute, he turns to the crowd: "Take care, and be on your guard against all covetousness, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15, ESV). Luke 16 then holds the two subjects in a single frame. Jesus says, "You cannot serve God and money," and Luke adds a detail we tend to skip: "The Pharisees, who were lovers of money, heard all these things, and they ridiculed him" (Luke 16:13–14, ESV). His answer ought to unsettle any church that reserves its strongest vocabulary for sexual sin: "You are those who justify yourselves before men, but God knows your hearts. For what is exalted among men is an abomination in the sight of God" (Luke 16:15, ESV). The word American Christians know chiefly from Leviticus is applied here to what a money-loving religious culture admires. Three verses later, in the same speech, comes one of his hardest sayings on divorce and remarriage (Luke 16:18), and then the story of the rich man who passed Lazarus at his gate every day on the way in. The love of money, the breaking of marriage and the neglect of the poor man at the gate belong to one diagnosis of a heart that serves itself.

The apostolic letters make the pairing almost a formula. Paul tells the Corinthians not to keep company with a professing believer guilty of sexual immorality or greed, naming the two side by side as grounds for the church's discipline (1 Corinthians 5:11). A chapter later he writes that "neither the sexually immoral, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor men who practice homosexuality, nor thieves, nor the greedy, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor swindlers will inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Corinthians 6:9–10, ESV). That is the list American churches quote in the sexuality debates. It has the greedy in it, and the swindlers. To the Colossians he writes, "Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5, ESV). Hebrews moves from the marriage bed to the bank account without starting a new paragraph: "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous. Keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you have" (Hebrews 13:4–5, ESV).

Paul's word for *covetousness*, *pleonexia*, means the craving for more. When he warns the Thessalonians against sexual immorality, "that no one transgress and wrong his brother in this matter" (1 Thessalonians 4:6, ESV), the verb rendered *wrong* comes from the same family: to overreach, to take more than your share. For Paul, sexual sin is a kind of grasping, and grasping is a kind of idolatry. Add 1 Timothy's warning that "those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare," because "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (1 Timothy 6:9–10, ESV), and James telling the rich that "the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you" (James 5:4, ESV), and the verdict is plain. The Bible does not rank these two sins so much as braid them.

## The case for the church's emphasis

Those who have kept the church's sexual ethic clear deserve their case at its strongest, because much of it is right.

The first argument is that doctrine gets defended where it is attacked. The church wrote the Nicene Creed in 325 not because the deity of the Son was the only thing that mattered but because Arius had denied it. By the same logic, the Christian sexual ethic has been under frontal pressure in the West for more than sixty years. The approval of the birth control pill in 1960, *Griswold v. Connecticut* in 1965, *Roe v. Wade* in 1973 and *Obergefell v. Hodges* in 2015 mark a steady, public reversal of what the culture assumed about sex, marriage and children. Nobody marched to demand that the church bless covetousness. The church spoke where the wall was being breached.

The second argument is Paul's. He gives sexual sin a gravity of its own: "Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body" (1 Corinthians 6:18, ESV). And he ties marriage to the gospel itself: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32, ESV). If marriage is a living picture of Christ's covenant with his people, distort the picture and you distort the preaching of the thing it pictures.

The third argument is the one critics most often miss. The sexual revolution was also an economic event, and its heaviest costs fell on those with the least. Charles Murray, writing from the right in *Coming Apart* (2012), and Robert Putnam, writing from the left in *Our Kids* (2015), found much the same pattern: over the late twentieth century, marriage and stable two-parent homes held up among the college-educated and eroded sharply among the working class, and children bore the difference. A church that insists on fidelity and the protection of children is not ignoring the poor. In an important sense it is defending them.

All three arguments are true, and they explain why the church should speak clearly about sex. They do not explain why it has gone quiet about greed, and the first argument contains the answer to its own puzzle. The sexual ethic was attacked openly and by name, and attacked doctrines produce defenders. The teaching on greed was never argued down. It was outgrown. An economy organized around wanting more never needed to refute Luke 12:15. It only needed to make the verse sound naive.

That is the distinction that changes the question. There is a contested sin, which the culture openly calls good and the church must openly call sin, and there is a rewarded sin, which the culture never has to defend because it simply pays for it. Covetousness came dressed as ambition and security, and it received promotions and the admiration of the neighbors. A doctrine under frontal attack produces a movement. A doctrine dissolved by habit produces nothing, because nobody notices it's gone. Jesus' word for the farmer who built bigger barns was *fool* (Luke 12:20). Our word for him would be *responsible*.

Taken seriously, the steelman cuts deeper still. If Murray and Putnam are right, the sexual and the economic were never separate subjects, and a church that preaches one without the other can't defend the family well. And the Paul who says sexual sin is against the body also calls covetousness idolatry. The canon never gives permission to preach one and go quiet about the other.

## How the church divided the sentence

The church's most honored preachers held the sentence together for a very long time, and they said things about wealth that would empty a modern stewardship campaign.

Around 368, probably during the famine that struck Cappadocia in those years, Basil of Caesarea preached on the rich fool of Luke 12 in a homily known by the farmer's own words, "I Will Tear Down My Barns." He treated the barns not as prudence but as a crime scene. In a passage translated several ways, he tells his hearers that the bread they store belongs to the hungry, the cloak in the chest to the naked, the shoes left to rot to the barefoot, the buried silver to the needy. He compares the rich to a man who arrives early at the theater and then bars the door, claiming as his own what was meant for all because he got there first. Twenty years later in Antioch, John Chrysostom preached a series of sermons on Lazarus and the rich man, arguing that the rich man was condemned not for robbing Lazarus but for passing him, and that failing to share with the poor is itself a kind of theft. The same Chrysostom scolded his congregation at length about the sexual displays of the theater. Neither man divided the sentence. It would not have occurred to them that a bishop might speak with courage about the body and with caution about the barns.

The division is modern, and it has dates. Industrial capitalism made the economic warnings urgent in a new way, and Walter Rauschenbusch, a Baptist who had pastored at the edge of Hell's Kitchen in New York, argued in *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) that the kingdom of God made claims on wages and housing, not only on souls. Much of what he saw was true. But the social gospel movement increasingly relocated sin from the heart to the structure, and many of its heirs came to treat the atonement and the bodily resurrection as optional. When the fundamentalist and modernist controversy broke open, marked by Harry Emerson Fosdick's sermon "Shall the Fundamentalists Win?" in 1922 and the Scopes trial in 1925, conservatives fought for the virgin birth, the atonement and the resurrection. They were right to; those doctrines are the floor. But in the heat of that fight they came to hear the vocabulary of economic justice as the liberals' language, and they let it go.

The sociologist David Moberg's *The Great Reversal* (1972) gave the result its common name: the heirs of evangelical abolitionists and rescue missions withdrew from social concern for most of a generation. Carl F. H. Henry, no liberal and later the founding editor of *Christianity Today*, rebuked his own movement in *The Uneasy Conscience of Modern Fundamentalism* (1947) for a gospel with nothing to say about the injustices around it. Meanwhile the mainline churches kept Rauschenbusch's concern for economic justice, and over the following decades many of them loosened and then revised the church's historic sexual ethic. American Protestantism had split the canon between two parties, each keeping half the sentence Paul wrote to Colossae.

Politics hardened the split. After 1973 conservative Protestants organized around abortion, the family and the schools, and by 1979, with the founding of the Moral Majority, they had become a durable part of a coalition that also championed free-market economics. Progressive churches found their allies among the movements carrying the sexual revolution forward. I have to be careful here. How to tax, regulate and help the poor are prudential questions on which faithful Christians disagree in good conscience, and this is no argument for any party's policy. The claim is narrower. Covetousness is not a policy position. The heart that wants more is present in every voting bloc, and each coalition handed its churches a list of sins it could safely name and a list it could not. For much of fifty years, the coalition wrote the sermon calendar.

## The money sermons we do preach

The American church does talk about money, constantly. The trouble is the kind of talk.

There is the budget sermon, which treats money as a resource God wants a portion of. There is the blessing sermon, which treats money as a reward God wants you to have. And there is silence, which treats money as none of God's business. Christian Smith and Michael Emerson, in *Passing the Plate* (2008), documented how the first of these usually fares: American Christians give away a small share of their income, far below the tithe their own churches often teach and far below what they could give without real hardship. A church that preaches the tithe and lives on a fraction of it has negotiated a truce with mammon and called it stewardship.

The blessing sermon has a long American history, and Kate Bowler's *Blessed* (2013) is its most careful account. Bowler traces the prosperity gospel through four themes, faith, wealth, health and victory, and distinguishes a hard version, which openly promises wealth for faith and giving, from a soft version spoken in the gentler language of positive thinking. She writes about its people with real sympathy, and they deserve it. Many came out of poverty and illness and heard something the respectable churches had stopped saying, that God cares about bodies, rent and grocery bills. That instinct is biblical. But the prosperity gospel did not neglect Scripture's teaching on money. It inverted it, turning a rival master into a sign of favor, and the soft version has seeped into many churches that would never host a faith healer. Whenever a congregation reads its own comfort as evidence of God's approval, a little of Bowler's subject has come into the building.

All three treat money as neutral, a thing to be managed well or badly. None says what Jesus said, that money is a master with claims of its own. Jacques Ellul made this the center of *Money and Power* (1954), arguing that Jesus' personification of Mammon recognizes money as a spiritual power in human life, one that demands trust and promises security, and that the Christian answer to it is not better management but the free, unrewarded gift that strips money of its sacred aura.

We have been slow to see this because the power works through habit rather than argument. James K. A. Smith's *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) describes the shopping mall as a temple with its own icons of the good life and its promise of redemption through purchase. We are formed less by the ideas we hold, he argues, than by practices that train what we love, and the market runs its liturgy six days a week. The same liturgy forms sexual desire. The self the market forms, a chooser whose wants are sovereign, is exactly the self the sexual revolution addressed. We fought the sexual revolution with one hand and enrolled our children in the market's catechism with the other, and rarely noticed that both taught the same lesson: I am what I want, and what I want I should have. The church opposed the fruit and watered the root.

Then there is the plainest reason, the one pastors are least eager to say. The members best able to fund a church are usually those for whom Scripture's warnings about wealth cost the most. This is a structural pressure, not a personal scandal, and it works through a thousand small decisions not to go quite that far this week. I have made those decisions. I have told myself that the congregation wasn't ready, that the political implications would be misread, that a hard word about money has to be earned through years of trust. Some of that is true. None of it is entirely honest.

## The sermon aimed at the absent

The Old Testament has a story about how prophecy works, and it binds the two sins together. After David took Bathsheba and arranged her husband's death, Nathan came to him with a case. A rich man with very many flocks had a visitor, and rather than take an animal from his own herds, "he took the poor man's lamb and prepared it for the man who had come to him" (2 Samuel 12:4, ESV). David pronounced the sentence himself: "As the LORD lives, the man who has done this deserves to die" (2 Samuel 12:5, ESV). Then Nathan said, "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:7, ESV).

To convict a king of adultery and murder, Nathan told a story about economic greed. David had taken a woman the way the rich man took the lamb, because he could and because what he had was never enough. The word of the Lord names that hunger: "I gave you your master's house and your master's wives into your arms and gave you the house of Israel and of Judah. And if this were too little, I would add to you as much more" (2 Samuel 12:8, ESV). The sexual sin was a form of the grasping.

Nathan also supplies an instrument that has to be applied to the right and the left alike. Denunciation names the sins of people who aren't in the room. Prophecy names the sins of the people who are, beginning with the prophet. By that measure, much American preaching on both subjects has been denunciation. In many conservative churches the sexual sermon lands hardest on those with the least standing: the teenagers, the unmarried, the gay neighbor who isn't there to answer, the culture at large. It rarely lands on the comfortable middle-aged members whose besetting temptation is not the hotel room but the barn. In many progressive churches the economic sermon has the same shape turned around. It lands on billionaires and unjust structures, which are real, and seldom on the congregation's own comfortable households, while Paul's sexual ethic gets filed as the prejudice of an earlier century. Both congregations let their hearers burn with anger at the rich man in the story. Both stop before verse 7.

I don't want to pretend a false symmetry. The two failures are not equally common in every tradition, and each preacher's first responsibility is the one his own tradition is most exposed to. But the grammar is shared. In both rooms the sin named is someone else's, and the preacher is safe.

The skeptic is owed a straight answer about the accusation this essay began with. It is largely correct, and the Bible made it first. The church did not go quiet about greed because God cares more about bedrooms than portfolios. It went quiet because one sermon costs the preacher little and the other a great deal, and because both of its political families found it easier to prosecute the absent than to convict the present. Basil said as much about the barns sixteen centuries ago. Nathan said it to a king.

## The man Jesus loved

Mark tells of a man who ran up to Jesus and knelt, asking what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus recited the commandments: "Do not murder, Do not commit adultery, Do not steal, Do not bear false witness, Do not defraud, Honor your father and mother" (Mark 10:19, ESV). *Do not defraud* is not in the Decalogue as Moses gave it. Matthew and Luke leave it out of their lists (Matthew 19:18–19; Luke 18:20); only Mark records Jesus adding it, in the same list as the commandment on adultery. The man said he had kept them all from his youth, and nothing in the text suggests he was lying. He was the member any church would want: serious, observant, honest in business, and eager.

"And Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me.' Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:21–22, ESV).

The command is not a rule that every disciple must sell everything; Zacchaeus gave half, and Jesus said, "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9, ESV). But the claim behind it is universal. Jesus found the one thing this man's heart was holding back and asked for it, and he did not lower the price when the man turned to go. He loved him, and he let him go. Then he told the disciples, "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God" (Mark 10:25, ESV). They "were exceedingly astonished, and said to him, 'Then who can be saved?'" (Mark 10:26, ESV). Jesus did not soften the saying. He answered it: "With man it is impossible, but not with God. For all things are possible with God" (Mark 10:27, ESV). The camel can go through, but by a miracle, not a negotiation.

That is where repentance has to begin, and it is a gospel word before it is a demand. Return to the list in 1 Corinthians 6, where the sexually immoral and the greedy stand side by side. Paul does not end it with a threat. "And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11, ESV). The washing covers both halves of the list. A few verses later he gives the reason a Christian's body is not his own: "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:19–20, ESV). The logic doesn't stop at the skin. If I am not my own, neither is my paycheck, my house or my retirement account. The price that purchased the body purchased the barns.

What would change is less dramatic than a crusade and harder to sustain. A church would read 1 Corinthians 6:9–10 aloud without stopping at the words that suit the current debate. It would preach Luke 16 in the season the budget is written, and teach giving as Ellul described it, the dethroning of a god rather than a membership fee. Its members would confess covetousness to one another as plainly as lust. None of that is financial advice. It is a question about worship, which is where Colossians put it when it called covetousness idolatry.

Beneath all of it stands the one who did the opposite of grasping, who, "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" (Philippians 2:6, ESV), and who, "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). The sexual sin and the economic sin are both a reaching for more than was given. The cross is where God reached the other way, emptying rather than filling, and it is the only power I know that can reorder a heart trained by a lifetime of wanting.

The man in Mark's story went away sorrowful, and Mark doesn't tell us whether he ever came back. He leaves him on the road, loved and grieving, still holding what he could not put down. Most of the American church is standing somewhere near him, and I am standing there too, having kept the commandments we find easy and preached the ones that cost us least. The disciples' question is still ours: then who can be saved? The answer is that it is impossible, and that God does impossible things. The first sign he is doing one is usually that a person can finally hear the whole sentence, including the half addressed to him.
