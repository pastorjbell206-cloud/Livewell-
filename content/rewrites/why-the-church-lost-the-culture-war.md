## The defeat, and the name we gave the fight

For a generation of American Christians the defeat is simply a fact of life. The public argument over marriage, sex and the family, which many churches treated as theirs to win, was settled against them with a speed that surprised both sides. When Gallup first asked Americans in 1996 whether marriages between people of the same sex should be recognized in law, about a quarter said yes. By the time the Supreme Court decided *Obergefell v. Hodges* on June 26, 2015, and required every state to license those marriages, a majority agreed, and within a few years the figure stood near seven in ten. In March 2021 Gallup reported that membership in a church, synagogue or mosque had fallen below half of American adults for the first time since it began asking in 1937. Whatever else those years were, they were not a victory.

The familiar explanations come from both directions. One says the church lost because it fought: it tied the gospel to a party, spent its moral authority on elections, and taught a generation to associate Jesus with a voter guide. The other says it lost because it fought too late and too timidly, abandoning the universities, the newsrooms and the entertainment industry decades before the ballots were counted. Each explanation holds something true. Neither asks the question underneath both of them, which is what the church thought it was doing when it agreed that a war was the right name for its public life.

A separate essay on this site traces how the religious right was built. This one is about the idea itself. Metaphors are not neutral. The words we use for our situation decide in advance what counts as success, who counts as an enemy and what we may do to win, and a church that calls its public life a war has settled a great deal before the first sermon is preached. I'm a Baptist pastor in the tradition that fought this war most loudly, and I'm not writing from the other side of the aisle. The losses are ours to count, and some of them are mine.

## Where the phrase came from

The phrase is German before it is American. In the 1870s, in the empire Otto von Bismarck had just assembled under Prussian leadership, the state set out to break the political power of the Roman Catholic Church, and German liberals gave the campaign its name: *Kulturkampf*, the struggle over culture. After the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility in July 1870, Catholic Germans looked to Bismarck and his liberal allies like citizens whose final loyalty lay in Rome. Between 1871 and 1875 came a cascade of laws. Political preaching from the pulpit was made a crime, the Jesuits were expelled, the May Laws of 1873 put the training and appointment of priests under state control, and civil marriage was made compulsory. Bishops were fined, imprisoned or driven into exile.

Two things about that first culture war deserve more attention than they get. The first is that the church lost the contest of power and kept its people. German Catholics closed ranks, filled their churches and voted in growing numbers for the Catholic Centre Party, and by the end of the decade Bismarck needed that party's votes more than he needed his campaign. After Leo XIII became pope in 1878, the laws were softened and most of them repealed over the following years. The second thing is darker. The Centre Party, built to defend the church when the state attacked it, became over the next half century a skilled broker of the church's interests, and in March 1933 its deputies voted for the Enabling Act that gave Adolf Hitler his dictatorship, on the strength of promises about the freedom of the church. Persecution had strengthened German Catholics. The political instrument built to protect them proved far more dangerous than the persecution had been.

The phrase entered American English through a sociologist. In 1991 James Davison Hunter of the University of Virginia published *Culture Wars: The Struggle to Define America*, and his argument was more careful than his title. The deepest religious division in American life, Hunter observed, no longer ran between Protestants, Catholics and Jews. It ran through the middle of each tradition. On one side stood what he called the orthodox impulse, a commitment to an external, definable and transcendent source of moral authority. On the other stood the progressive impulse, the tendency to reinterpret historic faith according to the prevailing assumptions of contemporary life. An evangelical Baptist, a traditional Catholic and an Orthodox Jew now had more in common on abortion, sexuality and the family than any of them had with the progressive members of their own communions. The conflict, Hunter argued, was finally about moral authority itself: on what basis anything could be called right or wrong, and who had the standing to say so.

Hunter was describing a fracture. Within a year a politician had turned it into a battle cry. At the Republican National Convention in Houston in August 1992, Patrick Buchanan, fresh from challenging President George H. W. Bush for the nomination, told the delegates that a religious war was under way for the soul of America, a cultural war, he said, as critical to the kind of nation America would become as the Cold War itself had been. The speech fixed the phrase in the common vocabulary. What Hunter had offered as a diagnosis became, for millions of Christians, a description of their calling.

The metaphor carried assumptions that most of us absorbed without noticing. A war has two armies, and everyone belongs to one. It has territory, which one side holds and the other wants. It has a scoreboard, and it has an end, after which the losing side accepts the winner's terms. Each of them misled us.

## Territory and garden

The word culture comes from the Latin *colere*, which means to till, to tend and to inhabit, and also to honor or worship; the word *cultus* grows from the same root. A culture, in that older sense, is what grows when a people tends something over a long time: the habits of a household, the shape of a week, the stories told to children. The first charge given to humanity in Scripture is cultural in exactly this sense: "The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it" (Genesis 2:15, ESV). You can seize territory in an afternoon. You can't seize a garden. You can only tend it or neglect it.

That is the distinction the culture war blurred. It treated culture as territory, held by institutions, defended by law and won or lost in courts and legislatures. Some of that territory mattered a great deal. But beneath it lay the garden, the formed habits and loves of a people, and the garden had been changing long before anyone drew a battle line across it.

Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) is the most patient account of that change. Taylor asks why it was nearly impossible not to believe in God in the Western world of 1500, while by 2000 belief had become one option among others, and often not the easiest one to hold. His answer is not chiefly that people were argued out of faith but that the conditions of belief changed. Modern Westerners, believers and unbelievers alike, live within what Taylor calls the immanent frame, a shared sense that the world is a self-sufficient natural order in which a person can live a full life without reference to anything beyond it. The frame can be held open toward transcendence or closed against it, but it surrounds the evangelical in the pew as fully as the atheist down the street. The ground was moving beneath both armies at once. Christians defending an inherited morality often did so from inside the same frame as their opponents, and their children could tell the difference between a way of life that flowed from a vision of God and one that was simply what their parents had always done. The first kind can survive losing its legal protection. The second rarely survives losing its social one.

Alasdair MacIntyre had diagnosed another part of the problem a decade before Hunter's book. *After Virtue* (1981) opens by imagining a catastrophe in which the natural sciences are destroyed and later generations recover only fragments, which they go on using with confidence but without the understanding that once made sense of them. MacIntyre's disquieting suggestion was that our moral language is in just that condition. We still use words like good, duty, right and justice, but they are fragments of older traditions, biblical, Aristotelian and Enlightenment, torn from the frameworks that once gave them meaning. His examples are the public arguments over war, abortion and justice, which never end because the rival premises are incommensurable: each side reasons validly from its starting point, and there is no shared standard for choosing between starting points.

Read Hunter through MacIntyre and the culture war looks different. What looked like a debate was a clash of moral languages that no longer translated into each other, and where argument can't persuade, each side reaches for what can settle a dispute without persuading anyone: the court, the statute, the election. The war metaphor accurately described what public moral argument becomes when the shared language is gone. The mistake was for the church to accept that description as its own.

That is why *Obergefell* felt like an ending even to people who had expected it. Justice Anthony Kennedy's majority opinion grounded the right to marry in the dignity and autonomy of the person, the four dissenters answered on the role of courts and the nature of marriage, and neither side persuaded the other. The decision did not settle the moral question. It settled who held the territory. For a movement that had understood itself as a moral majority, the discovery was worse than a loss: there was no longer a shared set of values to defend, and the majority had not been there for some time. Seven years later *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overturned *Roe v. Wade*, and some Christians took it as proof that the war could still be won. For those who hold what the church has held since the *Didache* forbade the killing of a child by abortion, the decision was a real good. But it returned the question to the states, not to a common conscience, and several of the ballot measures that followed, including one here in Michigan in 2022, went the other way. The territory had changed hands. The garden had not.

## Those who would fight on, and those who never would have fought

Two groups of Christians will object to all of this, and both deserve to be heard in the form they'd sign.

The first says the fight was right and the church should keep fighting. The stakes, it says, were never abstractions. They were children who would not be born, the freedom of Christian schools, charities and parents to teach what they believe, and the shape of the family in which the next generation would be raised. Thomas Aquinas argued in the *Summa Theologiae* that human law cannot make people virtuous, yet it restrains the gravest harms and trains a people by habit toward the good. The church has fought in public before and been honored for it: William Wilberforce and his evangelical friends labored twenty years in Parliament before the slave trade was abolished in 1807, and the Black churches of the South powered the movement behind the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Withdrawal, moreover, is not neutral. In a democracy, stepping back from the argument is a vote for whoever is already winning it, and the other side has shown no interest in disarming. A church that stops caring who holds the territory will soon find its garden standing on someone else's land.

The second group says the church never should have fought, and it has two voices. The older one belongs to the Anabaptist tradition and its modern heirs. Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon argued in *Resident Aliens* (1989) that the collapse of Christendom was a release rather than a disaster, and that the church should understand itself as a colony, an outpost of one way of life inside another, whose first political task is to be a people whose common life is the alternative it proclaims. On that account the culture war was Constantine by other means, the old dream that the nation belongs to the church. They can cite Paul: "For what have I to do with judging outsiders? Is it not those inside the church whom you are to judge? God judges those outside" (1 Corinthians 5:12–13, ESV). The newer voice is progressive. It says the war did real harm to real people, above all to gay and lesbian neighbors who heard from the church for thirty years that they were the enemy, and that a church cannot commend a gospel of grace to people it has spent a generation treating as a threat.

Each case sees something the other misses. Those who would keep fighting are right that law teaches, that the unborn and the poor are neighbors whose protection is not optional, and that silence has consequences. Those who would never have fought are right that the church's first politics is its own life, and that a people who treat their neighbors as enemies will struggle to convince them of anything, including the love of God. Yet the first case assumes what this essay has questioned: that the garden is kept by holding the territory. And the second, in its quietist form, forgets that there are hours when the church must say no to the state's claims, as the Confessing Church did in the Barmen Declaration of 1934, while in its progressive form it did not so much refuse the war as enlist on the other side.

The fighters have one objection I can't fully answer. Faithful presence is slow, and a child in danger needs protection now. Law can save a life this year that formation will not reach for a generation. Whatever the church learns from losing the culture war, it can't learn indifference to the vulnerable people the war was, at its best, trying to protect. The answer is not to keep fighting on the old terms, but the cost of changing them is not small, and anyone who says otherwise has not counted it.

## What losing revealed

The first thing the defeat exposed was how much of the agreement had never been conviction. The name Moral Majority assumed that the nation's morals were basically Christian and needed only to be mobilized. But much of that majority held its morality the way people hold an accent, as something received rather than chosen or understood. When the surrounding culture stopped reinforcing it, it went, and it went inside the church as well as outside. The sexual revolution didn't wait at the church door to be voted down. It came in with us, into our marriages, our screens and our children's assumptions, while our platforms were still promising to hold the line. We had handed the formation of our own people to a culture that happened, for a time, to agree with us, and we mistook its agreement for our strength.

The second thing the defeat exposed was the instrument we had been using. Hunter returned to the question in *To Change the World* (2010), where he describes three ways American Christians have related to the culture. The Christian right has been "defensive against" it, the progressive and seeker churches have sought "relevance to" it, and the neo-Anabaptists have sought "purity from" it. His argument is that all three share a deeper assumption: that the public life of Christians is chiefly political, so that faithfulness means gaining, using or resisting the power of the state. And all three, he argues, have been shaped by what Nietzsche called *ressentiment*, a politics fed by a narrative of injury, in which a group understands itself by what has been done to it and by whom. The right told its story as a faithful people dispossessed by elites, the left as the righteous standing against bigots, and even the separatists defined themselves by the state they refused. Hunter's alternative, which he calls faithful presence, gives up the project of winning back the culture and asks the church to be fully present in the places it already inhabits, its households, work and neighborhoods, seeking their flourishing.

The same instrument has to measure the left, and it gives a hard reading. The mainline Protestant churches largely aligned themselves with the progressive side of the argument, and on most of the questions they joined, their side won. Their decline did not stop. Mainline membership had begun to fall in the mid-1960s, well before the religious right existed, and it kept falling through every cultural victory the mainline cheered. Winning the culture war plainly didn't fill progressive churches. A church whose message is mostly what the educated culture already believes gives that culture no reason to come on Sunday. The scale of the political entanglement was not the same on both sides, and the right bears the heavier share for how the word evangelical came to sound. But the underlying error was shared. Both let the culture's argument set the terms of faithfulness, one by opposing it and the other by echoing it.

The third thing the defeat exposed was what the war had formed in us. A people who spend thirty years describing their neighbors as the enemy become a people who see enemies, and their children learn the tone long before the doctrine. Any pastor who has preached through these years knows how easily a sermon finds its energy in what "they" are doing to "us," and none of us who preach is exempt. My own tradition should have known better than most. Baptists learned their public voice as a persecuted minority. Roger Williams was banished from Massachusetts Bay and founded Providence in 1636 as a refuge for conscience, and in the years before the Revolution Baptist preachers in Virginia were jailed for preaching without a license from the established church. A tradition whose convictions about religious liberty were formed in jail learned, within living memory, to speak like an establishment that feared losing its place. The war failed to change the culture. It changed us instead, and the change shows exactly where the gospel should have been most distinct, in how we speak of those who disagree with us.

## The church that never had the culture

The church's founding documents were written by people who never held the territory. For nearly three centuries before Constantine's victory in 312 and the Edict of Milan in 313, Christians were a minority, often despised and sometimes hunted, with no legal standing and no expectation of any. The First Letter of Peter is addressed to "elect exiles of the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1 Peter 1:1, ESV), scattered congregations in what is now Turkey, slandered by neighbors whose ways they had left. Peter's instruction to them is compact:

> Beloved, I urge you as sojourners and exiles to abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul. Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation. (1 Peter 2:11–12, ESV)

Read it in its setting and notice where the war is. Peter does use the language of warfare, and he places the front line inside the believer: the passions of the flesh wage war against the soul. Toward the Gentiles, the neighbors who are slandering these Christians, his instruction is not combat but conduct: honorable lives, lived in plain view, which the accusers will eventually see. A few verses later he tells them to "honor everyone" and to "honor the emperor" (1 Peter 2:17, ESV), the head of the very order that despised them. These exiles stayed inside the life of their cities while refusing to let that life set the terms of their own.

We have a remarkable witness to how this looked from outside, and it comes from Bithynia, one of the very provinces the letter addresses. Around the year 112, Pliny the Younger, the Roman governor there, wrote to the emperor Trajan for advice about the Christians being brought before him. He reports that under questioning they admitted to meeting before dawn on a fixed day, singing a hymn to Christ as to a god, and binding themselves by oath not to commit theft, robbery or adultery, not to break their word, and not to refuse to return a deposit. He found nothing worse than what he considered a perverse and excessive superstition, and he executed those who would not renounce it. A hostile official who put Christians to death recorded as their crime a way of life he could not fault. Peter's hope that the accusers would "see your good deeds" was being fulfilled in the governor's own dispatch.

The sociologist Rodney Stark argued in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) that the church grew from a tiny movement to a substantial part of the empire by Constantine's time at something like forty percent a decade, and that it grew not by conquest or policy but through networks of family and friendship and through the conduct of Christians in crisis. When epidemics swept the empire in the second and third centuries, Christians nursed the sick, pagan neighbors as well as their own, while many others fled. Stark's figures are estimates, and historians debate them, but the pattern he describes is the one Peter asked for. The church did not win the culture of Rome. It grew inside it, and after three centuries the culture was different.

Jesus had set the terms on the night he was arrested. When the soldiers came to the garden, Peter drew a sword and cut off the ear of the high priest's servant, and Jesus said to him, "Put your sword into its sheath; shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?" (John 18:11, ESV). The letter to the exiles bears the name of the man who swung that sword. A few hours later, standing before the one man in Judea with real territory to defend, Jesus explained why: "My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would have been fighting, that I might not be delivered over to the Jews. But my kingdom is not from the world" (John 18:36, ESV). Jesus is speaking of the kingdom's source, not its sphere. His kingdom has everything to do with the world. It does not come from the world, and so it does not advance by the world's means. The culture war asked Christians to prove their seriousness by how hard they fought. Jesus offered the absence of fighting as proof of what kind of king he was.

None of this makes the church passive. Jeremiah wrote to the exiles in Babylon, who had lost everything a people can lose in public, and told them to "build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce" (Jeremiah 29:5, ESV), and then to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7, ESV). Hunter draws on this letter for faithful presence, and almost none of it concerns power. The exiles are to plant gardens, seek the good of a city that is not theirs, and pray for it. That is cultural work in the oldest sense of the word.

## The war Peter named

The defeat is not the whole story of American Christians in these years. Many quietly adopted children, ran pregnancy centers, fed the hungry and loved neighbors who voted against everything they believed, while the noise of the war drowned out their witness. Repentance begins where they began, with ordinary things. It looks like churches that take up the formation of their own people as their first public work: catechizing children in what the faith teaches and why, teaching marriage as a covenant before campaigning for it as a policy, and keeping a common life whose forgiveness, hospitality and generosity can be seen from the street. It looks like care for mothers and children that is as visible in a congregation's budget and nursery as it ever was at a rally. It looks like conservative Christians who can hold the historic teaching on marriage and still treat their gay neighbors as neighbors, and progressive Christians who can pursue justice for the poor and still treat their conservative neighbors as something other than bigots. It looks like Christians who still vote and still argue in public, as citizens should, but who no longer mistake the outcome of an election for the state of the kingdom.

None of this will win the culture war back, and that is not its purpose. The church was never promised the culture. It was promised the presence of its Lord, the power of his Spirit and the resurrection of the dead. Jesus lost every contest of territory that Friday: the crowd, the court and the capital. The men who held the territory nailed him to a cross outside the city walls. Three days later God overturned that verdict, and he did it without a vote, a statute or a sword. The resurrection is not the church's victory in a culture war. It is God's refusal to let the powers of this age have the last word, and it came first to a handful of frightened disciples who had just lost everything.

Peter told the exiles there was a war, and he told them where it was. It is fought in us, in the passions that wage war against the soul, the appetite for power and for grievance as much as the ordinary appetites of the flesh, and it does not end at an election or a court decision. For thirty years we fought the war we chose and neglected the one we were given. We lost the first. The second is still going on, in our homes and congregations and in the quiet places where we speak of our enemies. That is the war we were never allowed to stop fighting, and whether we have begun to fight it is a question no poll will answer.
