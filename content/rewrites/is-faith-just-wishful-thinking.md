## The thought nobody says at the bedside

Most people who suspect that faith is wishful thinking didn't get the idea from Freud. They got it standing near someone in trouble. A daughter who hasn't prayed since childhood starts praying in a hospital hallway. A widower tells the funeral director that he'll see her again. A friend whose marriage is coming apart says God has a plan, and says it a little too quickly. And somewhere in the person watching, a thought forms that no one voices in those rooms: of course they believe it, look how badly they need to. The need came first. The belief arrived afterward to meet it. On this reading faith isn't knowledge about anything outside the believer. It's a report on what the believer cannot bear.

I don't come to that thought as a believer swatting at a nuisance. I held it for years before I came to faith out of atheism, and I held it as the whole explanation rather than one objection among several. I thought I could see how it was made. Fear went in at one end and comfort came out at the other, and once you can see the factory you don't feel any obligation to inspect the product. So taking the suspicion seriously here isn't charity on my part. I'm describing a younger man whose reasoning I still understand from the inside.

The suspicion deserves better than the two answers it usually gets. The believer hears it as an insult and answers with wounded feelings, which is exactly what the suspicion predicts. The skeptic treats it as a proof and stops thinking, which is a comfort of its own. Neither is honest, because the suspicion is partly right. A great deal of what passes for faith in any age, including ours and including mine at various points, is a wish that has learned to use religious words.

**The great critics of projection saw something real, and the Hebrew prophets saw it first. Where a belief comes from can't settle whether it is true, and the psychology of wishing turns out to indict unbelief as readily as faith. And the distinction that decides the question is the one between wishful thinking, which is a way of avoiding what is, and Christian hope, which is a costly expectation fastened to something that has already happened.**

Whether faith is belief without evidence is weighed in [Is Faith Irrational?](/writing/is-faith-irrational), the evidence for Easter in [Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen), and what the resurrection asks of a life in [Why the Resurrection Matters](/writing/what-the-resurrection-changes). This essay takes up a narrower charge, that believers want too much to be trusted, and the word Christians use when they look toward the future. That word is hope, and among thoughtful people it has become faintly embarrassing, the thing said when the doctor runs out of options. It is the least wishful word the church owns.

## The case for projection at full strength

The suspicion is far older than psychology. In the sixth century before Christ the Greek poet Xenophanes observed that Ethiopians picture their gods as dark and snub-nosed while Thracians give theirs blue eyes and red hair, and that if cattle and horses could draw, they would draw gods shaped like cattle and horses. David Hume argued in *The Natural History of Religion* (1757) that the first religious ideas came from the hopes and fears of people facing events they couldn't control. People make gods out of what they are and what they dread. What the nineteenth century added was a system.

Ludwig Feuerbach built it in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841), which George Eliot translated into English in 1854. Theology, he argued, is anthropology in disguise. Everything believers ascribe to God, perfect love, wisdom and justice, belongs to human nature, lifted out of the mortal people who carry it in fragments, purified of its limits, and set over against them as a separate being. Feuerbach took doctrine more seriously than many of its defenders and claimed to find a human truth inside every article of the creed. His charge was that believers were talking about themselves without knowing it.

Karl Marx asked who holds the mirror. In the introduction to his critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, written in 1843 and 1844, he treated the criticism of religion, which Feuerbach had done most to carry through, as essentially complete and asked why people need the mirror at all. His answer is usually quoted by half. Religious suffering, he wrote, is both the expression of real suffering and a protest against it. Religion is "the sigh of the oppressed creature," and then, famously, "the opium of the people." In the 1840s opium was sold as laudanum, the painkiller in the household cabinet, and the image is almost tender: religion dulls a real pain without touching its cause. To abolish religion as the people's illusory happiness, he argued, is to demand their real happiness. The church has handed Marx evidence. In 1807, on behalf of the Society for the Conversion of Negro Slaves, a London printing produced *Select Parts of the Holy Bible* for enslaved people in the British West Indies. It passed from the Joseph story in Genesis straight to the nineteenth chapter of Exodus, so the escape from Egypt was simply gone, and it dropped Paul's "neither slave nor free" (Galatians 3:28), while the passages urging servants to obey their masters remained. That was Scripture edited to soothe and never stir.

Friedrich Nietzsche, a Lutheran pastor's son who had his madman announce the death of God in *The Gay Science* (1882), sharpened the charge in a direction the others missed. In *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) Nietzsche named the wish beneath Christian faith, and it wasn't comfort. It was revenge. The weak, unable to defeat the strong, relabeled their weakness as goodness and invented a world beyond this one where the strong would be punished. He quoted Thomas Aquinas on the blessed in heaven seeing the punishments of the damned so that their own bliss would please them more. Nietzsche doesn't accuse believers of being frightened children. He accuses them of being resentful ones.

Sigmund Freud gave the argument its best-known form in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927). The small child is helpless before a world that can crush it, and the father is both its protector and a figure it fears. The adult finds he is still helpless before nature, fate and death, and reaches again for a father, this time an infinite one. The gods, on Freud's account, exist to take the terror out of nature, to reconcile us to the cruelty of fate and above all of death, and to repay us for what civilization demands. He chose his word with care. An illusion, in his technical sense, is a belief in which a wish plays the leading part and the relation to reality is set aside. Unlike a delusion, it need not be false. He closed by placing his own hope in science, the god he called Logos.

The Christian owes an admission here. The Bible got there first. Isaiah describes a carpenter who cuts down a tree, burns half of it to bake his bread and warm his hands, and carves the rest into something to worship: "And the rest of it he makes into a god, his idol, and falls down to it and worships it. He prays to it and says, 'Deliver me, for you are my god!'" (Isaiah 44:17, ESV). The psalmist names the mechanism Feuerbach would describe many centuries later: "Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (Psalm 115:8, ESV). Israel's prophets criticized projection long before the Enlightenment, and they aimed first at their own people. The suspicion isn't a modern discovery Christianity has to survive. It is one of Scripture's own charges. The real question was never whether projection happens. It is whether everything is projection.

## Where a belief comes from, and whether it is true

Once its case is made, the argument takes a step it hasn't earned. Feuerbach, Marx and Freud each describe a mechanism, whether projection, sedation or a longing for the father, that would produce belief in God whether or not God exists. Then the argument slides from how the belief arose to whether the belief is true. Logicians call that slide the genetic fallacy, and it is a fallacy for a plain reason: the origin of a belief is not, by itself, evidence about its accuracy. A man waiting on a biopsy badly wants it to come back benign, and so he believes it will. His wanting is a poor reason to believe it. The biopsy may still be benign.

Freud knew this. He said outright that judging the truth of religious doctrines lay outside his inquiry, which is why he chose the word illusion rather than delusion. But the skeptic can press a stronger form, and fairness requires stating it. Suppose the only thing holding a belief in place is a process that would produce it whether or not it were true. Then the belief has no support left, even if it happens to be correct. This is a debunking argument, not the crude fallacy, and sometimes it works.

Alvin Plantinga took up exactly this form of the objection in *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000), where he called it the Freud and Marx complaint. Whether wish-fulfillment is a process aimed at truth depends on what kind of world we live in. If there is no God, then a longing for God is a longing aimed at nothing, and Freud's account is plausible. But if there is a God who made human beings for himself, then a deep longing for him might be precisely the way he designed them to find him, in which case the wish would be a faculty working as it should. Freud's argument doesn't show that belief is unwarranted unless we already know that God isn't there. It assumes the conclusion it was supposed to establish.

Plantinga saw that the argument cuts the other way too. Christianity has its own account of unbelief as a malfunction. Calvin, in the 1559 *Institutes*, described a sense of the divine planted in every human being, and Paul, in the first chapter of Romans, speaks of people who suppress what they know. On that account it's unbelief that needs a psychological explanation. Each side can tell a story in which the other's conviction is a symptom, and neither story wins on its own terms. They cancel out, and the question goes back where it belonged, to evidence and argument about the world.

Some unbelievers have put their own wishes on the table. The philosopher Thomas Nagel, no friend of religion, admitted in *The Last Word* (1997) that he hoped there was no God, that he didn't want the universe to be like that, and he named the fear the cosmic authority problem. The admission doesn't refute his atheism. It does refute the idea that unbelief is the wish-free ground a person stands on once he stops wanting things. A God would mean I am not finally my own, that there is a judgment I can't appeal and a standard I can't lower. The wish that no one holds a claim on my life is among the strongest a person can have, and it's hard to see because it feels like being reasonable.

The psychologist Paul Vitz pressed this in *Faith of the Fatherless* (1999). If a longing for a father can produce belief, he reasoned, a wound from a father can produce unbelief. He found that a striking number of prominent modern atheists had lost their fathers early or couldn't respect them: Nietzsche's father died when the boy was four, Bertrand Russell's when he was three, Jean-Paul Sartre's before he was two. The thesis needs more caution than its admirers show. Vitz chose famous lives after the fact, and the believers with strong fathers he set beside them were chosen the same way, so nothing in the book measures how common the pattern is among the fatherless or the well-fathered. A theory flexible enough to explain any life explains none in particular. Vitz offered it as a hypothesis, and its real force is Plantinga's point: psychology can tell a plausible story for either side, so it can't decide between them.

I have a stake in this, and I'd rather say so. I was raised without a father, and I was an atheist before I was a believer. Vitz's theory would read the first fact as the explanation of the second and would have nothing to say about why the atheism ended. I don't trust any theory to explain me that neatly. What I can say honestly is that when I finally turned the suspicion on myself, I found more wanting in my unbelief than I had ever admitted. There was a boy who had concluded early that no father was coming, and for whom a sky with no one in it felt less like a loss than like relief, because a sky with no Father in it couldn't disappoint him. I had run the projection test on every believer I knew. I had never run it on myself.

## The God nobody would have ordered

If faith were only projection, the projected God would be easy to recognize. He would approve of us as we are, take our side in every quarrel, and ask for nothing we weren't prepared to give. That God exists, and the church has manufactured him in bulk. When Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton studied American teenagers for *Soul Searching* (2005), they found that the working religion of most of them was what they called moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be nice, helps them feel good about themselves, and stays out of the way until he's needed. The teenagers learned it from adults, and from pulpits. Beside the therapeutic god stands his louder cousin, the national god who blesses our side, shares our enemies and never asks the country to repent. Feuerbach would recognize both at a glance, and the left and the right have each built one in their own likeness.

Christian theology at its best has said so. Karl Barth, in *Church Dogmatics* I/2 (1938), called religion, understood as humanity's attempt to reach God on its own terms, a form of unbelief, and argued that God's self-revelation contradicts our religion rather than confirming it. He conceded Feuerbach a great deal, but not that the God who speaks in Scripture fits inside the mirror.

That God keeps behaving in ways no frightened person would script. When Isaiah sees him in the temple, he isn't comforted. He comes apart: "And I said: 'Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!'" (Isaiah 6:5, ESV). Hebrews warns believers tempted to drift back into something easier: "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (Hebrews 10:31, ESV). And Jesus, who could have promised his followers anything, offered them this: "And he said to all, 'If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me'" (Luke 9:23, ESV). A wish is shaped like the wisher. These texts are shaped like something that stands over against him.

Nietzsche would answer that one doctrine at least is plainly wished for: judgment, so long as it falls on someone else. The charge lands. Christians have relished hell for others, and a good deal of American preaching about judgment has been a way of standing on the right side of it. But the biblical doctrine won't hold still for that use. Peter, writing to believers under pressure, says that judgment starts with them: "For it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God; and if it begins with us, what will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel of God?" (1 Peter 4:17, ESV). A doctrine invented by the resentful to punish their enemies would not begin by indicting the resentful. And at the center of the faith stands something stranger still. The judge takes the sentence himself.

Nothing about that was on anyone's wish list in the first century. The Law of Moses declared a man hanged on a tree to be cursed by God (Deuteronomy 21:23), so a crucified Messiah was, to a devout Jew, very nearly a contradiction in terms, and to an educated Greek or Roman it was an embarrassment too crude to discuss. Paul knew exactly what he was asking people to accept: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). Projection builds gods out of what we admire. Nobody in the Roman world admired a cross.

None of this makes Christianity true. Some human inventions are severe. But a wish that includes a holy God, a judgment that begins at home and a Savior executed as a criminal is a very strange wish. The softer gods, therapeutic and national, fit Freud's description. The God of the cross keeps breaking it.

## The desire that points past itself

A further reply takes the wish seriously instead of explaining it away. Freud treats human longing for God as a reason to distrust belief. Lewis asked whether the longing might be evidence of something.

In "The Weight of Glory," a sermon preached at the University Church of St Mary the Virgin in Oxford on 8 June 1941, he spoke of a desire most people carry and few can name, a longing stirred by music, by a remembered place, by certain books, that none of those things ever satisfies. The beauty, he argued, was never in them. It came through them, and if we mistake the things themselves for what we want, they turn into idols and break our hearts. In the chapter titled "Hope" in *Mere Christianity* (1952), built from his wartime broadcasts, he put the reasoning plainly. Creatures aren't born with desires unless some satisfaction for them exists: a baby feels hunger, and there is food; a duckling wants to swim, and there is water. So if we find in ourselves a desire that nothing in this world can satisfy, the most probable explanation is that we were made for another one.

Lewis was more careful about the limits than many who quote him. In the Oxford sermon he granted that a man's hunger doesn't prove he will get bread; he might starve adrift at sea. What the hunger does prove is that he belongs to a race that repairs its body by eating and lives in a world where food exists. The argument from desire isn't a demonstration. It's an inference about what kind of creature we are, and it can be resisted. A skeptic can reply that natural selection might produce a never-satisfied longing because restless creatures keep striving and striving creatures survive. That reply is coherent. But it is a story too, and it has to account for a longing that success, love and beauty at their best leave unsatisfied.

Augustine had made the same observation in the opening paragraph of his *Confessions*, around 397, when he told God that he had made us for himself and that our hearts are restless until they rest in him. Freud saw the longing and concluded that the belief was suspect. Augustine and Lewis saw the same longing and took it as a clue. Nobody disputes the longing. The disagreement is over what it means, and the projection argument can't simply assume that a desire with no earthly object has no object at all.

Here the question of wishing becomes the question of hope. A wish and a hope look alike from outside, since both lean toward a future that isn't here yet. They differ in what they rest on and what they cost, and on that difference the whole charge turns.

## Hope is not optimism

Terry Eagleton, the Marxist literary critic, drew the line that matters in *Hope Without Optimism* (2015). Optimism, he argued, is a temperament. It expects things to turn out well without needing reasons, and it is conservative at root, since its faith in a good future rests on trust that the present is basically sound. Hope must be able to give reasons, and it can look at the worst without looking away. Thomas Aquinas had drawn a related line in his own terms: hope reaches for a future good that is hard to attain but possible, and it can fail in two directions, by despair, which gives the good up as impossible, or by presumption, which counts on having it without the road that leads there, pardon without repentance, glory without merit. Much of what we call wishful thinking sits close to what that older vocabulary called presumption, and the church named presumption a sin centuries before Freud named religion an illusion.

Scripture stages the contrast. In the fourth year of King Zedekiah, around 594 BC, the prophet Hananiah stood in the temple and announced that Babylon's yoke would be broken within two years (Jeremiah 28). He was the optimist, and he was the false prophet. Jeremiah had already named the type: "They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14, ESV). Jeremiah's own letter told the exiles to build houses and plant gardens in Babylon, because the return would come only after seventy years, which meant most of them would die there. Only then comes the verse we print on graduation cards: "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope" (Jeremiah 29:11, ESV). We have made Jeremiah sound like Hananiah. His hope began by refusing the optimism.

Hebrews ties faith to hope of exactly this kind. "Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1, ESV). The chapter that verse opens is a roll call of people who died still waiting: "These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13, ESV). A wish can't survive that. It needs its object soon, or it needs to pretend the object is already here. Earlier the same letter gives hope its image: "We have this as a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul, a hope that enters into the inner place behind the curtain" (Hebrews 6:19, ESV). Read in context, the anchor isn't a feeling. It holds because of God's promise to Abraham, sealed with an oath, "two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie" (Hebrews 6:18, ESV), and it reaches into the presence of God, where Jesus has already gone "as a forerunner on our behalf" (Hebrews 6:20, ESV).

Paul places hope at the end of suffering rather than in place of it. "Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us" (Romans 5:3–5, ESV). Three chapters later, with the whole creation groaning around him, he says what kind of thing hope is: "For in this hope we were saved. Now hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what he sees? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience" (Romans 8:24–25, ESV). Wishful thinking can't wait. Hope is the capacity to.

Jürgen Moltmann learned this as a prisoner. Conscripted as a teenager, he surrendered to British troops in February 1945 and was held until 1948, first in a camp in Belgium, then in Scotland, and finally at Norton Camp in England, while the truth about his nation's crimes came into view. He later wrote that it was in those camps, with no earthly ground for optimism, that he came to Christian faith. In *Theology of Hope* (1964), written in conversation with the Marxist philosopher Ernst Bloch's *The Principle of Hope* (1954 to 1959), he argued that Christian hope rests on the resurrection of the crucified Jesus. Such hope doesn't sedate. It contradicts the present, and so it makes believers restless with the world as it is. That is the answer to Marx. Opium dulls pain. This hope refuses to call the pain final. It is also why enslaved Christians in America, told from pulpits that the Bible said only obey, went on singing about Moses.

Peter, writing to scattered Christians under real trials, fixes the ground in a single sentence: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1 Peter 1:3, ESV). The hope looks forward. Its reason lies behind it.

## A wish costs nothing

Wishful thinking is a form of avoidance. It looks away from the biopsy, the grave and the ledger. It lives entirely in the future, because it has nothing behind it but the wisher's need, and so it stakes nothing that anyone could examine. It asks nothing of the person who holds it. Christian hope is a costly expectation fastened to a past event. It looks straight at the grave, the way Abraham "did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, which was as good as dead" (Romans 4:19, ESV). It waits, sometimes for a lifetime. And because it rests on something that either happened or didn't, it can be lost. Paul said so without flinching: "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:19, ESV). A wish never exposes itself like that.

Which leaves the suspicion where it began, at the bedside, and hands it back to everyone in the room. Ask it of the believer, and you'll find plenty of faith that is only a wish in church clothes. Ask it of the church, which has sold the therapeutic god and the national god and edited Bibles to keep people quiet. Then ask it of yourself, in private: which wish have you never audited? I didn't audit mine for years.

Hope doesn't ask anyone at that bedside to stop grieving. Paul told the Thessalonians to grieve, only not "as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13, ESV). The tears stay, and so does the risk. A wish stakes nothing, because it rests on nothing. Christian hope is attached to an execution on a Friday and a report on a Sunday outside Jerusalem, and it has cost those who held it their comfort, their standing and sometimes their lives. That is what the daughter in the hospital hallway is being offered, whether or not she knows it yet: a claim heavy enough to be wrong about. Whether it is wrong is the question she, and the skeptic watching her, and I, have to carry out of that room.
