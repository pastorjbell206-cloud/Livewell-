Most people who go looking for Nehemiah are standing in some kind of rubble. A marriage has come through an affair and both people are still in the house. A family has broken along a divorce, or a death, or a long silence between a parent and a grown child. A faith that once held everything up has collapsed, and what's left is a pile of stones the person used to call certainty. Someone told them Nehemiah was the Bible's book about rebuilding, and they have come to find out whether it is.

It is, though not in the way it's usually sold. For a generation the book has been taught as a leadership manual: the executive who prays, surveys, delegates, handles his critics and finishes the project on schedule. Some of that is true. Nehemiah was a capable man. He inspected the damage at night before announcing anything (Nehemiah 2:12–15), organized the builders by families and towns (Nehemiah 3), and kept guards posted while they worked. But the manual reading has to pass quickly over the months of weeping before the first plan, the families handing over their daughters for debt while the wall went up, and the morning a whole city cried while the Law was read. And it has nothing to say about the last chapter, where the governor returns from a long absence to find nearly every reform undone, and answers with fury.

That ending is the reason the book is worth reading in a ruin. Nehemiah is not a story about a man who fixed something. It is the record of a people who were given their walls back and discovered that walls were the easy part. The stones went up in fifty-two days; the people inside them took the rest of the book and still weren't finished. If you're trying to rebuild something that was broken, and especially if you have already rebuilt it once and watched it slide, that is better news than a success story would be. It means Scripture knows where you are.

## A city in rubble and an empire's permission

The history matters here, because the ruin Nehemiah heard about was older and newer than it looks. Babylon took Jerusalem in 587 BC, burned the temple, broke down the walls and carried the leading families east. Cyrus of Persia took Babylon in 539, and in 538 he allowed the exiles of Judah to go home and rebuild their temple. The Cyrus Cylinder, the clay barrel inscribed after the conquest of Babylon, has the king returning displaced gods to their sanctuaries and their peoples to their homes. Amélie Kuhrt showed in "The Cyrus Cylinder and Achaemenid Imperial Policy" (1983) that this is the stock language of Mesopotamian royal inscriptions, that Assyrian kings before Cyrus had made the same kind of boast, and that the text is no charter of religious freedom. Pierre Briant's history of the Achaemenid empire, *From Cyrus to Alexander* (English translation 2002), shows the pattern at scale: the Persian kings governed a vast empire through cooperative local elites and their sanctuaries, and their tolerance was a method of rule. The returned exiles of Judah received real mercy through that method. They also received a governor, a tax and a leash.

The temple was finished around 516 BC, and when its foundations had first been laid, the old men who remembered Solomon's house wept aloud while the young shouted for joy (Ezra 3:12–13). The walls stayed down. By the time Nehemiah enters the story, in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes I, which is 445 BC, the city had been open to raiders and to contempt for about a hundred and forty years. And the ruin was not only ancient. Ezra 4 records an earlier attempt to rebuild the walls under the same Artaxerxes, stopped by royal order after local officials warned the king that Jerusalem had always been a rebellious city (Ezra 4:7–23). Many commentators read the report Nehemiah received as news of that fresh defeat. The people had tried, and had been made to stop, and the gates had been burned again. When his brother came from Judah and told him that the remnant was "in great trouble and shame" (Nehemiah 1:3, ESV), he was hearing about a second collapse on top of the first.

Anyone who has tried to rebuild a marriage or a family knows the difference that makes. The first ruin you can explain. The second one, the attempt that failed, is the one that teaches you not to try again.

Nehemiah himself was a cupbearer to the king in Susa, one of the great Persian royal residences, a trusted servant who stood close enough to the throne that a wrong expression could be dangerous. Joseph Blenkinsopp, in "The Mission of Udjahorresnet and Those of Ezra and Nehemiah" (*Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1987), set him beside Udjahorresnet, an Egyptian official who served Cambyses and Darius, won the restoration of the goddess Neith's temple in his home city of Sais, and was sent back to Egypt by Darius to restore its scribal institutions. Nehemiah was what the empire used and what God used, and the book never pretends otherwise. Persia had fought a long revolt in Egypt in the 450s, and many historians think a loyal, fortified Judah near the route to Egypt served the king's interest. The king's letters and timber were policy, and Nehemiah still wrote that the king granted them "for the good hand of my God was upon me" (Nehemiah 2:8, ESV).

How Nehemiah relates to Ezra, the priest and scribe whose mission is told first, is one of the knotted questions of Old Testament history. The Hebrew tradition counted Ezra and Nehemiah as one book, and it names the two men side by side on the day the Law is read (Nehemiah 8:9). The traditional order dates Ezra's arrival to the seventh year of Artaxerxes I, 458 BC, thirteen years before Nehemiah (Ezra 7:7–8). Since the late nineteenth century, following the Belgian scholar Albin van Hoonacker, many scholars have argued that Ezra's king was Artaxerxes II, which would bring him to Jerusalem in 398 BC, after Nehemiah's work was done; a third view emends the date to 428. Each view has real evidence behind it. H. G. M. Williamson's Word Biblical Commentary, *Ezra, Nehemiah* (1985), reads the two books in their final form as a single, purposefully edited work, and whatever the order of arrivals, that whole joins the priest who read the Law and the governor who built the wall into one story. It will not let either rebuild alone.

## The prayer before the plan

What Nehemiah did with the news is the first thing the book wants us to see. "As soon as I heard these words I sat down and wept and mourned for days, and I continued fasting and praying before the God of heaven" (Nehemiah 1:4, ESV). Nehemiah 2:1 dates his approach to the king to the month of Nisan, and the report reached him in Kislev (1:1), so the grief and the praying ran for something like four months. That is a long time to carry a ruin before lifting a stone, and the text treats it as the beginning of the rebuilding itself, because what happened in those months was not strategy. It was confession.

The prayer in Nehemiah 1:5–11 is built almost entirely out of Deuteronomy. Nehemiah appeals to the God "who keeps covenant and steadfast love" with those who love him, and he reminds God of the word to Moses that unfaithfulness would mean scattering and return would mean gathering (1:8–9, echoing Deuteronomy 30:1–4). Then he does something that the modern reader, trained to separate what I did from what was done to me, finds almost offensive. He takes the ruin into his own mouth: "confessing the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6, ESV). Nehemiah was, as far as we know, born in exile. He did not burn the temple or worship on the high places, and he was far away in Susa when the last attempt at the walls was stopped. And still he says *we*, and then he says *I*.

A distinction has to be drawn here, because this prayer can be turned into a weapon. Nehemiah is refusing to stand outside the history that made the ruin. There is a difference between blame and belonging. Blame asks who is responsible, and it's a necessary question, especially where one person has wronged another. Belonging asks what I am part of, and what I have added to it, and whether I will stand inside it before God or only point at it from a distance. Nehemiah knows the rebuilding cannot begin with a man who needs to be innocent before he can pray.

That distinction is where the book first touches a broken household. When a marriage has been wounded by one spouse's betrayal, the one who was betrayed does not owe a confession for the other's sin, and anyone who tells them otherwise is not reading Nehemiah. If you are being hurt at home, rebuilding never means enduring abuse, and sometimes the first wall that has to go up is the one between you and the person harming you; the National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233, and the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline answers a call or text to 988. But most ruins in families are not one-sided, and most of us came to the rubble carrying a few stones of our own: the years of silence, the contempt we let harden, the habits we inherited from our fathers' houses, or from a father's absence. Many of us know what it is to inherit a ruin we didn't build. Nehemiah's prayer doesn't let that be the end of the sentence. It says the inherited ruin is still ours to confess, because it is ours to live in.

Lewis Smedes drew a distinction in *Forgive and Forget* (1984) that sits close to this prayer. Forgiving, he argued, is something one person can do alone, in the heart, even when the one who did the harm never asks. Being reunited takes two, and it requires truthfulness, above all from the one who did the harm, because a relationship cannot be rebuilt on a lie about what broke it. Nehemiah's confession is the truth-telling half of that. He will not rebuild the city on a story in which Jerusalem simply had bad luck with Babylon.

Only after the grief does the risk come. When the king noticed his face, Nehemiah writes, "Then I was very much afraid" (Nehemiah 2:2, ESV), since a sad face before the king could be read as disloyalty. Asked what he wanted, he prayed to the God of heaven and then answered (2:4–5). The months of prayer had not made the moment safe. They had made him a man who could stand in it. And when he gathered the officials in Jerusalem, he did not come as a rescuer from the capital. "You see the trouble we are in," he told them (Nehemiah 2:17, ESV), and the pronoun is the same one he used in the prayer.

## Building with one hand

The opposition arrived as soon as the plan did. Three names recur: Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite servant, and Geshem the Arab (Nehemiah 2:10, 19). They are not stock villains. Among the Aramaic papyri from the Jewish garrison at Elephantine, on the Nile, is a letter of 407 BC in which the garrison's leaders tell the Persian governor of Judah that they have also written about their ruined temple to Delaiah and Shelemiah, the sons of Sanballat, governor of Samaria. That Sanballat is very probably Nehemiah's, a generation on, with his sons now handling his affairs. Bezalel Porten's *Archives from Elephantine* (1968) reconstructs that community and its correspondence. The other two are harder to pin down. Tobiah may stand at the head of the Tobiads, a powerful house east of the Jordan in later sources, and the name Tobiah is cut into the rock at 'Araq el-Emir in Jordan, in an inscription whose date is disputed. A silver bowl from Tell el-Maskhuta in the eastern Nile delta, from the late fifth century BC, names Qaynu son of Gashmu as king of Qedar, and many historians take this Gashmu to be Nehemiah's Geshem, though the identification can't be proved. On the likeliest reading, the men who mocked Nehemiah were a provincial governor, a well-connected family bound by marriage and oath to Jerusalem's nobles and related to its high priest (Nehemiah 6:18; 13:4), and an Arab chief with a kingdom behind him, all with reason to prefer a Jerusalem that stayed small.

Their first weapon was contempt. Sanballat stood before the army of Samaria and asked about the builders, "Will they revive the stones out of the heaps of rubbish, and burned ones at that?" (Nehemiah 4:2, ESV). Tobiah added that a fox could knock the wall down. Everyone who has tried to rebuild something has heard that question, most often in their own voice. Burned stones are exactly what a family has after a betrayal or a divorce or a death: the same people, the same memories, the same house, all of it scorched. You don't get new materials. The mockery lands because it's true: you are building with what was ruined.

The builders' answer was not a rebuttal: "So we built the wall. And all the wall was joined together to half its height, for the people had a mind to work" (Nehemiah 4:6, ESV). When contempt turned to the threat of attack, Nehemiah armed the families and put them where the wall was lowest, and he gave them a reason to stand that had nothing to do with civic pride: "Do not be afraid of them. Remember the Lord, who is great and awesome, and fight for your brothers, your sons, your daughters, your wives, and your homes" (Nehemiah 4:14, ESV). "Those who carried burdens were loaded in such a way that each labored on the work with one hand and held his weapon with the other" (Nehemiah 4:17, ESV).

That picture is truer to rebuilding a household than any plan with milestones. A couple trying to recover trust after an affair is building the ordinary structures of a shared life, meals and money and bedtime and church, while guarding against the forces that nearly took it down: the old contacts, the old resentments, the friends who think you're a fool for staying. The wall went up family by family, many of them repairing the section opposite their own houses (3:10, 23, 28–30), and among the builders are Shallum, ruler of half the district of Jerusalem, "he and his daughters" (Nehemiah 3:12, ESV). The rebuilding was done by the people, near their own doors, with their children beside them.

The last attacks were subtler. The enemies invited Nehemiah to a meeting on the plain of Ono, four times, and he answered, "I am doing a great work and I cannot come down" (Nehemiah 6:3, ESV). They circulated a letter accusing him of plotting to be king, and hired a prophet to frighten him into hiding in the temple. Behind all of it Nehemiah saw a single aim: "For they all wanted to frighten us, thinking, 'Their hands will drop from the work, and it will not be done.' But now, O God, strengthen my hands" (Nehemiah 6:9, ESV). The prayer is one line, prayed in the middle of the building. For most people in a ruin that is what prayer looks like: not a quiet hour but a sentence, said with a trowel in your hand.

"So the wall was finished on the twenty-fifth day of the month Elul, in fifty-two days" (Nehemiah 6:15, ESV). The surrounding peoples, the text says, lost their confidence, because they saw that the work had been done with the help of Israel's God (6:16). It is the high point of the book. It comes in chapter 6 of thirteen.

## The rot inside the walls

Chapter 5 interrupts the building story with a crisis that has nothing to do with Sanballat. "Now there arose a great outcry of the people and of their wives against their Jewish brothers" (Nehemiah 5:1, ESV). A famine and the king's tax had driven ordinary families into debt, and the lenders were their own countrymen. People had mortgaged fields, vineyards and houses; some had already handed over their daughters into servitude, "but it is not in our power to help it, for other men have our fields and our vineyards" (Nehemiah 5:5, ESV). The wall was rising on the backs of families being consumed by the people building beside them.

Nehemiah's response was anger, a public assembly and a demand that the fields be returned and the interest dropped. "The thing that you are doing is not good," he told the nobles (Nehemiah 5:9, ESV), and he made them swear it before the priests. He also refused, through twelve years as governor, the food allowance his office entitled him to, because the burden on the people was already heavy (5:14–18). The law behind his demand is treated in the essay on [the Year of Jubilee and debt](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded), and the neighboring question of the laborer's pay in the one on [fair wages](/writing/theology-fair-wages). What matters here is where the chapter sits, in the middle of the building, between the threats of chapter 4 and the finished wall of chapter 6. The book will not let us imagine that the external project and the internal injustice can be handled in sequence.

A household can put its visible structures back in order, the schedule restored, the counseling appointments kept, the photographs back on the wall, while someone inside it is still quietly paying for the recovery. Often it's a child who has become the family's peacemaker, or a spouse carrying all the repair so the other never feels the weight of what happened. The wall goes up, and inside it the same exploitation that caused the collapse goes on in a gentler form. Nehemiah stopped the celebration and called the assembly. Rebuilding that devours its builders is not rebuilding.

Two chapters later the book gives the whole problem one sentence. Once the gates were hung, Nehemiah saw what he had: "The city was wide and large, but the people within it were few, and no houses had been rebuilt" (Nehemiah 7:4, ESV). The walls stood around mostly empty ground. You can restore the boundary of a marriage, set the rules, close the accounts, block the numbers, and find yourself standing inside a perimeter with no home in it yet. The boundary was necessary. It wasn't the house.

## The morning the city wept

What filled the empty city first was not houses but a book. On the first day of the seventh month, the people gathered in the square before the Water Gate and asked Ezra to bring out the Law of Moses. He read it standing on a wooden platform "from early morning until midday, in the presence of the men and the women and those who could understand. And the ears of all the people were attentive to the Book of the Law" (Nehemiah 8:3, ESV). Levites moved among the crowd, and "they read from the book, from the Law of God, clearly, and they gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8, ESV). Whether "gave the sense" included translation into Aramaic is debated. Either way, a people was hearing its own story as if for the first time.

They wept. "For all the people wept as they heard the words of the Law" (Nehemiah 8:9, ESV). They were hearing what they had lost, and why. Then Nehemiah, Ezra and the Levites told them to stop. "Go your way. Eat the fat and drink sweet wine and send portions to anyone who has nothing ready, for this day is holy to our Lord. And do not be grieved, for the joy of the LORD is your strength" (Nehemiah 8:10, ESV). The grief was right, and it would have its day; in chapter 9 the people fast in sackcloth and pray Israel's whole history back to God, confessing, "But you are a God ready to forgive, gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and did not forsake them" (Nehemiah 9:17, ESV). But the first word the returned people heard after their weeping was a command to feast and to feed the ones who had nothing.

When faith collapses, the instinct is either to leave the ruin or to rebuild the old structure exactly as it was, the same certainties, the same answers that failed. The people at the Water Gate did neither. They listened to the text again, slowly, with someone beside them giving the sense, and the listening broke them before it fed them. A faith rebuilt that way is not the faith that fell. It is older and humbler, and it can weep and eat in the same week. The essay on [exile and the welfare of the city](/writing/exile-is-not-the-end) follows this people's long wait; Nehemiah shows what the word sounded like when the wait was over and the promise still looked small.

## The chapter that won't let the book end well

In chapter 10 the people signed a covenant: no marriages outside the community, no trade on the Sabbath, the release of debts every seventh year, the Levites supported and the temple kept. In chapter 13 almost every clause has been broken. Nehemiah had gone back to Artaxerxes in the king's thirty-second year, 433 or 432 BC, and on his return he found that the priest Eliashib had given Tobiah, of all people, a room in the temple courts. The Levites, unpaid, had gone back to their fields. Men were treading winepresses on the Sabbath and merchants from Tyre were selling fish in the city. Nehemiah threw Tobiah's furniture out of the room, shut the gates for the Sabbath, and turned to the marriages. He found men of Judah married to women of Ashdod, Ammon and Moab, half of whose children "could not speak the language of Judah" (Nehemiah 13:24, ESV). "And I confronted them and cursed them and beat some of them and pulled out their hair and made them take oath in the name of God" (Nehemiah 13:25, ESV), an oath that they would give no more of their children in such marriages. Earlier in the chapter, on hearing Deuteronomy's exclusion of Ammonites and Moabites read aloud, the people "separated from Israel all those of foreign descent" (Nehemiah 13:3, ESV).

These verses deserve their strongest defense before any judgment. The community was small, surrounded by larger and richer neighbors, and just back from an exile the prophets had blamed on idolatry. Nehemiah reminds them that foreign wives led even Solomon into sin (13:26). His own argument is about worship, not blood, and so is the law he stands on, which forbids such marriages "for they would turn away your sons from following me, to serve other gods" (Deuteronomy 7:4, ESV). The separation in 13:1–3 leans on a harder text, Deuteronomy 23:3–6, which bars Ammonites and Moabites from the assembly by descent. Still, a people whose children could no longer understand the Scriptures in their own tongue was a people one generation from disappearing into its neighbors' gods. That danger was real. The essay on [marrying outside the faith](/writing/interfaith-marriage) treats the parallel crisis in Ezra 9 and 10 more fully.

But the canon sets other voices beside this chapter. The same Scriptures hold the book of Ruth, in which a Moabite widow tells her Israelite mother-in-law, "Your people shall be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16, ESV), and becomes the great-grandmother of David. Ruth has to be read honestly on both sides. Her Moabite birth is exactly what Deuteronomy 23 names, and her story overturns any reading that makes descent decisive; but it turns on her taking Israel's God as her own, which is the very thing Nehemiah's worry was about. They hold Jonah, whose last verse is God's question to a sulking prophet: "And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city, in which there are more than 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left, and also much cattle?" (Jonah 4:11, ESV). And they hold Isaiah's word, "Let not the foreigner who has joined himself to the LORD say, 'The LORD will surely separate me from his people'" (Isaiah 56:3, ESV), with its promise, "for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isaiah 56:7, ESV). Scholars disagree about when Ruth and Jonah were written and whether they were meant as answers to Ezra and Nehemiah; that can't be settled here. The church received all of them as Scripture, so the reader of Nehemiah 13 is never left alone with it. Chapter 13 is Nehemiah's own account of an embattled reformer's zeal, set down with his prayer that God remember it. It is not the canon's last word on the stranger.

Miroslav Volf's *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996) supplies the distinction that makes sense of this. Volf argues that boundaries in themselves are not sin; a self without boundaries has no self to give. What he calls sin is exclusion, which includes the will to cut the bonds that tie us to the other and keep them out. A wall can serve either purpose. Nehemiah's wall began as protection for the vulnerable, the brothers and daughters and wives and homes of chapter 4. By chapter 13 the community has separated out everyone of foreign descent and its governor is pulling out men's hair. Whether that was the wall the hour required is a question faithful readers answer differently, as they do for Ezra's covenant, and the text won't let us hurry to either verdict. What the canon won't allow is treating it as the whole of God's mind. Every family that rebuilds after a betrayal builds walls, and must. The danger is that the fear that raised the right wall goes on raising walls against the wrong people: the spouse who has truly repented, the child who disappointed, the stranger who doesn't fit the family's new rules for staying safe.

## Remember me, O my God

The book ends on its governor's prayer, the last of several like it: "Remember me, O my God, for good" (Nehemiah 13:31, ESV). It is not a victory speech. It's the prayer of a man whose work would not stay done, who had rebuilt a city and watched its people unbuild the covenant within a few years, and who had nothing left to offer but the request that God not forget him. Anyone who has repaired a marriage and seen it slip, or come back to faith and felt it thin again, knows that prayer.

The walls did not last either. Rome burned Jerusalem in AD 70. Nehemiah had built the wall "that we may no longer suffer derision" (Nehemiah 2:17, ESV), a word the older English versions render *reproach*. Some five hundred years after Nehemiah, the Letter to the Hebrews looked at a crucifixion outside that city's gate and drew the opposite conclusion. "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Hebrews 13:12, ESV). "Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured" (Hebrews 13:13, ESV). "For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Hebrews 13:14, ESV). The wall was rebuilt to take away reproach. The Son of God was put outside the wall and carried it.

The walls a broken home needs are real, and it has to build them: honesty, boundaries, protection for whoever is weakest. Nehemiah was right to raise them, and the Scriptures around him show what a wall becomes when fear keeps raising it. But no wall of ours holds by itself, and the One who rebuilds a ruined people did it by going outside the wall, bearing the reproach the wall was built to keep away. John saw the city that is coming and wrote that "its gates will never be shut by day" (Revelation 21:25, ESV). Nehemiah shut his at dusk to keep the Sabbath merchants out, and he had to. We are not in that city yet. The rubble is still here, and so are we, and the prayer at the end of Nehemiah is still the honest one to carry out of it: remember me, O my God, for good.
