## When faith ends the conversation

The word usually arrives at the end of a conversation rather than the middle of one. You press a question as far as it will go, and somewhere near the bottom a good and sincere person looks at you and says that you just have to have faith. Most of the time what they mean is that the questioning should stop now. And if you're the kind of person who can't make the questioning stop, the sentence doesn't sound like an invitation. It sounds like a door closing, with the interesting part of the room on the other side.

I know how it sounds because I said it to myself for years, and I said it as a verdict. I came to faith from atheism, and before I did, faith was the name I gave to the thing reasonable people had outgrown. The line between us was the line between looking at the evidence and looking away from it, and I knew which side I stood on, and I did not think it was close.

The definition I was working with is the one almost everybody knows. Faith is believing something without evidence, or in spite of it. Reason follows the facts wherever they lead, and faith plants a flag and refuses to move, so that the greatest faith would be believing the most improbable thing on the least support. Stated that way, faith is a flaw of character wearing the clothes of devotion.

That definition is wrong twice over: wrong about what the word has meant in Scripture and in the church's long argument with itself, and wrong about how anyone, the skeptic included, actually comes to know anything. But the objection deserves to be heard at its strongest first, and the church deserves to hear how much of it we earned.

## The objection at full strength

In 1877 the English mathematician W. K. Clifford published an essay called "The Ethics of Belief," and he set down a principle he meant to bind believer and unbeliever alike: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." The word he chose was wrong, a moral failure and not merely a lapse of prudence. He illustrated it with a shipowner who has doubts about an aging emigrant ship, talks himself out of them because repairs would be expensive, and sends the vessel to sea, where it goes down with everyone aboard. Clifford insisted the man would have been just as guilty had the ship made port, because he had no right to a belief he had not earned. Beliefs steer actions that fall on other people, and they train the believer in credulity, which Clifford thought corrupts a whole society's habits of mind.

I found that argument bracing, and I still do. Its heirs made it blunter. Richard Dawkins leaned throughout *The God Delusion* (2006) on the definition of faith as belief held without evidence, a way of holding a conclusion that argument can neither install nor remove, and he argued that the deepest harm lay in teaching children that unquestioning faith is itself a virtue, since a belief that needs no justification can be made to license anything. Peter Boghossian, in *A Manual for Creating Atheists* (2013), proposed that we stop saying "faith" at all and say instead what he thinks the word conceals, pretending to know what one doesn't know. Neither man invented the charge. They inherited it.

The inheritance runs back further than the Victorians, and the history explains why the charge feels so obvious now. When Luther stood at Worms in 1521, Christendom lost its single court of appeal. For more than a century Europeans killed one another over whose tradition spoke for God, until the Peace of Westphalia closed the Thirty Years' War in 1648. A generation later John Locke published *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) and proposed a way out of the bloodshed: let each person regulate belief by evidence and proportion assent to it, and let revelation be accepted only when reason has judged it to be revelation. Nicholas Wolterstorff argues in *John Locke and the Ethics of Belief* (1996) that Locke's epistemology was precisely a response to the fracturing of a shared religious tradition. When you can no longer appeal to what everyone believes, you appeal to what anyone can check. Clifford's rule is Locke's program hardened into a moral law in the shadow of Darwin, and the New Atheism of the last generation is Clifford's rule with a larger audience.

Before a believer answers any of this, he has to concede what is true in it, and much is. A great deal of religious faith really is what the objection says: belief held because a confident man said it from a stage, gripped tighter the more it is contradicted, and called loyalty. The church has too often praised not-thinking, treated the person with questions as a weaker brother rather than a serious one, and used "just have faith" as a lid on a pot it didn't want boiling. In 1994 the historian Mark Noll opened *The Scandal of the Evangelical Mind* with a sentence that indicted his own tradition: "The scandal of the evangelical mind is that there is not much of an evangelical mind." When a skeptic says faith often names an evasion, he isn't slandering us. He's quoting our worst Sundays back to us. I have heard the word do exactly that work from pulpits, and I've felt it do that work in my own mouth.

So the popular definition has two parents: the Enlightenment's argument against religion, and the church's own habit of hiding from hard questions. If the argument stopped there, it would simply be right. But it assumes two things it never examines: what the word has actually meant, and whether the person making the charge is standing where he thinks he is.

## What the word meant before the caricature

The New Testament word translated "faith" is *pistis*, and in the ordinary Greek of the first century it didn't mean a leap into the dark. It meant trust, and it meant faithfulness, the reliability that runs in both directions between two parties who have proved themselves to each other. Teresa Morgan's study *Roman Faith and Christian Faith* (2015) shows how thoroughly *pistis* and its Latin partner *fides* belonged to the language of relationship in the Roman world, the trust that bound friends, households, soldiers and worshippers. Matthew Bates pressed the point further in *Salvation by Allegiance Alone* (2017), arguing that when Paul speaks of saving *pistis* toward Jesus, the word often carries the sense of allegiance or loyalty to him as the enthroned king, a trust that includes mental assent, sworn fidelity and a life lived under his rule. His critics, many of them Reformed and Lutheran exegetes, answer that folding loyalty and obedience into the definition of faith blurs the Reformation's distinction between the faith that receives and the works that follow, and that in Paul *pistis* often reads most naturally as reliance, the empty hand held out, rather than as pledged fidelity. Bates replies that allegiance is a response to grace, not a way to purchase it, and the argument is not settled. But the direction of the research is plain. The word the apostles chose was a word for trusting a person.

The verse most often quoted to prove the opposite proves the point when it is read where it stands. "Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1). The letter's first readers were a congregation under pressure. They had once "joyfully accepted the plundering of your property" (Hebrews 10:34), and now some of them were tempted to drift back from their confession. The writer's plea just before chapter 11 is "Therefore do not throw away your confidence, which has a great reward" (Hebrews 10:35). Chapter 11 is his argument for holding on, and what he offers is history: a long file of people who trusted God, on the ground of what God had already said and done. Of Sarah he writes that she received power to conceive "since she considered him faithful who had promised" (Hebrews 11:11). That clause holds the logic of the chapter: faith reckons the promiser trustworthy and acts on the promise before it is fulfilled. "Things not seen" are things not yet in view, the future and the unseen God, not things disbelieved or contradicted, and the writer's confidence about them rests on what has already been seen. Faith in Hebrews 11 is memory pointed forward.

The rest of the New Testament writes the same way. Luke tells Theophilus he has investigated carefully and written an orderly account "that you may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught" (Luke 1:4). John says the signs in his Gospel "are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (John 20:31). Paul, listing witnesses to the risen Christ, says he appeared "to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive" (1 Corinthians 15:6), which is the sort of thing you write only if you're inviting people to go and ask. Even the story most often used to prove that Christianity prizes not-looking proves the reverse. Thomas won't believe until he sees the wounds, and Jesus gives him what he asks before he says, "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29). The blessing falls on everyone after that room, who will have to trust the testimony of the witnesses rather than touch the hands themselves, which is how all of us hold nearly everything we know about the past.

The church eventually gave this biblical pattern a technical shape. The Lutheran and Reformed teachers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries distinguished three strands in saving faith: *notitia*, knowledge of what God has said and done; *assensus*, assent that it is true; and *fiducia*, personal trust in the God who said and did it. The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 puts the same thing pastorally, describing true faith as both a sure knowledge by which one holds true what God has revealed and a wholehearted trust that the Holy Spirit works through the gospel. Clifford's principle governs *assensus*, the holding of a proposition as true, and on that point the tradition asks for no discount. But it insists that assent alone isn't faith. James observes that the demons believe God is one, and shudder (James 2:19). They have the proposition right and no trust at all. Faith in the full sense is what happens when knowledge and assent become reliance on a person, and reliance on a person is a different act from concluding a proof.

## Nobody lives on proof

Now the second question, the one I never asked myself. Take Clifford at his word and try to believe nothing your evidence doesn't compel. You won't last a morning.

You trust that your reasoning is reliable, but you can't certify reason without using it. You trust that other people have inner lives like yours, though no experiment reaches inside another skull to check. You trust that the future will resemble the past. David Hume saw the size of that one in *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) and couldn't argue his way out of it: every argument from experience that the future will be like the past already assumes that the future will be like the past. We believe it anyway, and stake our lives on it every time we eat, cross a bridge or plan for a child, because we couldn't live otherwise and because it has held.

The dependence on other people runs deeper still. The philosopher C. A. J. Coady argued in *Testimony: A Philosophical Study* (1992) that testimony is a basic source of knowledge, not a second-rate substitute for seeing for yourself. Hume had tried to justify our trust in testimony by our own observation that it usually proves reliable. Coady pointed out that none of us has made anything like the observations that would require. Your birthday, the shape of continents you've never visited, the results of experiments you'll never run, even the meanings of the words on this page all reach you on someone's word. Strip out everything you know only by testimony, and almost nothing is left with which to check the testimony.

Michael Polanyi reached the same conclusion from inside the laboratory. He was a working physical chemist before he turned to philosophy, and in *Personal Knowledge* (1958) he argued that there's no knowing without a committed knower. Science itself runs on apprenticeship, on trust in the authority of a community of inquirers, on skills and judgments the scientist holds and cannot fully put into words. He called for what he named a fiduciary programme, the recovery of belief as a source of knowledge, and he reached back explicitly to Augustine, and to the line from Isaiah that Augustine made his watchword, to name it. Remove it in the name of Clifford's rule and you arrive at paralysis, not pure reason.

There is a sharper point here, and philosophers have made it against Clifford for a long time. On what sufficient evidence does anyone believe that it is wrong always, everywhere and for anyone to believe on insufficient evidence? The principle isn't self-evident, and no experiment confirms it. By its own standard it shouldn't be believed. A rule that fails its own test is one more commitment held for reasons, like every other, and has no claim to be the neutral ground of all reasoning.

This is the thing I hadn't seen, and it is said against myself. For years I accused believers of the posture I was standing in. I called my own position pure reason, and it wasn't. It was a structure of enormous trust: trust in my senses and my logic, trust in the order of a universe I had inspected a vanishingly small corner of, trust in a hundred authorities whose experiments I had never repeated. I believed almost everything I believed on someone else's word. I had simply never counted it, because trust is invisible to the one doing the trusting. What I had wasn't the absence of faith. It was faith I had refused to notice and so never had to defend.

None of this makes credulity respectable. It changes the question. If everyone lives by trust, the question was never whether to trust but whom, and on what grounds.

## How the question has been answered

Framed that way, the question has had serious answers, and they don't all agree.

The first came from outside orthodoxy. In 1896 the Harvard philosopher William James answered Clifford directly in a lecture called "The Will to Believe." He granted that where evidence can settle a question, we should wait for it. But some choices, he argued, are genuine options: they're live, because both possibilities are real to us; forced, because declining to choose is itself a choice; and momentous, because the stakes are high and the opportunity may not return. Where such an option can't be settled on intellectual grounds, James said, our passional nature may lawfully decide, and in fact must. He also noticed that Clifford's rule is itself a passional choice, a temperament that fears being duped more than it hopes for truth, and that a rule which would keep us from recognizing certain truths even if they were real is not obviously the rational one. Some truths, he added, come only to those who first extend trust, as friendship comes only to the person willing to risk being a friend. Its weakness, pressed by critics ever since, is that taken loosely it can license believing whatever a person wants to be true. James was no orthodox Christian, and the church can't simply adopt him. But he broke the spell of Clifford's rule.

The second answer accepts Clifford's standard and argues that Christian belief meets it. This is evidentialist apologetics, and its most rigorous modern voice is the Oxford philosopher Richard Swinburne. In *The Existence of God* (1979) he built a cumulative case in the language of probability: an ordered universe, conscious persons, moral awareness and religious experience, taken together, make theism more probable than not. The instinct is the apostles' own. In Thessalonica, Paul "reasoned with them from the Scriptures" (Acts 17:2). Its strength is that it concedes nothing to the charge of evasion. Its weakness is that it can make the faith of an ordinary believer who has never weighed a probability look irrational, which seems to prove too much.

The third answer rejects Clifford's standard as a universal rule. This is Reformed epistemology, and its founders were Alvin Plantinga and Nicholas Wolterstorff, who with others made the case in *Faith and Rationality* (1983). They argued that the demand for evidence rests on a picture of knowledge called classical foundationalism, in which a belief is rational only if it is self-evident, incorrigible or evident to the senses, or else inferred from beliefs that are. That picture fails its own test, since the principle itself is none of those things. Wolterstorff pressed the point that our beliefs are, as it were, innocent until proven guilty: we are entitled to them unless we have good reason to give them up. Plantinga completed the argument in *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000). Some beliefs are properly basic, held rationally without being inferred from arguments: that other people have minds, that I had breakfast this morning. Plantinga argued that belief in God can be properly basic in the same way, formed by a faculty Calvin called the sense of divinity, and that belief in the great things of the gospel can arise just as directly through what Plantinga, following Aquinas and Calvin, called the internal instigation of the Holy Spirit. A properly basic belief isn't groundless. It's grounded in experience without being derived from argument, and it can be defeated if a real objection goes unanswered.

Plantinga's most important move is easy to miss. He argued that the question of whether Christian belief is rational can't be separated from whether it is true. If Christianity is false, belief in it probably lacks warrant. If it is true, if there is a God who made human minds to know him, then belief in him very likely has warrant. So the skeptic can't dismiss faith as irrational without first showing it false, and the charge of irrationality turns out to be the real argument in disguise rather than a shortcut around it.

Faithful Christians disagree about which path is better, a question of method rather than of the faith itself. The evidentialist worries that Reformed epistemology leaves the skeptic nothing to examine; the Reformed epistemologist worries that evidentialism makes the grandmother's faith hostage to the philosopher's calculations. Both agree on the one thing the popular definition denies: faith is not believing without grounds.

## Faith seeking understanding

The older tradition had known this long before the Enlightenment asked. Augustine, writing at the turn of the fifth century, returned again and again to a line from Isaiah as his Latin Bible rendered it: unless you believe, you will not understand (Isaiah 7:9). The Hebrew actually makes a play on the word for firmness, which the ESV preserves: "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all." Augustine's reading still caught something true. In his sermons on John he taught that understanding is the reward of faith, so that one should not seek to understand in order to believe, but believe in order to understand. He meant that some things are seen only from inside a trust already extended, and he never meant that belief replaces thought.

Anselm of Canterbury gave the principle its most famous form. His *Proslogion* (1077-1078) was first titled *Fides quaerens intellectum*, faith seeking understanding, as he explains in its preface, and in its opening chapter he prays, *Neque enim quaero intelligere ut credam, sed credo ut intelligam*: I do not seek to understand in order to believe, but I believe in order to understand. The formula *credo ut intelligam* is Anselm's phrasing, though the principle was Augustine's first. What follows is the ontological argument, among the most disputed pieces of reasoning in philosophy. For Anselm, faith was where inquiry began.

Thomas Aquinas, two centuries later, drew the lines precisely. In the *Summa Theologiae* he distinguished the preambles of faith from the articles of faith. The preambles, such as that God exists and is one, can be known by natural reason, and faith presupposes them. The articles, such as the Trinity and the Incarnation, exceed what reason could discover alone but do not contradict it. Aquinas held that truth cannot contradict truth, so that an argument against the faith must contain an error reason itself can find. He wrote thousands of pages of the most rigorous argument the medieval world produced, because he took for granted that faith and reason work the same ground.

Blaise Pascal is usually recruited for the other side, and usually misread. In the *Pensées* (1670), "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know" (Brunschvicg 277, Lafuma 423) was never a license for sentiment. The next fragment says that it is the heart, not reason, that perceives God, and another (Brunschvicg 282, Lafuma 110) explains what Pascal meant by the word: the faculty by which we know first principles directly, that there is space, time, motion and number, things we know with certainty and cannot demonstrate. The heart in Pascal is a way of knowing, not a mood. His point is Polanyi's and Plantinga's, three centuries early: reasoning rests on knowledge that reasoning didn't produce. The wager is misread the same way. Pascal addressed it to a person who has concluded that reason can't settle the question of God, and argued that such a person is already committed, since declining to choose is itself a choice with a life at stake. Given a finite stake and an infinite possible good, the prudent course is to seek God, and Pascal counsels the seeker to begin living as believers live, to quiet the passions that hinder belief. The wager has real problems, not least that it doesn't say which God to wager on. But Pascal meant it as one fragment of a larger apology built on history, prophecy and the person of Christ, and never as a proof or a substitute for evidence.

Kierkegaard is the hardest case, and the one most often flattened. "Leap of faith" is the phrase usually pinned on him, but what he described under the word leap was never a jump in the dark against the evidence. In *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846), written under the pseudonym Johannes Climacus, he took up Gotthold Lessing's complaint of 1777 that the accidental truths of history can never prove the necessary truths of reason, an ugly broad ditch Lessing said he couldn't cross however earnestly he tried to leap. The image of the leap came partly from Lessing. Kierkegaard's reply was that historical inquiry, however careful, yields only approximation, and no approximation can by itself become the unconditional commitment of a whole life. The leap is the passage from considering Christ to belonging to him. And Kierkegaard does push further than this essay would. He held that God in time is the absolute paradox, which the understanding cannot master and at which it may take offense, and he was suspicious of apologetic arguments as a way of avoiding decision. But his target was the Danish Christendom of his day, a nation that assented to the creed by birth and had never once staked its life on it. His warning was that evidence, even sufficient evidence, never makes the decision for you, which is a long way from urging anyone to believe against it.

## Trust with a track record

Everything turns, in the end, on one distinction. Believing without evidence is one thing. Trusting a person for good reasons, beyond what can be proved, is another, and the popular definition has confused them.

Anyone who has married knows the second kind. The reasons to trust a spouse are real, years of kept promises and loyalty tested, but they never amount to a proof. You commit anyway, because some things can only be known by committing to them. You can't learn whether a friendship will hold by examining it from outside forever; at some point you extend trust, and only then does the deeper evidence arrive. To wait for certainty before trusting anyone is the recipe for a life in which no one is ever married, trusted or loved. Faith in God is not less than this. It rests on reasons: the testimony of witnesses who had much to lose by giving it, and an empty tomb that, Matthew reports, the church's earliest opponents tried to explain rather than deny (Matthew 28:11-15). And it goes beyond the reasons in exactly the way trust in any person does, because its object is someone who speaks and promises and can be relied on.

The tradition adds something the skeptic won't grant and the believer mustn't forget. This trust is also a gift. Paul places the whole of salvation, the faith by which it is received included, under grace: "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8). Reasons clear the ground, and the apostles spent their lives giving them. But trust in God is finally God's work in a person, which is why arguments alone have never made a Christian.

If faith is grounded trust, it carries obligations, and they fall hardest on believers. A faith that refuses to examine its grounds, treats every hard question as an attack, and prefers the warmth of certainty to the labor of thinking has betrayed its own nature. It's weaker for being unexamined, and it knows it, which is why it's afraid. Paul's instruction to a young church was "test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Testing comes first.

The skeptic carries a question too, one no one can answer on his behalf. Everyone trusts something he can't stand outside of and prove. The atheist trusts that the universe is intelligible and that his mind is fitted to read it, a large and hopeful belief about a cosmos that his own account says wasn't made to be understood by anyone. The Christian trusts that the intelligibility is there because a mind is behind it, and that this mind has spoken in a person. Neither of us is keeping his hands clean of belief. We are trusting different things, and the grown-up question is which of them can bear the weight.

Christians stake their answer on something specific. The one they trust is a man who said he would lay down his life and take it up again, and who, his witnesses said, did. Faith in him is a wager of the whole self on what happened in Jerusalem, made by people who don't yet see him on the testimony of those who did. The New Testament doesn't pretend the wager is easy. The most honest prayer in the Gospels comes from a father whose son no one has been able to help, who cries out to Jesus, "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24). He doesn't pretend to a certainty he lacks, and Jesus doesn't demand one before he heals the boy. Belief and unbelief sit in the same breath, and the man reaches anyway.

That is not the enemy of reason. It's what every one of us is already doing, trusting what we can't prove and hoping it holds. What remains is whether the one we're trusting has ever been known to break his word, and what it would cost us to find out.
