Start where the conviction is strongest, because the version worth examining is the strong one. Is social justice biblical? It isn't cargo the left smuggled into the gospel while no one was looking. It's the gospel's own freight. Justice and righteousness are declared at Sinai, sung in the Psalms and thundered by the prophets, and they are embodied by a Galilean who stood up in the synagogue at Nazareth, read Isaiah's promise of good news to the poor and liberty to the captives, rolled up the scroll and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21, ESV). The American church has cared too little about the poor, the wrongly imprisoned, the family at the border, the names we learned only after the cameras arrived. When historians weigh us they won't be kind, and they shouldn't be. A Christianity with no passion for justice is no more spiritual for the lack; it's a lie that has learned to fold its hands.

Hold all of that. It's true, and nothing below walks it back.

But there's a distinction the church keeps losing, and everything turns on it. Justice can be the fruit of the gospel, or it can become a gospel. As fruit, it grows from grace received: we do justice because God has been just and merciful to us, and our justice carries that mercy in it. As a gospel, it becomes the thing that saves: the diagnosis of what's wrong with the world, the account of who's to blame, the path to redemption, the community of the redeemed. The two look nearly identical from the outside. They use the same words, march in the same streets, and care about the same victims. What separates them is what happens to the enemy. A justice that grows from the gospel keeps mercy for the people it opposes. A justice that has become its own gospel can't, because mercy would release the villain, and the villain is holding up the building.

The most dangerous idols are always cut from the best material. Nobody bows to what they despise. The golden calf at Sinai was not built by people who hated God; it was built by people who couldn't see him fast enough and made a god they could. When the calf was finished, they said, "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!" (Exodus 32:4, ESV), and Aaron built an altar and announced, "Tomorrow shall be a feast to the LORD" (Exodus 32:5, ESV). They worshiped the idol under God's own name, as the God of the exodus, the God who frees slaves. That's what made it deadly. It was a liberation god, and it was made of gold.

## The case at full strength

The progressive Christian's case deserves to be heard in the form its best defenders would sign before it's questioned.

It goes like this. The gospel is not only about individual souls going to heaven. Jesus announced a kingdom, and a kingdom is a social reality: it has an economy, a politics, a way of treating the weak. When the church says "just preach the gospel" and stay out of justice, it has usually meant "leave our arrangements alone," and the arrangements have usually favored the people saying it. White American churches told Black Christians for generations that the gospel was spiritual and that segregation was a political question. Churches that were loudly silent about poverty were rarely silent about the sins of the poor. To insist that justice is central to the Christian life is not to replace the gospel but to recover the part the comfortable church cut out. And those who work for justice are often the ones taking the gospel most seriously, while the rest of us sing about a kingdom we have no intention of letting interfere with our week.

I agree with most of that, and I have to say where I stand in it. I'm a pastor in an American church, and pastors like me have preached the comfortable version, kept quiet about things we should have named because naming them would cost us, and called the silence prudence. The conservative church, my own kind of church, has no standing to lecture anyone about turning a good thing into an idol, because it spent much of the last century turning a privatized piety into one. Isaiah buried that idol long before anyone reading this was born. People who fasted and asked why God ignored them got their answer: "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke? Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house" (Isaiah 58:6–7, ESV). A faith that won't do justice has already built its calf.

So the question here isn't whether Christians should care about justice. It's what happens when justice, cut loose from the rest of the faith, begins to grow a faith of its own.

## A gospel has four parts

Watch the substitution, because it's quiet.

Every gospel has four parts: a diagnosis of what's wrong, an account of who's to blame, a way of salvation, and a people who carry that salvation forward. The Christian gospel says the wrong is sin, which runs through every human heart including mine; the one to blame is each of us, together and alone; the salvation is a crucified and risen Lord; and the people are the church, a community of forgiven sinners. Now take justice, cut it loose from Christ, and let it stand by itself. It doesn't go quiet. It grows its own four parts almost at once. The wrong becomes injustice. The one to blame becomes a class of people, and notice that it's never me; it's always someone outside the circle. The salvation becomes the correct politics. The people become those who see clearly.

Line those up beside the Christian four and they rhyme. There's a fall: the world splits into the wronged and the wrongdoers, and the split is the most real thing about it. There's an election: a people who have seen the split and carry knowledge the others lack. There's something like atonement: the wrong is paid down, not by mercy but by exposure, the guilty named and diminished until the ledger feels even. And there's an end: the world set right by our hands, because no one else is coming to set it right. It's the same architecture with the cross removed from the center and a tribunal moved in. It's a complete gospel, missing exactly one thing. It has no grace. And a gospel with no mercy for its enemies isn't a gospel. It's a court.

I've sat in that court, and I have prosecuted from its bench. There's a particular pleasure in it, a righteous anger that costs nothing and flatters you while it burns someone else. The pleasure is most of why people stay. And the pleasure isn't limited to one side. The conservative church has its own court, with a different list of defendants, and it convenes just as eagerly.

## The experiment the church already ran

This has happened before, and the clearest record of how it goes is just over a century old.

In 1917 Walter Rauschenbusch, a Baptist who had pastored a German immigrant congregation on the edge of New York's Hell's Kitchen and watched poverty kill his parishioners, published *A Theology for the Social Gospel*. He had earned the right to be heard. He had seen an industrial economy grind human beings into profit and call it progress, and he had read his Bible and found that God was not neutral about it. He saw that the prophets were no chaplains to the powerful, and that the kingdom Jesus preached was not a private arrangement between a soul and its Savior but a claim on the whole social order. It's only fair to add that Rauschenbusch himself never abandoned personal conversion or the reality of personal sin; his book has a whole account of what he called the kingdom of evil, sin woven into institutions and handed down. He kept something the church around him had nearly lost: the public weight of the gospel.

But over the generation that followed, much of the movement he inspired drifted in a direction he hadn't intended. As the kingdom moved from a gift God gives to a society we build, sin slowly came to seem less like a condition of the human heart and more like a feature of unjust systems. The cross began to function less as the place where God bears the guilt of the world and more as a moral example meant to inspire us to build. And once the cross is only an example, grace has nothing left to do. You don't need to be forgiven by a program of social reconstruction. You need to be useful to it, or out of its way.

Reinhold Niebuhr was raised in that world and loved it, and after 1932 he spent much of his career arguing against his own teachers. In *Moral Man and Immoral Society* (1932) and the books that followed, he said that their doctrine of sin was too shallow to account for what human beings actually do, that they had mistaken the goodwill of comfortable people for the will of God, and that even our justice is shot through with self-interest we can't see. He wasn't abandoning their passion for justice. He was giving it back its only honest foundation: the line of evil runs through the reformer too. Each generation since has been tempted to repeat the experiment, each sure it's the first to discover that God cares about the poor, each surprised when its justice turns hard.

## What a justice-gospel cannot do

You can tell when justice has become a gospel by what it loses the ability to do.

It can't forgive. The Christian gospel runs on forgiveness. At its center stands a man praying for the soldiers who are driving the nails: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34, ESV). But a justice that has become its own gospel has no place for forgiveness, because forgiveness would release the villain, and the villain holds the structure up. That's why such movements so often need new enemies, why the circle of the unforgivable keeps widening, why yesterday's ally can be named today's traitor. Movements that consume their own aren't always corrupt. Sometimes they're just consistent. A gospel without grace has to keep finding sinners, and when it runs out of obvious ones it turns on the faithful.

It can't rest. The Sabbath is one of the most radical economic commands in the Hebrew Scriptures, and in Deuteronomy it is grounded in exactly the memory of injustice: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. Therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15, ESV). The rest is itself an act of justice, a weekly refusal of Pharaoh's economy. But a justice that is the gospel can't stop, because if it stops, injustice wins, and there's no God holding the world while you sleep. So the most conscientious burn out, and the burnout is read as a failure of commitment rather than what it is: the collapse of a creature trying to be the savior. The church should have handed those people a Sabbath and a Savior.

It can't leave vengeance to God. Peter, writing to Christians who were already being slandered and mistreated, holds up Jesus in his passion: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23, ESV). We tend to hear that as a restraint and miss that it's a relief. Jesus did not surrender justice on the way to the cross. He handed it to the one Judge who could be trusted with it. Near the end of *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996), the Croatian theologian Miroslav Volf makes an argument that surprises readers who expected a gentler theology: that our ability to renounce violence and revenge depends on trusting that God will judge justly. Take away the final Judge and the whole burden of setting things right falls on us, and people who must settle every account themselves will not be merciful. The doctrine of God's judgment, so often treated as the harsh part of the faith, turns out to be what makes human mercy possible.

And it can't extend mercy to the dead. A justice rooted in the gospel can look at the slaveholder, the colonizer, the complicit grandmother, and say the true and terrible thing about what they did, while remembering that they too stood under a judgment they couldn't survive without mercy, the same mercy I'll need. A justice that has become its own gospel can only condemn the dead, because the dead can't repent and so can't complicate the story. We're very brave toward people who can't answer back.

## Why a good thing makes a convincing god

Timothy Keller, drawing on a long line of the church's teachers, put the mechanism in plain terms in *Counterfeit Gods* (2009). An idol, he argued, is usually a good thing that has been made into an ultimate thing. Justice is a very high good. That's exactly why it makes such a convincing god. No one is tempted to worship something they know is beneath them. We're tempted by the things just below God, things so good that loving them ultimately feels like loving him. One of the ways he suggested finding your own is to look at your most uncontrollable emotions: the places where you're disproportionately angry, anxious, or desperate when something is threatened. Try that on a room of progressive Christians, or on yourself, and don't question the justice. Question only its standing as the center of everything, by suggesting that the cause is good but is not Christ. If panic rises, the panic is information. The thing you can't bear to have questioned is the thing you've made into a god. Then try it on a room of conservative Christians by questioning the nation, or the family, or the party. The panic rises there too. The idols differ; the test doesn't.

Alasdair MacIntyre's *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988) explains why justice cut loose from its root becomes so loud. The book's title is its argument. There is no neutral, tradition-free concept of justice that everyone shares, MacIntyre contended; there are rival accounts, each at home inside a larger tradition of what human beings are and what they're for. Take the word "justice" out of the tradition that gave it meaning and you keep the vocabulary while losing the grammar. What's left is a set of powerful words used as weapons in a contest that no shared ground can settle, so it's settled by whoever can press hardest. That's what our public arguments about justice often sound like, on every side. Take the cross out of the center and you don't get a calmer justice. You get a louder one.

## Justice downstream of the cross

The way back isn't to care less. The way back is to put justice where it belongs: downstream of the cross, not upstream of it. Justice as obedience, not salvation. Justice as the fruit, never the root.

The prophet Micah gave the whole shape of it in a single verse: "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8, ESV). There are three clauses, and the last two aren't decoration. They're the leash on the first. Love kindness, the covenant loyalty God shows to the undeserving, toward the very people justice is tempted to consume. Walk humbly, knowing the prosecutor is also a defendant. Pull any one of the three out and the other two go wild. The progressive church is tempted to keep the first and lose the last two. The conservative church is tempted to keep a counterfeit of the third and lose the first. Micah won't let anyone choose.

There are models of justice that stayed downstream, and they are not distant ones. Martin Luther King Jr., in *Stride Toward Freedom* (1958), his account of the Montgomery bus boycott, described nonviolence as a method that does not seek to defeat or humiliate the opponent, but to win his friendship and understanding. The goal was not victory over enemies but what King called the beloved community, a reconciliation in which the oppressor was also set free. King didn't soften the demand for justice by an inch; he went to jail for it and was killed for it. But he refused to let the struggle become a gospel that needed its enemies, because his gospel already had a cross in it. "But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, ESV) was the engine of his movement, not a brake on it.

A generation later, South Africa tried something similar on a national scale. After the end of apartheid, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, established by an act of parliament in 1995 and holding hearings from 1996, offered amnesty to perpetrators who gave full public testimony of their crimes. Desmond Tutu, who chaired it, defended it in *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999) as a form of restorative justice, rooted in a Christian account of the person and of forgiveness. It was not perfect, and many victims felt, with reason, that the reparations promised them never fully came. Honesty requires saying that. But it showed that a nation could tell the full truth about monstrous injustice without either burying the truth or making vengeance its salvation. Paul had named the pattern long before: "that is, in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them, and entrusting to us the message of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:19, ESV).

Paul also measured the whole matter once, and the measure is unbearable: "If I give away all I have, and if I deliver up my body to be burned, but have not love, I gain nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:3, ESV). It's aimed straight at the activist heart, and at the heart of the defender of tradition too. You can give everything, be consumed for the cause, and end with nothing if the engine wasn't love. And the love Paul means has a description: "Love is patient and kind; love does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant or rude. It does not insist on its own way; it is not irritable or resentful" (1 Corinthians 13:4–5, ESV). That's a precise inventory of everything a justice turned gospel can't do. It insists on its own way. It keeps the record. It's resentful by design, because resentment is its fuel.

So I'll keep doing justice. The text gives me no other option. But I'm trying to do it as a beggar and not as a judge, with open hands instead of a pointing one, knowing that the line between the oppressor and the oppressed doesn't run neatly between the parties. It runs through my own chest.

There's a justice that flows from the gospel like water from a struck rock, and there's a justice that has quietly replaced the rock. They look the same from the outside. What tells them apart is what happens when the cause is threatened, and what happens to the enemy when the cause wins. The first kind grieves, and keeps a place at the table for the one it opposed. The second reaches for a stone. I've held the stone. I've held it with a cause I was proud of and a verdict ready on my lips, and the person I most needed mercy for was never the one I was aiming at. The man on the cross prayed for the soldiers before they had asked for anything. That prayer is where every Christian justice has to begin, and it's the prayer that, sooner or later, every one of us will need someone to pray for us.
