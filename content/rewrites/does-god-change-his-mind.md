The objection usually arrives as a handful of verses laid side by side. Numbers says God is not a man, that he should change his mind. Jonah says God saw Nineveh turn from its evil and relented of the disaster he had threatened. Genesis says the LORD regretted that he had made man at all. Put them together and the skeptic seems to have caught a contradiction in the act, while a good many believers quietly turn the page. I came to faith out of atheism, and for years I read the Bible the way a lawyer reads a hostile witness, hunting for the place where the story trips over itself. A God who repents looked like an obvious trip. It took me a long time to notice that I'd been reading the seam backward.

The question underneath the verses isn't academic. If God can't be moved, why pray? If he can be moved, how can he be trusted? Those are the questions of someone awake at three in the morning after the diagnosis or the funeral, wondering whether anyone is steering and whether the one steering can hear.

This essay argues that God does not change his mind the way we change ours, because he lacks nothing a change could supply, and that the texts in which he relents are among the clearest expressions of his constancy. That is the historic reading of the church, and it is where this essay lands. But the question has a shape worth seeing first. That God is unchangeable in some real sense belongs to the creeds: the council at Nicaea in 325 condemned anyone who called the Son of God changeable or alterable, and the council at Chalcedon in 451 confessed that the Word became flesh without change. How precisely to state that unchangeableness (whether God stands outside time, whether he can suffer, whether he knows the choices we haven't made yet) is a question on which Christians who confess the same creeds have disagreed. The first is the floor. The second is the house built on it.

## The texts that will not sit still

Start with the verses that say God does not change.

"God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind. Has he said, and will he not do it? Or has he spoken, and will he not fulfill it?" (Numbers 23:19). The speaker is Balaam, a seer hired by Balak, king of Moab, to curse Israel. When the oracle comes out as a blessing, Balak moves him to another hilltop and tries again, as though a new angle might produce a different God. Balaam's answer is that God can't be shopped. His blessing on Israel is not for sale.

Malachi's version comes to a people who have cheated God in their offerings and wearied him with their words: "For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed" (Malachi 3:6). Their survival rests on his constancy, not theirs. James reaches for the sky, where every good gift comes down "from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (James 1:17). The lights move and throw shifting shadows. Their maker doesn't.

Then turn to the other set. "And the LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart" (Genesis 6:6). After Moses stood in the breach over the golden calf, "the LORD relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people" (Exodus 32:14). And at Nineveh: "When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them, and he did not do it" (Jonah 3:10).

The word doing most of the work in the second set is the Hebrew *niḥam*. Its range runs from regret to relenting to being comforted, and in another form the same root opens Isaiah's great consolation, "Comfort, comfort my people" (Isaiah 40:1). It names a turning of the inward disposition toward someone. What should stop us is that the same root sits on both sides of the ledger. Numbers 23:19 says God is not a son of man that he should *niḥam*. Genesis 6:6 says the LORD did.

Nowhere is this sharper than in 1 Samuel 15, where the tension is packed into a single chapter by a single narrator. Early on the LORD says to Samuel, "I regret that I have made Saul king, for he has turned back from following me and has not performed my commandments" (1 Samuel 15:11). At the end the narrator repeats it in his own voice: "And the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel" (1 Samuel 15:35). Between the two, Samuel tells Saul, "And also the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret" (1 Samuel 15:29).

A writer who put those three verses in one chapter could count. He was answering two different questions. Verse 29 comes as Saul, caught in disobedience, seizes Samuel's robe and pleads against the verdict that the kingdom will be torn from him. His confession has the right words, but what he asks next is to be honored before the elders, and he treats God as a judge who might be talked out of a ruling if the defendant says the right things. Samuel's answer is that God can't be flattered or pressured into unsaying what he has said. Verses 11 and 35 say something else: God's relation to Saul has changed because Saul has changed, and God grieves over what the king he anointed has become.

Hosea gives the same logic a surprising turn. After the long indictment of Israel, the LORD says, "I will not execute my burning anger; I will not again destroy Ephraim; for I am God and not a man, the Holy One in your midst, and I will not come in wrath" (Hosea 11:9). "Not a man" in Numbers means God can't be bought. "Not a man" in Hosea means God won't give up. In both, the difference between God and us is the ground of his faithfulness. Read carefully, the two sets of texts make one claim from two sides: God is faithful to what he has said, and what he has said includes how he will answer those who turn.

## How the church learned to read a God who relents

The church didn't discover this tension in the age of the skeptic. It was reading these verses aloud in the fourth century, and the answer it reached is more careful than its critics usually allow.

Augustine prays near the opening of the *Confessions* (c. 397–400) to a God who is unchangeable and yet changes all things, and he names a distinction that carries much of what follows: God changes his works without changing his counsel. What God does in time really does change. He speaks and is silent, wounds and heals, threatens and spares. The purpose all of it flows from does not. In *The City of God* (413–426) he takes up Genesis 6:6 directly, arguing that God's anger does not disturb his mind and his repentance does not overturn his plans; Scripture uses such words because they are fitted to move human hearers.

Boethius, writing *The Consolation of Philosophy* (c. 524) in prison while he awaited execution, supplied the piece many readers still find clarifying. He defined eternity as the complete and simultaneous possession of endless life. We live along a line, the past gone and the future not yet arrived. God does not stand somewhere on that line waiting to find out what happens next. He does not learn on the fortieth day what Nineveh will do. He holds the warning, the sackcloth, and the sparing in one undivided present, and the change is real, but it is in Nineveh, which moves, and not in God.

Anselm, in the *Proslogion* (1077–1078), turned the same instinct toward mercy. He asked how God can be compassionate if he is not moved by suffering, and answered that God is compassionate in what we experience, not in what he undergoes. That can sound cold. Anselm meant it as good news: the mercy that reaches us is not a mood that the right sorrow can trigger and a hard day can exhaust.

Thomas Aquinas gave the tradition its most exact form. In the *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1274) he argues that God is pure act, with no unrealized potential, and that change is the movement from potential to actual. A seed changes into an oak because it lacks the oak. God lacks nothing, so there is nothing for him to gain by changing and nothing he could lose. On the will of God he draws a distinction worth memorizing: it is one thing to change one's will, and another to will that certain things change. God wills from eternity that Nineveh be warned, that Nineveh repent, and that repentant Nineveh be spared. The sequence is real, and his will for the whole of it is one.

John Calvin came at the same texts as a preacher and gave them the image most readers remember. In the *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1559), discussing how Scripture speaks of God (I.13.1), he says in effect that God lisps to us as nurses do with small children, stooping to our capacity because we could neither bear nor understand him speaking at his own height. Later in the first book he takes up God's repentance directly and treats it as a description of God's dealings as they appear to us, while his counsel stays fixed. Calvin's point was not that the relenting texts are false. They are true the way a parent's simple words to a child are true: adequate for the child's life, and never the whole of what the parent knows.

The Reformation confessions, across several traditions, wrote this into their doctrine of God. The Belgic Confession (1561) calls God immutable in its first article. The Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles (1563) open with a God "without body, parts, or passions." The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) describes him as "without body, parts, or passions; immutable," and Congregationalists at Savoy (1658) and Particular Baptists in the Second London Confession (1689) carried the same language into their own confessions. Stated at the strength its defenders would sign, the classical position does not say God is inert, indifferent, or frozen. It says he is so completely alive that nothing can be added to his life, so perfectly loving that his love can't be increased by our worthiness or diminished by our failure, and so fully in possession of his purpose that no event can surprise it. The texts of relenting are true descriptions of his dealings with creatures in time, given in human words because those are the only words we have.

## The strongest case for a God who changes

The classical reading has had serious critics for a century, and the best of them weren't trying to shrink God. They were trying to take the Bible at its word.

Abraham Joshua Heschel argued in *The Prophets* (1962) that the God of Israel's prophets is a God of pathos, moved and affected by what his people do, wounded by betrayal and delighted by faithfulness. He set this deliberately against the unmoved mover of Greek philosophy: a theology that explains away God's anguish has explained away the prophets. The Old Testament scholar Terence Fretheim pressed the case inside Christian exegesis in *The Suffering of God* (1984). The metaphors Scripture uses for God, he argued, depict reality rather than decorate an abstract essence, and a God who has entered genuine relationship with creatures has opened himself to being affected by them.

Among evangelicals this took a sharper form. In *The Openness of God* (1994), Clark Pinnock, Richard Rice, John Sanders, William Hasker, and David Basinger argued that the classical doctrine owed more to Greek metaphysics than to Scripture, and that a timeless, impassible, exhaustively foreknowing God cannot have the relationship the Bible describes. Their proposal, open theism, holds that the future free choices of creatures do not yet exist to be known. God knows everything knowable, every possibility and every way he might respond. What he does not know is which possibility a free creature will choose, because until the choice there is nothing there to know.

Gregory Boyd carried the case to a wider readership in *God of the Possible* (2000), arguing that the future is partly settled, wherever God has determined to act, and partly open, wherever he has left room for creatures to decide. He pointed to the texts the classical view reads as accommodation. After the binding of Isaac, God says, "for now I know that you fear God" (Genesis 22:12). In Jeremiah the LORD says he thought Israel would return to him, and she did not. Let these texts mean what they appear to mean, Boyd pleaded, and he added a pastoral argument: on the open view, the sufferer is not left with a God who foresaw every atrocity from eternity and made the world anyway. At its strongest, then, open theism claims to be more biblical, to give prayer real effect, and to let love be love. Its defenders affirm the Trinity, the deity of Christ, the bodily resurrection, and the authority of Scripture.

Many of their fellow evangelicals disagreed. The Evangelical Theological Society, whose members affirm the inerrancy of Scripture, had adopted a resolution in 2001 affirming God's complete knowledge of future events, including the free decisions of creatures. At its 2003 annual meeting the Society voted on charges that the views of Pinnock and Sanders were incompatible with its affirmation of inerrancy. Removal required a two-thirds majority, and neither man was removed. The vote settled nothing, but it showed where the fault line ran: over whether the God who speaks in Scripture knows the end from the beginning.

Process theism goes further, and it deserves its strongest statement too. Alfred North Whitehead, in *Process and Reality* (1929), described God as holding all possibilities in a primordial nature and receiving everything that happens into a consequent nature, luring the world toward the good by persuasion rather than force; his closing pages call God "the fellow-sufferer who understands." Charles Hartshorne developed this in *The Divine Relativity* (1948), arguing that the most perfect being is not the least affected but the most fully related. Hartshorne did not give up a constant God. On his account God's character, his goodness and wisdom and love, never varies, while his concrete experience grows with every event in the world. Its defenders prize two things in this picture: a God who is truly touched by every creature's joy and pain, and an answer to evil, since a God who never coerces is not the author of horrors he could not prevent.

The trouble is not that this God is moved. It is what he is. For Whitehead, God does not bring the world into being from nothing; God and the world are both instances of a more basic creativity, each needing the other. That reverses the first line of the creeds, which confess one God, Maker of heaven and earth, on whom everything else depends and who depends on nothing. And a God who can only persuade cannot promise what the creeds' last line promises, the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Hartshorne was candid here: he held that our lasting existence is in God's everlasting memory of us, not in a continuing personal life. A process thinker can reply, with integrity, that persuasion is the only power love would ever use. But measured by the creeds, the view has lost the God who creates and the God who finishes what he began. That, and not its talk of divine feeling, is why the churches have not received it as a faithful option, even where they have learned from its compassion.

The classical side has answered. Paul Gavrilyuk's *The Suffering of the Impassible God* (2004) challenged the claim that the fathers borrowed impassibility wholesale from the philosophers. They used it chiefly to separate the true God from the jealous, lustful gods of the pagans, and they held it together, deliberately, with the confession that the Son truly suffered in the flesh. Thomas Weinandy's *Does God Suffer?* (2000) argued that denying divine suffering is not denying divine love: God's love is already fully in act and can't be increased by distress, and a God who suffered as we do would be one more sufferer in need of rescue. Both sides, it should be said, bring philosophy to the text, the classical view an ancient account of perfection and open theism a modern account of freedom. Neither reads the Bible without spectacles. The question is which spectacles let more of the text come into focus.

## Sorting the question, and where this essay lands

What the creeds bind is this: God is unchangeable in his being, his character, and his purpose. That is why Nicaea condemned a changeable Son, why Chalcedon insisted that the Word took flesh without ceasing to be what he was, and why Hebrews can say of the risen Lord, "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" (Hebrews 13:8). No party in the modern debate, open theists included, denies that God's goodness and faithfulness are unchangeable; Hartshorne affirmed it too. Process theism leaves the floor for a different reason: its God does not create from nothing, depends on the world for what he becomes, and cannot promise the world to come.

What divides faithful Christians is how to state that unchangeableness. Is God timeless, as Boethius and Aquinas taught, or everlasting, without beginning or end but moving through time with us, as a number of Christian philosophers have argued? Is he impassible in the strict classical sense, or does the language of divine grief name something real in God, as Heschel and Fretheim urged? These are second-order questions, and a reader who lands on the other side of them from this essay is not outside the faith. Open theism stands at the far edge of that ground. The ecumenical creeds don't mention foreknowledge in so many words, and its defenders confess them. But on this point Augustine and Pelagius agreed, and so did Aquinas, Calvin, Arminius, and Wesley, who agreed on little else; the whole argument over [predestination and free will](/writing/predestination-and-free-will) assumed on both sides that God knows what free creatures will do. When open theism appeared, some bodies wrote the older consensus down explicitly. The ETS resolution was one; to take one denomination as an example, the Southern Baptist Convention's 2000 revision of *The Baptist Faith and Message* added language affirming that God's knowledge extends to the future decisions of free creatures. Open theism is a departure from what every major branch of the church had held, and it should be weighed as that.

This essay lands where the historic church has landed. God does not change his mind the way we do. He has no new information to take in, no second thoughts, no better plan he wishes he had thought of sooner. He is unchangeable in his character and his purposes, and he is not static in his relationships. He really warns, hears, answers, and spares, and his dealings with us differ as we differ, but they flow from a will that does not waver.

The reason is not a taste for tidy metaphysics. It's that the alternative asks us to trust a God who is still learning how our stories end, and Scripture seems to go out of its way to deny that. "Declaring the end from the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done, saying, 'My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose'" (Isaiah 46:10). At Pentecost Peter says Jesus was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," and in the same sentence that his killers acted "by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). The most culpable free act in history was foreknown and folded into God's purpose without ceasing to be the guilt of those who did it. If God can know that, he can know Nineveh. And a comfort resting on his best estimate of what we'll do next would be exactly as reliable as a guess.

The strongest objection this position has not fully answered is about relationship, not foreknowledge. Aquinas held that the relation between God and creatures is real on the creature's side but not on God's, since nothing in God is changed by it. Critics hear a love that runs in one direction, and they point back to Genesis 6:6, where it "grieved him to his heart." The classical reply, that God's love is not less than our grief but more, full where ours is needy, is true as far as it goes. It can't quite picture what it affirms, and a reader who finds it too thin is not being stubborn.

Each view also has a popular form that betrays its best version, and most of us have lived in one of them. The classical view gone lazy becomes a shrug: it's all planned, so there's nothing to grieve and no point in asking. That fatalism has made prayerless Christians of people who could define immutability. The open view gone lazy becomes flattery: a God whose next move waits on ours. Both shrink God to something we can manage.

## What relenting is for: the potter's house

Scripture opens its own window onto the relenting texts in Jeremiah 18. The prophet watches a vessel go wrong in a potter's hands and the potter rework the same clay, and the word comes: "Behold, like the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand, O house of Israel" (Jeremiah 18:6). What follows is a published rule. "If at any time I declare concerning a nation or a kingdom, that I will pluck up and break down and destroy it, and if that nation, concerning which I have spoken, turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I intended to do to it" (Jeremiah 18:7–8). The rule runs the other way too: a nation promised good that turns to evil will find the good withdrawn (Jeremiah 18:9–10). God tells his people in advance how he will answer them. When he relents, he is keeping his word.

That changes how a prophecy of judgment reads. The word at the potter's house ends in an appeal: "Return, every one from his evil way, and amend your ways and your deeds" (Jeremiah 18:11). A doom God meant to carry out no matter what would not need a prophet. It would need only a date. That God sends someone to announce the disaster is itself a sign he hopes it won't have to come.

Jonah understood this, and he hated it. When Nineveh was spared he told God why he had run: "for I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster" (Jonah 4:2). He is quoting the name God proclaimed to Moses on Sinai (Exodus 34:6), with the relenting added, as Joel also adds it (Joel 2:13). What sent Jonah toward Tarshish was the knowledge that God would not change. Relenting was already in the name.

The golden calf shows the same thing from the side of prayer. Told to stand aside so that God's wrath may burn, Moses argues instead, and every argument appeals to something God has already said or done: the people he brought out of Egypt, the name the Egyptians will mock, the oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (Exodus 32:11–13). Moses brings no new information. He holds God to his own promises. Intercession, then, means standing where God has invited us to stand and asking him to be what he has said he is.

Aquinas, facing the old objection that prayer is pointless before an unchanging God, answered that we pray not to alter what God has arranged but to obtain what he has arranged to give through prayer. Our prayers are among the means by which his purposes come to pass, which is why the site's essay on [how to pray](/writing/how-do-you-actually-pray) starts with a Father who spoke first. The unchanging God doesn't make prayer pointless. He makes it safe. We aren't coaxing a volatile power into a better mood.

The potter's house also carries a warning. The rule that spares a repentant city withdraws good from a people who treat it as a possession. Judah in Jeremiah's day trusted the temple to protect it and was wrong. Every nation that has taken God's favor as a birthright rather than a covenant has made the same mistake, American Christians as confidently as anyone, and so has every believer who reads God's patience as a promise that consequences never come. What the relenting texts promise is that God will answer repentance, a comfort only the one who turns can receive.

## The cup that did not pass

One event seems to break all of this, and a skeptic deserves to see it raised. If God doesn't change, what of the claim that the eternal Son became a man, grew tired, wept at a grave, and died? At Chalcedon the church answered that the Son took a full human nature to himself without ceasing to be God, the two natures united without confusion and without change. The fixed point came near without moving, and the site's essay on [why the incarnation matters](/writing/why-the-incarnation-matters) follows that confession further.

In that human life there is one prayer that stands across every relenting text in the Bible. On the night he was betrayed, the Son asked the Father to relent: "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matthew 26:39). Moses had asked and been heard. Nineveh had turned and been spared. This time the Father did not relent. The cup did not pass.

That changes how every earlier sparing reads. Paul says God put Christ forward as a propitiation by his blood, and did it "to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins" (Romans 3:25). The disaster turned aside from the repentant, in every generation, did not simply evaporate. It was drawn against a cup someone would one day drink. At Golgotha God changed his mind neither about sin nor about us. He kept both words, the judgment and the mercy, in the body of his Son.

Here Malachi's "therefore" comes home. A people who had given God every reason to change his mind about them were not consumed, because he didn't. People who come apart in grief or guilt rarely stop believing God exists. What undoes them is the suspicion that his care is revisable, that he loved them when they were doing better and has cooled since. The unchanging God can't love you more on your good days or less on your worst, because his love was never a reaction to you. It is older than you are. "For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29).

So the contradiction is real on the page and resolved beneath it. The steadiness is in God, the movement is in us, and the verses in which he turns are what it looks like from inside time when a mercy that does not move meets a world that cannot stop moving. But that leaves the question somewhere uncomfortable. The relenting texts never ask whether God will change. They ask whether we will. The potter still works the clay, the name proclaimed on Sinai still says slow to anger, and the cup has already been drunk. Every one of those is fixed. What stays open in Jeremiah 18 is the turning, for the nation sure of its blessing, the church sure of its standing, and the person awake at three in the morning sure it is too late.
