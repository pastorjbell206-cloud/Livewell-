You're angry at God, and somewhere along the way you learned you weren't supposed to be. Nobody said it outright. It came sideways: a verse handed to you at the wrong moment, a worship set that moved straight to victory, a friend who changed the subject when you asked "why" out loud. So the anger went underground, and in private you stopped praying, because the only thing you had to say to God was the one thing you'd been taught not to say. Now the question sits in a search bar late at night: is it okay to be angry at God?

The Bible's answer is more surprising than most churches' and more demanding than the culture's. By most scholars' count, the largest single family of psalms is the lament, prayers that accuse God of forgetting, sleeping and hiding, and Israel sang them in worship. Jesus died with the first line of one in his mouth. But the same Scriptures record a different kind of complaint, one God judged severely: the grumbling of a generation in the wilderness that talked about God among themselves and started planning the road back to Egypt. Both are angry, and both say God has failed. The difference between them is the whole of this essay.

Lament is protest addressed to God. It holds him to his own promises, to his face, and stays in the room while it does. Grumbling is complaint that has turned away from God, spoken about him rather than to him, and it is already packing. The church has mostly flattened the two into one forbidden thing, and so it has lost the prayer Scripture gave for the worst days of a human life. Pastors lead the church's prayers, and I'm a pastor. I have rushed grieving people toward a resolution they hadn't reached, because their unresolved darkness made me uncomfortable. That failure belongs inside the indictment too.

## What a lament psalm is made of

Lament is a form before it is a mood. Hermann Gunkel taught modern readers to ask what kind of prayer each psalm was and what setting in Israel's life produced it, and his introduction to the Psalms, finished by his student Joachim Begrich and published in 1933, sorted the Psalter into families: hymns, thanksgivings, royal psalms, communal laments and individual laments. Most form critics since have judged the individual lament the largest of these families, and counting the communal laments with it, estimates run to something like a third of the book, depending on where the borders are drawn.

Claus Westermann, whose studies were gathered in English as *Praise and Lament in the Psalms* (1981), argued that praise and lament are the two poles of Israel's speech to God. If he is right, a prayer life with only one pole isn't a smaller version of biblical prayer but a different thing. He traced the lament's anatomy: an address calling on God by name, a complaint, a turn to confessed trust, a petition, and usually a closing vow of praise, the parts not always in that order. He also noticed that the complaint runs in three directions, against the enemy ("they"), about the self ("I"), and against God ("you"). The third isn't the slip of an unguarded poet. It is built into the form.

Psalm 13 shows the whole anatomy in six verses. Address and complaint arrive fused: "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1, ESV). The petition is blunt: consider, answer, light up my eyes before I sleep the sleep of death (Psalm 13:3). Then, with no explanation, the turn: "But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation" (Psalm 13:5, ESV). And the vow: "I will sing to the LORD, because he has dealt bountifully with me" (Psalm 13:6, ESV).

That turn has puzzled scholars for a century. Begrich proposed in 1934 that a priest may have spoken an unrecorded oracle of salvation between complaint and trust; others place the turn inside the one praying, as memory rises against experience. What the psalm makes plain is the order. Trust comes after the complaint, not instead of it, and nothing suggests the man had to take back his accusation to be allowed to sing.

Walter Brueggemann named this movement in *The Message of the Psalms* (1984). Songs of orientation are sung when the world holds together; songs of disorientation, when it has come apart and God seems absent or hostile; songs of new orientation, by people surprised by God on the far side of the dark. New orientation isn't a return to the old innocence. The one who comes through the lament trusts God the way a man trusts a bridge he has watched hold under weight, and there's no getting that trust by skipping the test.

## The psalms that argue with God

Athanasius of Alexandria, writing to Marcellinus in the fourth century, observed that most of Scripture speaks to us while the Psalms speak for us. John Calvin, in the preface to his Psalms commentary (1557), called the book an anatomy of all the parts of the soul. The Psalter isn't the feelings we ought to have. It's the feelings we do have, given a direction.

Psalm 44 is a communal lament after defeat, and its offense is that the people insist they haven't earned it: "Yet for your sake we are killed all the day long; we are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered" (Psalm 44:22, ESV). It ends close to a shout: "Awake! Why are you sleeping, O Lord? Rouse yourself! Do not reject us forever!" (Psalm 44:23, ESV). Yet Paul, in the chapter most loved for its assurance, quotes this lament in Romans 8:36 and answers it in the next breath with "more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Romans 8:37, ESV). He didn't set the lament aside to reach the confidence. He placed the confidence inside it.

Psalm 77 shows how the turn is made. After a comfortless night Asaph asks what a believer is least supposed to ask: "Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he in anger shut up his compassion?" (Psalm 77:9, ESV). What follows is no answer from heaven but a decision to remember (Psalm 77:11). He goes back to the exodus and writes one of Scripture's most honest lines about how God works in the dark: "Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters; yet your footprints were unseen" (Psalm 77:19, ESV). The psalm argues from what God has done against what God seems to be doing now.

Psalm 22 makes the same argument. Two verses after the cry of abandonment it sets God's character against the silence: "Yet you are holy, enthroned on the praises of Israel" (Psalm 22:3, ESV). That "yet" is the hinge of all lament. The one praying neither denies God's holiness to complain nor stops complaining because God is holy.

Psalm 88 never turns. Its title calls it a song for the choirmaster, yet it ends in darkness with no word of trust. What it keeps is the address: "Every day I call upon you, O LORD; I spread out my hands to you" (Psalm 88:9, ESV). The man in the pit has nothing left but the direction of his prayer, and Israel judged that worth singing. If you're reading from something like his pit, and the darkness has started telling you your life isn't worth continuing, please call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, tonight. Psalm 88 is the prayer of someone still alive and still speaking. Reaching for help is the same act.

Then comes the ninth hour on Golgotha: "And about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying, 'Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?' that is, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Matthew 27:46, ESV). Jesus was praying a psalm, and many interpreters hold that its first line called up the whole of it, vindication included. But he wasn't merely reciting; the Son was addressing the Father as "my God" from inside the worst the world could do. Hebrews describes his prayer in just these terms: "In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence" (Hebrews 5:7, ESV). Loud cries and tears, and Scripture calls it reverence.

## Job, Jeremiah and a city in ruins

Outside the Psalter, lament attaches to named people and dated catastrophes. Job is the longest argument with God in Scripture. As the silence lengthens, he speaks: "Therefore I will not restrain my mouth; I will speak in the anguish of my spirit; I will complain in the bitterness of my soul" (Job 7:11, ESV). The book sets two other paths in front of him. His wife offers one: "Do you still hold fast your integrity? Curse God and die" (Job 2:9, ESV). Let the anger end the relationship. His friends offer the other: confess a sin you didn't commit, and let the explanation spare God the accusation. Job refuses both, and names his own path in one verse: "Though he slay me, I will hope in him; yet I will argue my ways to his face" (Job 13:15, ESV). That is lament in its purest form, the opposite of cursing God and dying.

Jeremiah's prayers in chapters 11 to 20, which scholars call his confessions, carry the argument into a prophet's calling. He grants God's righteousness first and presses his case anyway: "Righteous are you, O LORD, when I complain to you; yet I would plead my case before you. Why does the way of the wicked prosper? Why do all who are treacherous thrive?" (Jeremiah 12:1, ESV). Then he goes further than almost anyone dares: "Why is my pain unceasing, my wound incurable, refusing to be healed? Will you be to me like a deceitful brook, like waters that fail?" (Jeremiah 15:18, ESV). A deceitful brook is a streambed that looks wet from a distance and is dry when you reach it. The prophet is asking whether God is a mirage.

Lamentations belongs to the catastrophe Jeremiah foresaw. In 586 BC Babylon broke Jerusalem's walls, burned the temple and carried much of the people into exile. The book is five poems from the rubble, four of them acrostics, each stanza beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet: grief put in order, named completely from first letter to last. Its accusations are direct: "Look, O LORD, and see! With whom have you dealt thus?" (Lamentations 2:20, ESV). The same chapter holds the Old Testament's most famous word of hope: "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end" (Lamentations 3:22, ESV). The church sings its next verse as "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" and has mostly forgotten that the line was first spoken among the dead.

The book ends without resolving: "unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us" (Lamentations 5:22, ESV). When the synagogue reads Lamentations on the Ninth of Av, the fast that remembers the fall of both temples, the reader by long custom repeats the verse before it, the prayer for restoration, so the reading won't end on rejection. But the text itself wasn't changed. You don't edit the scream out of Scripture. You answer it by the next thing you say to God.

So anger at God has a shape. It is addressed to him, it appeals to his steadfast love and past deeds and promises, and it waits for him. The wilderness generation shows what happens when complaint loses that shape. After the spies' report the people wept through the night and grumbled against Moses and Aaron, and then: "And they said to one another, 'Let us choose a leader and go back to Egypt'" (Numbers 14:4, ESV). The complaint went sideways, to one another, and then it turned around. Paul held that generation up as a warning: "nor grumble, as some of them did and were destroyed by the Destroyer" (1 Corinthians 10:10, ESV). Scripture condemns the grumbling and canonizes the lament. The difference isn't the intensity of the feeling. It's where the feeling is aimed.

## Is it wrong to be angry at God?

The Christians who say you shouldn't be angry at God aren't fools, and their case deserves the form they'd sign. Anger is a moral judgment; to be angry at someone is to hold that he has done wrong. God cannot do wrong. He is the standard of righteousness, not a party measured against it. So anger at God is always misdirected, a creature putting the Creator in the dock, and the fitting response to loss is Job's on the first day: "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21, ESV). Paul seems to close the matter: "But who are you, O man, to answer back to God? Will what is molded say to its molder, 'Why have you made me like this?'" (Romans 9:20, ESV). A long tradition of patience stands behind this, down to Thomas Brooks's *The Mute Christian Under the Smarting Rod* (1659), a Puritan treatise built on a single verse: "I am mute; I do not open my mouth, for it is you who have done it" (Psalm 39:9, ESV). Much of it is right. God is not a defendant, and a person who has decided God is guilty has left lament behind.

But the argument proves too much. It would condemn the Psalms, Job, Jeremiah and the cry of Christ, and it misreads its own texts. Three verses after the psalmist says he is mute, he is begging God not to hold his peace at his tears (Psalm 39:12). Romans 9:20 answers an objector who uses God's sovereignty to excuse himself, not a sufferer, and the same chapter opens with Paul's own grief: "that I have great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" (Romans 9:2, ESV). And the Job who blessed God's name in chapter 1 went on to argue to God's face through chapter after chapter, and at the end God sided with the arguer against his defenders (Job 42:7). The friends said pious things about God. Job said hard things to God. Only one of them was praying.

So the distinction has to be drawn more carefully than "anger is allowed" or "anger is forbidden." There is anger that pronounces a verdict: God has done wrong, and I have judged him. And there is anger that makes an appeal: this isn't what you promised, and I'm holding you to your word. The first puts the self on the bench. The second stands before the bench, pleading, and makes its case on God's own terms, his steadfast love, his covenant, his name. Lament is an appeal to God against God, to the God of the promise against the God of the present darkness, and it only makes sense to someone who still believes the promise. You don't rage at a God you've given up on. You rage at the one you're still holding to his word.

The limits are real, and Scripture names them. When God answers Job from the whirlwind, he explains nothing; he asks questions Job can't answer. And he names the line Job had begun to cross: "Will you even put me in the wrong? Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" (Job 40:8, ESV). Lament may say "why," "how long" and "where were you." It may not conclude that God is guilty so that I can be innocent. What Job receives is not an explanation but a presence; he had heard of God, and now he sees him (Job 42:5). Jeremiah is answered the same way. After he compares God to a dry brook, God replies: "If you return, I will restore you, and you shall stand before me. If you utter what is precious, and not what is worthless, you shall be as my mouth" (Jeremiah 15:19, ESV). God doesn't punish the lament. He also doesn't leave the prophet inside it.

Nicholas Wolterstorff learned this at the worst cost. In 1983 his son Eric, twenty-five, died in a climbing accident in the Austrian Alps, and the philosopher wrote *Lament for a Son* (1987) in short fragments, because grief doesn't come in chapters. He refused the comforters' explanations and confessed he couldn't fit his faith and his son's death together. Yet he didn't walk away from God. He came to hear lament as a kind of love song, the depth of the complaint measuring the love for what was lost. That is lament's limit and its freedom together. It doesn't demand an answer as the price of staying. It stays, and keeps asking.

## The psalms that curse

Some laments go further than anger at God. They ask God to do harm. These are the imprecatory psalms, and the church has quietly dropped them from its readings and songs. Psalm 109 is the fiercest. It begins with a man repaid evil for good: "In return for my love they accuse me, but I give myself to prayer" (Psalm 109:4, ESV). Then for fifteen verses (Psalm 109:6-20) it calls down ruin on an accuser: short life, lost office, fatherless children, a name blotted out. (Some scholars read those verses as the enemies' curse, quoted back to God; most of the tradition hears them as the psalmist's own.) Psalm 137 ends by pronouncing happy whoever would kill Babylon's infants. And the apostles used these texts: Peter applied Psalm 109:8 to Judas's empty place: "Let another take his office" (Acts 1:20, ESV).

These psalms are not moral instruction. Jesus commanded love for enemies, and Paul forbade private revenge: "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19, ESV). A Christian who prays Psalm 109 as a script for his feelings about a coworker has misread both testaments.

But Paul doesn't say the wrong is unimportant. He says to leave it to God, which is precisely what the imprecatory psalm does. The man in Psalm 109 picks up no weapon; he hands his rage to God: "But you, O GOD my Lord, deal on my behalf for your name's sake; because your steadfast love is good, deliver me!" (Psalm 109:21, ESV). The Catholic scholar Erich Zenger made this case in *A God of Vengeance?* (German 1994; English 1996), arguing that these psalms of enmity are prayers of people with no power left, who surrender the right to revenge by placing their cry for justice in God's hands, and that removing them from the church's prayer, as the reformed Catholic Liturgy of the Hours had partly done, takes away the voice they give to victims. To the survivor of abuse who was told to forgive quickly and say nothing, these psalms say the wrong was real and that God cares about it more than the abuser does. If that's your situation, the prayer belongs to you, and so does help: the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

C. S. Lewis, in *Reflections on the Psalms* (1958), called the spirit of the cursings plainly wicked, but noticed two things. The absence of such indignation can be a worse sign than its presence, since people who feel no outrage at cruelty may have stopped believing right and wrong are real. And injustice tempts its victims toward hatred, so part of the oppressor's guilt is the hatred he plants. That turns the psalm on the reader. Most of us cast ourselves as the one praying Psalm 109. It's worth asking whether someone prays something like it about us, our nation or our church.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, in *Psalms: The Prayerbook of the Bible* (1940), took the last step. Christians pray these psalms through Christ, he argued, and God's vengeance on the wicked did fall, but it fell on the one sinless man who stood in the place of sinners. God doesn't ignore the demand for justice. He meets it in his own Son, and so the Christian can pray for justice and for the enemy's forgiveness in the same breath, because both have already met at Golgotha.

## How the church lost the prayer

No one voted for the loss. For most of Christian history the church prayed the whole Psalter; the Rule of Benedict (c. 530) had monks pray all 150 psalms every week, and the Genevan Psalter, completed in 1562, set every psalm to music for ordinary congregations. Then, in the English-speaking world, a quieter change came. In 1719 Isaac Watts published *The Psalms of David Imitated in the Language of the New Testament*, a gifted attempt to make the psalms sound Christian, and, as his own preface says, he left out several whole psalms and large parts of others, keeping what he judged suited to Christian worship. He had his reasons. But the hymn gradually replaced the psalm, the hymn chose its emotions, and revival songs and later the praise chorus chose more narrowly still. Glenn Pemberton, in *Hurting with God* (2012), set the songs congregations most often sing, as reflected in CCLI's licensing lists, beside the Psalter, and found lament rare in the one, though scholars count it the largest family in the other. A church that sings only orientation will eventually pray only orientation.

Brueggemann saw the cost. In "The Costly Loss of Lament" (1986) he named two costs. The first is the loss of real covenant interaction: a worshiper permitted only praise and confession is no longer a partner who can press a claim, only a subject who agrees. The second is that the question of justice is stifled, and whatever arrangement of power already holds goes unchallenged. A church without lament has no liturgy for naming injustice, so it learns to explain injustice or ignore it. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, drawing on a national study of American teenagers in *Soul Searching* (2005), named the faith that results: moralistic therapeutic deism, in which God wants people to be nice and feel good and mostly stays out of the way. That God can't be lamented to. He was never close enough to be accused of absence.

Soong-Chan Rah pressed the cost home in *Prophetic Lament* (2015), a reading of Lamentations for the American church. He argued that much of American evangelicalism runs on a theology of celebration, formed among people for whom things have generally gone well, with little room for the theology of suffering that grows among people for whom they haven't. A church that only celebrates hears the grief of others as negativity or politics, never as prayer; celebration without lament forgets who is suffering. The Black church didn't lose the prayer. James Cone showed in *The Spirituals and the Blues* (1972) that enslaved Christians made songs that told God the truth about their suffering and still called him deliverer. Those of us who inherited the cheerful hymnbook lost the very language our own history required. We never learned to grieve what was done, because we had long since stopped grieving at all.

The distinction at the center of this essay cuts both ways across the church's political quarrels. The triumphalist church, especially where faith has been fused with the nation, can't lament, because lament would mean admitting that the story has gone wrong and that church and nation have sinned, not simply won. A more progressive church can fail the same test from the other side. It is quick to name injustice, and often right to, yet its protest is frequently addressed to the public rather than to God, a complaint about God's absence made to one another. That is the wilderness pattern in respectable clothes. Lament that stops being prayer becomes grievance, on either side.

And both begin where I do. The pastor who hurries a grieving family toward resolution, the worship leader who cuts the lament because it would slow the set, the congregation that answers someone's anger at God with a verse instead of silence: these are failures of nerve more than doctrine. We don't trust God to survive the honesty of his people, and in protecting him we abandon the people he gave these prayers to.

## The prayer that was heard

If you've read this far with anger at God still in your chest, Scripture doesn't ask you to put it down before you come. It asks you to bring it to the right place. Say it to him, in the psalms' words if you have none of your own: how long, why do you hide, where were you, have you forgotten to be gracious. What Scripture forbids is not the anger but the turning away: the verdict pronounced from the bench, the complaint carried to everyone except God, the quiet decision to choose a leader and go back to Egypt. Anger still addressed to God is faith, even when it doesn't feel like faith. Anger that has stopped addressing him is already becoming something else, and it's worth noticing which way yours is facing.

This is possible because God has taken the lament into himself. On the cross the Son prayed Israel's prayer of abandonment as his own. He didn't bless lament from a distance; he went further into it than any of us will go, with loud cries and tears to the Father who was able to save him from death, and Hebrews says he was heard. The answer didn't come on Friday, or on Saturday, when the one who had cried "why" lay silent in a tomb. It came on the third day, and it wasn't an explanation. It was a body raised from the dead.

The resurrection doesn't cancel Psalm 88. It tells you the man in the pit was praying to a God who would go down into the pit himself and come back up. Your "how long" rises to a Father who heard his Son's "why" and answered it with Easter. That doesn't promise your answer will come soon, or in the form you asked. Job never learned why. Jeremiah was carried off to Egypt still unvindicated in the eyes of his people. They kept praying, and their prayers were kept.

So the church owes its people the whole Psalter, including the psalms that argue, the psalms that curse and the one that ends in the dark, and it owes them pastors who can sit with unresolved grief without hurrying it. It owes God the honesty he asked for when he put these prayers in his book. He has already heard "why have you forsaken me" from his own Son. The question isn't whether he can bear your lament. It's whether you'll keep speaking to him long enough for him to answer, and whether the rest of us will stay in the room while you wait.
