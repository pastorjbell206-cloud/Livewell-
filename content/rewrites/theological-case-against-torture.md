In April 2009 the Pew Forum on Religion & Public Life asked American adults whether the torture of suspected terrorists to gain important information could be justified. Among Americans who attend religious services at least once a week, 54 percent said it could often or sometimes be justified. Among those who seldom or never attend, 42 percent said the same. White evangelical Protestants were the most willing of the religious groups Pew reported, at 62 percent, well above the religiously unaffiliated, and a pastor can't read that line as a report about somebody else. It was one small survey of 742 people taken in a single week, and it shouldn't be made to carry more than it can. But it can carry this much. At the moment the question was being argued in public, the people who sat most often under Christian preaching were not the ones most troubled by the answer.

That ought to stop a pastor cold, and it stops me. The deliberate breaking of a human being to get something out of him should be the kind of question the church answers from its catechism, before anyone opens a newspaper. That it needs an essay at all is the symptom. The cause runs deeper than a single war or a single administration, and it isn't mainly political. It is a failure of formation: a church that had learned to hear the claims of Christ as one voice among several, to be weighed against fear, national interest and the logic of emergency, and that discovered in a frightened decade how easily the weighing tipped. I am inside that failure. For years I said nothing about this, because the subject was coded as politics and I feared the coding more than I feared the silence.

This essay tries to answer the question honestly, which means answering the strongest argument on the other side rather than the weakest, telling the truth about the church's own record, and then asking what the confession that God took flesh, and was tortured in it, requires of people who say it every Sunday.

## The ticking bomb, stated at full strength

The argument for torture that deserves an answer isn't sadism, and treating it as sadism is a way of avoiding it. It comes from people who have looked at a real horror and refused to pretend that clean hands are the only moral good. After September 11, 2001, that argument moved quickly from seminar rooms into government. On August 1, 2002, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel signed a memorandum, drafted largely by John Yoo and signed by Jay Bybee, that defined torture so narrowly that almost nothing short of pain equivalent to organ failure or death would count. The memo was withdrawn in 2004, long after the program it supported had been built.

The most candid version of the argument came from a civil libertarian. In *Why Terrorism Works* (2002), the Harvard law professor Alan Dershowitz put the question in its classic form. A captured terrorist knows where a bomb is hidden, the bomb will kill thousands within hours, and he refuses to talk. Dershowitz did not claim torture was good. He claimed that in such a case officials would do it anyway, and that a democracy should prefer open accountability to secret brutality. So he proposed that interrogators be required to seek a judicial torture warrant, as police seek a search warrant, so that any use of coercion would be rare, recorded and answerable to a court. Whatever one thinks of the proposal, it was an attempt to take the hard case seriously without letting the executive decide in the dark.

Charles Krauthammer's essay "The Truth About Torture," published in *The Weekly Standard* in December 2005, pressed further. Krauthammer was arguing against John McCain's amendment to ban cruel treatment of detainees, and he granted at the outset that torture is a terrible thing that should generally be forbidden. But he argued that there are two exceptions a serious person cannot refuse. The first is the ticking bomb itself. The second is the captured high-level planner whose knowledge could stop attacks not yet scheduled, a slower fuse but a fuse all the same. In those cases, Krauthammer argued, a leader who refused to allow coercion would be choosing his own moral comfort over the lives of the people he was sworn to protect, and calling the choice principle.

That last move has deep roots, in Christian realism among other places, and it's the part a Christian can't dismiss. Michael Walzer's essay on "dirty hands" (1973) described the politician who must sometimes do wrong to prevent a greater wrong, and who remains guilty even when he chooses rightly. The Christian ethicist Jean Bethke Elshtain, writing in Sanford Levinson's collection *Torture: A Collection* (2004), drew on that tradition. She held firmly against making torture routine or giving it legal standing in advance, but she argued that forms of coercive interrogation short of torture might be permissible in extreme cases, and that a statesman who acted in such a case should acknowledge the moral cost and bear it openly rather than claim clean hands. She also warned that a moralism which refuses every hard choice lets the refuser feel clean while others pay for his purity. Behind all of them stands Paul's description of the ruling authority: "for he is God's servant for your good. But if you do wrong, be afraid, for he does not bear the sword in vain. For he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4, ESV). The state exists to protect the innocent. An official who could have saved a city and declined, so that his own conscience would stay unstained, has not obviously chosen righteousness. He may have bought private purity with his neighbors' children.

Stated that way, the argument persuaded morally earnest people, including veterans and intelligence professionals who sit in American churches every Sunday. It deserves a direct answer in two parts, the practical and the theological, and the order matters. A Christian who skips straight to theology can be accused of never having looked at the bomb.

## What the scenario hides

The first answer came from inside the legal academy. In "Liberalism, Torture, and the Ticking Bomb," published in the *Virginia Law Review* in 2005, the legal ethicist David Luban argued that the ticking-bomb case wins by stipulation. It asks us to assume that we have the right man, that he knows exactly what we need, that the bomb is real, that torture will make him tell the truth rather than whatever stops the pain, and that it will do so in time. Grant all of that and the conclusion follows almost by arithmetic. But every one of those certainties is exactly what a real interrogator lacks. Luban's deeper point was institutional. Torture in the real world is never one agonized decision by one man in one room. It requires people trained to do it, practice to become good at it, doctors to keep the subject alive, lawyers to write the memos, and a bureaucracy that learns to think of all this as normal work. Luban's warning was that authorizing torture builds a culture of torture, and that the ticking bomb is the story that makes such a culture thinkable. The scenario asks us to authorize one terrible act. What it actually authorizes is a profession.

The philosopher Henry Shue had seen part of this in 1978, in an essay simply titled "Torture." Shue granted that one could construct an artificial case in which torture looked permissible, but he warned that artificial cases make bad law, and he located the peculiar wrong of torture somewhere the ticking bomb never looks. Even in war, Shue argued, killing a soldier in battle is killing someone who can still fight back. The tortured prisoner is already captured, bound and helpless. Torture is an assault on the defenseless, and that is why it violates something even the laws of war protect.

Then there is the question of whether it works, which has to be answered from named sources and not from films. Darius Rejali's *Torture and Democracy* (2007), a long historical study of modern interrogation, concluded that torture is a poor instrument for gathering accurate information, because under enough pain people say what they think will end it, and interrogators cannot reliably tell the difference. The fullest official review of the American program is the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's study of the CIA's detention and interrogation program, whose executive summary and findings were released in December 2014. Its first finding was that the CIA's enhanced interrogation techniques were not an effective means of acquiring intelligence or gaining cooperation from detainees, and the summary recorded cases in which detainees under those techniques fabricated information. Fairness requires saying that the finding was contested. The CIA disputed it in a formal response, and the committee's Republican minority filed dissenting views. A reader who wants certainty on the empirical question will not get it from the record. But a moral argument that depends on torture working has to carry the burden of that record, and it cannot simply assume what the most detailed review available rejected.

John McCain, a Republican senator who had been tortured as a prisoner in North Vietnam, spoke on the Senate floor the day the summary was released and supported publishing it. He had made the same argument for years: that the practice produced bad information and that it betrayed what the country claimed to be. His witness matters here because it cannot be filed under one party's grievance. One of the strongest American voices against torture in those years came from a man of the right who knew it from the inside.

The Christian tradition had its own witness to the practical problem long before the modern debate. In the nineteenth book of *The City of God*, written in the early fifth century, Augustine describes a judge who tortures a man to find out whether he is guilty. The judge inflicts certain pain to resolve an uncertain charge, and if the man is innocent, the judge has tortured an innocent man in order not to execute one. Honesty requires reading Augustine all the way through. He did not forbid the practice. He treated it as one of the miseries of a fallen world, which a wise judge would lament and still feel bound to carry out. That is a real limit in Augustine, and it would shadow the church for a thousand years. But the diagnosis is exact. Torture punishes before it knows, and so it corrupts the very justice it claims to serve.

## What the church has done, and what it has said

A Christian argument against torture that begins with the church's innocence will not survive the first informed skeptic, and it shouldn't. The record is mixed, and some of it is dark.

The early church knew torture from the receiving end. Tertullian, writing his *Apology* around 197, noticed a grim irony in the Roman courts: other accused people were tortured to make them confess, while Christians were tortured to make them deny. The instrument that was supposed to reach the truth was being used to force a lie. Christians of that age did not need a theory of human dignity to know what the rack was for. Their martyrs had been on it.

When the church did gain the ear of rulers, one of its early answers was clear. In 866 Pope Nicholas I answered a long list of questions from Boris, the newly baptized ruler of the Bulgars, about governing as a Christian. One concerned the Bulgar custom of beating an accused thief until he confessed. Nicholas answered, in chapter 86 of his reply, that neither divine nor human law allows it, and that a confession must be given freely rather than extracted by force. He saw the practical danger as well, the one that should have settled the matter: an innocent man who cannot bear the pain will confess to what he never did.

The church did not hold that line. As Roman law was recovered in the universities of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, judicial torture came back into European courts, and church tribunals adopted it. On May 15, 1252, Pope Innocent IV issued the bull *Ad extirpanda*, addressed to the rulers and officials of the Italian cities, authorizing them to coerce suspected heretics by torture into confessing and naming others, so long as they stopped short of killing or maiming. The limit tells you the church knew what it was doing. Torture served the prosecution of heresy for centuries afterward, and in the witch trials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it was used by Protestant and Catholic courts alike.

It was a Jesuit priest who wrote the most searching Christian attack on that system. Friedrich Spee had seen the German witch trials of the 1620s at close quarters, and in 1631 he published, without his name, the *Cautio Criminalis*, a book of cautions for judges. Spee's argument was that torture manufactures the crime it claims to discover. Under enough pain the accused will confess to anything, and then name others, who will be tortured and name others in turn, until the fire has no natural end. He wrote as a priest who had come to doubt the guilt of the condemned, and a later tradition, passed on by Leibniz, has him saying that he believed none of the women he had accompanied to their deaths was guilty. The book was widely read and helped slow the trials in parts of the Empire.

The final abolition of judicial torture in Europe, though, owed more to the Enlightenment than to the bishops. Cesare Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) made the case that torture rewards the strong and condemns the weak, since the hardened man endures and the frail one confesses, and within a few decades most European states had abandoned it. A Christian ought to be able to say that without flinching. The critics of the church were right, and the church was late.

The Catholic Church has said so itself. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, first published in 1992, teaches in paragraph 2297 that "Torture which uses physical or moral violence to extract confessions, punish the guilty, frighten opponents, or satisfy hatred is contrary to respect for the person and for human dignity." The next paragraph admits that in earlier times cruel practices were used by legitimate governments, often without protest from the church's pastors, who adopted Roman law's rules on torture in their own tribunals, and that these practices were neither necessary for public order nor in keeping with the legitimate rights of the human person. It pleads, in mitigation, that the church always taught clemency and mercy, and a reader may judge that plea as he likes. Protestants can still learn from that paragraph. It is what the beginning of repentance looks like when an institution does it: naming its own sin in its own teaching document.

The distinction this history forces is between the church's confession and the church's practice. At its clearest, the church taught what Nicholas taught and Spee risked his standing to say. At its worst, the church did what Innocent authorized. The gap between them is not an argument against the teaching. It is the evidence that the teaching was right, and that the church needed it as badly as any state.

## The image of God and the tortured God

The theological answer begins where Scripture begins its account of us. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The image isn't a reward for good behavior, and it isn't withdrawn from the guilty. James assumes it in ordinary sinners when he rebukes the tongue: "With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse people who are made in the likeness of God" (James 3:9, ESV). The man in the interrogation room, whatever he has done and whatever he knows, bears it while he is there.

A careful reader will notice that the same doctrine has been used on the other side. After the flood God tells Noah, "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image" (Genesis 9:6, ESV). The image of God grounds the authority to punish murder, and Paul's sword in Romans 13 stands in that line. So the Christian case against torture can't rest on the claim that the state may never use force. It has to rest on a distinction, and the distinction is the heart of the matter.

Punishment, rightly done, answers a wrong already proven, in proportion to it, and it addresses the guilty person as a responsible agent who can be judged. Force in a just war is directed at a combatant who can still fight back. Torture is neither. It is aimed at a person already captured and helpless, and its purpose is not to answer what he has done but to break his will so that he becomes an instrument of someone else's purposes. It treats the image-bearer as a mechanism to be opened. Even the law of Moses, which permitted beatings as punishment, set a hard ceiling of forty stripes, "lest, if one should go on to beat him with more stripes than these, your brother be degraded in your sight" (Deuteronomy 25:3, ESV). Israel's law protected the dignity of the guilty man in the very act of punishing him. Torture exists to degrade, because degradation is the method.

Then the New Testament places the Son of God inside that room. After his arrest Jesus is questioned by the high priest, and one of the officers standing by strikes him in the face for the way he answered. Jesus says, "If what I said is wrong, bear witness about the wrong; but if what I said is right, why do you strike me?" (John 18:23, ESV). It's a small scene, and it contains the whole argument. Jesus asks for evidence and is given a blow, and he names the blow for what it is: force substituting for truth. Later, in Acts, the Roman tribune in Jerusalem orders that Paul "should be examined by flogging, to find out why they were shouting against him like this" (Acts 22:24, ESV). That is interrogation by torture, stated in the ordinary language of Roman procedure, and Paul escapes it only because he is a citizen: "Is it lawful for you to flog a man who is a Roman citizen and uncondemned?" (Acts 22:25, ESV). The protection belonged to the citizen. The provincial and the slave had none.

The cross itself was state torture. Martin Hengel's *Crucifixion in the Ancient World* (1977) showed from the ancient sources how deliberately Rome used it: a punishment for slaves, rebels and the lowest classes, carried out in public, designed to prolong dying and to terrify anyone watching. Cicero, prosecuting the corrupt governor Verres, called it the cruelest and most repulsive of penalties. The Gospels record the scourging, the mockery and the crown of thorns without lingering on them, because their first readers needed no description. The church confesses that the Son of God submitted to the most refined instrument of terror the empire possessed. Whatever else the crucifixion means, it means that God has occupied the place of the tortured prisoner, and has done so as a man with a body.

That changes how Christians are meant to see every other prisoner. In the parable of the sheep and the goats, the King identifies himself with those in prison: "Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40, ESV). The letter to the Hebrews asks the church to feel its way into the cell: "Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body" (Hebrews 13:3, ESV). The reason given is simple and physical. You also are in the body. The Christian faith doesn't treat the body as a shell the real person lives inside. It confesses that God made bodies, took one, suffered in it, and raised it. A people who believe in the resurrection of the body cannot be casual about what is done to bodies in the dark.

## Torture and Eucharist

The deepest Christian account of what torture is came out of a church that lived under it. William Cavanaugh's *Torture and Eucharist* (1998) studies the Catholic Church in Chile during the military dictatorship of Augusto Pinochet, which ruled from 1973 to 1990 and tortured thousands of its own citizens. Cavanaugh's argument is that torture under that regime was not mainly about information. It was a kind of anti-liturgy, a ritual the state performed on bodies to produce fear and isolation, so that people withdrew into private life and stopped trusting one another. The disappeared and the tortured were the visible part. The larger work was done on everyone else, who learned to keep quiet.

The church in Chile learned slowly, and Cavanaugh does not flatter it. But it learned. In 1976 the archdiocese of Santiago established the Vicariate of Solidarity, which documented abuses and defended victims in court. In 1980 Chilean bishops issued a decree of excommunication aimed at torturers, many of them baptized Catholics. And from 1983, in what became the Sebastián Acevedo Movement Against Torture, priests, religious sisters and laypeople organized public, nonviolent actions outside the places where people were tortured, standing in the street and saying aloud what the state wanted unspoken. Cavanaugh's point is that the Eucharist is the church's counter-practice. Where torture scatters people into fearful, isolated bodies, the Lord's Supper gathers them into one body that cannot be disappeared, and it makes the tortured and the torturer members of a communion that has something to say to both. Excommunication, in that light, was the church telling the torturer that what he did on Monday could not be brought to the table on Sunday.

American Christians made their own, smaller answers during the years of the CIA program. In January 2006 the Princeton Theological Seminary theologian George Hunsinger convened a conference on theology, international law and torture, and out of it came the National Religious Campaign Against Torture, an interfaith coalition that pressed for an end to the practice. In 2007 a group called Evangelicals for Human Rights, led by the Baptist ethicist David Gushee, issued *An Evangelical Declaration Against Torture*, which grounded its opposition in the image of God and the rule of law, and the board of the National Association of Evangelicals endorsed it. Not every evangelical scholar agreed; some criticized the declaration sharply as naive about national security. But it showed that evangelical conviction could speak clearly here when it chose to, and the Pew survey two years later suggests how far that clarity had reached the pews.

International law had already said what those declarations said. The United Nations Convention against Torture, adopted by the General Assembly in December 1984 and ratified by the United States in 1994, states in Article 2 that "No exceptional circumstances whatsoever, whether a state of war or a threat of war, internal political instability or any other public emergency, may be invoked as a justification of torture." That sentence is a direct refusal of the ticking bomb. The nations that wrote it had lived through the twentieth century and knew that every regime that tortures believes its circumstances are exceptional.

## One instrument, and the man in the back pew

If the church is going to say this, it has to say it to everyone with the same instrument. The failure of the American right in those years was to let national security work as a theological trump card, to treat the absolute claims of Christ as negotiable in wartime and to call the negotiation realism. The failure of the left is older and just as real. In the 1930s many Western admirers of the Soviet Union explained away the Moscow show trials of 1936 to 1938, where defendants confessed in open court to crimes they had not committed, and Arthur Koestler, himself a former Communist, wrote *Darkness at Noon* (1940) to show how such confessions were manufactured. The same selective silence recurs whenever a regime the left admires is caught at it. Outrage about torture that switches on only when the other side does it is not a conviction. It is a team uniform. And there is a quieter failure on the left as well, the contempt that treats the interrogator as a monster rather than a neighbor, which abandons exactly the person the church most needs to reach.

That person may be sitting in the back pew. The psychiatrist Jonathan Shay, working with Vietnam veterans, gave a name in *Achilles in Vietnam* (1994) to the wound that comes when what is right is betrayed by someone who holds legitimate authority, in a high-stakes situation: moral injury. Clinicians since have applied the term to people who carry out acts that violate their own conscience, and the essay on [moral injury and veterans](/writing/moral-injury-veterans) takes up that wound at length. Torture wounds the torturer too. The one who trains himself to be unmoved by another man's suffering has done more than perform a task. He has formed himself, and he often finds afterward that something in him cannot be undone. If you carry that, or if the weight of it has ever made you think about ending your life, there is real help. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, and veterans can press 1 to reach the Veterans Crisis Line.

The church can offer that man only as much mercy as it has the honesty to name his wound. If it can't say plainly that what he was asked to do is condemned by God, it can't say plainly that he can be forgiven for it, because absolution is only as serious as the diagnosis. A fog of moral uncertainty gives him nothing. Clarity and mercy arrive together, or neither arrives.

Some questions remain open, and serious Christians will answer them differently: where lawful pressure in interrogation ends, how a believer serves faithfully inside military and intelligence institutions, and how a just state supervises the people it asks to do hard things. Those require expertise the pulpit doesn't have. But the prohibition itself sits closer to the foundation. It follows from what the church confesses about the image of God, the incarnation and the resurrection of the body, and it doesn't wait for a policy paper.

I came to faith from atheism, and part of what drew me was that Christianity refused to treat what happens to bodies as merely political. The God who made a body his own was struck in the face by an officer who wanted a different answer, flogged by soldiers under orders, and nailed up by a state that believed its circumstances required it. He rose in that body with the wounds still in it. The church that bears his name has sometimes held the whip. The only forgiveness open to it is the one that was open to the officer and the soldiers: to stand in the light of those wounds and let them tell the truth about us. Remember those who are in prison, the letter says, since you also are in the body. We have not yet finished remembering.
