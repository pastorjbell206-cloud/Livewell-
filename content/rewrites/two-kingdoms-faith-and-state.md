The phrase usually arrives as a way to end a conversation. A Christian raises a question about a law, a war, a border or a court, and someone answers with Jesus: render unto Caesar what is Caesar's. The line is meant to settle things. Faith goes in one drawer and politics in another, and a serious believer keeps the drawers shut. Just as often it arrives from the other direction, paired with Romans 13, to say that whatever the government commands, a Christian's job is to comply.

Both uses leave people uneasy. Many Christians carry two fears at once. They're afraid the church has become a wing of a political party, praying in the colors of a campaign. And they're afraid that if the church steps back, it will abandon the poor, the unborn, the immigrant and the prisoner to whoever holds power. Skeptics carry a sharper version of the same worry. They have watched Christians quote Romans 13 for governments they like and Acts 5:29 for governments they don't, and they suspect the Bible is being used as a weathervane. The fears are right, and the suspicion is often earned. That is what makes the question hard.

I have preached the render line as if Jesus came to teach us how to keep our heads down. I was wrong, and the text knew it before I did. The passage has to be read in its setting, beside the texts the church has always read with it, and then through the traditions that tried to draw the line between faith and state, including where each went badly wrong. The claim to be earned is this. Jesus did not divide life into two drawers of equal size. He handed Caesar back a coin and claimed for God everything that bears God's image, Caesar included. That leaves the state real, limited and answerable, and it leaves the church with neither a throne nor permission to be silent.

### The coin in the Temple courts

The question was asked in the last week of Jesus's life, in the Temple courts, by men who had been sent. Mark is blunt about the purpose: "they sent to him some of the Pharisees and some of the Herodians, to trap him in his talk" (Mark 12:13, ESV). The pairing matters. The Pharisees resented Roman rule as an affront to Israel's God. The Herodians owed their standing to the Roman order and to the house of Herod that served it. They agreed on very little, except that this man had to be stopped, a point Mark had already made nine chapters earlier (Mark 3:6). Just before they arrive, Jesus has told the parable of the tenants who kill the owner's son, and the leaders "perceived that he had told the parable against them" (Mark 12:12, ESV). The tax question is their reply.

The tax was not an abstraction. When Rome took direct control of Judea in AD 6 and ordered a census to assess it, Judas the Galilean raised a revolt on the ground that paying tribute to Rome meant accepting mortal masters in place of God. Josephus records the rising in *The Jewish War* (c. 75), and Luke has Gamaliel remember it before the Sanhedrin (Acts 5:37). The revolt was crushed. Its conviction wasn't. For a devout Jew the head tax was a small, recurring confession of who owned the land. So the question "Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" (Mark 12:14, ESV) had two edges. Say yes, and the crowd that longed for God's kingdom hears a collaborator. Say no, and the Herodians have a charge of sedition to carry to the governor. The flattery that opens the question, that he truly teaches the way of God, is the bait.

Jesus asks them to bring him a denarius. He doesn't produce one himself; they do, inside the Temple precincts, which is its own quiet indictment. The coin most scholars identify as the tribute money of the period is a silver denarius of Tiberius, whose inscription named him the son of the divine Augustus and whose reverse styled him high priest. To a scrupulous Jew it was a graven claim to divinity carried in the purse. "Whose likeness and inscription is this?" Jesus asks, and they answer, "Caesar's" (Mark 12:16, ESV). Then comes the line: "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Mark 12:17, ESV).

Two words in the Greek carry most of the weight. The verb, *apodote*, means to give back, to pay what is owed. Caesar's claim is not denied. The coin bears his image, circulates in his economy and goes back to him. Jesus leads no tax revolt, and any reading that turns every government into Pharaoh has to explain why he didn't. The second word is *eikōn*, likeness or image, the word the Greek Old Testament uses when God creates humanity "in his own image" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The question left hanging in the courts is what bears God's image, and the answer is standing there holding the coin. Tertullian saw it early in the third century. In *On Idolatry* he reads the saying as giving Caesar's image, stamped on the money, to Caesar, and God's image, stamped on the human being, to God: the coin to the emperor, yourself to God.

That is why the two-drawer reading fails, though it isn't foolish. It assumes the drawers are roughly the same size, the soul and Sunday for God, the body and the budget and the army for Caesar. The saying doesn't divide reality in half. It puts a fence around a coin. Everything that bears God's image belongs to God, including the emperor whose face is on the silver. And yet the fence is real, and the coin does go back. Jesus neither sanctifies Caesar nor abolishes him. He relativizes him, which is the one thing Caesar can't abide.

Rome understood the answer better than many modern readers do. A few days later the accusation brought to Pilate was, "We found this man misleading our nation and forbidding us to give tribute to Caesar, and saying that he himself is Christ, a king" (Luke 23:2, ESV). The first charge was false. A man who says the emperor is owed his coin but not his subjects has not stayed out of politics. He has denied the emperor the last word.

The saying settles that the state has a genuine but bounded claim, and that God, not Caesar, draws the boundary. It doesn't say where the boundary runs in a particular law, war or court case. For that the church has read Mark 12 alongside other texts, and argued, for two thousand years.

### Romans 13 and Revelation 13: the same power, two faces

Romans 13 opens with the most quoted and least read sentence Paul wrote about the state: "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God" (Romans 13:1, ESV). Read in its setting, it does something narrower and more demanding than bless whoever rules.

Paul wrote to the Christians in Rome in the mid-50s, early in Nero's reign and a few years after Claudius had expelled Jews from the city (Acts 18:2). Within a decade, as Tacitus records in his *Annals*, Nero would blame the fire of 64 on the Christians and burn some of them alive as torches in his gardens. Paul was writing to a vulnerable minority in the capital, and the paragraph just before chapter 13 matters: "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Romans 12:19, ESV). A few sentences later the governing authority is "the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4, ESV). The connection is deliberate. The Christian lays down private vengeance, and God doesn't leave wrong unanswered in this age; he has appointed an office to restrain it. That office is defined by a job: "rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad" (Romans 13:3, ESV). A job defined is a job that can be failed.

Then Paul echoes his Lord. "Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed, revenue to whom revenue is owed, respect to whom respect is owed, honor to whom honor is owed" (Romans 13:7, ESV). The verb is the one Jesus used over the coin, *apodote*. And the very next sentence widens the debt past anything Caesar can collect: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other" (Romans 13:8, ESV). The state receives its due. Love is a debt never paid off, and it isn't owed to the state.

Now set beside this the other thirteenth chapter. Revelation was written, on the most common dating, in the 90s, when the churches of Asia were pressed to honor the imperial cult. John sees a beast rise from the sea, and "to it the dragon gave his power and his throne and great authority" (Revelation 13:2, ESV). The word for authority, *exousia*, is the word Paul used. The same Roman power Paul called God's servant, John sees as a beast. And its crime is not taxation or order. It is worship: "they worshiped the beast, saying, 'Who is like the beast, and who can fight against it?'" (Revelation 13:4, ESV). Even here its reach is bounded, for "it was allowed to make war on the saints" (Revelation 13:7, ESV), and the saints aren't told to take up arms. "Here is a call for the endurance and faith of the saints" (Revelation 13:10, ESV).

The two chapters don't contradict each other. They describe one institution under two conditions. When the state judges, restraining wrong and protecting the neighbor, it is a servant, and Christians honor it. When the state claims worship, when it asks to be the source of meaning, the final object of loyalty, the power no one can fight, it has become a beast, and Christians refuse it, still without taking up the sword.

Between the two stands Acts 5:29. Ordered by the Sanhedrin to stop teaching in Jesus's name, "Peter and the apostles answered, 'We must obey God rather than men'" (Acts 5:29, ESV). Notice what happened next. They were beaten and released, and they left "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (Acts 5:41, ESV). They disobeyed the command and didn't resist the penalty. That points to the distinction the whole tradition turns on. Submission and obedience are not the same thing. Paul's word in Romans 13:1 is "be subject," which means to recognize an order as God's. Obedience is doing what a particular command requires. A Christian can refuse a command that would require sin and remain subject, accepting the court, the fine or the cell. The apostles were never more subject to the governing authorities than when they took the flogging for disobeying them. And the Jesus who told Pilate, "You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above" (John 19:11, ESV), let that authority crucify him.

### Two, not one: Augustine and Gelasius

The church's first great attempt to hold these texts together came after the faith had moved from the arena to the palace. Constantine made Christianity legal in 313; Theodosius made it the religion of the empire in 380. Then in 410 Alaric's Goths sacked Rome, and pagans blamed the Christians for abandoning the gods who had kept the city safe. Augustine's answer, *The City of God*, written between 413 and 426, gave the Western church its basic grammar. Two loves, he wrote, have made two cities: love of self to the contempt of God made the earthly city, and love of God to the contempt of self made the heavenly (14.28). The cities aren't addresses. They run mingled through every empire and every congregation until the judgment. The pilgrim city makes use of the earthly peace, obeys its laws and seeks its order, so long as the worship of the true God isn't hindered (19.17), but it never mistakes that peace for home. And Augustine could be unsparing about the state's pretensions. Take away justice, he asked, and what are kingdoms but great robberies (4.4)?

Augustine also shows the tradition's first failure, and it is his own. In the long struggle with the Donatists he came to defend imperial coercion against schismatics, reading the parable's "compel people to come in" (Luke 14:23, ESV) as warrant, in letters from around 408 onward. The theologian who best explained why no earthly city is the city of God asked the earthly city's sword to do the church's work. Every tradition that follows carries a version of that contradiction, and none of us gets to judge it from outside.

Eighty years later, in 494, Pope Gelasius I wrote to the emperor Anastasius in Constantinople a sentence that framed a thousand years of argument. There are two, he wrote, by which this world is chiefly ruled: the sacred authority of the bishops and the royal power. His Latin words were *auctoritas* and *potestas*, and the distinction is sharper than it looks. Power is the capacity to compel. Authority is the standing to judge what is right. In the sacraments, Gelasius told the emperor, he bows his head to the priests; in matters of public order, the ministers of religion obey the emperor's laws. Gelasius claimed the heavier weight for the priests, because they must answer to God even for kings, but his letter describes two offices, each limited by the other.

The balance didn't hold. Through the investiture struggles of the eleventh century to Boniface VIII's bull *Unam Sanctam* in 1302, which placed both the spiritual and the temporal sword in the church's power, the two became in practice one with a subordinate. From the other side, kings spent centuries treating bishops as officers of the crown. What survived the misuse was Gelasius's insight: a people is safer when the one who holds the sword isn't also the one who pronounces final judgment on the soul.

### The Reformation's three answers

Martin Luther wrote *Temporal Authority: To What Extent It Should Be Obeyed* in 1523, after several German princes banned his translation of the New Testament. God, he argued, governs the world through two governments. The spiritual government makes people righteous through the Word and the Spirit; the temporal government restrains the wicked by the sword so that there is outward peace. If everyone were a true Christian the sword would be unnecessary, but the world isn't, and to rule it by the gospel alone would be, in Luther's image, like penning wolves and sheep together and telling them to keep the peace. So the Christian, who needs no sword for himself, may serve it for his neighbor's sake, as judge or constable or prince. But the sword's reach ends at body and property. It can't compel faith, because faith is free, and heresy is a spiritual thing that no iron can cut and no fire can burn. If a prince orders you to surrender your New Testament, Luther told his readers, refuse him and bear what follows. There Acts 5:29 applies.

That is the two-kingdoms doctrine at its strongest: real honor for the state, a real limit on it, and the conscience guarded from pope and prince alike. Its weakness showed within two years. In the Peasants' War of 1525, Luther's *Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants* urged the princes to crush the uprising without mercy, and tens of thousands died. A doctrine that forbade the sword to touch the conscience had too little to say to princes about how they used it on the poor. Over the following centuries a strand of German Lutheranism hardened the two governments into two sealed rooms: the church preaches to souls, the state governs by its own laws, and the church has no word to speak into the state's room. That hardened version isn't what Luther wrote. It grew in his soil.

John Calvin closed the final edition of his *Institutes* (1559) with a chapter on civil government (IV.20), agreeing with Luther that human life stands under a twofold government. But Calvin honored the magistrate more highly. He called civil authority not only lawful before God but the most sacred and honorable of all callings. He also gave the state a religious task Luther had hesitated over, to protect the outward worship of God and defend sound doctrine. That conviction had consequences. In Geneva in 1553 Michael Servetus was burned for heresy with Calvin's approval of the verdict. Yet Calvin also built the brake. Where lesser magistrates exist to restrain the willfulness of kings, as the ephors did at Sparta and the tribunes at Rome, he argued that they betray the people if they wink at tyranny (IV.20.31), and a long line of Reformed resistance grew from that paragraph. He ended the entire *Institutes* on Acts 5:29, reminding his readers that they were bought with a price and must not become slaves to the wicked desires of men (1 Corinthians 7:23).

The most radical answer came from the Reformation's own victims. In February 1527 a gathering of Swiss Brethren at Schleitheim adopted seven articles drafted largely by Michael Sattler. The sixth concerns the sword, and it doesn't call the state evil. It says the sword is ordained of God outside the perfection of Christ, to punish the wicked and protect the good. But inside the church the only discipline is the ban, and a Christian follows the Christ who refused to be made king (John 6:15) and refused to judge a property dispute between brothers (Luke 12:14). So a Christian should not be a magistrate or bear the sword. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) gave that conviction its strongest modern form: the church is itself a visible political community whose politics is the way of the cross, or, as Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon put it in *Resident Aliens* (1989), the church doesn't have a social strategy; the church is a social strategy.

The authorities answered with the sword. Felix Manz had already been drowned by the Protestant council of Zurich in January 1527, and Sattler was tortured and burned by Catholic authorities at Rottenburg that May. Protestants and Catholics, who agreed on almost nothing, agreed that such people had to be stopped, which ought to sound familiar from Mark 12.

I pastor a Baptist church, and Baptists owe more to these people than we usually admit. The conviction that faith cannot be coerced, that the church is a gathered people rather than a parish of everyone born within its bounds, and that the magistrate has no business with the conscience was paid for in their blood. Roger Williams, the founder of Rhode Island and for a short time of the first Baptist church in America, wrote in 1644 of a hedge or wall of separation between the garden of the church and the wilderness of the world. By the time Thomas Jefferson wrote of a wall in his 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists, the image had long meant, for dissenters, protection of the church from the state, never the banishment of faith from public life.

### Kuyper, and the test at Barmen

Abraham Kuyper gave the modern Reformed answer in a lecture titled *Sphere Sovereignty*, delivered at the opening of the Free University of Amsterdam on 20 October 1880. Society, he argued, isn't a pyramid with the state at the top. Family, church, school, science, art and commerce each hold an authority directly from God rather than on loan from the state, and the state's task is to guard the boundaries between them, protect the weak inside them and keep the whole in justice without swallowing any part. In that lecture he said there is not a square inch of human existence that Christ, sovereign over all, does not claim as his own. Kuyper later served as prime minister of the Netherlands (1901–1905). He refuses both the private drawer and the state that owns everything. His weakness is practical: someone has to draw the boundaries of the spheres, the one with the power usually does, and Christians with majorities have heard "every square inch" as a license rather than a limit.

The hardest test came in Germany. After Hitler took power in January 1933, the German Christians, a movement seeking a church conformed to National Socialism, swept the Protestant church elections that July, and the Aryan paragraph was pressed on the church to bar pastors of Jewish descent. Much of the acquiescence drew on the sealed-room version of two kingdoms. At the end of May 1934 a synod at Barmen adopted a declaration drafted largely by Karl Barth. It confessed Jesus Christ, as Scripture attests him, as the one Word of God the church must hear and trust and obey in life and in death, and it rejected the teaching that there are areas of life in which we belong not to Christ but to other lords. Its fifth thesis, the least quoted and the most useful, drew the line in both directions. The state has by divine appointment the task of providing for justice and peace in a world not yet redeemed, by the threat and use of force. The church rejects the teaching that the state should become the single and total order of human life. And it rejects, just as firmly, the teaching that the church should take on the character, tasks and dignity of the state and become one of its organs. Within weeks a group of Lutheran theologians answered with the Ansbach Memorandum, grounding obedience in the orders of creation and thanking God for the Führer. That is what two kingdoms becomes when we forget that both answer to one Lord.

Barmen has its own silence. It defended the church's freedom to confess Christ more clearly than it defended the neighbors being stripped of their rights, and it did not name the Jews at all. A church can say every true thing about Christ's lordship over the state and still fail the people the state is crushing.

### Where the argument lands, and what it leaves open

Oliver O'Donovan's *The Desire of the Nations* (1996) gives the most careful account of why these pieces belong together. After the resurrection and ascension, he argues, rulers aren't abolished but desacralized. Christ has triumphed, and the authority left to governments in the time between his ascension and return is essentially the authority of judgment, discerning right from wrong and giving it public effect. They no longer carry a people's salvation or identity. That is Romans 13's servant exactly, and it names the beast's crime in Revelation 13: an office of judgment that has claimed to be a god.

The order of the question needs naming. That Jesus Christ is Lord, seated at the Father's right hand and coming to judge the living and the dead, is first-order; the creeds bind it. How that lordship bears on civil government, whether a Christian may bear the sword, whether the state should protect true worship, how far resistance may go, is second-order. Lutherans, Reformed, Catholics, Anabaptists and Baptists have disagreed about it for five centuries and remained Christians. The argument here lands where its sources lead, close to Augustine and Barmen: two, and one Lord. The state is real and good in its place, and Christians owe it taxes, honor and submission. It is also limited, and the church is the community that says its limit out loud, from outside, with the freedom of a people whose King the state once executed. When the state commands what God forbids, the Christian disobeys and remains subject.

The strongest objection comes from Schleitheim, and this essay hasn't fully answered it. If the state's vocation is judgment backed by force, and Jesus said, "Do not resist the one who is evil" (Matthew 5:39, ESV), can the same disciple be the one who carries out wrath (Romans 13:4)? Paul sets 12:19 beside 13:4 as though they fit, but never says a Christian holds the office. Luther's answer, that the Christian bears the sword for his neighbor and never for himself, is serious. It has also covered a great deal of violence that served no neighbor. The Anabaptists paid for their answer with their own lives; the magisterial churches often paid for theirs with other people's. A reader who lands with Schleitheim stands in an honorable line.

### What we have handed to Caesar

The same instrument cuts in every direction: what are we giving Caesar that bears God's image? On the right, the temptation is the old one Barmen refused, the church as the nation's chaplain, certain the kingdom is one election away. Jesus was offered every kingdom of the world for a single act of worship (Matthew 4:8–9) and refused. On the left, the church becomes the conscience of a party, its statements predictable from the platform, quietly expecting the right state to deliver a redemption only Christ brings. One makes the state guardian of the church's identity, the other the agent of the kingdom's justice, and both ask Caesar for more than a coin. The private drawer is no refuge. William Cavanaugh argues in *The Myth of Religious Violence* (2009) that the story of the secular state rescuing Europe from the wars of religion has served to legitimate the state's own claim on the loyalty people will die for. Something always fills the space God is told to vacate. Not every error weighs the same, and the church has to say which are graver, but the test is the same for all.

I have stood in election seasons and felt the pull to believe that if my side simply won, the kingdom would advance. The gauge of my hope on election night has told me which kingdom I was serving, and it hasn't always flattered me.

The man who said "render to Caesar" was executed by Caesar's governor on the charge of forbidding it. Pilate's authority was real, given from above, and he used it to crucify the one who gave it. On the third day God overturned the verdict, and the ascended Christ now holds the throne every Caesar borrows. So pay the tax and honor the office. But the question the trap sprang on Jesus now springs on us. It isn't whether we'll pay. It is what we've quietly handed to Caesar that was never his: our fear, our hope, the last word about who holds history. Caesar's face is on the silver. God's image is on us, and on the neighbor the state is tempted to forget, and on Caesar himself.
