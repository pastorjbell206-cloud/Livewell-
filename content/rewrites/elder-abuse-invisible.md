Most of us, if we think about elder abuse at all, picture a scandal: a nursing home exposed by the local news, an aide caught on a hidden camera, a stranger on the phone talking a widow out of her savings. Those things happen. But they flatter the rest of us, because they put the harm somewhere else, in an institution we would never choose or a criminal we would never become. The quieter truth is that much of the harm done to older adults is done by people they know, often by the very people they love and depend on, and very little of it is ever reported.

The commandment to honor father and mother was not first spoken to toddlers. It was spoken at Sinai to grown men and women, and its first meaning is the one we least want to hear: when the people who carried you can no longer carry themselves, you carry them. The church has preached that in general and mostly missed it in particular. The particular looks like a power of attorney quietly turned into a private bank, a parent left unwashed and underfed by the relative who moved in to help, a grandmother frightened into silence by the grandson whose name is on her lease, and a congregation that sees her every Sunday and notices nothing.

So before the argument, the care. If you're an older adult being hurt, or you fear that someone you love is, you don't need to be certain before you ask for help. The Eldercare Locator, at 1-800-677-1116, is a free national line that will connect you with Adult Protective Services and other help where you live. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. If you're in crisis, including if you're a worn-out caregiver frightened by what you feel or thinking about ending your life, call or text 988. A call is a question, not a verdict; sorting out what is happening belongs to people trained for it. This essay is written by a pastor. Nothing in it is legal, medical or financial advice, and the law on reporting, guardianship and powers of attorney differs from state to state.

Elder abuse is not mainly a problem of monsters. It grows in a culture that stopped giving old age its weight, among families and churches, mine included, that absorbed that culture without noticing. The Bible's answer is an obligation owed by adults to adults, bound to the fear of God, and it makes the church responsible for seeing.

## What elder abuse is, and why so little of it is seen

A widely used definition comes from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, which published uniform definitions in 2016, prepared by Jeffrey Hall, Debra Karch and Alex Crosby. In paraphrase, elder abuse is an intentional act, or a failure to act, by a caregiver or someone else in a relationship where trust is expected, that harms a person sixty or older or puts her at serious risk of harm. The CDC names five forms: physical abuse, the deliberate use of force that causes pain or injury; sexual abuse, any sexual contact that is forced or unwanted, including with someone who can no longer consent; emotional or psychological abuse, words and behavior that frighten, humiliate or control, including cutting a person off from those who would notice; neglect, the failure of someone responsible for her to meet basic needs such as food, hygiene and medical care; and financial abuse or exploitation, the improper use of her money, property or benefits for someone else's gain. The National Center on Elder Abuse adds abandonment to the list and treats self-neglect as a concern as well.

One phrase in that definition carries the whole subject: a relationship where trust is expected. It draws the distinction this essay turns on. Strangers harm the old too, but the definition is built around harm done inside trust: inside families and caregiving arrangements, the relationships we most want to believe are safe. A stranger has to find a way in. A son already has the key.

How common is it? In 2017 Yongjie Yon and colleagues pooled prevalence studies from many countries in *The Lancet Global Health* and estimated that roughly one in six people sixty and older living in the community had experienced some form of abuse in the previous year, with psychological abuse reported most often and financial abuse next. In the United States, the National Elder Mistreatment Study led by Ron Acierno (2010) found that roughly one in ten had suffered emotional, physical or sexual mistreatment or potential neglect in the past year, before financial abuse was counted. Both depend on people well enough to answer a survey, so both likely miss the most impaired.

The more unsettling figure concerns what goes unseen. *Under the Radar*, a 2011 study of New York State by Lifespan of Greater Rochester, Weill Cornell Medical Center and the New York City Department for the Aging, compared what older residents reported in a survey with the cases known to the state's agencies. For every case the agencies knew about, the researchers estimated, roughly twenty-four others remained unknown to any of them. One state is not the nation, and the method has limits, but the gap is hard to ignore.

The silence makes sense once the trust in the definition is taken seriously. The woman being harmed often depends on the person harming her for meals, rides, medicine and company, and naming the harm may cost her all of it. She may fear being moved out of her home, or be ashamed because the one hurting her is the child she raised, or still love him. She may be losing the very capacities she would need to recognize and report it. Some of it, too, is violence between aging spouses, which the essay on [the church and domestic violence](/writing/church-domestic-violence) takes up. Abuse of the old is not invisible because it is rare. Everything about it, the dependence, the shame, the love, the failing memory, works to keep it quiet.

## How old age lost its weight

The deeper cause of that silence runs through the way whole societies decide what an old person is worth, and Israel's law answered that question in its own way. "You shall stand up before the gray head and honor the face of an old man, and you shall fear your God: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:32, ESV). Its closing clause appears earlier in the same chapter. "You shall not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:14, ESV). The fear of God is attached to wrongs the victim may never detect. The deaf man cannot hear the curse, and the blind man cannot see who set the stone in his path, so the law reminds the one tempted to do it that someone else can. An old woman who no longer follows her own bank statements stands where the blind man stood. The law's warning is not that she will catch you. It is that God sees what she cannot.

The early church inherited that law and widened it. First Timothy 5 shows a congregation already keeping a list of widows sixty and older; whatever the list's exact purpose, the chapter assumes the church is feeding widows who have no one else. Around 197 Tertullian described in his *Apology* the common fund Christians kept, and among those it supported he named old people who could no longer leave their houses. Demetrios Constantelos showed in *Byzantine Philanthropy and Social Welfare* (1968) that the Christian East endowed houses for the aged, the *gerokomeia*, alongside hospitals and orphanages, as works of piety. And in 1839 a poor Breton woman named Jeanne Jugan took an elderly blind widow into her home in Saint-Servan and gave her own bed to her, and the Little Sisters of the Poor grew from that act. The line from Leviticus to Jugan is not a line of institutions. It is a line of people who believed the old belonged to them.

What changed in the modern West was first a way of life, not a doctrine. Industrial work separated the home from the place of labor, and an older person who could no longer work became a cost to the household rather than a contributor to it. American poorhouses filled with the aged poor, and the twentieth century answered that cruelty with real goods: the Social Security Act of 1935, then Medicare and the Older Americans Act in 1965. Poverty among the old fell sharply. Christians tempted to romanticize the multigenerational farmhouse should admit that it had its own neglect behind the same closed doors, and that a pension check has kept more widows fed than any sermon.

But the goods came with a shift in imagination. In 1969 the psychiatrist Robert Butler coined the word *ageism*, and in *Why Survive? Being Old in America* (1975) he argued that the country's neglect of its old expressed what it valued: productivity, youth and independence. Atul Gawande argued in *Being Mortal* (2014) that our institutions for the aged were designed around safety and medical management rather than around what the old person herself was living for. Neither was describing abuse. Both were describing the conditions that make it easy. Once old age is a problem to be managed, the old person becomes a case and a cost, and the man helping himself to his mother's accounts can tell himself he is only managing.

The church did not resist this shift. It absorbed it. Many congregations built their ministries around young families, the people we call the church's future, while the old sat in the pews they had filled for fifty years, faithful and largely unattended. We handed much of the care of the old to the state and the market, which was often wise. Then we forgot that the honor had never been handed over with it.

## The fifth commandment was written for grown children

"Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12, ESV). We teach it to children, but the Ten Commandments were addressed to the adults of a covenant people, the ones who owned oxen and stood as witnesses in court. Its first audience included the forty-year-old man whose father could no longer walk to the well.

The Hebrew verb translated *honor* comes from a root that means to be heavy. To honor someone is to give him weight, to treat his life as something that counts. Proverbs applies it to old age. "Listen to your father who gave you life, and do not despise your mother when she is old" (Proverbs 23:22, ESV). It names a late temptation: a formidable mother grows slow and needy, and her grown child begins to find her tiresome. Contempt for the old rarely starts with a blow. It starts with a sigh, and with the quiet decision that her wishes no longer weigh very much.

Jesus pressed this commandment harder than the religious experts of his day, and when he did, the issue was money. In Mark 7, challenged over his disciples' unwashed hands, he turned the charge back on his accusers.

> "You have a fine way of rejecting the commandment of God in order to establish your tradition!" (Mark 7:9, ESV)

He then cited the fifth commandment and described the practice of *Corban*. As it is usually reconstructed, a man could declare his property dedicated to God, and once the vow was spoken, the tradition held that he could no longer use it to support his father or mother, though he might go on enjoying it himself. Jesus named what that accomplished: "thus making void the word of God by your tradition that you have handed down. And many such things you do" (Mark 7:13, ESV). A religious vow had become a device for walking away from aging parents, with the added advantage of sounding devout.

We don't say Corban. We say we have our own family to think about, that the church needs our time, that Mom's money is coming to us eventually, so there's no harm in using some of it now. That last sentence is the most dangerous in this whole subject, because it lets a son exploit his mother while feeling like an heir rather than a thief. Those of us who work in churches should hear that most sharply, since we have the most practice at making religious duty a reason to be somewhere else.

Paul, instructing Timothy on the care of widows, begins not with the congregation but with the family. "But if a widow has children or grandchildren, let them first learn to show godliness to their own household and to make some return to their parents, for this is pleasing in the sight of God" (1 Timothy 5:4, ESV). *Make some return* is the language of a debt. The care a grown child gives an aging parent is not charity. It is repayment, partial and never complete, for years of being carried.

A few verses later the tone grows severe. "But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever" (1 Timothy 5:8, ESV). The comparison had force because pagan neighbors already expected children to support aging parents, so a Christian who abandoned his mother fell below their ordinary decency. And from the cross Jesus himself committed his mother to the disciple he loved: "And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home" (John 19:27, ESV). If the Son of God did not treat his mother's keeping as a distraction from redemption, none of us can treat it as a distraction from our work.

One limit must be named, because this commandment has been used as a weapon. Honoring a parent never requires an adult child to submit to abuse, to move back into the house where she was harmed, or to become the hands-on caregiver of a father who beat her. Some adult children cannot safely be in the room. For them honor may mean making sure others arrange the care, that someone checks, and that the parent is neither exploited nor abandoned, from a distance that keeps the child safe. The commandment asks that we give our parents weight. It does not ask anyone to be crushed under it.

## The exhausted caregiver and the line that does not move

Anyone who has cared for a declining parent will have read the last section with a tightening in the chest, not because they are guilty of Corban but because they are exhausted. In 1981 the social worker Dorothy Miller named the "sandwich generation," adult children pressed between the needs of their own children and the needs of their aging parents. People now live longer, often with long stretches of dementia or chronic illness, and much of the daily work of keeping them fed, bathed, medicated and safe falls on unpaid relatives, most often daughters and wives, many of whom are also holding jobs.

In 1980 Steven Zarit and his colleagues gave researchers a way to measure what they called caregiver burden among relatives of people with dementia, and they found that the sense of burden tracked less with how impaired the patient was than with whether the caregiver was left alone with it: it was lighter where other relatives visited more often. That is the truth behind the tired woman at the back of the sanctuary who hasn't missed a Sunday in years and hasn't slept through a night in months.

The strongest objection to an essay like this deserves its full hearing. The church is not an investigative agency, and pastors are not trained in geriatric medicine or forensic accounting. A person with memory loss may accuse a devoted daughter of theft because she moved the checkbook, or insist all is well when it is not. A false accusation can destroy a caregiver who has given up her career and her health to keep her mother at home, and a congregation that treats every hard season as possible abuse will wound the innocent and teach families to hide. Prudence, fairness and humility about what we cannot see are not cowardice. They are justice owed to the accused, who also bears the image of God.

All of that is true, and none of it cancels the obligation. The way through is a distinction: burden is not abuse. The daughter who snaps at her father at the end of an eighteen-hour day, and then weeps in the car because she hardly recognizes herself, is carrying a burden and needs help carrying it. The son who has taken over his mother's accounts, controls who may visit, answers every question put to her and has let her go without her medicine is doing something else. A church that doesn't understand exhaustion has no business speaking into these homes. But exhaustion never turns exploitation into care or makes neglect acceptable. The line does not move because the person on the wrong side of it is tired.

The two are connected. Isolated, unsupported caregiving is one of the conditions in which harm grows more likely, so help given to the caregiver is often protection given to the one cared for. The Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116, also connects families with caregiver support and respite services through their local agencies on aging. A caregiver who fears what she is becoming should call it, and tell her pastor. A caregiver who is thinking about harming herself, or is afraid she may hurt her parent, should call or text 988 today, and call 911 if anyone is in immediate danger.

In 1991 the ethicist Gilbert Meilaender wrote an essay for *First Things* under a deliberately startling title, "I Want to Burden My Loved Ones," answering the wish, common in living wills, not to be a burden to one's family. His argument, in paraphrase, was that wanting to spare our families every burden misunderstands a family, whose unchosen bonds mean precisely that we carry each other when we cannot carry ourselves. Paul put it more briefly. "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2, ESV). The command is addressed to the church, which means the daughter in the back pew was never supposed to bear her father alone, and if she has, the failure is not only hers. The essay on [how to die well](/writing/how-to-die-well) takes up the last stretch of that road.

## Money, strangers and the modern Corban

Financial exploitation deserves its own attention because it is the form most easily hidden in a family that looks fine from the pew. The woman arrives at church each week dressed and cheerful while at home her accounts are being emptied a withdrawal at a time. The instrument may be a power of attorney signed in good faith years earlier, a joint account, a quiet change to a will, or a house signed over to a relative who promised she could always live there. The person doing it often has legitimate access, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed. The bank sees an authorized signature. The congregation sees a devoted son.

Beside that sits the harm done by strangers, which lies at the edge of the formal definition but runs on the same logic. Older adults are targeted by callers posing as a grandchild in trouble who needs money wired tonight, by impostors claiming to be from a government agency or a bank, and by romance schemes that spend months building a relationship before asking for money. People over sixty report losses of billions of dollars a year to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center alone. The old are targeted because they often have savings, many live alone, and some are undergoing changes in judgment they cannot perceive in themselves. That brings us back to Leviticus. The scammer is the man who puts a stumbling block before the blind, and he chooses his victims because they cannot see it.

The church is not innocent here. A preacher on television who promises a harvest in return for a "seed gift" asks of a lonely widow on a fixed income the same thing any con artist asks: trust me with your money because of what I say will come back to you. Dressing the request in Scripture makes it worse, as the essay on [the prosperity gospel](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel) argues. But the temptation isn't only a celebrity's. Every pastor, and I am one, knows which members give most faithfully, and in many congregations a good share of them have gray hair. A church glad not to ask how a declining member is managing so long as her envelope keeps coming stands on the ground Jesus exposed in Mark 7.

The protections are ordinary and work best when set up early, while a person is well: deciding in advance whom she trusts with her affairs, and making sure more than one person has sight of them. An elder law attorney can explain how powers of attorney, trusts and guardianship work in your state; nothing here is legal or financial advice. What the church offers is not expertise but relationship. A deacon who has visited a woman for years is the person she is most likely to tell when something feels wrong, if anyone has ever asked.

## What a congregation is placed to see

The church sees what others miss, not because its people are wiser but because it gathers the same people in the same room week after week, for decades, and so can interrupt the isolation of the old. An abuser who cuts an old woman off from everyone who might notice has built the room in which everything else becomes possible. A congregation that keeps visiting her takes the walls down.

What it notices is rarely dramatic. A woman who never missed a Sunday has missed four. A talkative man has gone quiet, or seems afraid when a certain relative is present, or has a relative who now answers every question for him. Someone always neat looks unkempt or thinner, or is confused by bills she once managed easily. None of these proves anything. They are reasons to visit, to ask gently when the person can be spoken to alone, and, when the worry does not lift, to call the Eldercare Locator or the state's Adult Protective Services line and let people trained for the work decide what is happening. A congregation that has learned the signs and keeps the number where its visitors can find it has done most of what is asked. The rest is courage.

Proverbs puts that courage in the form of a command. "Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute" (Proverbs 31:8, ESV). The person whose impairment or fear has made it impossible to speak for herself is exactly whom the verse has in view. Scripture's widow, orphan and stranger were never an exhaustive list but the people with no one to speak for them, as the essay on [the widow, the orphan and the stranger](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger) traces, and the declining widow on the membership roll stands in their line.

Our blindness comes from more than one direction. Churches organized around growth have quietly ranked members by usefulness to the plan. A wider culture that measures dignity by autonomy has little to say to people whose autonomy is leaving them, except to speak more comfortably about ending their lives. Beneath both lies one assumption, that a person's worth tracks what she can still do.

Scripture refuses that assumption. The image of God does not wear out with the body that bears it. The woman whose memory is going is no less herself before the God who made her. John Swinton argues in *Dementia: Living in the Memories of God* (2012) that our identity is finally secured not by our own remembering but by God's remembering of us, so that a person who has forgotten almost everything has not been forgotten. (The essay on [disability and the image of God](/writing/disability-dignity-and-the-image-of-god) follows that conviction further.) So taking a confused woman's savings is not a gray area or a private family matter. It is a wrong done to a bearer of God's image in the hour of her greatest weakness, which on the Bible's own terms is the hour when God's attention to her is closest.

I have to put myself inside this. I have sat with older members, sensed that something was not right, and said nothing, because I wasn't sure, because I didn't want the conflict, because I didn't know what speaking up would set in motion. More than once I chose my own comfort over their safety. Naming that doesn't excuse it. It tells me what the correction will cost, and that the blindness this essay describes is not somebody else's.

The Psalms keep a prayer that only the old can pray with full understanding. "Do not cast me off in the time of old age; forsake me not when my strength is spent" (Psalm 71:9, ESV). Later in the psalm the fear becomes a purpose: "So even to old age and gray hairs, O God, do not forsake me, until I proclaim your might to another generation, your power to all those to come" (Psalm 71:18, ESV). The old are the memory of the people, and a church that sets them aside forgets its own story. God answers through Isaiah in the voice of a parent who never stops carrying the child, even when the child has gone gray: "even to your old age I am he, and to gray hairs I will carry you. I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save" (Isaiah 46:4, ESV). The God who carries the old will raise them imperishable, and nothing done to them in their weakness will stay hidden on that day. Until then the church is the body through which God carries them, and when we do not, the promise goes unkept in our hands.

"You shall stand up before the gray head and honor the face of an old man, and you shall fear your God: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:32, ESV). The verse holds three commands together as one motion. Many of us have learned the first two as manners. The third was always the one that mattered. On the membership roll of a great many churches there is a woman who has missed four Sundays, whose son now answers her phone, and whose money is going somewhere she can no longer follow. God sees her. The question the text leaves with us is whether anyone who sits near her will.
