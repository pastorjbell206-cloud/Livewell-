Most people who go looking for a church begin the same way. They move to a new town, or they leave a congregation that split or soured, or they come back to faith after years away, and they make a list. On the first Sunday they visit the first name on it. They notice the parking and the greeting, the music, whether the children's wing looks clean and whether anyone learns their name. They grade the sermon and compare notes on the drive home. The next week they try the second church. Some settle within a month. Many are still visiting a year later, and somewhere along the way the visiting became the habit, so that they belong everywhere a little and nowhere at all.

None of that is foolish. You can't join a church without choosing one, and where a dozen congregations sit inside a ten-minute drive, choosing means comparing. Some of what gets compared matters a great deal: whether the preaching is faithful, whether your children will be safe, whether you can worship there without being ambushed by memories of the place that hurt you. The question deserves a serious answer.

But the posture most of us bring to the search wasn't handed to us by Scripture or by the long memory of the church. It was built over roughly two centuries by a religious market that made American congregations compete for members, and it has been rehearsed in us every week by an economy that trains us to walk into any room asking what it offers. The searcher sits in the back row as a customer, and the church at the front has very often arranged itself as a store. This essay argues that there is an older and better guide, the marks by which the church has recognized itself since the fourth century and the sixteenth. It turns on one distinction. There is a difference between choosing a church and being received into one. The first is something you do. The second is something done to you, and it is the one that forms you.

## How Americans Learned to Shop for Church

For most of Christian history nobody shopped for a church, because there was nothing to shop among. In medieval Europe you belonged to the parish where you were born, and after the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 the ruler of a territory largely decided its confession. The American colonies carried the pattern over, with Congregational establishments in New England and an Anglican one in Virginia. Then the arrangement broke. The First Amendment in 1791 barred a national establishment, and the states followed one by one until Massachusetts, the last, disestablished its churches in 1833. Religious liberty is a real good, and Baptists, whose name the church I serve carries, were among its most stubborn champions. But it had a consequence nobody fully planned. Once no church was guaranteed its people, every church had to win them.

The sociologists Roger Finke and Rodney Stark told this story in economic terms in *The Churching of America, 1776-1990* (1992). Their argument is that when the state stops protecting a religious monopoly, the churches become competing suppliers in a free religious economy, and participation rises rather than falls. By their estimate only about 17 percent of Americans were church adherents on the eve of the Revolution, and the share climbed through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as upstart movements outworked the old establishments. The winners were the Methodists and Baptists, whose circuit riders and farmer-preachers lived close to their people; the losers were the old colonial establishments. But notice the direction their evidence cuts. The movements that grew were the ones that asked more of their members, not less, and denominations tended to decline as they made their demands easier. Even the market, read honestly, does not reward the church that tries hardest to please.

Nathan Hatch's *The Democratization of American Christianity* (1989) supplies the cultural side of the same story. Between the Revolution and about 1830, Hatch shows, a wave of populist preachers (Methodists, Baptists, the "Christians" gathered around Barton Stone and Alexander Campbell, and Black preachers building their own churches) told ordinary people they could read the Bible for themselves and judge any minister by it. In 1804 Stone and his colleagues dissolved their presbytery in a document they called the Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery, and "no creed but the Bible" became a watchword of the movement that followed. Hatch honors what this gave the poor, the unschooled and the enslaved, who found a voice the establishments had denied them. He also shows its cost. The audience became sovereign. A preacher who couldn't hold a crowd lost it to one who could, the hearer became the judge of doctrine, and choice became the native posture of the American Christian.

The twentieth century turned the posture into a method. In *Understanding Church Growth* (1970), the missiologist Donald McGavran argued that people like to become Christians without crossing racial, linguistic or class barriers, and a generation of church planters learned to design congregations around a single kind of person. Willow Creek Community Church, founded in 1975, famously began by asking its unchurched neighbors why they stayed away and then built a service that removed the reasons. Much of this was done in real love for outsiders, yet its shape was the shape of a store. In *Working the Angles* (1987) Eugene Peterson described American pastors becoming a company of shopkeepers, preoccupied with keeping the customers happy and luring them away from the competition down the street. I write as a pastor, and that description falls on those of us who pastor before it falls on anyone in the pews. We taught the searcher to shop by arranging ourselves to be shopped for.

James K. A. Smith's *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) explains why the habit is so hard to shed. Smith argues that human beings are not first of all thinking things but lovers, creatures whose desires are aimed by liturgies, repeated bodily practices that carry a picture of the good life and train us to want it. His most memorable example is the shopping mall, which he reads as a temple with its own rites: the pilgrimage, the window icons of the life you lack, the transaction that promises to make you whole. Most of us have attended that liturgy more faithfully than any church's, so we carry it through the church door and, without deciding anything, treat the congregation as a supplier and ourselves as the judges of its offer. The consumer instinct isn't a belief we hold. It is a habit that holds us.

Scripture saw it coming. In his last letter, written from prison to a young pastor, Paul charges Timothy to preach the word in season and out of season, and then he explains why the charge is urgent: "For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" (2 Timothy 4:3, ESV). The warning falls first on hearers who assemble a church to their own specification, and only then on the preachers who oblige them. Itching ears are a customer's condition before they are a preacher's temptation, and none of us is immune to them.

## What Makes a Church a Church

Before any question of preference comes a prior one: what are you looking for when you look for a church at all?

In 381 the Council of Constantinople expanded the creed of Nicaea into the form most Christians still say, and it confesses belief in "one holy catholic and apostolic Church." The church appears there as an article of faith, alongside the Father, the Son and the Spirit, which means the church is something Christ gives before it is something anyone builds. The four words are marks, the signs by which a congregation can be recognized as belonging to the one body he is gathering across every century.

The Reformers asked more sharply where the church can be known to be present. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 answered in its seventh article that the church is the assembly of believers in which the gospel is purely taught and the sacraments are rightly administered. It added that agreement on the gospel and the sacraments is enough for true unity, and that human traditions, rites and ceremonies need not be the same everywhere. John Calvin said much the same in his *Institutes* (1559, book 4, chapter 1, section 9). The Belgic Confession, written in 1561 by Guido de Brès, a pastor to the persecuted Reformed churches of the Low Countries who was hanged for his faith in 1567, named a third mark beside those two in its twenty-ninth article: the exercise of church discipline to correct sin. The same article describes the false church too. It gives more authority to itself and its own ordinances than to the word of God, and it persecutes those who live holy lives and rebuke it for its errors, its greed and its idolatry. Four and a half centuries ago, a man who would die for the gospel described an institution that protects itself from the people telling it the truth.

Behind all of these stands the first description we have. After Pentecost the believers in Jerusalem "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42, ESV). It says nothing about size, style, production or convenience.

This yields a working distinction for anyone searching: marks and amenities. The marks tell you whether Christ's church is present: the word, the sacraments, the discipline that takes sin seriously, and the unity, holiness, wholeness and apostolic faith the creed confesses. The amenities tell you whether you'll be comfortable: the music, the building, the length of the service, the coffee. Some amenities carry real weight, as we'll see. But only the marks belong at the top of the list, and the consumer instinct reliably reverses the order.

## What to Look For in a Church

Each of the Nicene marks points to something a visitor can watch for over a few months.

The church is apostolic, which first of all means it holds to what the apostles taught. So watch how a congregation handles Scripture. In one kind of church the preacher stands under the text, following the passage where it goes, including into the places that correct the congregation and himself, and over time you hear whole books of the Bible rather than a rotation of favorite verses. In another kind the text stands under the preacher, quoted to decorate what he already meant to say. Hebrews gives the test in two parts: "Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God. Consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith" (Hebrews 13:7, ESV). What they say must be God's word and not their brand, and how they live must be worth imitating. Apostolic also means tethered to the faith the apostles handed down, what Jude calls "the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (Jude 3, ESV). Ask whether the church confesses the Apostles' or Nicene Creed, or whether its statement of faith at least holds the Trinity, the deity and humanity of Christ and his bodily resurrection. The heirs of "no creed but the Bible" still have a creed; the only question is whether it's written down where you can check it. A church unmoored from the historic creeds has to invent its gospel afresh in every generation, and a gospel invented afresh drifts toward whatever its audience wants to hear.

The church is holy, and in a congregation that is most visible in how it handles power. Paul's qualifications for an overseer are almost entirely about character: he "must be above reproach, the husband of one wife, sober-minded, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach, not a drunkard, not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome, not a lover of money" (1 Timothy 3:2-3, ESV). Charisma is not on the list. Peter tells elders to shepherd the flock "not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock" (1 Peter 5:3, ESV). And Paul expects that leaders will sometimes sin and that the church will deal with it in the open: "Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses. As for those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear" (1 Timothy 5:19-20, ESV). The instruction protects leaders from slander and the church from leaders at once. So ask plain questions. Who can remove the pastor? Is leadership shared among people who can tell one another no? Can a member see the budget? Baptists, Presbyterians and Anglicans answer these differently, a second-order difference, but every faithful polity has a place where a leader can be told no by someone he cannot fire.

Holiness also shows in how a church treats the vulnerable, and above all how it responds when someone reports abuse. A church worth joining has a written child-protection policy, screens the people who work with children, keeps two unrelated adults with them, and reports suspected abuse to the police and child protective services rather than to an internal committee. You may ask to see the policy and ask how the church handled the last accusation it faced. The psychologist Diane Langberg argues in *Redeeming Power* (2020) that abuse in churches is sustained not only by individual abusers but by systems that shield the powerful and silence the people who speak. Paul required that a leader "must be well thought of by outsiders" (1 Timothy 3:7, ESV), which means the church does not get to be the only judge of its own conduct. If you or someone in your home is being harmed, you do not need a church's permission to get help. Call the police for any crime, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline at 1-800-422-4453, or the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233. If the weight of it has pressed you toward despair, call or text 988.

The church is one, and that unity is the reason a church worth joining is free of partisan capture in either direction. On one side is the congregation where the flag has crept into the liturgy and the kingdom of God has been fused with a vision of national greatness. On the other is the congregation where the other party's platform sets the terms of faithfulness and the gospel mostly arrives as an endorsement of positions the members already held. Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon argued in *Resident Aliens* (1989) that conservative and liberal Protestants had made the same mistake from opposite ends: both measured the church by its usefulness in making America better on their own terms, and both forgot that the church's first political act is simply to be the church, a colony of heaven with its own way of life. The test is practical. Could a faithful Christian who votes against the majority here be a full member, teach a class and serve the Supper without being made to feel like a guest? Does the preaching ever cost the congregation's own side anything? A church that only ever indicts the other tribe has stopped proclaiming Christ's lordship over both.

The church is catholic, a word that before it named a communion meant whole, the faith of the entire church across every place and time. In a single congregation catholicity looks like a body that holds more than one kind of person. Robert Bellah and his colleagues, in *Habits of the Heart* (1985), distinguished a community, which binds together people of different callings and generations in a shared memory, from what they called a lifestyle enclave, a gathering of people who resemble one another in how they spend and dress and relax. The homogeneous church is an enclave with a steeple. James saw the danger in the first century. He pictured a congregation giving the man with the gold ring a good seat while telling the poor man to stand over there or sit on the floor, and he began his rebuke plainly: "My brothers, show no partiality as you hold the faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory" (James 2:1, ESV). Look for the widow and the teenager in the same room, the plumber and the professor at the same table. The discomfort of sitting beside people you'd never have chosen is one of the surest signs that you're in a church rather than an enclave.

## The Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

Every church will fail you, because every church is made of sinners, you included. The useful distinction isn't between churches that fail and churches that don't. It's between a church that fails the way a family fails, out of weakness, in ways that can be named, confessed and repaired, and a church that fails out of corruption, in ways that are hidden, denied and defended. The first is the cost of belonging anywhere. The second is a reason to leave.

The first is celebrity joined to control. When a leader's personality has become the brand, when loyalty to him is treated as loyalty to God, when those who raise concerns are labeled divisive, the conditions for harm are in place. Mars Hill Church in Seattle dissolved after its founding pastor resigned in October 2014 amid charges of domineering leadership. In April 2018 Bill Hybels, whose Willow Creek had taught a generation of pastors the seeker model, retired early as allegations of misconduct mounted, allegations he denied; the church's elders at first defended him and later apologized. The store and the star are not unrelated. A church organized to attract consumers will tend to need a product, and the most marketable product is usually a man.

The second is the absence of accountability, and the plainest recent example bears the Baptist name, the name the church I serve carries, so I can't read it as someone else's story. In May 2022 Guidepost Solutions released the independent investigation that messengers of the Southern Baptist Convention had voted for the year before. It reported that leaders of the Convention's Executive Committee had for years treated survivors of abuse as a legal threat, resisting reforms to protect the institution from liability, while Executive Committee staff kept a private list of ministers accused of abuse. That is the Belgic Confession's false church in modern dress: an institution that gave itself more authority than the word of God and turned against the people who told it the truth. Ezekiel's indictment of Israel's shepherds still stands over every church that does this: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4, ESV). When you watch a church close ranks around its reputation and against a victim, you have your answer, and no quality of preaching should keep you in that room.

The third is political capture of either kind, which becomes a red flag rather than a weakness when party loyalty becomes a test of fellowship. The fourth is prosperity teaching, the message that faith and giving are investments God repays in health and wealth. Kate Bowler's history of the American prosperity gospel (2013) traces how deeply it has entered churches that would never use the name. It is consumer religion at its most candid, God as the supplier and the believer as the customer who has learned the terms of the deal. Paul described teachers "imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (1 Timothy 6:5, ESV), and the line needs no updating.

## What Your Preferences Are Allowed to Decide

It would be unfair to treat every preference as consumerism. Some of what searchers weigh is wise.

Your convictions on second-order questions properly shape where you join. Whether a church baptizes infants or believers, how it understands the Lord's Supper, whether it ordains women as pastors, how it handles the gifts of the Spirit: these divide faithful churches without dividing the faith, and they aren't trivial, because you'll have to decide whether to present your children for baptism and you'll sit under that teaching for years. Proximity matters too. The old parish instinct that you belong to the church where you live is wisdom, since a congregation twenty-five minutes away is one you'll rarely see between Sundays. The safety and formation of your children are rightly near the top of the list. So is whether the church has work for your gifts. And for someone recovering from a church that abused its power, a quieter liturgy or a polity with stronger guardrails isn't a luxury. It may be the only way they can worship at all.

The Augsburg Confession helps sort the rest. If rites and ceremonies need not be alike everywhere, then worship style is a freedom. That cuts two ways. You are free to prefer one form of worship to another, and you are not free to make that preference a mark of the true church. A useful order follows. What the creeds confess is not negotiable. Second-order convictions may rightly decide which faithful church you join. Everything else (the music, the length of the sermon, the size, the building) should be held loosely and allowed to break a tie, never to make the decision.

Then there is the harder discipline of forgiving what should be forgiven. Calvin warned in the *Institutes* (book 4, chapter 1, sections 12 and 13) against deserting a church over errors that don't touch the heart of the faith, or over the moral imperfection of its members. Dietrich Bonhoeffer went further in *Life Together* (1939). He wrote that Christian community is not an ideal we must realize but a reality God has already created in Christ, in which we are invited to take part, and he warned that the person who loves his dream of community more than the actual community in front of him becomes its destroyer, however sincere his intentions. The searcher who never stops searching is often in love with that dream. Forgive the sermon that didn't land, the greeter who was awkward, the small group that stumbled on its first night, the people who are, like you, in the middle of being made new. Paul's instruction to the divided house churches of Rome was never to find people who don't try your patience: "As for the one who is weak in faith, welcome him, but not to quarrel over opinions" (Romans 14:1, ESV).

## Choosing a Church or Being Received Into One

Everything so far concerns choosing, and choosing is unavoidable. But it is meant to end in something different in kind.

Paul brings the argument of Romans to its practical climax with a sentence addressed to congregations of Jewish and Gentile believers who disagreed about food and holy days and would not, left to themselves, have picked one another: "Therefore welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God" (Romans 15:7, ESV). The verb is the one the King James Version rendered "receive." The command rests on something other than congeniality: Christ received you first, and not because you were impressive. Paul makes the same point about the body in Corinth: "But as it is, God arranged the members in the body, each one of them, as he chose" (1 Corinthians 12:18, ESV). You can choose a building and a time slot. The people in the body were chosen by someone else.

The church has long used a family image for this. Around 251, in *On the Unity of the Church*, Cyprian of Carthage wrote that no one can have God for his Father who does not have the church for his mother, and Calvin took up the image in the *Institutes* (book 4, chapter 1, section 4), describing the church as the mother who conceives us, gives us birth, nourishes us and keeps us under her care until we put off mortal flesh. The image teaches something the market cannot. Nobody chooses a mother. A mother receives you, feeds you before you can thank her, and keeps you long after you've disappointed her. A shopper evaluates what is offered. A child is taken in.

This is why commitment forms us in a way that shopping cannot. If Smith is right that we are shaped by repeated practices, then a liturgy attended occasionally forms occasionally, and a church sampled from the back row forms a critic. Membership places you under the care of particular people. Hebrews tells believers to submit to their leaders "for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account" (Hebrews 13:17, ESV), which is impossible if no one in the room knows your soul well enough to watch over it. It warns against "neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some" (Hebrews 10:25, ESV), and the word habit is exact, because not belonging is also something we practice until it practices us. Eugene Peterson borrowed a phrase of Nietzsche's for *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* (1980) and filled it with the Psalms of Ascent, songs of pilgrims walking the same road to Jerusalem year after year. Formation happens at that speed, never at the speed of a new church every season.

So give the search an end. Visit long enough to see the marks, since the polish is visible on the first morning and the substance takes weeks. Ask the hard questions. Then stop evaluating and ask to be received. Take the membership class, make the promises, and let people learn your name, your children's names and the things you would rather they didn't know. You will be disappointed. You'll also be carried, in ways a customer is never carried.

The deepest reason for all this lies at the cross. Paul says that "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). The welcome Romans 15 commands was bought there, extended to people who had nothing to recommend them and who would not have chosen him. Every congregation of forgiven sinners is a small outpost of that welcome, which cannot be acquired by comparison shopping because it can only be given. There is a church near you that will meet this Sunday with too few volunteers, a sermon that runs long and people you would not have picked. It will not be the best church you could find. It may be the one Christ is holding open for you. The question you carry out of the search is not whether it deserves your commitment, since no church does. It is whether you will let yourself be received by people who will one day know you well enough to be disappointed in you, and who will, if you stay, be the ones who sing over your grave.
