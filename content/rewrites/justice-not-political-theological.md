Say the word "justice" from an American pulpit and you can watch a room sort itself. Some people lean forward, because they have waited years to hear the church say it. Others fold their arms, because they've heard it used as the opening move in a sermon that ends somewhere on cable news. A congregation that runs a legal clinic for immigrant families is suspected of either finally getting the gospel or quietly leaving it, depending on who is watching. Both reactions assume the same thing: that the word belongs to a party, and the only open question is which one.

That assumption did not come from the Bible. The Hebrew Scriptures speak of justice hundreds of times, and they speak of it first as a description of God. The prophets announced it. Jesus read it aloud in a synagogue as the content of his own mission. For long stretches of American evangelical history, few heard it as a partisan word. We made it one, within roughly the last century, and we did it from both directions.

I have done it from both directions myself. There have been seasons when I carried the word into a conversation as a banner, a way of feeling righteous that asked nothing of me beyond the right vocabulary. There have been other seasons when I flinched from it because of the company it had been keeping, let a political allergy talk me out of a biblical command, and called the retreat pastoral caution. Those look like opposite failures. They are one failure in two sets of clothes, because in both cases I let a political map redraw a theological one.

So, is biblical justice political? If political means public, touching courts and wages and markets and the way a society treats its weakest people, then yes, and a church that says otherwise has stopped reading the prophets. If political means partisan, owned by one coalition in the contest for power, then no, and a church that says otherwise has stopped reading them too. Justice is public but not partisan. And the church's first political act is not the endorsement of a party. It is being a people in whom God's justice can be seen.

## Two Hebrew words and a Greek one

The Old Testament's language of justice turns on a pair. *Mishpat*, most often translated "justice," comes from the verb for judging. It carries the sense of a verdict rightly given, a wrong put right, what is due rendered to the one it is owed. *Tsedaqah*, usually translated "righteousness," is broader and more relational. It describes a community in which people are rightly related to God and to one another, where obligations are honored before anyone has to go to court. The two travel together so often that they work almost as a single idea. Isaiah builds a pun out of them in his song of the vineyard: "he looked for justice, but behold, bloodshed; for righteousness, but behold, an outcry!" (Isaiah 5:7, ESV). In Hebrew, justice is *mishpat* and bloodshed is *mispach*; righteousness is *tsedaqah* and the outcry of the oppressed is *tse'aqah*. God came looking for one thing and found a word that sounded nearly the same and meant its opposite.

Where does the pair live first? In God. "Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne; steadfast love and faithfulness go before you" (Psalm 89:14). Moses tells Israel that the Lord is "the great, the mighty, and the awesome God, who is not partial and takes no bribe. He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing" (Deuteronomy 10:17–18). God's refusal to take a bribe and his care for the orphan are one character, and the command follows from it: "Love the sojourner, therefore, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:19). Justice is first a covenant memory. You were slaves; the God who freed you is like this; now be like him.

And it starts earlier than Sinai. The Lord says of Abraham, "I have chosen him, that he may command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice" (Genesis 18:19). There was no nation yet, no law code, no king. Before justice was ever the policy of a state, it was the way of life of a household God had called.

Nicholas Wolterstorff gives this biblical grammar its most careful modern statement in *Justice: Rights and Wrongs* (2008). He distinguishes primary justice, people treating one another as their worth requires, from rectifying justice, what is owed once that has been violated. He argues against the common story that rights talk was invented by Enlightenment individualism, tracing its roots through the medieval canon lawyers to the Hebrew and Christian Scriptures. And he grounds human worth not in any capacity a person has but in the fact that each person is loved by God, a worth bestowed rather than achieved. That is why the widow's claim on us does not depend on her usefulness. Tim Keller brought this vocabulary into the pew in *Generous Justice* (2010), treating *mishpat* as giving people their due and *tsedaqah* as the daily life of right relationships, and arguing that together they come close to what English speakers mean by "social justice," while insisting the biblical idea is not any ideology's property.

Then comes the Greek. When the Hebrew Scriptures were translated into Greek before Christ, *tsedaqah* was regularly rendered *dikaiosyne*, and the New Testament inherits the word. English Bibles render it "righteousness," which is defensible, but Wolterstorff notes the cost: an English reader rarely meets "justice" in the New Testament and may conclude the apostles lost interest in it. Read with its Hebrew background, "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied" (Matthew 5:6) includes a hunger for the world set right, not only for private moral excellence. And the same word family sits at the center of the gospel. Paul says God put Christ forward "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26). *Dikaios*, *dikaioō*, *dikaiosyne*: just, justify, justice. The cross is where God's justice is displayed, not where it is set aside. The forgiveness of sinners and the rights of the poor belong to the same word, and neither side of our politics has much patience for that.

## The prophets, read where they stood

Amos preached at Bethel around the middle of the eighth century before Christ, under Jeroboam II, in a season of prosperity and full sanctuaries. The feasts were crowded and the offerings generous, and that is exactly what God rejects: "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21). The charge is economic and judicial. They "sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6); they "take a bribe, and turn aside the needy in the gate" (Amos 5:12), and the gate was the town's courtroom. Amos is not complaining about private attitudes. He is naming a rigged court and a rigged market, and then he gives the line every American has heard: "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24). When the priest Amaziah tells him to go home, the reason he gives is revealing: "never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom" (Amos 7:13). The house of God had become an organ of the state. Amos's word fell hardest on the chaplaincy of power.

Isaiah opens his book the same way. He addresses Jerusalem's worshipers as "you rulers of Sodom" and "you people of Gomorrah" (Isaiah 1:10), tells them God is weary of their sacrifices, and says, "When you spread out your hands, I will hide my eyes from you; even though you make many prayers, I will not listen; your hands are full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15). The cure is concrete: "learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause" (Isaiah 1:17). And the very next verse is the offer of cleansing: "though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18). The demand for justice and the promise of forgiveness sit side by side in one speech. Neither cancels the other.

Micah 6 is framed as a lawsuit, for "the LORD has an indictment against his people" (Micah 6:2), and the cornered people start bidding up the price of their religion, from calves to thousands of rams to "Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression?" (Micah 6:7). The answer refuses the auction: "what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8). The word for kindness is *hesed*, the covenant love God shows Israel, so the verse describes a life that mirrors the God of the covenant rather than a platform.

Isaiah 58 takes up fasting, about as purely religious as a practice gets. On the day of their fast, God says, they "oppress all your workers" (Isaiah 58:3). "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke?" (Isaiah 58:6). The same chapter ends by calling the Sabbath "a delight" (Isaiah 58:13). The prophet refuses to let worship and justice be pulled apart.

Jeremiah 22 is the sharpest of all, because of whom it addresses. "Hear the word of the LORD, O king of Judah, who sits on the throne of David" (Jeremiah 22:2). A few verses later the king in view is Jehoiakim, building himself a palace with unpaid labor: "Woe to him who builds his house by unrighteousness, and his upper rooms by injustice, who makes his neighbor serve him for nothing and does not give him his wages" (Jeremiah 22:13). Jeremiah sets him beside his father Josiah: "He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 22:16). To know God is to judge the cause of the poor. And the word goes to the house of David, the dynasty of promise, the one family on earth that could most plausibly claim God was on its side. The prophets stood outside every faction because they stood under the covenant, and they held the anointed line to it more strictly than anyone.

None of this was new. The prophets were not innovators. They recalled Sinai, where the law had already said, "The wages of a hired worker shall not remain with you all night until the morning" (Leviticus 19:13). Justice in the prophets is covenant faithfulness made public.

## Nazareth and the seating chart

Jesus begins his public ministry in Luke by reading Isaiah 61 in the synagogue at Nazareth: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty those who are oppressed, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:18–19). The phrase about setting the oppressed at liberty is folded in from Isaiah 58:6, and "the year of the Lord's favor" echoes the Jubilee of Leviticus 25, when debts were released and land returned to the families that had lost it. Then he sits down and says, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21).

The room likes it at first (Luke 4:22). The turn comes when Jesus reminds them that God sent Elijah to a widow in Sidon and healed Naaman the Syrian (Luke 4:25–27), and then they try to throw him off a cliff. Everyone in that synagogue could approve of care for the poor in principle. The offense was that God's favor crossed the boundary of their people and their claim on him. That cuts both our tribes. It will not let the right have a spiritual gospel without the reversals Mary sang, the mighty brought down and the hungry filled (Luke 1:52–53). It will not let the left have the reversals without the Messiah who says they are fulfilled in him, because "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled" is a claim about a person before it is anything else.

James, the Lord's brother, writing to scattered Jewish believers, locates justice first in the church's own room. "My brothers, show no partiality as you hold the faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory" (James 2:1). A man with a gold ring walks into the assembly, a poor man in shabby clothes walks in behind him, and the congregation gives the good seat to the first and tells the second to stand. "But you have dishonored the poor man," James says (James 2:6). He goes on to the hungry brother or sister sent away with "Go in peace, be warmed and filled" (James 2:16), and concludes, "So also faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead" (James 2:17). For James, the first arena of justice is the seating chart.

In chapter 5 he turns outward, and his voice becomes Amos's: "Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:4). The law had warned that a poor worker cheated of his wage would "cry against you to the LORD" (Deuteronomy 24:15), and James says the cry has arrived. But look at what he tells the church next: "Be patient, therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord" (James 5:7). He neither tells the harvesters to seize the fields nor tells them the fraud is none of the church's business. He names the injustice in public, entrusts final judgment to God, and requires the congregation to be a place where favoring the rich is sin: public denunciation, no partisan program, and a people ordered differently.

## How American evangelicals lost the word

For much of the nineteenth century, the American Christians most eager to save souls were also among the most eager to reform society. Timothy L. Smith made the case in *Revivalism and Social Reform in Mid-Nineteenth-Century America* (1957), showing that the revival currents of the mid-1800s, especially the holiness movement, fed the campaigns against slavery and the missions to the urban poor, and arguing that the later Social Gospel grew partly out of that revivalist soil. Theodore Weld, a convert of Charles Finney's revivals, led the Lane Seminary debates on slavery in 1834; many of the students who left Lane over it went on to Oberlin, where Finney came to teach. Methodist women opened a mission in New York's Five Points slum in 1850. The same people who pleaded with sinners to repent campaigned to end the sale of human beings.

Honesty requires the other half of that century. The same Bible was preached in defense of slavery across the South, and Southern Presbyterians under James Henley Thornwell developed the doctrine of the spirituality of the church, which held that the church must not speak to civil questions. In practice it forbade the church to challenge slavery while leaving its ministers free to defend it, and Thornwell himself defended it. Its modern defenders are not Thornwell, but the history warns what the doctrine can shelter.

Then came the Social Gospel. Walter Rauschenbusch pastored a German Baptist congregation near Hell's Kitchen from 1886 to 1897 and watched poverty grind down families who had done nothing to deserve it. In *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) and *A Theology for the Social Gospel* (1917), he argued that sin is built into institutions, passed down through them, and outlives the sinners who built them; he called this the kingdom of evil. Amos would have recognized the insight. Its weakness lay in the liberal theology around it, in which the kingdom tended to become social progress, the cross an example, and sin a selfishness that moral progress could outgrow. H. Richard Niebuhr summed up where that liberal road ended in *The Kingdom of God in America* (1937): "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross."

The fundamentalist and modernist controversy of the 1920s turned a theological quarrel into a sorting of the word itself. Harry Emerson Fosdick preached "Shall the Fundamentalists Win?" in 1922, and J. Gresham Machen argued in *Christianity and Liberalism* (1923) that liberal Protestantism was not a version of Christianity but a different religion. Machen was right about the doctrine. But because the modernists had fused social reform to a theology that surrendered the supernatural gospel, many conservatives came to treat social concern itself as a symptom of drift. Premillennial expectation added its weight; Dwight L. Moody had described the world as a wrecked vessel and his calling as saving all he could in a lifeboat. After the Scopes trial in 1925, fundamentalists withdrew from much of public life and built institutions of their own.

The sociologist David O. Moberg named the pattern in *The Great Reversal* (1972): a movement that had led social reform in the mid-nineteenth century abandoned it in the early decades of the twentieth century, in reaction to the Social Gospel. What was lost was not a political program but half of what the prophets meant by knowing God.

Some saw it at the time. Carl F. H. Henry, later the founding editor of *Christianity Today*, published *The Uneasy Conscience of Modern Fundamentalism* in 1947 and told his own movement that it had become the priest and the Levite in Jesus's parable, passing the wounded man on the far side of the road. He did not want the Social Gospel back; he wanted a regenerate church that brought the whole gospel to bear on war, racial hatred and the exploitation of labor. In 1974 at Lausanne, with John Stott chairing the drafting committee and Latin Americans such as René Padilla and Samuel Escobar pressing the case, evangelicals from across the world expressed penitence "for having sometimes regarded evangelism and social concern as mutually exclusive" and affirmed "that evangelism and socio-political involvement are both part of our Christian duty," while holding that in the church's mission "evangelism is primary." The American church never fully received that settlement, and the partisan realignment of the late 1970s soon gave both sides fresh reason to hear the word as a party signal. That realignment is the proximate cause. The reversal of the 1920s is the deeper one.

## Two strong arguments and one test

Two positions deserve to be heard as their own defenders would state them. The first says: justice is a fruit of the gospel, not its content, and the church must keep that order or lose both. Kevin DeYoung and Greg Gilbert make this case carefully in *What Is the Mission of the Church?* (2011). Christians are commanded to do good to all, they argue, but the mission Jesus gave the church is to make disciples, and when the gathered church takes on every good cause as its mission, the one thing only the church can do gets crowded out. Behind this sits Abraham Kuyper's old distinction between the church as institution, which preaches, baptizes and disciplines, and the church as organism, its people scattered into every vocation. The mainline Protestant story gives the argument force: churches that led with social causes and let the doctrine thin out ended up with fewer members, less to say, and no more justice than before.

The second says: justice is not an implication of the gospel added later; it belongs to the good news itself, because Jesus announced good news to the poor and liberty to the oppressed in his first sermon. Gustavo Gutiérrez argued in *A Theology of Liberation* (1971) that salvation touches every level of human bondage, and he was more careful than his critics usually allow: he named liberation from sin as the deepest level, the one the others depend on. A gospel that forgives the slaveholder and says nothing to the slave, this view insists, is not neutral. It has chosen a side, the side of whoever holds power, and has called that choice spirituality. After Thornwell, that charge cannot be waved away.

Both views are serious, and the disagreement between their better forms is a second-order question. It divides faithful churches without dividing the faith. The first-order claims underneath are shared by orthodox Christians: God is just, his people are commanded to act justly, and the cross is where his justice and mercy meet. This essay's conclusion is that each view guards something true and each has a characteristic danger. The second is right that justice belongs inside the good news rather than in an appendix to it, but where justice is made the whole of the gospel, sin, forgiveness, resurrection and the particular claims of Christ get crowded out, and that is the mainline's road entered by another door. The first view's warning is true and its remedy is wrong. The mainline churches that led with social causes did not fail because they pursued justice. They failed because they emptied out the theology that makes justice coherent, and a justice cut loose from the image of God, the reality of sin, the kingdom and the resurrection becomes the moral politics of the moment in clerical robes, with no foundation the secular world cannot also supply. The answer to that failure is not to retreat from the word. It is to recover the architecture: the image of God, which grounds the worth of every person; the doctrine of sin, which explains why structures fall as well as individuals; the kingdom, which gives justice its horizon and its hope; the resurrection, which vindicates the God who will set all things right. Justice is not optional, and a church that treats it as a special interest has misread the prophets.

Nor does the essay accept the choice between changing hearts and naming structures. The prophets condemned particular merchants who dealt with false balances (Amos 8:5) and a whole rigged system in the same breath, because systems are made of people and people are formed by systems. A church that preaches against private sin but will not name the arrangements that make certain sins profitable has made itself useful to those arrangements. And it must practice justice first in its own life, as James demands, before it lectures anyone else. The strongest objection this view has not fully answered comes from the first view's own evidence. Once a pulpit begins naming structures, the line between a prophetic word and a policy preference is hard to see, and churches that crossed it without noticing ended with neither the gospel nor the justice. That line has to be walked with fear and trembling, and it is not always clear.

What both our tribes need is one test applied without favor. Can a given account of justice read the whole of Amos, Luke 4, James and Romans 3 without flinching? The right tends to hear justice as rectifying justice alone, punishment for wrongdoing, and to go quiet when James names withheld wages. The left tends to hear justice as the rights of the vulnerable and to go quiet when Paul says the justice-seeker also needs to be justified, that "every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world may be held accountable to God" (Romans 3:19). The failures are not identical in shape, and they have not cost the same people the same things. But the instrument is the same, and it has to be pointed first at whichever tribe we belong to.

## Public, not partisan

The distinction that runs through all of this is simple to state and hard to keep. Justice is public. It concerns the gate and the market, the court and the wage, the laws a society writes and the way those laws land on the weakest. You cannot shrink it into a private feeling or a devotional mood without silencing most of the prophets. But justice is not partisan. Its measure is the character of God, not the platform of a coalition, and it judges every coalition, including the one that believes itself anointed. Jeremiah's word to the throne of David is the proof.

That leaves the church with a question about what its political life actually is. Paul tells the Philippians, "Only let your manner of life be worthy of the gospel of Christ" (Philippians 1:27), and the verb he uses is built on the Greek word for a city: live as citizens. Luke says of the Jerusalem church that "there was not a needy person among them" (Acts 4:34), which echoes the law's own hope that "there will be no poor among you" (Deuteronomy 15:4). The first Christians fulfilled that hope without capturing a single office in Rome, by becoming a community where it was true. John Howard Yoder argued in *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) that Jesus's way of the cross forms a new social reality rather than a private spirituality waiting to be applied to politics by other means, and Stanley Hauerwas compressed the point in *The Peaceable Kingdom* (1983): the church does not have a social ethic; it is one.

So the church's primary political act is being a people. Christians will still vote, serve in office and argue about laws, and they should, because the organism reaches everywhere. They will disagree about minimum wages, immigration levels, sentencing and welfare, much of which is prudential judgment on which faithful people differ, and they can differ as brothers and sisters because the measure they share stands above both parties. But voting is not the church's first act. The first act is whether the poor man gets a seat, whether the church pays its own workers justly, whether the deacons' fund keeps a widow in her house, whether the immigrant family in the pew is treated as a neighbor or a category, and whether the man coming home from prison finds a table. And its preachers will have to do what the prophets did, who were sent not to the irreligious but to worshiping congregations, and say the thing that embarrasses the comfortable, including the comfortable in their own pews. A church that does these things has a public witness no party can buy. A church that doesn't cannot recover it with any number of statements.

## The just and the justifier

God put Christ forward "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26). The cross does not suspend God's justice to make room for his mercy. It satisfies his justice and displays his mercy in the same act, and that is the only ground on which any of us can seek justice without becoming one more accuser with clean hands and a dirty heart. Isaiah's hearers were told to seek justice and, in the next breath, that their scarlet sins could be made white. They needed both words, and so do we.

That is what neither tribe can give, and it is why this word finally belongs to neither. The activist who has never been forgiven will eventually need someone to blame for everything. The conservative who has never heard the harvesters crying will eventually confuse his comfort with God's peace. Both need the cross, and both need the resurrection, in which the Son who was condemned unjustly in a Roman court was vindicated by his Father, and every unanswered cry from every rigged gate is promised a hearing.

The word comes back to us through the prophets, through a carpenter reading a scroll in Nazareth, and through a brother of the Lord watching who got the good seat. It comes back as the character of the God we claim to know, and Jeremiah has already told us how that knowledge is tested. The seating chart is still there on Sunday, and so are the wages and the widow and the door. Before we argue about which party owns justice, we will have to answer for who, in our own room, has been standing.
