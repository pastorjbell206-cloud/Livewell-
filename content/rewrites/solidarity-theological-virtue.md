The word *solidarity* gets sorted before anyone hears the argument. In one room it sounds like a union hall or a protest sign, and a certain kind of American evangelical files it at once under progressive politics: not our word, not our concern. In another it works as a badge of which side you're on, and those who wear it rarely ask what it owes anyone outside the circle. The sorting takes about a second.

That's a strange fate for a word the church has more claim to than anyone. The idea runs from the God who saw his people's misery in Egypt, through Paul's body in which one member's pain is everyone's, to a Polish priest preaching to shipyard workers in 1980 and a pope who put the word at the center of Catholic social teaching in 1987. We didn't borrow solidarity from the Left. Left and Right have both borrowed pieces of something the church once held whole.

Two claims hold this essay together. Solidarity is a Christian virtue, a settled commitment of the will to the good of others, not a mood or a slogan. And justice pursued without love doesn't become a purer justice. It becomes ideology, a system that runs on a correct idea and has quietly stopped needing the people the idea was for. Between the two claims stands a distinction. Solidarity binds me to the wounded, including the wounded who aren't mine. Tribal loyalty binds me to my own, against whoever isn't. They can march under the same banner and still be opposites.

Other essays here treat [what Scripture means by justice](/writing/justice-not-political-theological), [how charity differs from justice](/writing/charity-is-not-justice-the-difference) and [what Micah 6:8 demands](/writing/micah-6-8-demands). This one asks what keeps justice human.

## Where the word solidarity came from

The word began in law, not in politics. Roman law knew an obligation *in solidum*, a debt for which each of several debtors was answerable for the whole amount. French carried it forward as *solidarité*, and in the nineteenth century social thinkers took it out of the law books to name the bonds of modern society, often as a secular substitute for Christian charity. The legal origin holds the moral core in miniature: to stand in solidarity is to accept that someone else's debt is, in some real sense, yours to pay.

Catholic thought gave the word theological weight. Heinrich Pesch, a German Jesuit economist, built a system around it in his five-volume *Lehrbuch der Nationalökonomie* (1905–1923). He called it solidarism and meant it as a third way. Against liberal individualism, which treated society as contracting atoms, he insisted that persons are bound to one another by nature and by God's design. Against socialism, which folded the person into the class and the state, he insisted that the person and the family come first. His thought fed the Catholic social teaching of the following decades. The church's word was born as a refusal of both sides of a political quarrel.

Then the word acquired a face. In August 1980 the workers of the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk went on strike after the firing of Anna Walentynowicz, a crane operator near retirement. The strike spread, the government signed an agreement on 31 August, and within weeks Solidarność had formed, the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, soon some ten million strong. Martial law crushed it in December 1981. It survived underground and returned in 1989 to negotiate the end of communist rule.

Its chaplain, in effect, was Józef Tischner, a priest and philosopher from Kraków. On 19 October 1980 he preached to the union's delegates in the Wawel Cathedral, and his sermons and essays from those months were gathered as *Etyka solidarności* in 1981 (in English, *The Spirit of Solidarity*, 1984). Tischner built his ethic on one verse from Paul: "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2, ESV). As he described it, solidarity is born when conscience sees a wound that one person has inflicted on another, suffering that did not have to happen, and refuses to look away. And he insisted, in a country with every reason to hate its rulers, that such solidarity is directed toward everyone and against no one. It does not need an enemy to hold it together.

When John Paul II preached to a vast crowd at Gdańsk-Zaspa in June 1987, he returned to the same verse. Solidarity, he said in substance, means one person and another, and when there is a burden, a burden carried together. It never means one against another. He added a warning a movement at war with a regime needed to hear: the program of struggle must never be placed above the program of solidarity. Six months later, in the encyclical *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* (1987), he gave the word its standard definition. Solidarity, he wrote, is "not a feeling of vague compassion or shallow distress at the misfortunes of so many people, both near and far," but "a firm and persevering determination to commit oneself to the common good; that is to say to the good of all and of each individual, because we are all really responsible for all" (§38). Two paragraphs later he called it a Christian virtue and said that in the light of faith it reaches beyond itself toward total gratuity, forgiveness and reconciliation (§40).

That definition excludes a great deal. It excludes solidarity as a feeling, which costs nothing and can be dropped without notice. It excludes solidarity as a faction, because the good in view is the good of all and of each. And by reaching toward forgiveness it excludes a solidarity that depends on keeping an enemy in view. The same encyclical named "structures of sin," treated in [the site's essay on personal and systemic sin](/writing/individual-sin-and-systemic-sin-explained), but the virtue came first. Before telling the world what was wrong with its arrangements, John Paul II described the kind of people who could change them.

## One body in a divided church

Tischner and John Paul II were reading Paul, and Paul's clearest picture of solidarity comes from a letter to a church that had very little of it. Corinth, refounded as a Roman colony in 44 BC, was commercial and status-hungry, and the congregation reflected its city. Its members lined up behind rival teachers, sued one another, and ranked spiritual gifts the way the city ranked people. And at the Lord's Supper, which in that period was still a real meal in a household, the divisions became visible at the table: "For in eating, each one goes ahead with his own meal. One goes hungry, another gets drunk" (1 Corinthians 11:21, ESV). The well-off, it seems, ate first and ate well. Those who came later, probably including laborers and slaves who didn't control their own hours, found little left. The table meant to show one body showed two classes.

Margaret Mitchell argued in *Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation* (1991) that the whole letter is a plea for concord to a factional community, using the tools ancient orators used to urge a divided city toward unity. So when Paul calls the church a body, he is reaching for one of the most familiar political images of his world. Livy tells how Menenius Agrippa, in the early Roman republic, persuaded the plebeians who had walked out of the city to return with a fable: the limbs rebelled against the belly, starved it, and wasted away themselves. The moral was plain enough: the lower orders need the higher, so stay in your place. Dale Martin argued in *The Corinthian Body* (1995) that this was the ordinary use of the body image in ancient political speech. It kept hierarchy intact by calling it harmony.

Paul turns it upside down. "For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ" (1 Corinthians 12:12, ESV). Then he aims the warning upward, at members who think they need no one: "The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you,' nor again the head to the feet, 'I have no need of you'" (1 Corinthians 12:21, ESV). And then he says the thing no Roman orator would say: "On the contrary, the parts of the body that seem to be weaker are indispensable" (1 Corinthians 12:22, ESV). God has arranged the body so that the lesser parts receive greater honor, and he has done it for a stated reason: "that there may be no division in the body, but that the members may have the same care for one another" (1 Corinthians 12:25, ESV). In Agrippa's fable the weak exist for the strong. In Paul's body the strong are rearranged around the weak.

Only then comes the verse that gives solidarity its biblical shape: "If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (1 Corinthians 12:26, ESV). The verb for suffering together is *sympaschei*, to suffer with. Paul doesn't tell the members to feel for one another. He states a fact about a body: when one part is injured the whole is injured, whether or not it has noticed. The question for the church isn't whether it is joined to its suffering members. It is. The question is whether it will live as though it knows.

The same fact runs through the New Testament. "Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body" (Hebrews 13:3, ESV). The writer to the Hebrews grounds that command in the solidarity of Christ himself: "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15, ESV). And behind all of it stands the God of the exodus, who told Moses, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings" (Exodus 3:7, ESV). God sees, hears and knows, and then comes down, toward the ones whose pain the powerful would rather manage from a distance.

Here solidarity parts company with charity. Charity can give from where it stands; a check travels well. Solidarity has to go where the other person is, and it can't be done anonymously. The early church's strangeness to its neighbors lay partly here, in tables where slave and free, Jew and Greek, ate together because baptism had redrawn the circle of concern. I have to be honest about where I stand in that picture. I am better at charity than at solidarity. I have helped from a position that protected my comfort, giving from surplus while keeping the option to step away when nearness became costly. That is standing near, not standing with.

## What 1 Corinthians 13 is actually arguing

Then Paul writes the chapter on love, which we mostly hear at weddings. That hides its argument. Chapter 13 sits between chapter 12, on the body and its gifts, and chapter 14, on the disorder of Corinthian worship, where everyone speaks at once and a visitor would think the church had lost its mind. He is telling a gifted, divided, competitive congregation why its gifts had become worthless.

He starts with the gift the Corinthians prized most: "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1, ESV). Then he raises the stakes to the gifts every church would want: "And if I have prophetic powers, and understand all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but have not love, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2, ESV). And then he reaches the case that ought to stop every movement that has ever marched: "If I give away all I have, and if I deliver up my body to be burned, but have not love, I gain nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:3, ESV).

Paul isn't describing a stingy or cowardly person. He is describing someone who has given away everything, and his body too, and he says it can be done without love. Sacrifice is not proof of love. A person can empty himself for the poor and still be doing it for his standing among the committed, or for the enemy he gets to despise along the way. The most costly acts of justice can be performed by a heart that has stopped seeing anyone but itself.

Paul's description of love that follows is a portrait of Corinth in reverse. "Love is patient and kind; love does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant" (1 Corinthians 13:4, ESV). The word behind "arrogant" means to be puffed up, and Paul has been using it about the Corinthians all through the letter. When some claimed a knowledge that freed them to eat idol meat whatever it did to weaker believers, Paul answered: "This 'knowledge' puffs up, but love builds up" (1 Corinthians 8:1, ESV). The knowledge wasn't false. The strong were right that an idol is nothing. Their error was using a correct idea in a way that crushed the people it should have served. That is the pattern of ideology in miniature, and Paul saw it in a church small enough to gather in a few households.

Then the description continues: love "does not insist on its own way; it is not irritable or resentful" (1 Corinthians 13:5, ESV), and "it does not rejoice at wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6, ESV). That last line matters. Love in Paul is not the soft alternative to truth and justice; it cannot be pleased by wrongdoing, so it cannot be indifferent to injustice. It isn't what we reach for when we are tired of justice. It is what keeps justice from becoming something else. The chapter is a warning to the gifted and the committed, to people doing real good with real conviction, that the good can be done in a way that makes it nothing. Paul was writing about a church. He could have been writing about any cause.

## How justice hardens into ideology

The word ideology is often just an insult for views we dislike. Hannah Arendt gave a sharper account in the chapter "Ideology and Terror," added to *The Origins of Totalitarianism* in its 1958 edition. An ideology, she argued, is quite literally the logic of an idea. It takes one premise and deduces everything from it, and in doing so it frees itself from experience. Nothing that happens can count against it, because the system already knows what everything means. She was describing totalitarian movements, and not every hardened cause is totalitarian. But she named a temptation that lives in every movement for justice. The idea is true. The injustice is real. And somewhere along the way the idea stops answering to the people it began with and starts answering only to itself.

Carl Schmitt supplied the other half. In *The Concept of the Political* (1932) he argued that the political, at bottom, is the distinction between friend and enemy. Schmitt, who joined the Nazi party in 1933, wasn't describing a danger. He was describing what he took to be the truth, which is why he is useful. Ideology, once it hardens, runs on exactly his distinction. It needs the enemy, because the enemy organizes the world: who belongs, who is suspect, what any means is justified by. Take the enemy away and the whole structure loses its shape. Set that beside Tischner telling workers under a hostile regime that their solidarity was directed against no one, and you have the whole argument of this essay. Prophets had enemies. The prophetic word never depended on them. It depended on God and on the suffering of the people God would not abandon.

In *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880), Dostoevsky's elder Zosima tells of a doctor who confessed that the more he loved humanity in general, the less he loved people in particular; he could dream of dying for mankind and could not share a room with anyone for two days. That doctor is the patron saint of justice without love. His love attached to an abstraction, and actual people interrupted it.

The counterfeit is not hard to recognize. Justice that has become ideology requires the dehumanizing of opponents, because a person who disagrees in good faith threatens the system. It develops a hierarchy of suffering, in which some pain is amplified and other pain is explained away, and the deciding factor is usually which tribe the sufferer belongs to. It grows more interested in the position than the person. And it produces communities of moral performance, where what matters is whether you said the right thing, not whether anyone's life got better.

Jonathan Haidt describes the mechanism from the side of moral psychology. In *The Righteous Mind* (2012) he argues that morality binds and blinds: it knits people into teams capable of extraordinary cooperation, and it makes them nearly unable to see the other team's concerns as moral at all. Haidt got the symptom right, and Christians should admit it. His account falls short in its anthropology. He treats our groupishness as an evolved trait to be managed. Scripture calls it pride and idolatry, and commands what no team can produce: "But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, ESV). Tribal loyalty can do many things. That is the one thing it cannot do.

The same instrument has to cut both ways, or it isn't an instrument at all. On the right, justice without love wears the clothes of order. It speaks sincerely about law, responsibility and the deserving poor, and those are real goods. But it can make a body of the nation in which the immigrant, the prisoner and the addict are simply outside the skin, so that their suffering never registers as the body's own. On the left, justice without love wears the clothes of liberation. It speaks sincerely about the oppressed and the marginalized, and those are real people. But it can draw a circle of concern whose edge is enforced by denunciation, in which the unborn child, the religious conservative or the former ally who said the wrong thing are outside the skin in the same way. The consequences on each side are not identical, and pretending they are would be its own kind of dishonesty. But the grammar is shared. Each side has a body, and each side decides in advance whose pain doesn't count.

I can't write this from outside. I have felt that hardening. I know what it is like when anger becomes so clarifying that the people I began by being angry for become secondary to the clarity itself. The faces blur, and the point becomes the defeat of whoever I've decided is responsible. I have also been sentimental about injustice, moved by it without being changed by it, willing to speak a hard word without paying anything for it. Both are failures. The tradition that holds love and justice together is harder than either.

## Love and justice from Augustine to King

The deepest reason justice cannot do without love is that, in the Christian tradition, justice was never a separate thing from love in the first place. In *The City of God* (completed around 426) Augustine offered what he called a brief and true definition of virtue: the right ordering of love (XV.22). Justice, on that account, isn't love's stern rival. It is love arranged rightly, God loved above all and neighbor loved for God's sake, each thing loved in its proper measure. Later he defined a people as a gathering of rational beings bound together by agreement about what they love (XIX.24). You learn what a people is by what it loves, and how good it is by whether it loves in the right order.

If justice is ordered love, then justice without love isn't justice purified of sentiment. It is disordered love, bent toward the cause, the tribe, the self that enjoys being right. Augustine also asked what kingdoms are, once justice is removed, except great bands of robbers (IV.4). A cause can go the same way. It can keep all its vocabulary and become a gang with a program.

Augustine did not think love was soft. In his homilies on the First Epistle of John, usually dated to around 407 though some scholars place them later, he gave his famous rule, love and do what you will, in a context anything but sentimental. A father may discipline his son severely while a kidnapper caresses the child he means to sell, he observed; the outward acts can't tell you which is which. What matters is the root. Love can wear a hard face and malice a gentle one. So the test of justice is not its temperature. Fierce justice may be full of love, and mild justice may have none. The test is whether the person it began with is still the reason for it.

Reinhold Niebuhr brought a realist's suspicion to the same question. In *An Interpretation of Christian Ethics* (1935) he described the love Jesus commands as an ideal no society will achieve, which still stands over every achieved justice as its judge and its lure. A decade later, in *The Children of Light and the Children of Darkness* (1944), he turned the same insight on idealists. The children of light, he argued, are foolish because they underestimate the power of self-interest, including their own. His famous line compresses the whole problem: "Man's capacity for justice makes democracy possible; but man's inclination to injustice makes democracy necessary." The ideologue believes the inclination to injustice lives on the other side. Niebuhr found it in everyone, the reformer included.

Martin Luther King Jr. read Niebuhr as a seminarian and wrote his 1955 Boston University dissertation partly on Paul Tillich, whose *Love, Power, and Justice* (1954) argued that the three belong together. On 16 August 1967, addressing the Southern Christian Leadership Conference in Atlanta in a speech that also took up the quarrel over Black Power, King argued that love and power had too often been set against each other as opposites. Read in that setting, the passage can fairly be taken as an answer to both camps, to those who thought any reach for power betrayed love and to those who thought love meant weakness. "What is needed is a realization that power without love is reckless and abusive, and that love without power is sentimental and anemic," he said. "Power at its best is love implementing the demands of justice, and justice at its best is love correcting everything that stands against love."

King's two sentences hold both failures at once. Love without power is sentimentality. It weeps for the poor and changes nothing, because changing something would require giving something up. Power without love, which in a movement for justice means justice without love, is ideology. It is the will to be right more than the will to see people flourish, and it can outlast the people it began with, because the argument is cleaner without them. King refused both, and paid for it from every direction.

## Attention to the afflicted

If ideology is the logic of an idea freed from experience, the remedy isn't a better idea. It is attention. Simone Weil, the French philosopher who died in England in 1943 at thirty-four, wrote about it with unsettling severity. In a 1942 essay on the right use of school studies, collected in *Waiting for God* (1951), she argued that learning to attend to a geometry problem trains us to attend to a person. Those who suffer, she wrote in substance, need above all people able to give them their attention, and the fullness of love for one's neighbor is simply being able to ask, and really mean, what are you going through? That kind of attention, she thought, is so rare that it is almost a miracle.

Weil distinguished ordinary suffering from affliction, *malheur*, which crushes a person's standing and marks the soul, so that others turn away. That is exactly where justice without love fails. Ideology can speak eloquently about the afflicted as a category while being unable to look at a single afflicted person for long, especially one who doesn't fit the story, who votes the wrong way, or who is angry at the people trying to help. Attention keeps the face in view. It is slow, it can't be performed for an audience, and it produces no slogans.

Weil never entered the church by baptism, but here she was reading the Gospels well. Jesus kept stopping for one person: the woman who touched his cloak in a pressing crowd, the blind man the crowd wanted quiet, the tax collector in the tree. The crowd was the cause. The person was the reason. John put the test where no movement can avoid it: "If anyone says, 'I love God,' and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who does not love his brother whom he has seen cannot love God whom he has not seen" (1 John 4:20, ESV). The brother whom he has seen. Not humanity. The one in the room.

Solidarity is finally the shape of what God did. Paul told the Philippians to look to the interests of others and grounded it in Christ, who "emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men" (Philippians 2:7, ESV). The God who saw Israel's affliction in Egypt came down to deliver them. The Son who can sympathize with our weaknesses took them into his own body. And he didn't do it only for his friends. "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). That is the one thing tribal loyalty can never imitate. The solidarity of the cross reached its enemies. It bore a debt it did not owe, *in solidum*, the whole of it, for people who were on the other side.

That leaves a question no essay can settle. The right and the left will keep using the word, and the church will keep being tempted to borrow one side's moral energy. The better test is personal and uncomfortable. Does my justice still need an enemy? Is there a suffering person I have stopped seeing because their pain belongs to the wrong tribe, or because they are inconvenient to my argument? When I say solidarity, do I mean the wounded, or do I mean my own? Paul said that without love a person could give away everything and gain nothing, and the Corinthians he wrote to were not wicked people. They were gifted, committed, and right about a great deal. That is what makes the warning hard to set down. The face I have stopped seeing is still there, and the God who came down has not stopped seeing it.
