Open the calendar on your phone and look past the appointments to the year underneath them. The fiscal quarter closes, back-to-school sales arrive in July, the Friday after Thanksgiving opens its doors before dawn, a weekend in May remembers the nation's war dead, and the commercial approach to the twenty-fifth of December starts somewhere around Halloween. Nobody sat you down and taught you this year. You absorbed it the way you absorbed your first language, and like a first language it now does some of your thinking for you.

Each of those calendars tells a story about what time is for. The market's year says time is a series of occasions to want something and then buy it. The nation's year says time is the memory of a people's founding and sacrifices, rehearsed so the people will go on being that people. Neither story is simply wicked, and I haven't escaped either one. But neither has any room for a crucified Jew from Nazareth, except as a seasonal ornament on something else.

**The church keeps its own calendar because Christianity is a claim about an event, and a people who stop rehearsing an event slowly stop being shaped by it.** The Christian year, which runs from Advent through Christmas, Epiphany, Lent, Holy Week, the fifty days of Easter, Pentecost and the long green stretch called Ordinary Time, is an older way of counting time, rooted in Israel's appointed feasts and in the first Christians' stubborn habit of gathering on the day Jesus rose. Whether Christians are obliged to keep it is a real question, and faithful churches answer it differently. Whether we'll be formed by some calendar isn't a question at all.

## Time Is Never Neutral

We speak of time as an empty container, blank until we fill it. Charles Taylor argues in *A Secular Age* (2007) that this picture is a recent and costly achievement. Medieval Christians, he observes, lived in a time that had depth. Ordinary days were threaded through with what he calls higher times, the feasts and holy seasons in which eternity gathered past and present together, so that a believer keeping Good Friday could stand nearer to the crucifixion than to an ordinary afternoon a few months before. Modernity flattened that depth into what Taylor, borrowing a phrase from Walter Benjamin, calls homogeneous, empty time, every hour interchangeable with every other. Once every hour is interchangeable, every hour can be priced.

Emptied time doesn't stay empty. James K. A. Smith argues in *You Are What You Love* (2016) that we're lovers before we're thinkers, and that our loves are trained less by the ideas we hold than by the practices we repeat. He calls those practices liturgies, and he insists the mall has them as surely as the cathedral does. A year is the largest liturgy most of us ever live inside. The historian Leigh Eric Schmidt shows in *Consumer Rites* (1995) how American retailers, printers and advertisers rebuilt the holiday year in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, turning Christmas into the great season of shopping and Easter into a parade of new clothes. Merchants didn't invent the human wish to mark time with gifts. But the calendar they helped build repeats one lesson every few weeks: the good life lies just past the next purchase.

The nation keeps a year too. Robert Bellah's essay "Civil Religion in America" (1967) described a public faith with its own sacred texts, its martyrs and its holy days, Memorial Day and Thanksgiving chief among them. Bellah wasn't sneering. He thought a civil religion could hold a people to a standard above itself. But even at its best, the nation's calendar remembers the nation.

So the difference isn't that the church's year is religious and the others secular. All three are liturgical. The difference lies in what gets remembered and who stands at the center of the memory. The market's calendar hardly remembers at all; it lives on anticipation. The nation's calendar remembers us. The church's calendar remembers what God has done, and part of what it remembers is what we did to him. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus gave the church its word for that kind of memory: "And he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, 'This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me'" (Luke 22:19, ESV). The Greek word behind remembrance, *anamnesis*, means more than nostalgia. It's a remembering that places the one who remembers back inside the event, and it judges before it heals. A market has no use for memory like that, and a nation can rarely afford it.

## Israel's Appointed Times

The instinct to order time around the acts of God begins on the first page of Scripture, where creation moves in a cadence of evening and morning toward a day set apart: "So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it God rested from all his work that he had done in creation" (Genesis 2:3, ESV). Before Israel receives a single instruction about worship, the week already has joints in it.

Leviticus 23 turns the week into a year, and it opens with a claim of ownership: "Speak to the people of Israel and say to them, These are the appointed feasts of the LORD that you shall proclaim as holy convocations; they are my appointed feasts" (Leviticus 23:2, ESV). The calendar belongs to God, not to the people who keep it. It begins with the weekly Sabbath and moves through the farming year: Passover on the fourteenth day of the first month, the week of Unleavened Bread, the sheaf of firstfruits, the Feast of Weeks counted fifty days later, and in the autumn the trumpets, the Day of Atonement and the Feast of Booths. Harvest and history are braided together, so the farming schedule becomes a rehearsal of what God did. Families living in shelters at Booths are told the reason: "that your generations may know that I made the people of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 23:43, ESV).

That phrase, *your generations*, carries the logic of the whole system. The people who crossed the sea didn't need a feast to remember it. Their grandchildren did. So Passover was built to provoke a question at the table: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?'" (Exodus 12:26, ESV). The feasts were a defense against forgetting. (The weekly Sabbath gets its own treatment in [What Is the Sabbath and Should Christians Keep It Today?](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it).)

The same Scriptures record the calendar going hollow. To a Jerusalem that was religious and violent at once, God said through Isaiah, "Your new moons and your appointed feasts my soul hates; they have become a burden to me; I am weary of bearing them" (Isaiah 1:14, ESV). He didn't retract the feasts he had given. He refused to be flattered by them while the poor were crushed outside the sanctuary. A sacred calendar sanctifies no one. It only gives a people a recurring occasion to remember whose they are, and people can turn even that into a way of forgetting.

Then the New Testament makes its claim about time: "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4, ESV). The Son entered a people who kept this calendar, and he kept it with them. Then the calendar bent around him. He died at Passover, and Paul could tell a largely Gentile church that "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7, ESV) and expect them to follow. The Spirit came at the Feast of Weeks, on the fiftieth day: "When the day of Pentecost arrived, they were all together in one place" (Acts 2:1, ESV). The spine of the Christian year is Israel's year, fulfilled. Pascha is Passover opened to the nations, and Pentecost is the harvest feast on which the first sheaf of the church was gathered.

## How the First Christians Learned to Keep Time

The church's first feast wasn't annual. It was weekly. Luke describes believers at Troas meeting "on the first day of the week" to break bread (Acts 20:7, ESV), and John dates his vision on Patmos by a new name for that day: "I was in the Spirit on the Lord's day, and I heard behind me a loud voice like a trumpet" (Revelation 1:10, ESV). By about 155 Justin Martyr could explain the pattern to a pagan emperor in his *First Apology* (chapter 67): on the day named for the sun, believers gathered, heard the apostles and prophets read, prayed and shared the bread and cup, because God began creation on that day and Christ rose on it. Sunday stayed an ordinary workday until Constantine made it a legal day of rest in 321, so for nearly three centuries the Lord's day was kept at a cost, fitted around a working day.

The oldest annual feast is Easter, the Pascha. Thomas Talley's *The Origins of the Liturgical Year* (1986), long the standard scholarly account, argues that the paschal feast reaches back close to the apostolic generation and was the church's first annual feast, with the fifty days of rejoicing after it and a fast before it growing up around it. The church's first quarrel over the calendar was about this feast. The churches of Asia Minor kept Pascha on the fourteenth of the Jewish month of Nisan, the date of Passover, whatever the day of the week, and they traced the custom to the apostle John; from the Latin for fourteenth they came to be called Quartodecimans. Rome and most other churches kept it on the following Sunday. Irenaeus of Lyons, in a letter Eusebius preserves in his *Ecclesiastical History* (5.24), recalls that around 155 Polycarp of Smyrna visited Anicetus of Rome, that neither persuaded the other, and that they shared the Eucharist before parting. In the 190s Victor of Rome tried to cut the Asian churches off from communion, and Irenaeus, himself a Sunday observer, admonished him. The gist of his reasoning deserves to be remembered: the churches' difference over the fast confirmed their agreement in the faith.

The Council of Nicaea met in 325 chiefly to answer Arius on the deity of Christ, but it also settled that every church should keep Easter on the same Sunday, no longer depending on the Jewish reckoning of Passover. The familiar formula keyed to the spring full moon was worked out in later tables, not written into the canons. Honesty requires one more sentence. The letter of Constantine that Eusebius preserves in his *Life of Constantine*, announcing the decision, justifies the break from Jewish reckoning in language of open contempt for the Jewish people. The church's calendar was born from Israel's, and when it declared its independence it spoke of its mother with scorn. That's a sin to confess, not a footnote to skip.

Christmas came later, and its origin is disputed. There's no clear evidence of a feast of Christ's birth in the second century. The first firm witness is a Roman list of commemorations preserved in the Chronograph of 354, which reflects Roman practice as of 336 and places Christ's birth in Bethlehem on 25 December. Many Eastern churches meanwhile kept the birth and the baptism together on 6 January, the feast that became Epiphany.

Two explanations of the December date compete. The first, usually called the history of religions theory, holds that the church set the Nativity on the Roman festival of the unconquered sun, Sol Invictus, whose cult the emperor Aurelian had raised to prominence in 274, answering the sun's birthday with the birth of the Sun of righteousness. The weakness is that the earliest clear evidence of a solar festival on 25 December comes from the same mid-fourth-century collection as the evidence for Christmas, and no Christian writer of the time says the date was chosen to displace it. The second, the calculation theory, was proposed by the French historian Louis Duchesne in 1889 and developed by Talley. It holds that some early Christians believed Christ was conceived on the same day of the year on which he died, and that Latin tradition dated the crucifixion to 25 March; nine months later is 25 December, and an Eastern reckoning of the passion on 6 April would yield 6 January. Its weakness is that the texts making the link explicit are few, and some postdate the feast. The question is open. What both theories share matters more than where they part: either way the church placed Christ's birth where it could say something true about him, and either way the popular claim that Christmas is a pagan holiday with Christian paint runs well ahead of the evidence.

## The Shape of the Year

The year that grew from those roots starts in the dark rather than in January, on the fourth Sunday before Christmas, with Advent. Newcomers expecting a countdown to the manger are often surprised to find its first Sundays given to judgment and the return of Christ. Advent looks back to the first coming and forward to the second, and it teaches a people to wait for what they know has already begun (the season has [its own essay](/writing/what-is-advent)). Christmas follows as a season of twelve days, beginning where the stores' celebration ends. On 6 January comes Epiphany, the feast of the magi and of Christ shown to the nations, and the Sundays after it follow his baptism and first signs.

Then the year turns toward the cross. Lent begins on Ash Wednesday and runs forty days, Sundays not counted, patterned on Christ's forty days in the wilderness; it's the church's season of repentance and of preparing new believers for baptism, treated fully in [a separate essay on Lent](/writing/why-lent). It ends in Holy Week: the entry into Jerusalem, the supper and the washing of feet, the crucifixion, the silence of Saturday and the vigil that breaks into Easter morning. Easter itself isn't a day. It's fifty days, a season of rejoicing longer than the fast that prepared for it, as [the essay on Easter](/writing/easter-is-not-a-day) argues, running through the Ascension to Pentecost and the Spirit poured out. After Pentecost comes the longest stretch of all, Ordinary Time, which runs back to Advent and also fills the weeks between Epiphany and Lent.

Every twelve months, then, the church walks through the whole story of Jesus, from promise to the gift of the Spirit, and spends the rest of the year living in its consequence. The story comes as time to live in rather than information to recall: in Advent people wait, in Lent they repent with their bodies by going without, and at Easter the church feasts for seven weeks because one Sunday is too small for what happened.

Alexander Schmemann, the Orthodox theologian whose *For the Life of the World* (1963) has been read by Protestants ever since it appeared, gave this its deepest account. He argued that Christianity doesn't sanctify time by carving a few sacred days out of a profane year. The Lord's day, in the tradition he inherited, is the eighth day, the first day of the new creation breaking into the old week. When the church keeps its feasts, Schmemann said, it enters the kingdom those events opened instead of commemorating them from a safe distance, and then it's sent back into ordinary days to carry that kingdom into the world. Time, on this view, is redeemed rather than escaped.

Robert Webber, an evangelical who taught for decades at Wheaton College, made the same case to Bible churches and Baptists. In *Ancient-Future Time* (2004) he argued that the Christian year is itself a spirituality, a way of being shaped into the life of Christ by walking through that life season by season. Bobby Gross, writing for a similar readership in *Living the Christian Year* (2009), put the thesis in his subtitle: the seasons are time given to inhabit the story of God. Those of us who came to faith from outside the church, as I came from atheism, arrive with none of this in our bones. The church's year has to be learned one December and one spring at a time, and the learning mostly feels like repetition. Repetition here doesn't mean a loop, though. The market's calendar circles back to the same wanting and goes nowhere, because going nowhere is its purpose. The church's calendar tells an unchanging story to a person who has changed since the last telling, and so the same Advent lands somewhere new in us each time it returns.

## The Long Green Season

Ask most people what they know about the church year and they'll name Christmas and Easter. Almost nobody can name the season that fills more of the calendar than all the others combined. Ordinary Time runs thirty-three or thirty-four weeks, a few after Epiphany and the long remainder from Pentecost to Advent, with no carols and no cultural footprint. The name refers to counting: its Sundays are numbered in order, the way ordinary weeks actually pass.

The name misleads, and then the misleading sense turns out to be true. Most of a life is the long middle: the same commute and the same dishes, prayers that seem to rise no higher than the ceiling, a faithfulness asked of us on a Wednesday that nobody will notice. The Gospels keep that proportion. They narrate about three years of Jesus' life, and nearly two decades are covered by a single sentence: "And Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52, ESV). The eternal Son spent most of his human life at work no one recorded, in a town with a poor reputation. If God incarnate gave some thirty years to the unrecorded and three to the public, we may have our own proportions badly out of order.

Eugene Peterson saw the cost of that disorder. *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* (1980) took its title, pointedly, from Friedrich Nietzsche, and its substance from the Psalms of Ascents, the songs pilgrims sang on the road up to Jerusalem for Israel's appointed feasts. Peterson argued that American Christianity had trained believers to crave the instant and the dramatic and had left them little patience for discipleship as it actually is, the same small fidelities repeated for years in one direction. Paul, whose own conversion was as dramatic as conversions get, urged the Thessalonians "to aspire to live quietly, and to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands" (1 Thessalonians 4:11, ESV). We hardly know what to do with an aspiration like that. We aspire to be seen.

I don't stand outside that indictment. As a pastor I've been tempted to measure ministry by the big Sundays and to treat the unrecorded weeks of visiting and teaching as the part that didn't count, when those weeks were the ministry. The green season teaches a distinction our culture can't make: faithfulness isn't fruitfulness, and we're answerable for the first. Jesus described a farmer who scatters seed and goes on with his ordinary life: "He sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows; he knows not how" (Mark 4:27, ESV). The growth belongs to God. The sowing belongs to us.

## Is the Church Calendar Biblical?

Many Christians who don't keep the church year decline it on principle, and the principle deserves to be stated as its defenders would state it. God alone may say how he's to be worshiped, and the New Testament appoints one holy day, the Lord's day. Everything else in the calendar is human invention, however old and lovely, and the apostles warned against binding consciences with it. Paul was alarmed at the Galatians: "You observe days and months and seasons and years!" (Galatians 4:10, ESV). He told the Colossians, "Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath" (Colossians 2:16, ESV). History, the objectors add, proved the danger. By the late Middle Ages the Western calendar was crowded with saints' days, many binding under pain of sin and tied to relics, pilgrimages and a piety of earning favor, and the gospel of free grace was hard to hear beneath them. Clearing the ground back to what Christ commanded meant the calendar had to go.

Parts of the Reformed tradition drew that conclusion, the Scottish church and the English Puritans most sharply. Scotland's First Book of Discipline (1560) judged that Christmas, Epiphany and the feasts of Mary and the saints should be abolished, because Scripture gave them neither command nor warrant. The Westminster Confession (1646) states the governing idea, usually called the regulative principle: the acceptable way of worshiping God is instituted by God himself, not left to human imagination. In June 1647 Parliament abolished the observance of Christmas, Easter and Whitsun as festivals. The ban provoked rioting in Canterbury that December, and Christmas returned with the monarchy in 1660. Massachusetts fined anyone caught keeping Christmas five shillings from 1659 until 1681. Stephen Nissenbaum shows in *The Battle for Christmas* (1996) that the Puritans weren't only objecting to a doctrine. The Christmas they knew was a season of drink, misrule and license, and they saw little of Christ in it.

Other Protestants read the same Bible and kept the year. The Augsburg Confession (1530), in its fifteenth article, keeps holy days and festivals that can be observed without sin and serve good order, provided no one is taught that keeping them earns grace. Thomas Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer (1549) carried the seasons into English worship with a collect for each Sunday. So the Reformation didn't divide those who loved Scripture from those who didn't. It divided two convictions about how Scripture governs worship: what Scripture doesn't forbid may be kept if it edifies, or what Scripture doesn't command must be left out. Baptists, who grew out of English Separatism in the early seventeenth century, carried the Puritan suspicion and for much of their history kept little of the church year beyond, in time, Christmas and Easter. In recent decades that has changed. Advent wreaths, Ash Wednesday, Holy Week services and lectionary readings have found their way into many Baptist and evangelical congregations, a hunger Webber had already traced in *Evangelicals on the Canterbury Trail* (1985), his account of evangelicals drawn toward liturgical worship.

The question is second-order. The creeds confess Christ's death, resurrection and ascension and the gift of the Spirit, but they bind no one to a calendar, and churches that differ here can recognize one another as faithful. The texts the objectors cite, read in their settings, point the same way. In Galatians, Paul is alarmed at Gentile believers turning back to "the weak and worthless elementary principles of the world" (Galatians 4:9, ESV), keeping the law's days as though their standing with God hung on them. In Colossians the festivals are "a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:17, ESV), and the warning shields believers from those who would judge them for not keeping such days. What Paul refuses is a day kept as a condition of grace or laid on another's conscience. In Romans he makes room for the day kept to the Lord: "One person esteems one day as better than another, while another esteems all days alike. Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind. The one who observes the day, observes it in honor of the Lord" (Romans 14:5–6, ESV). This essay concludes that the church year is an ancient and wise gift that Christians are free to keep and should never be compelled to keep. Those who kept it were right that it can serve the gospel. The Puritans were right that it can't be made a law.

The strongest objection should stay visible, because this essay can't fully answer it. A feast the church sets apart, the Puritans warned, the church can't protect. Christmas is the proof: the feast they tried to abolish is now kept by believers and unbelievers alike and is also the retail economy's largest season, just as Schmidt and Nissenbaum describe. Advent can be sold back to us as a candle and a chocolate calendar. The best reply is that abandoning the calendar didn't protect the Puritans' heirs either, since the market simply wrote their year for them. That's true, but it doesn't close the question. A calendar is only as faithful as the people keeping it, which Isaiah said long before the Puritans did.

## Whose Time Are You Keeping?

The first Christians knew time was contested ground. The Roman year was full of festivals to the gods and the emperor, and Tertullian, writing *On Idolatry* around 200, scolded believers who joined the Saturnalia and the New Year gift-giving, noting that pagans would never keep the Lord's day or Pentecost for fear of being taken for Christians. To keep a different calendar in a Roman city was a public act of allegiance.

Our year has its Caesars too, and the pull comes from both directions. A church on the right can let the nation's calendar set its rhythm until the Sunday nearest the Fourth of July outweighs Pentecost. A church on the left can let a calendar of causes and observance months organize its preaching just as thoroughly. Both are the civil religion Bellah described, keeping its feasts inside the house of God, and nearly all of us let the market decide when we feel generous and what a season owes us. Pastors, and I'm one, feel that pull as much as anyone in the pews.

That's the distinction the whole question turns on. The market's time is for acquiring, and the nation's time is for remembering ourselves. The church's time is for remembering Christ, and it's the only one of the three that begins by accusing us. At the center of the Christian year stands a Friday on which a religious feast and an imperial order worked together. Passover crowds filled Jerusalem, Caesar's governor kept the peace, and between the temple's calendar and Rome's the Son of God was put to death. Every calendar a people uses to celebrate itself was represented at the cross and found guilty there. No nation keeps a day to confess that its sins crucified the Son of God. The church keeps one every spring.

Then the year does what no other calendar can. The resurrection came on the first day of the week, an ordinary workday. The women went to the tomb at dawn with spices, before the week's labor began, on a morning no calendar in Jerusalem or Rome had marked as anything, and the new creation began there, unannounced, in ordinary time. Every Sunday since has dated the world from that morning.

None of this settles what you'll do with your own year, and it shouldn't. The psalmist's prayer is the one to carry out: "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (Psalm 90:12, ESV). Your days are being numbered either way. Next week is already scheduled, and the year is already telling you what it's for. What remains is which story gets to count the days you have left, and whether it's one in which you're the hero or one in which you needed to be saved.
