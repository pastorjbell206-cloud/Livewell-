On most Sundays, in most churches on earth, a strange thing happens near the end of the service. People who didn't come for a meal receive a piece of bread about the size of a coin and a swallow of wine or juice. To a visitor it can look like a leftover, a ritual whose meaning wore off long ago. Plenty of Christians half agree. We keep the practice because Jesus commanded it, and we're a little embarrassed by it, because we've absorbed the assumption that the more spiritual a faith is, the less it should need things.

The assumption deserves a fair hearing. Religion, on this view, is the department of life that deals with the soul, and a mature faith would live in ideas and inward experience, with bread and water as training wheels for people not yet able to meet God in the mind. There is truth in the instinct. "God is spirit," Jesus told the woman at the well (John 4:24, ESV), and the prophets were merciless with people who trusted the temple while their hearts were elsewhere. A religion of objects can become a religion of magic, and the Reformation was fought partly over that danger.

But the assumption can't explain what Christianity actually does. It washes people in water, feeds them bread and wine, and anoints the sick with oil. Its central claim is not that a soul climbed out of a body but that God took one on. The skeptic's question is fair: why would a faith that calls itself spiritual keep putting its deepest meaning into things you can chew? The churches have never agreed on how Christ is present at his table. They have agreed, nearly all of them, that the table is not empty, and the reason they agree tells you more about Christianity than most of its arguments do.

## Why would a spiritual faith need bread?

The suspicion that matter is beneath God is older than the church. In the second century it took a form the church learned to call Gnosticism, a family of movements rather than a single sect. Its teachers differed, but they shared a verdict: the physical world was a mistake or a prison, made by a lesser power, and the enlightened soul's task was to escape it. The body was not really you. Salvation meant getting out.

It is still an attractive idea. It tells us our truest self is the weightless one inside, untouched by the failures of the flesh, and people who have been hurt in their bodies have sometimes found real comfort there. The comfort deserves respect before it is questioned.

The church questioned it anyway, and the reason was the first page of its own Scriptures. Genesis has God pronouncing each made thing good, until the sixth day ends with a verdict on the whole physical order: "And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31, ESV). When some early teachers began forbidding marriage and certain foods as if holiness meant withdrawal from created things, Paul's answer to Timothy was blunt: "For everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4, ESV). In context Paul isn't endorsing appetite for its own sake. He is refusing a spirituality that treats the Creator's gifts as contamination, and he names the right posture toward them as thanksgiving. The Greek word behind that thanksgiving is the one the church would later use for its meal, *eucharistia*.

Then the faith goes further than any creation doctrine requires. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14, ESV). The eternal Son took a body that got hungry and slept and, in the end, was killed. Some in the early church found that indecent and said Christ only seemed to have a body, a view the church called Docetism, from the Greek for "to seem." And the risen Jesus, far from shedding his body, insists on it: "See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me, and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have" (Luke 24:39, ESV).

What is striking is how quickly these convictions attached themselves to the meal. Ignatius of Antioch, writing to the church at Smyrna around 110 while under guard on the way to his death in Rome, observed that the people who denied Christ's real flesh also stayed away from the Eucharist. They couldn't confess, he said, that the bread was the flesh of the Savior who suffered for our sins and whom the Father raised. For Ignatius the two refusals were one refusal. If the flesh didn't matter, the meal didn't either.

Irenaeus of Lyons, writing *Against Heresies* around 180, turned the argument around and made the meal a witness against the Gnostics. In the fourth book (IV.18) he asks how these teachers can offer bread and wine at all, as the firstfruits of creation, if they believe creation is the product of ignorance and defect. Their worship contradicts their doctrine. Then comes the claim that has echoed through the tradition since: the church's teaching agrees with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn confirms the teaching. Bread that has received the invocation of God, he argues, is no longer common bread but a Eucharist made of two realities, an earthly and a heavenly, and bodies that receive it are not left to corruption but carry the hope of resurrection. The meal, on this logic, is a small argument for the resurrection of the body.

Christian hope is not a soul's escape but the raising of the body: "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44, ESV), where "spiritual" in Paul's sense means a body wholly animated by God's Spirit, not a body abolished. A God who made matter, entered it and means to raise it is exactly the kind of God who would keep meeting his people through water and bread. The physical signs are not a concession to the simple. They fit the whole shape of the story.

## What happened at the Last Supper

The meal goes back to a specific night, and the setting governs the meaning. Jesus was keeping Passover, or a meal set deliberately in its season. Passover was a meal Israel ate its way into, with the bread of affliction and the retelling that made each generation speak as if it had been there. The instruction in Exodus puts the words in the father's mouth: "You shall tell your son on that day, 'It is because of what the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt'" (Exodus 13:8, ESV). Not what the Lord did for them, long ago, but for me. The Mishnah, compiled around 200, states the principle outright: in every generation a person is bound to regard himself as though he personally came out of Egypt.

Into that meal Jesus put himself. Mark's account is the barest: "And as they were eating, he took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to them, and said, 'Take; this is my body'" (Mark 14:22, ESV). The night before his death, he didn't hand his friends a doctrine of the atonement. He gave them something to eat and told them what it was. Luke's wording, the closest to Paul's, adds the phrase Luther's catechism would later build on: "This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19, ESV), and of the cup, "This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20, ESV).

The earliest written account, though, comes in a letter Paul wrote to Corinth in the mid-50s, some twenty years after the crucifixion, handing on a tradition he had received:

> For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread, (1 Corinthians 11:23, ESV)

> and when he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, "This is my body, which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me." (1 Corinthians 11:24, ESV)

> In the same way also he took the cup, after supper, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me." (1 Corinthians 11:25, ESV)

> For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes. (1 Corinthians 11:26, ESV)

Paul doesn't quote these words to settle a theory of presence. He quotes them because the Corinthians were eating in a way that shamed their poorer members, and he tells them that what they are eating is not the Lord's Supper at all. That quarrel belongs to the site's essay on [what the Bible says about hunger and food insecurity](/writing/church-food-insecurity), which treats it in full. What matters here is how Paul closes it. Careless eating, he says, makes a person "guilty concerning the body and blood of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 11:27, ESV), and then:

> For anyone who eats and drinks without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment on himself. (1 Corinthians 11:29, ESV)

Christians have read "discerning the body" two ways: as recognizing Christ's body given in the meal, or as recognizing the church gathered at the table, including its poorest members. In context neither reading can be dropped, since the offense is contempt for the hungry and the guilt is "concerning the body and blood of the Lord." Paul holds both together. You can't honor Christ at the table while despising the people he has seated there.

A chapter earlier, warning the same church against meals in pagan temples, Paul had written:

> The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a participation in the body of Christ? (1 Corinthians 10:16, ESV)

> Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread. (1 Corinthians 10:17, ESV)

The word translated "participation" is *koinōnia*, fellowship or sharing, and the argument assumes the meal is more than a private thought. Eating at an idol's table joins you to something real, Paul says, and so does eating at Christ's; you can't share both, because each table makes you a partner of its host. Whatever else Paul believed about the Supper, he believed something happens there. Augustine pressed the same verse on the newly baptized of North Africa in the early fifth century. If you are the body of Christ and its members, he told them in the sermon numbered 272, it is your own mystery that lies on the Lord's table, and he sent them to it with a line the church has kept: *Estote quod videtis, et accipite quod estis*, be what you see, and receive what you are.

Then there is the phrase at the center of the argument, "in remembrance of me." The Greek is *anamnēsis*, and the English "remembrance" can mislead us into hearing a purely mental act. Gregory Dix, an Anglican Benedictine, argued in *The Shape of the Liturgy* (1945) that in Scripture and the early church the word carried a stronger sense: a recalling that brings a past saving act into the present so that the worshiper stands inside it, much as the Passover father spoke of what the Lord did "for me." Not every scholar follows Dix all the way. But the convergence text the World Council of Churches issued at Lima in 1982, *Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry*, drew on this recovered sense, describing the memorial of Christ as the living and effective sign of his sacrifice rather than a bare mental recollection, and it was drafted by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant theologians working together. Anamnesis is remembering that includes you in what is remembered.

## Four answers to one small word

The churches agree that Jesus said "This is my body." They have divided for five hundred years over what he meant by *is*. Here are the four main answers, in the form their own defenders would recognize. It is a map, not a verdict.

The first answer is the Roman Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation. The Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 used the word in its opening canon, confessing that Christ's body and blood are truly contained in the sacrament under the appearances of bread and wine, the bread and wine changed by divine power. Thomas Aquinas gave the teaching its classic form a half-century later, distinguishing a thing's substance, what it is, from its accidents, how it appears: at the consecration the substance changes while the appearances remain. The Council of Trent, in its decree of October 1551, reaffirmed the teaching and judged the word fitting and proper. A Catholic defender would press three points. Jesus said "is," and the plain sense should stand. The earliest witnesses speak with a realism hard to reduce to symbol. And the doctrine is not a claim about chemistry, since it concerns what the thing is before God, not what it is under a microscope. The Eastern Orthodox hold as firmly that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood, and they have generally preferred to leave the manner of the change a mystery.

The second answer is Martin Luther's. In *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) he attacked transubstantiation as a piece of Aristotle imposed on the gospel, and offered an image instead: iron in the fire, where fire and iron are both fully present without either ceasing to be itself. So Christ's body and blood are present with the bread and wine remaining. His heirs gave the view its confessional form in the *Formula of Concord* (1577), which calls it a sacramental union and teaches that the body and blood are present in, with and under the bread and wine, received by the mouth, and received even by those who eat unworthily, though to their judgment. A Lutheran defender would say the point is promise: the meal carries its own word, "given for you," and in it Christ offers forgiveness to the one who eats. The gift doesn't depend on the strength of my faith; it depends on the one who said it.

The third answer is John Calvin's, set out most fully in the final edition of the *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1559). Calvin thought Rome and Luther both brought Christ's body down into the bread, when Scripture places his risen body at the Father's right hand. Yet he was sure the Supper is no empty sign, since God does not mock his people with bare pictures. So he reversed the direction. By the secret power of the Holy Spirit, who joins things separated in space, believers are lifted up to Christ and truly fed on his body and blood. The old liturgical call to lift up your hearts was, for Calvin, nearly the whole doctrine. He admitted the mystery was something he would sooner experience than understand. The Westminster Confession (1646) gave this view its lasting English wording, teaching that worthy receivers feed on Christ crucified really and indeed, yet not carnally and corporally but spiritually, and the Baptists who wrote the Second London Confession of 1689 took over the same language nearly word for word.

The fourth answer is Ulrich Zwingli's, from Zurich. In *On the Lord's Supper* (1526) he argued that "is" in "This is my body" works the way it does when Jesus says he is the door or the vine, meaning "signifies." His anchor text was a verse from John 6: "It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life" (John 6:63, ESV). Christ's body, he reasoned, is in heaven, and faith, not eating, is how a person receives him. The Supper is the church's thanksgiving and remembrance and its public pledge, the old Latin sense of *sacramentum* as a soldier's oath. A Zwinglian defender would say this view guards two things the others endanger: that grace comes to faith and never by a ritual performed on its own, and that Christ's true humanity is not dissolved into a body everywhere at once. His best heirs have never meant the Supper is empty, only that the gathered church remembering in faith is itself a real meeting with the living Lord. Many Baptist and free-church Christians hold some form of this view, often calling the meal an ordinance rather than a sacrament.

At Marburg in 1529 Luther and Zwingli agreed on nearly everything put before them and parted over this one question, a story told in the site's essay on [why there are so many Christian denominations](/writing/why-there-are-so-many-christian-denominations). The split has lasted. But none of the four ever said the meal didn't matter.

## How much the disagreement matters

Questions should be sorted by weight before they're argued. Some things the historic creeds bind: the Trinity, the full deity and humanity of Christ, his death for sinners, his bodily resurrection. The Supper rests directly on that floor, a meal of the Word made flesh, given for us and raised, eaten "until he comes." Other questions divide faithful churches without dividing the faith itself. The manner of Christ's presence at the table belongs there, along with who may receive and who may preside. Still others are open matters of wisdom and custom, like the kind of bread or how often the meal is kept.

That sorting needs a caveat. The Nicene Creed says nothing about the Eucharist. But the Catholic and Orthodox churches would not accept the label second-order. For them the Eucharist is bound up with the reality of the church, which is why they don't share the table with Protestants, and a confessional Lutheran feels the question nearly as keenly. To call it second-order is already to stand somewhere, and the sorting here is offered as the way most Protestant churches have learned to live with one another, not as a neutral ruling.

Where does this essay land? It doesn't choose among the four, and it doesn't pretend the choice is unimportant. It lands on the conviction traced here from Genesis forward, which all four at their best can sign. The bread and the cup are not pegs on which we hang a private thought. The table is a place God has chosen to meet his people, as he chose water and flesh before it, and what is given there is the crucified and risen Lord himself, not merely an idea about him. Those who eat are joined to him and to one another. Even Zwingli, the most symbolic of the four, didn't think a believer walked away from the table with nothing; he thought the church met its Lord in the act of remembering his death until he comes. That shared conviction is the ground the essay stands on.

The strongest objection to that landing comes from the Catholic and Lutheran side, and it hasn't been fully answered here. Jesus said "is," they would say, and to stop at "something happens" is to answer the question by declining it. That is a fair charge. The only reply this essay can offer is that five centuries of faithful and learned Christians, reading the same texts, have not resolved it, and that the deeper danger for most readers is not choosing the wrong one of the four. It is quietly holding a fifth view that none of the Reformers held: that the meal is a nice custom and nothing more. A Zwinglian will object from the other side that talk of God meeting his people in bread opens the door to superstition, and he too is owed a hearing. A reader can weigh both, disagree, and remain a brother or sister. The parallel argument at the font, sacrament or ordinance, is taken up in the site's essay on [what baptism is and why it matters](/writing/why-baptism).

## How the table became rare

The first Christians ate this meal often. Luke's summary of the Jerusalem church lists it among four marks of the community's life: "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42, ESV). At Troas the church gathered "on the first day of the week" (Acts 20:7, ESV) to break bread. Justin Martyr, explaining Christian worship to the emperor around 155 in his *First Apology*, describes a Sunday gathering where Scripture is read and taught and bread and wine are given thanks over and shared, with deacons carrying portions to the absent. This food, he insists, is not received as common bread. In the same passage he describes a collection from those with means, held for orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. The meal and the care of the poor sat side by side at the same weekly table.

Over the next thousand years the eating drifted to the edges. By the high Middle Ages in the West the mass was said daily, but most laypeople received rarely, watching the elevated host with devotion and going home. The Fourth Lateran Council's twenty-first canon, which required every believer who had reached the age of discretion to confess at least once a year and to receive communion at least at Easter, tells you what practice had become; a church doesn't legislate a yearly minimum for something people are already doing weekly.

The Reformers set out to give the meal back to the people, restoring the cup to the laity and inviting the congregation to eat rather than watch. Calvin argued in the *Institutes* that the Supper ought to be set before the church at least once a week. He never persuaded the Genevan magistrates, whose church ordinances of 1541 settled on four celebrations a year, and Zwingli's Zurich had already fixed a similar pattern. In Scotland the rhythm grew rarer still. Leigh Eric Schmidt's *Holy Fairs* (1989) traces how many Scottish and then American Presbyterian parishes kept the Supper once a year or less, in multi-day communion seasons, and how the camp meeting at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801, often remembered as a founding moment of frontier revivalism, began as one of those sacramental occasions (a story told in the site's essay on [the Great Awakenings](/writing/the-great-awakenings-and-the-making-of-american-faith)). Much of American Protestantism settled into a monthly or quarterly habit that most of us inherited without ever deciding it.

The reasons for that rhythm deserve a fair hearing. Many kept the Supper rarely out of reverence, not neglect. They feared frequency would make it routine, and they took Paul's command to examine oneself seriously enough to want weeks of preparation. Churches that eat weekly and carelessly haven't answered that instinct. But the fear has an odd shape. We worried that the meal would become ordinary, and it became rare instead, and rarity turned out to be its own way of making something unimportant. What we keep once a quarter, we slowly learn to treat as an extra.

Charles Taylor gave a name to the deeper current in *A Secular Age* (2007). He calls it excarnation: the long movement in Western Christianity, running through late medieval reform and the Reformation into the modern period, by which faith was relocated out of bodily, communal, ritual forms and into the head. Protestants did it most thoroughly, for reasons we thought good, since we were fighting superstition. The result was a faith that lives in the one place the modern world has already isolated us, the inside of our own minds, and a faith drained of its bodily forms becomes easier to hold privately and easier to put down.

Alexander Schmemann, an Orthodox priest and dean of St. Vladimir's Seminary in New York, saw the same loss from the other side. In *For the Life of the World* (1963) he argues that the human being was made to be the priest of creation, the creature who receives the world as God's gift and offers it back in thanks. All food was meant as communion with God, and the fall was the moment we began to eat the world for its own sake, as if it were ours. The Eucharist, then, is not a sacred corner walled off from ordinary life. It is the church doing in concentrated form what humanity was made to do with every meal, and being sent back out to see the whole world as gift. His criticism lands on every tradition that has made the sacraments a religious department of life, his own included.

Read together, Taylor and Schmemann explain why the quarterly habit and the disembodied faith grew up together. When bread stops bearing grace, the world goes quiet around us. We didn't lose the meal by argument. We lost it by forgetting what it was for.

## Known in the breaking of the bread

On the first Easter, two disciples walked toward Emmaus with a stranger who explained the Scriptures to them for miles, and they didn't recognize him. The explanation didn't open their eyes. "When he was at table with them, he took the bread and blessed and broke it and gave it to them" (Luke 24:30, ESV). Then: "And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And he vanished from their sight" (Luke 24:31, ESV). When they ran back to Jerusalem they told the others "how he was known to them in the breaking of the bread" (Luke 24:35, ESV). Luke narrates the moment with the actions of the Last Supper, taking, blessing, breaking and giving, the four actions Dix found underneath the shape of every ancient liturgy, for readers who would never walk with the risen Jesus on a road. They would meet him where the two finally did.

That is the answer to the skeptic's question. A faith of the spirit uses bread because its God is not a spirit fleeing matter but the Creator who called matter good, the Son who took flesh and kept it, and the Spirit who will raise the dead. A religion of pure inwardness has only one word for the body, rise above it, and that is cold comfort to a creature who will die in one. Christ gave his body for us, and he gives himself to the whole creature who eats and tires and ages, the one he means to raise.

The churches will go on disagreeing about how Christ is at his table. But most of us in the churches of the West are not in danger of holding a mistaken theory of the Supper. We are in danger of having quietly made it small, kept occasionally, received absently, eaten alone in a crowd with the neighbor at the end of the row unseen. The meal was never meant to be small. It was a Passover and a covenant, many people made one body from one loaf, and the proclamation of a death that did not stay a death.

Jesus ended that first supper by looking past it: "Truly, I say to you, I will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new in the kingdom of God" (Mark 14:25, ESV). Every time the church eats, it eats between his death and that day, proclaiming that death "until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26, ESV). The bread is ordinary. The table is often poorly kept, and the people around it, ourselves first, are rarely what the meal says they are. I have come to the table myself with nothing to offer and no faith I could feel, and received anyway, because the meal does not run on the strength of my feelings that morning. None of that has stopped him from keeping the appointment. The question left is the one the two at Emmaus carried home: whether our eyes are open when he breaks the bread, and what becomes of every other table in our lives once they are.
