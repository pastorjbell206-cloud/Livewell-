Two people type nearly the same words into a search box, and they are asking different questions. One is a Christian, often in her late twenties or thirties, who has met a man kinder and steadier than most of the church boys she grew up with, and he doesn't believe in God. She wants to know whether "unequally yoked" really means she has to walk away. The other has been married for years. Maybe he came to faith after the wedding, and his wife watches him leave for church every Sunday with a mixture of bafflement and loss. Maybe she lost her faith in her forties, and her husband now prays alone in the car before he comes inside. Both of them type "married to an unbeliever." Both of them are usually handed the same verse.

That is the first thing to set right. The New Testament speaks one word to the Christian deciding whom to marry and a different word to the Christian already married to someone who doesn't believe, and the two words don't contradict each other. A great deal of harm has come from confusing them. A text meant to govern a choice gets fired at a covenant already made, so that a believing wife hears her marriage described as a sin she is living in. Or a text meant to hold a marriage together gets quoted to excuse walking into one with eyes half shut. The distinction this essay turns on is the one Paul himself draws, between the counsel given before the vow and the counsel given inside it.

The question isn't marginal. As more Americans have come to claim no religion at all, marriages across the line of belief have become an ordinary part of church life. The question sits in our pews every Sunday, often as one person at the end of a row with an empty seat beside them. I came to faith from atheism, and I read this subject with more sympathy for the person on the far side of the yoke than a good deal of Christian writing on it shows. That sympathy is part of why I want to say both words plainly.

## The case for marrying a good man who doesn't believe

Begin with the strongest case for the marriage, because it deserves better than the caricature it usually gets. He is good. He is honest with money, patient with her mother, gentle with children, and faithful in ways that several professing Christians she dated were not. Scripture itself teaches that God's common grace is at work outside the church; in Romans 2 Paul says that Gentiles who don't have the law sometimes do by nature what the law requires, because its work is written on their hearts. Why should a label on a survey outweigh a character she has watched for three years? She knows Christian marriages that are cold, controlling and miserable. She knows church men who can quote Ephesians 5 and can't wash a dish. And the field is thin. Christian women in particular often find congregations with fewer single men in them than single women, so "wait for a believer" can sound a great deal like "be alone."

The case has a public version too. Robert Putnam and David Campbell argued in *American Grace* (2010) that Americans became more tolerant of one another's religions largely through personal ties across the lines: friendships, neighbors and, above all, marriages. They called it the Aunt Susan principle: it's hard to believe that everyone outside your faith is lost when your Aunt Susan, the most saintly person you know, is one of them. Then there is the missionary hope, the oldest argument of all. Plenty of people have come to faith through a believing spouse. Who is to say he won't?

Some of that is simply true, and the church has earned part of the skepticism behind it. We've often taught young Christians to look for a "Christian guy" as if the word were the substance, as if attendance and a youth-group vocabulary guaranteed a shared faith. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton found in *Soul Searching* (2005) that the working religion of many American teenagers raised in church was what they named moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be nice, wants them to feel good about themselves, and otherwise stays out of the way until he's needed. Many of those teenagers are now the adults in the singles class. If the church's counsel amounts to "marry anyone who checks the box," it has no answer for the woman who can see that the box-checker is less faithful than the unbeliever. We did that. The rule came to sound tribal because we taught it tribally.

But notice what the steelman asks marriage to be. It treats marriage as a reward for goodness, a verdict on a person's character: if he's good enough, he qualifies. The Christian account of marriage has never worked that way. Marriage is a shared life with a direction, two people bound to one task, and the question it asks isn't whether he is good. It's whether the two of you can pull the same plow.

## What the yoke is for

The phrase everyone quotes comes from 2 Corinthians 6:14: "Do not be unequally yoked with unbelievers. For what partnership has righteousness with lawlessness? Or what fellowship has light with darkness?" (ESV). The image comes from the farm law of Israel: "You shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together" (Deuteronomy 22:10, ESV). The rare Greek verb Paul uses is related to the word the Greek Old Testament uses in Leviticus 19:19 for letting cattle breed with a different kind. The point of the Deuteronomy law is not that the donkey is a bad animal. The two animals differ in height, stride and strength, so that yoked together they drag against one another, the weaker one is chafed raw, and the furrow runs crooked. The image carries no contempt for the unbeliever. It's realism about the plow.

Read in its place, though, the passage is broader than marriage. Paul is in the middle of a long and wounded defense of his ministry. He asks the Corinthians to widen their hearts to him (6:13; 7:2), and between those two appeals comes this sharp passage built on the language of the temple: "What agreement has the temple of God with idols? For we are the temple of the living God" (2 Corinthians 6:16, ESV). The same church had already asked whether it could eat in pagan temples (1 Corinthians 8 to 10), and the most natural reading is that Paul is warning against any binding partnership that draws the church back into the worship of idols, any alliance that makes the temple of God share a roof with another god. The passage closes, "let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of body and spirit, bringing holiness to completion in the fear of God" (2 Corinthians 7:1, ESV). So it isn't right to say, as some do, that the passage has nothing to do with marriage. It concerns every yoke, and marriage is the heaviest yoke most people ever wear.

For the unmarried Christian the direct word is elsewhere, and it is short. Writing about a widow, Paul says she "is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:39, ESV). The freedom is wide. The limit is one phrase. Around the year 200, Tertullian wrote two small books to his wife, *Ad Uxorem*, and in the second he took up exactly the argument our steelman makes. Some Christians were citing Paul's instruction that believers already married to pagans should stay, and reading it as permission to marry pagans. Tertullian answered that Paul was speaking to people whom grace had found already married, not granting leave to contract new marriages. Then he described what such a marriage would cost in practice: a Christian wife whose pagan husband arranges the household so that she can't keep a fast, can't slip out at night for the church's gatherings, can't go through the streets to visit the poor or take in a traveling believer, and must hide even her prayers. He closed with the other kind of marriage, two believers who pray together, fast together, teach and encourage one another, and stand as equals in the church of God, and he said that Christ rejoices to see it and sends it his peace.

That is the real answer to the steelman, and it has nothing to do with labels. Peter Berger and Hansfried Kellner argued in a well-known essay, "Marriage and the Construction of Reality" (1964), that marriage is the place where two people build a shared world through years of ordinary conversation. Over breakfast and in the car they settle, mostly without noticing, what is real, what matters, what a good day looks like and what a death means. The deepest things in a marriage are held up by that unending exchange. When one spouse's world has God at its center and the other's does not, the marriage can still be loving and faithful. But there's a room in the house where the conversation can't go, and it's the room the believer lives in most.

Naomi Schaefer Riley's *'Til Faith Do Us Part* (2013), a study of interfaith marriage in America built on a national survey she commissioned, argues that many couples marry at the point in life when religion matters least to them and seldom talk the difference through beforehand. The questions come back, often hard, when the children arrive. That's the plow making itself felt. There's one more reason for the counsel, and the steelman rarely considers it: it protects him. A woman who marries a good man while quietly planning his conversion has made him a project, and he will feel it for decades. "Only in the Lord" is a kindness to the unbeliever as much as a discipline for the believer.

So the argument of this essay, for the one still deciding, comes to this. Paul's "only in the Lord" is the boundary Scripture sets around the choice, and it is wisdom before it is a rule. Whatever you decide, decide with your eyes open. Don't assume the difference will resolve itself, or that love will be enough to bridge it, or that he'll eventually come around. The reason for Paul's limit isn't that he's less good than you, or that God keeps a scorecard on your romance. Such a marriage asks him to share a life whose center he doesn't share, and asks you to keep the center of your life in a room he can't enter. And be honest in the other direction as well. "In the Lord" doesn't mean in the building. Marrying a churchgoer who holds the nice, distant God of Smith and Denton's teenagers isn't obedience to 1 Corinthians 7:39 either.

## The hard background in Ezra and Nehemiah

Anyone who reads the whole Bible on this question eventually reaches two of its hardest chapters. Ezra came to Jerusalem, by the traditional dating in 458 BC, to a small and fragile community of returned exiles. The officials told him that the people had married among the surrounding nations, "so that the holy race has mixed itself with the peoples of the lands" (Ezra 9:2, ESV). Ezra tore his garments, pulled hair from his head and beard, and prayed one of the great confessions of Scripture, owning the sin as his own and his people's. Then a man named Shecaniah proposed, "let us make a covenant with our God to put away all these wives and their children" (Ezra 10:3, ESV). The assembly gathered in the open square in the ninth month, "trembling because of this matter and because of the heavy rain" (10:9, ESV), and a list of the men was drawn up. The book ends on a sentence that won't let the reader look away: "All these had married foreign women, and some of the women had even borne children" (10:44, ESV). Some years later Nehemiah found men of Judah married to women of Ashdod, Ammon and Moab, and heard their children in the street: "half of their children spoke the language of Ashdod, and they could not speak the language of Judah" (Nehemiah 13:24, ESV). He writes that he "confronted them and cursed them and beat some of them and pulled out their hair" (13:25, ESV), and he reminded them that foreign wives had led even Solomon into sin.

These chapters have to be read with care, and two things have to be said together. The first is that the concern was worship, not blood. The law behind them reads, "You shall not intermarry with them, giving your daughters to their sons or taking their daughters for your sons, for they would turn away your sons from following me, to serve other gods" (Deuteronomy 7:3–4, ESV). The same Scriptures honor Rahab of Jericho, and Ruth the Moabite, who bound herself to Israel's God and became the great-grandmother of David. The danger was a small remnant, just back from an exile brought on by idolatry, dissolving into the gods of its neighbors within a generation. The second thing is that the cost of the remedy fell hardest on women and children who hadn't chosen the arrangement and had nowhere to go. Faithful interpreters differ on whether the narrator holds up Ezra's covenant as a model or records a painful emergency measure, and the text doesn't let us hurry to either verdict. What it doesn't allow is using these chapters to tell a Christian to leave a spouse.

Paul makes sure of that, and the way he does it is one of the quiet wonders of the New Testament. A prophet of the same restoration era, Haggai, put a question of ritual law to the priests. If holy meat touches ordinary food, does the food become holy? No. If someone made unclean by a corpse touches the food, does it become unclean? Yes (Haggai 2:11–13). Under the old arrangement, uncleanness spread by contact and holiness didn't. The Corinthians seem to have feared exactly this about their mixed marriages, that union with an unbeliever made the believer unclean, the way Paul had warned that union with a prostitute would (1 Corinthians 6:15–17). Paul reverses the direction of the contagion: "For the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife, and the unbelieving wife is made holy because of her husband. Otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is, they are holy" (1 Corinthians 7:14, ESV). He doesn't mean the spouse is saved; two verses later he says the believer can't know that. He means the marriage and the household are set apart for God by the presence of the believer, so that the believer isn't defiled by staying and the children aren't outside the covenant's reach. In Christ, holiness is the stronger contagion. That's why the church never took up Ezra's remedy, and why no one should take it up now.

## If you are already married to someone who doesn't believe

So the word to the married is different, and Paul gives it with unusual care: "To the rest I say (I, not the Lord) that if any brother has a wife who is an unbeliever, and she consents to live with him, he should not divorce her. If any woman has a husband who is an unbeliever, and he consents to live with her, she should not divorce him" (1 Corinthians 7:12–13, ESV). "I, not the Lord" doesn't make the counsel optional. It means Jesus never addressed a situation that only arose once the gospel crossed into pagan households, and his apostle now does. The believer stays. Your marriage isn't second-class, and it isn't a sin you're living in. It's a covenant God honors and sets apart.

Then comes the other side: "But if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved. God has called you to peace" (7:15, ESV). Whether "not enslaved" frees a deserted believer to remarry is a second-order question on which faithful churches have divided for centuries. Catholic canon law's "Pauline privilege," and on the Protestant side the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), which names wilful desertion that cannot be remedied as a ground for divorce, both read the verse as releasing the believer from the marriage itself, so that remarriage becomes possible. Others read it more narrowly, as release from any duty to chase the departing spouse, but not as permission to marry again. Each reading has serious defenders, and this essay won't pretend to settle it. Verse 16 carries a similar debate: "For how do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband? Or how do you know, husband, whether you will save your wife?" (ESV). Some hear hope in it: stay, because you don't know that he won't come to faith. Others hear release: don't cling to a marriage he has walked out of on the chance of saving him, because you can't know you will. Both readings agree on one thing. The believer isn't the spouse's savior, and doesn't know how the story ends.

Most of these marriages didn't begin mixed. Two people married inside the same belief or the same unbelief, and then one of them changed. If you're the one who came to faith after the wedding, remember that your spouse never agreed to this. The person they married went to bed one kind of person and, as it seemed to them, woke up another, with new friends, a new calendar and a new vocabulary about sin. Their resistance isn't necessarily hostility to God. It may be grief for you. If you're the one whose spouse has lost faith, the grief runs the other way and is just as real: the prayers you used to pray together, the future you pictured in a pew with grandchildren, the conversation about death you can no longer have in the same terms. Stanley Hauerwas made a much-repeated point in a 1978 *Christian Century* essay: we never really know whom we marry, and even if we did, time and marriage itself would change them, so the task is learning to love the stranger we find ourselves married to. Conversion and deconversion are the sharpest cases of that rule, and the vow was made for them.

Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) describes our time as one in which belief has become one option among others, and in which conviction is "fragilized," in his term, by the steady presence of neighbors who see the world differently and live good lives. Most Christians feel that at work or online. The believer in a mixed marriage feels it at the breakfast table, in the person they love most. Don't be ashamed that your faith feels fragile there. It is fragile there, and that's simply what it means to live the secular age inside one household. Winning every argument won't steady it, and neither will going silent. What steadies it are the practices that hold faith up when the conversation at home can't: worship with the church each week, prayer, Scripture, friendships with believers who know your situation. Many believers slowly surrender their church life to keep peace at home, and the surrender feels like love. Over years it becomes a slow starvation.

Much of the rest is ordinary wisdom. Talk about the children before they force the question, and don't recruit them against the other parent. Take your spouse's world seriously: read what they read, ask why it matters to them, let them be a whole person rather than a spiritual deficit. And the church has a word to hear here too. We've often treated these believers as half-members. Every time we ask "Where's your husband?" in the lobby, we tell them their marriage is a failure of their faith. It isn't. It's a holy household with an empty chair, and the church should seat them as full members of the family.

## Won without a word

Peter wrote to churches in exactly this situation, and his counsel has been used both as a gift and as a weapon. "Likewise, wives, be subject to your own husbands, so that even if some do not obey the word, they may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives, when they see your respectful and pure conduct" (1 Peter 3:1–2, ESV). To hear it rightly you have to know what was expected of a wife in a Greco-Roman household. Plutarch, in his *Advice to the Bride and Groom*, written around AD 100, told the new wife to worship only the gods her husband honored and to shut the door on strange rites and foreign superstitions. A Christian wife married to a pagan had already broken that expectation simply by keeping her faith. David Balch argued in *Let Wives Be Submissive* (1981) that 1 Peter's household instructions answer the Roman suspicion that foreign cults corrupted respectable homes. Peter isn't telling her to surrender her faith to keep the peace, which is what her culture demanded. He's telling her to keep it, and to let the household see that it has made her better rather than worse.

"Without a word" doesn't mean the believer may never speak of Christ; the same letter tells Christians to be ready "to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you" (1 Peter 3:15, ESV). It means no campaign: no pointed prayers at dinner, no marriage turned into an evangelistic project with a deadline. The unbelieving spouse, and this applies just as surely to a believing husband with an unbelieving wife, is to be met by a life, and a life takes years to read. Peter also knew these homes could be frightening. He ends the passage by telling the women that they are Sarah's children "if you do good and do not fear anything that is frightening" (3:6, ESV). He wasn't blind to the fact that some of them lived with intimidation.

The church's most famous example comes from Augustine. In Book 9 of the *Confessions*, written in the late 390s, he describes his mother, Monica, and his father, Patricius, a pagan citizen of the North African town of Thagaste. Augustine says his father was unusually kind but quick to anger, and unfaithful to his wife. Monica bore it without quarreling, tried to win him to God by her character, in language that echoes Peter's, and waited for the mercy that would make him faithful once he believed. Near the end of his life Patricius did come to faith. It's a moving story, and Christians have told it for sixteen centuries.

Honesty requires telling the rest of it. Augustine also records, with an admiration we can't share, that the wives of Thagaste came to Monica with bruised faces from husbands gentler than hers, and that she counseled them toward silence and deference. The church has told Monica's story, and preached 1 Peter 3, to keep women in homes where they were being hurt. We did that, and some of us did it from pulpits. Peter's instruction to be unafraid in a frightening house was never an instruction to stay in a dangerous one.

## When faith becomes a weapon

So let it be said without qualification. Nothing in 1 Corinthians 7 or 1 Peter 3 requires anyone to endure abuse. Paul's word to the believer whose spouse departs is "God has called you to peace" (1 Corinthians 7:15, ESV), and a home where one person is afraid of the other isn't a home of peace.

Faith differences become instruments of control in both directions. An unbelieving spouse may forbid worship, mock prayer in front of the children, cut the believer off from Christian friends, police their giving, or threaten to take the children if the faith continues. A believing spouse may do the mirror image: wield Scripture to demand compliance, threaten a spouse with hell, frame every disagreement as rebellion against God, withhold affection until the other attends church, or enlist the children in a campaign of pressure. The theology differs and the pattern is the same. When belief is used to isolate, frighten, demean or control, the problem is no longer a difference of conviction. It is abuse, and the church should call it abuse no matter which spouse holds the orthodox creed.

If that describes your home, tell someone safe, and don't wait for a pastor's permission. In the United States the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at 1-800-799-7233, and if you're in immediate danger, call 911. A lawyer, not this essay, is the one to advise you on custody and property; nothing here is legal advice. The church's first task is to protect you. The marriage can be talked about afterward.

## The one who loved first

For the one still deciding, Paul's limit is hard, and it deserves to be felt as hard. Saying no to a good person you love would be no small thing, and the church shouldn't pretend that a verse makes it painless or that God owes you a better match for obeying. What "only in the Lord" says is that Christ isn't a private preference kept in a side room. He's the center, and a marriage is built around a center. If honest discernment leads you to a no, it may cost you more than most of us in the church have ever given up for this faith, and the limit is still worth taking seriously.

For the one already married, the counsel is harder in a different way, because it has no deadline and no guaranteed ending. You don't know whether you will save your spouse. Paul says so outright. What you do know is that your spouse isn't an enemy in your house, or a project, or a punishment. They are a person set apart by the covenant you share, and loved by God before you ever prayed for them. "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). Every Christian was once on the far side of that sentence. I was. God didn't wait for us to believe before he loved us, and he didn't win us by pressure. He won us by giving himself.

That is the shape of the marriage Paul and Peter describe: a believer who loves first, keeps faith without contempt, and leaves the ending to God. It may end the way Monica's did. It may not. You may pray for thirty years beside someone who never kneels, and meet the resurrection with the question still open. What you carry out of this isn't a method for converting your spouse. It's a vow that was always meant for a stranger, kept in a house Paul calls holy, beside a person for whom Christ died.
