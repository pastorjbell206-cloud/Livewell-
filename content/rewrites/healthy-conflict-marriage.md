Most couples don't come apart over one catastrophic fight. They come apart over the same fight, held a hundred times. There is the one about money that is really about trust, the one about his mother that is really about loyalty, the one about the dishes that is really about whether anyone in the house has noticed how tired she is. By year ten the fight has a script. One pushes harder and the other goes quiet, or both go quiet and call it peace, and underneath the quiet a ledger keeps growing that neither of them will read aloud.

Christians have usually been offered one of two cures. The older one, still alive in plenty of churches, holds that good Christian couples don't really fight, and that raised voices in the kitchen are a sign something has gone wrong with your walk with God. The newer one arrived with the counseling shelf at the Christian bookstore. It says conflict is normal and the answer is to fight fair: no name-calling, no "always" and "never," speak in "I" statements, stay on one issue, take a break before it boils over. The first cure produced a great deal of pretending. The second has done real good, and I want to honor it before I say what it cannot do.

This essay turns on a single distinction. Conflict management and reconciliation are different things. Management asks how two people can disagree without doing each other damage. Reconciliation asks how two people who have actually wronged each other can be made one again. The rules for fighting fair belong to the first, and they are worth learning. But a marriage is a covenant, and a covenant isn't kept by a ceasefire. What heals it is the older work the church has always called confession and forgiveness, and that work begins in a place no rulebook was written to reach.

## Where quarrels come from

The New Testament's most direct word on the subject comes as a question. "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you? You desire and do not have, so you murder. You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel" (James 4:1–2). James was writing to churches, not to couples, and his word "murder" is meant to startle. He has just spent a chapter on the tongue, which he calls "a restless evil, full of deadly poison" (James 3:8), and he knows that most of the killing people do to one another is done with sentences. What matters for a marriage is where he locates the war. It is "within you" before it is between you. The dishes are the occasion of the fight. The cause is a desire that has hardened into a demand and found a person standing in its way.

In the middle of an argument the cause always seems to be sitting across the table. James isn't saying the other person has done nothing wrong. He is saying that what turns a disagreement into a quarrel is something I bring into the room, a want I have decided I cannot live without: respect, or rest, or control of the calendar, or simply being right. Often the thing I want is good, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

Augustine saw this more clearly than anyone in the ancient church. In *The City of God* (written between 413 and 426) he defines virtue, briefly, as rightly ordered love, and he describes sin less as loving bad things than as loving good things in the wrong order. A husband's desire to be respected is good. A wife's desire to be seen is good. The trouble begins when a good thing is loved as though it were God, when I need it so badly that I will wound the person who withholds it. Augustine's famous account of the two cities rests on this: one city built by love of self pressed all the way to contempt of God, the other by love of God pressed all the way to contempt of self. Every marital fight is a small skirmish on that border.

Martin Luther, lecturing on Romans in 1515 and 1516, took Augustine's diagnosis and gave it an image that has lasted five centuries. The sinful heart, he said, is *incurvatus in se*, curved in on itself, so bent that it uses even God, and certainly everyone else, for its own ends. Watch two people in a bad fight and you can almost see the posture. Each body curls around its grievance. Every married person, and I don't exclude myself, knows the particular pleasure of the unanswerable point, and knows the hollowness an hour later, when it becomes clear what the point was for.

So the first thing to say about healthy conflict is that it has to begin with a question aimed inward. Before "what did you do," there is "what do I want so badly that I am willing to hurt you for it." The rules for fighting fair don't ask that question. James asks it first.

## What anger is for

None of this makes anger the enemy. Where the church has taught as if it were, couples have swallowed every grievance in the name of peace until it came out sideways, years later, as coldness or contempt. Scripture is more honest than that, and more demanding.

The key text is Ephesians 4:26–27: "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil." From 4:17 onward Paul is describing what it looks like to put off the old self and put on the new, and he does it with a series of concrete trades. Falsehood gives way to truth, "for we are members one of another" (Ephesians 4:25). Corrupting talk gives way to speech "good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear" (Ephesians 4:29). Anger sits inside that list, which means Paul treats it as a matter of the body's shared life. If that is true of a congregation whose members belong to one another, it is doubly true of a marriage, which Paul will call "one flesh" a chapter later (Ephesians 5:31).

"Be angry and do not sin" is not Paul's coinage. He is quoting Psalm 4:4 as it appeared in the Greek Bible of his day, and the psalm in our Hebrew-based English goes on in a way that matters: "Be angry, and do not sin; ponder in your own hearts on your beds, and be silent" (Psalm 4:4). The command assumes anger will come and that it can be right. Anger is, at its best, a moral perception. It notices that something has been violated. A spouse who is never angry at anything may not be holy; he may simply have stopped expecting anything from the marriage. But the same breath commands restraint, and the psalm sends the angry person to bed, not to the kitchen table at one in the morning, to search his own heart in silence.

That matters, because Ephesians 4:26 has become the rule "never go to bed angry," and some couples have turned it into an obligation to argue until one of them collapses. The Greek word Paul uses for anger in the sunset clause is not the same as the verb just before it. It names anger that has settled into a condition, the provocation that has become a mood and then a standing grievance. Paul's concern is not that every disagreement be resolved before dark. It is that anger not be allowed to take up residence. The next clause explains why. "Give no opportunity to the devil" uses the ordinary word for a place, a piece of ground. Anger held overnight is anger that has been handed territory. The letter to the Hebrews names what grows there: a "root of bitterness" that "springs up and causes trouble, and by it many become defiled" (Hebrews 12:15).

James supplies the order in which anger is to be handled. "Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:19–20). The sequence is the instruction. Hearing comes before speaking, and speaking comes before anger, which is exactly the reverse of the order most of us follow once the temperature rises. And James's verdict on anger is a sober one. Even when my anger has noticed something real, my anger alone will not make it right.

One more line keeps the passage honest. Five verses after "be angry," Paul writes, "Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice" (Ephesians 4:31). The same apostle who permits anger tells us to put it away. He is distinguishing anger as a flash of moral sight from anger as a way of life, and he is telling us that the second must go. Then he gives the measure that replaces it: "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you" (Ephesians 4:32). The standard isn't fairness. It is the way we were forgiven, which was never fair.

## How the church learned to fight fair

For most of Christian history, conflict between spouses was spoken of in the vocabulary of sin, patience and reconciliation. When the English church wrote its marriage service in the Book of Common Prayer of 1549, it had couples promise to hold each other "for better for worse, for richer for poorer, in sickness and in health." The vow assumed disappointment. It asked two people to decide in advance that the other's failures would not dissolve the bond, and it placed the remedy for conflict inside the promise and inside the church's ordinary life of confession and absolution.

The twentieth century changed the vocabulary. Philip Rieff, in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966), argued that the West was trading religious man, formed toward salvation, for psychological man, whose aim is his own well-being. Conflict moved with the culture. It stopped being primarily a moral event, in which someone might have sinned and need to confess, and became a communication problem, in which two people needed better skills. At the end of the 1960s George Bach and Peter Wyden published *The Intimate Enemy*, whose subtitle promised to teach couples *How to Fight Fair in Love and Marriage*. In 1970 Thomas Gordon's *Parent Effectiveness Training* popularized the "I-message." These tools soon found their way into church premarital classes.

The law was moving in the same direction and in the same years. California's Family Law Act of 1969 replaced fault grounds for divorce with "irreconcilable differences," and state after state followed during the 1970s. Whatever one thinks of no-fault divorce as policy, notice the word the law chose. Conflict became something a court could simply declare beyond reconciling. Robert Bellah and his colleagues, in *Habits of the Heart* (1985), found the resulting outlook in the way ordinary Americans talked about love: commitment understood as lasting as long as it continued to meet the needs of the selves inside it. The fair-fighting manual and the no-fault statute are different things, but they share a grammar. Both treat conflict as something to be managed or exited, and neither has much to say about who must repent.

Now the case for the rules, as their best defenders would make it, because it is a strong case. Most marriages in trouble are not full of villains. They are full of habits, many learned in childhood homes where no one ever saw a disagreement end well. Habits can be changed, and a changed habit changes the person over time. James K. A. Smith argues in *You Are What You Love* (2016) that we are formed less by what we think than by what we repeatedly do, and a couple who practices not saying "always" is slowly practicing truthfulness. The rules, looked at closely, are moral. Refusing "always" and "never" is refusing to exaggerate. Staying on one issue is a small act of forbearance. Taking a break before you boil over is James's "slow to anger" given a clock. Speaking of your own hurt instead of your spouse's motives is an honest attempt to obey Jesus's warning against judging (Matthew 7:1).

The research backs much of this. John Gottman, whose long study of married couples is summarized for general readers in *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work* (1999, with Nan Silver), found that couples who stay together do not necessarily fight less than couples who divorce. They fight differently. He named four patterns that predict the end of a marriage: criticism, which attacks the person rather than the behavior; contempt, which communicates disgust and superiority; defensiveness, which refuses to receive any complaint; and stonewalling, which shuts down altogether. Of the four, he found contempt the most dangerous, the single strongest predictor of divorce in his studies. He described "flooding," the state in which a body under stress is so aroused that it can no longer take in what is said, and he found that the couples who last are the ones whose small attempts at repair, a joke or a softened tone or a hand on the arm, are made and received in the middle of the fight.

A pastor who waves this away because it comes from a laboratory rather than a pulpit is being foolish. Its defenders can add a point that stings. The church's alternative has too often been to tell struggling couples to pray about it, and they have prayed, sincerely, and gone home and done the same damage in the same words. You can't teach repentance on a worksheet. You can teach a man to stop rolling his eyes. That is not nothing, and I think most of what the defenders of fighting fair say is right.

It is worth noticing, though, how close Gottman's central finding sits to something Jesus said. "Everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment; whoever insults his brother will be liable to the council; and whoever says, 'You fool!' will be liable to the hell of fire" (Matthew 5:22). Jesus traced murder back to contempt nearly two thousand years before a researcher found contempt at the root of divorce. The research got the symptom right. The question is whether its tools can reach the cause.

## What the rules cannot reach

The first limit is that the rules govern the form of a fight and not its object. They can make desire speak politely. They cannot change what it wants. A husband can make his demands in flawless "I" statements and remain, under the grammar, a man who has decided his wife exists to supply his comfort. James's question about where quarrels come from is still waiting for an answer after every rule has been kept.

The second limit is that the rules can themselves become weapons. Couples who learn them often turn them on each other: you said "always," you raised your voice, we agreed to take breaks and so I am leaving this conversation now. The old contest was never about the dishes. It was about who is right and who gets to be the reasonable one, and fair-fighting rules give that contest a more respectable vocabulary. C. S. Lewis observed in *Mere Christianity* (1952) that pride is essentially competitive; it takes no pleasure in having something, only in having more of it than someone else. Give pride a rulebook and it will compete at composure. The calmer spouse, itemizing the other's failures in measured tones, can be the more contemptuous of the two while feeling like the only adult in the room. Paul anticipated this: "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1). Love, he goes on, "does not insist on its own way; it is not irritable or resentful" (1 Corinthians 13:5), and no rule can make a person stop insisting.

The third limit comes from Gottman's own work. He found that most of the recurring conflicts in the marriages he studied were never solved. They were perpetual, rooted in lasting differences of temperament and need, and the couples who did well were the ones who learned to live with them in good humor rather than the ones who resolved them. He also found that marriages go better when each spouse, and husbands in particular, is willing to accept influence, to be moved by what the other says. Both findings point past technique. Nothing in a communication workshop can make you willing to live forty years beside a difference you would change if you could, or willing to be changed by a person you are angry with. Those are not skills. The older words for them are forbearance, "bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other; as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive" (Colossians 3:13), and humility.

The fourth limit is the deepest, and it brings us back to the distinction this essay rests on. Management aims at a ceasefire. Reconciliation aims at communion restored, and communion cannot be restored between two people while a wrong that one of them did remains unnamed. Miroslav Volf, writing out of the wreckage of the Balkan wars in *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996), distinguishes the will to embrace, which a Christian owes even an enemy and which does not wait for the other to change, from the embrace itself, which cannot happen without truth being told about what was done. Fair-fighting rules are built for symmetry. Each person speaks, each listens, each owns a share. Much of the time that symmetry is right. But sometimes one spouse has done most of the wrong, and a frame that insists on equal shares will quietly require the injured party to confess to things she didn't do so that the other can feel the scales are level. Reconciliation is often asymmetrical. It requires the person who did the wrong to say so, without dividing the blame first.

A ceasefire is not peace. It is the absence of shooting, and the two can look the same from outside for a very long time.

## Confession between husband and wife

Jesus's teaching on conflict is ordered in a way that most of us reverse. "Why do you see the speck that is in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye? ... You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye" (Matthew 7:3, 5). He doesn't forbid removing specks. He sets the order of the operation, and the first cut is on myself. He also sets the priority. "So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:23–24). Reconciliation ranks ahead of worship. If that is the order at the altar, it is the order at the kitchen table. And when Jesus describes going to someone who has sinned against us, he names the goal plainly: "If he listens to you, you have gained your brother" (Matthew 18:15).

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote *Life Together* (1939) after the Gestapo shut down the Confessing Church seminary he led at Finkenwalde in 1937, and its chapter on confession is the best thing I know on why this is so hard. He argues that a pious fellowship often permits no one to be a sinner, so everyone hides his sin from everyone else, and the result is people who live side by side in loneliness. Sin, he says, wants to be alone; it withdraws from others, and confession to another person is the breakthrough into real community. Confessing only to God can quietly become a way of forgiving ourselves, because nobody else hears it and nothing is risked. To say it aloud to a brother makes it real. The one Bonhoeffer had in view was a fellow believer, but no one stands closer to us than a spouse, and no human relationship offers more practice at hiding. "Confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16) is a command a marriage can obey every week.

A real confession has a recognizable shape. It names the specific thing. It carries no "if" and no "but." It doesn't demand to be forgiven on a schedule, and it can bear the other person's anger without rushing to manage it back to calm. The counterfeit is familiar: "I'm sorry you feel that way," "I'm sorry if that hurt," "I'm sorry, but you have to understand." Each is a defense wearing an apology's clothes, and the other spouse can feel the clothing even when she can't name it. Paul draws the line between the two: "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Worldly grief is sorry about the consequences, or about the damage to my picture of myself. Godly grief turns toward the one I wronged and toward God.

Why is it so hard to say "I was wrong" to the person who knows me best? Timothy Keller argues in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011, with Kathy Keller) that marriage exposes the self-centeredness each spouse brought into it, and that only the gospel, which tells us we are more sinful than we dared admit and more loved than we dared hope, makes that exposure survivable. I think he is right, and the reason reaches back to Luther. Most of us argue as though our standing depended on the verdict, as though the argument were where we get justified. A person who needs to be right because being right is his righteousness can't afford to lose. A person who has been justified by grace can lose an argument and still be whole. That is the freedom confession requires, and no technique can supply it.

Forgiveness is the other half, and it is not the same as trust. Forgiveness cancels a debt; it is the decision to stop making the other person pay, through coldness or punishment or the grievance kept on a shelf within reach. It can be given by one person alone, the way Christ forgave from the cross people who were not asking. Trust is rebuilt, by two people, over time, as changed behavior forms a new pattern.

And here a line has to be drawn without qualification. No teaching on conflict, forbearance or forgiveness asks anyone to endure abuse. If your spouse hits you, threatens you, controls your money or your movements to punish you, cuts you off from the people who love you, or leaves you afraid in your own home, you are not in a communication problem that better skills will fix, and you are not failing to forgive when you seek safety. Forgiveness never requires you to remain in danger, and reconciliation is never wise where the danger is real. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233. Call it. The church has too often sent people back into harm in the name of peace, and it must not do so again.

## The fight beneath the fight

Stanley Hauerwas once proposed, half in jest and entirely in earnest, that we always marry the wrong person (*Christian Century*, 1978). He did not mean that everyone chose badly. He meant that no one knows at the altar whom they are marrying, because neither person is finished, and the one you wake beside in year fifteen is someone the one at the altar never met. Conflict is often the moment the stranger appears. Often the fight is the first sign that your spouse has become someone new, or that you have, and the question underneath it is whether you will love the person who is actually there.

Wendell Berry, in his essay "Poetry and Marriage: The Use of Old Forms" in *Standing by Words* (1983), compares the form of marriage to the form of a poem. The form works, he argues, precisely by obstructing us. It refuses the easy way out, and the moment we no longer know what to do may be the moment our real work begins. Healthy conflict looks something like that. The fight is not proof that the marriage has failed. It may be the place where the marriage is actually being made.

What makes that bearable is the one conflict in the whole story that was not managed. Paul describes it in the same letter that tells us to be angry and not sin: "For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" (Ephesians 2:14). God didn't negotiate a ceasefire with us. He didn't wait until the blame had been fairly divided. "For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life" (Romans 5:10). He went first, while we were still in the wrong, and the cost fell on him. Then Paul hands the same work to us: "All this is from God, who through Christ reconciled us to himself and gave us the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18). Most Christians first practice that ministry, or first refuse it, in their own homes.

Every child in every house is taking a lesson from the kitchen, learning what a husband does when he is angry at the wife he loves, and what she does when she is angry at him. They are not learning it from the rules we recite. They are learning it from whether anyone comes back.

The next fight is already on its way. It will probably be about something small that is really about something else. You can get through it by the rules, and you should keep the rules. But the question it will put to you is older than any rule: whether you are willing to lose the argument in order to gain your spouse, and whether you will go first, before you have been vindicated, while you still feel you are owed. That is what the One who is our peace did. It is the only kind of peace a covenant was ever meant to hold.
