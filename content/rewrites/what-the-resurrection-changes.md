## A doctrine we affirm, or a fact we live inside

Almost every Christian in America would sign a statement that Jesus rose bodily from the dead, and some of us can argue for it with real skill. The harder thing is to find where the claim touches the rest of our lives. We treat the body as private property, the courts of the powerful as the final judges of who mattered, grief as something faith should hurry past, and our ordinary work as something the years will erase. None of those habits would change much if Easter turned out to be a legend. I say that as a pastor who has preached the resurrection for years, in seasons when it held my full weight and in seasons when I only kept affirming it. The words were the same in both seasons. What happened to me when the week went badly was not.

That gap is the subject of this essay. Whether the resurrection happened is weighed in [Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen), and what the renewed creation will be like in [What Is Heaven Actually Like?](/writing/what-is-heaven-actually-like). The question here is easier to dodge than either. If Jesus was raised, what does that change now, for a person with a body, a grief, a job and a conscience?

The distinction the whole essay turns on is between the resurrection as a doctrine to affirm and the resurrection as a reality that reorders everything it touches. A doctrine to affirm sits in the mind the way a signed form sits in a drawer: true, filed, consulted when challenged. A reality that reorders works like discovering that the ground under your house has moved. You don't file that. You check every wall. The first Christians clearly lived with the second kind of knowledge. Much of the church in our time, the part I belong to included, mostly lives with the first.

Some of that drift is understandable; a claim repeated for twenty centuries loses its shock. But when the resurrection becomes a happy epilogue, a bonus scene in which Jesus comes back to prove the cross worked, the cross becomes the main event and Easter becomes the reassurance. The apostles told it the other way around. For them the resurrection was the hinge of history, God's promised future arriving early, inside the ordinary world, in one body, on one Sunday morning.

Two twentieth-century theologians saw this sharply. In *The Resurrection of the Dead* (1924), drawn from lectures on 1 Corinthians he gave at Göttingen the year before, Karl Barth argued that the fifteenth chapter isn't an appendix to a letter about church quarrels but the key to the whole of it. On his reading, the divisions, the lawsuits, the sexual confusion and the chaos at the Lord's Table grow from the same root, a failure to reckon with the resurrection. Forty years later Jürgen Moltmann opened *Theology of Hope* (1964; English 1967) by arguing that Christianity doesn't keep its hope in a closing chapter on the last things. It is hope from beginning to end, and the future God promised in raising Jesus is the key in which every other doctrine is set. The resurrection isn't one belief among others. It's the light by which the others are read.

## How Easter became something we defend

The earliest Christians kept the resurrection in a calendar before they kept it in a creed. From the start the church gathered on the first day of the week, the day the tomb was found empty; Luke records believers at Troas meeting "on the first day of the week, when we were gathered together to break bread" (Acts 20:7). Around 110, on his way to execution in Rome, Ignatius of Antioch wrote to the church at Magnesia that those who had walked in the old ways had come to a new hope, no longer keeping the Sabbath but living by the Lord's day, the day on which our life rose through Christ. The Letter of Barnabas, from roughly the same period, called it the eighth day, the first day of a new creation. About 112 the governor Pliny told the emperor Trajan that the Christians in his province met on a fixed day before dawn to sing to Christ as to a god. And around 155 Justin Martyr explained that Christians assemble on the day of the sun because God began the world on that day and Jesus rose from the dead on it.

Every week, before any argument was made, the church's clock was reset by an empty tomb. Sunday was an ordinary workday until the fourth century, so slaves and laborers gathered before sunrise and then went to work, having reminded one another that the world's real week had already turned over. The resurrection wasn't only an idea they held. It was a time they lived in.

The arc from there is long, and not all of it is decline. In 321 Constantine made Sunday a legal day of rest, and in 325 the Council of Nicaea moved to have the whole church keep Easter on the same Sunday. The day of resurrection became the rhythm of a civilization, and that was a real gift. But a truth a culture honors by law can quietly stop being one anyone has to stake a life on. Through the centuries of Christendom the resurrection stayed in every creed and liturgy. What faded in much ordinary piety was the sense that it was news.

The modern period made it a battlefield. In 1777 Gotthold Lessing, who had been publishing Hermann Samuel Reimarus's attack on the Easter accounts, argued that accidental truths of history can never prove necessary truths of reason, and that between them lay what he called an ugly, broad ditch. From then on, Western Christians tended to meet the resurrection in one of two postures, and both deserve a fair hearing.

The first posture treated it as a proof. Believers saw rightly that everything was at stake and built careful historical defenses, a tradition that runs through popular works like Josh McDowell's *Evidence That Demands a Verdict* (1972) and into serious scholarship today. I'm grateful for that work. But a fact you spend your energy defending can become a fact you rarely inhabit. The resurrection became the evidence for Christianity rather than its substance.

The second posture treated it as a symbol, and its greatest advocate was pastorally serious. In the 1941 lecture published as "New Testament and Mythology," Rudolf Bultmann argued that modern people can't honestly inhabit the ancient picture of the world and that the church mustn't require them to. His best-known line, in paraphrase, is that no one can use electric light and the radio and rely on modern medicine while still believing in the New Testament's world of spirits and miracles. What matters, he said, is not a corpse leaving a grave, which history can't settle, but the saving meaning of the cross met in preaching and received in faith; Easter names the rise of that faith in the disciples. His defenders would say he was protecting the gospel from being held hostage to a historian's verdict. That isn't a cynical project, and many pastors live closer to it than they'd say aloud.

Paul had already closed that door. To Corinthians who believed in Jesus and doubted bodily resurrection he wrote, "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). Remove the event and you haven't kept the gospel in a safer form. You've traded it for something smaller.

The two postures share a grammar. Both made the resurrection a question for the mind, one to defend and one to interpret, and neither, in its ordinary church form, asked much of the body, the calendar, the grave or the courtroom. The conservative congregation could affirm the empty tomb with total confidence and live as if Monday were unchanged. The progressive congregation could celebrate new life every spring and mean nothing that would survive a funeral. Most of us know which room we sit in, and it's rarely the one we criticize. Moltmann's book and Wolfhart Pannenberg's *Jesus: God and Man*, both from 1964, were in part a protest against Bultmann's settlement, a call to treat Easter again as an event that opens the world's future and so lays claim to the believer's present.

## Reading 1 Corinthians 15 as one argument

The chapter is usually quoted in pieces, a verse at a funeral and another on Easter morning. Read straight through, it's an argument, and the shape is the point. Paul begins with the gospel he received and handed on, the death, burial, raising and appearances of Christ (15:1–11). Then he turns to his real problem. Some in Corinth were saying there is no resurrection of the dead (15:12), most likely because, like many educated Greeks, they found a raised body crude and preferred a salvation that left the flesh behind. Paul runs their premise forward to show them where it ends.

If there is no resurrection of the dead, Christ wasn't raised; and if he wasn't, the apostles are false witnesses, the Corinthians are still in their sins, and the believers who have died are simply gone. Then comes the sentence that ought to make every comfortable Christian sit up: "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:19). Paul doesn't call a faith without resurrection a fine moral philosophy with less consolation. He calls it pitiable. The middle position, a Christianity that keeps the ethics and the uplift while quietly letting the raised body go, is the one he names as the saddest of all.

Then the turn: "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). Paul is borrowing from Israel's calendar. Leviticus commanded that the first sheaf of the harvest be waved before the Lord on the day after the Sabbath (Leviticus 23:10–11), which is the day of the week the women found the tomb empty. Jesus isn't the exception to death. He is the first of a harvest. And the harvest has an enemy: "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26). Notice the word. The New Testament never calls death a friend or a natural passage. It calls death an enemy, and the resurrection announces that the enemy's defeat has begun.

The next paragraph, often skipped, may be the most honest in the chapter. Paul asks why he risks his life every hour if none of this is true, and answers with the slogan of his age: "If the dead are not raised, 'Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die'" (1 Corinthians 15:32). He's conceding that if death is final, the pleasure-seekers have a point. The strongest modern version of that conclusion is nobler than the slogan. In "A Free Man's Worship" (1903), Bertrand Russell argued that all the work of the ages, every devotion and every flash of genius, is destined for extinction when the solar system dies, and that the only safe foundation for a human life is to face that fact in a despair that refuses to flinch. He counseled courage, not indulgence: a refusal to bow before blind and indifferent power, and a resolve to cherish the good and show compassion to fellow sufferers anyway. I came to faith from atheism, and I don't think that view is foolish. It has a grave dignity. But its cost is plain, and Russell saw it: the kindness no one saw, the faithfulness that came to nothing, the work that washed away, all of it is finally lost. The good is real, and it's written in sand.

Paul says the opposite, and he says it about Monday. After the mysteries of the raised body (15:35–49) and the great taunt at the end, "O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?" (1 Corinthians 15:55), the chapter doesn't end with a description of heaven. It ends with a word about work: "Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58). The *therefore* is the whole argument. The Bible's longest treatment of the resurrection lands on the next morning's work.

N. T. Wright presses this in *Surprised by Hope* (2008). His argument, in paraphrase, is that what we do in the present, in the Lord and by the Spirit, isn't wasted. We don't build God's kingdom; only God raises the dead. But we build for it. The prayer, the mended fence, the long nights in a hospital waiting room and the honest work for a boss who never noticed are taken up into what God is making, in ways we can't yet picture. In a world headed for resurrection, nothing done in the Lord finally evaporates.

## God's verdict on the cross, and on the courts of the world

The first public preaching of the resurrection sounds less like a funeral sermon than a closing argument. At Pentecost, in the city where Jesus was executed, Peter told the crowd that they had put Jesus to death by the hands of lawless men, and then said, "God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it" (Acts 2:24). He ended with a verdict: "Let all the house of Israel therefore know for certain that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified" (Acts 2:36). Not long afterward, at the temple, he put the same reversal into a single line: "and you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead" (Acts 3:15). The grammar is careful and it never varies. You killed him. God raised him.

That grammar tells us what the resurrection first meant. It was the Father's judgment on a case the world thought it had closed. Jesus had been condemned by the council as a blasphemer and by Rome as a rebel, and crucified, the death Rome kept for slaves and insurgents and Deuteronomy tied to God's curse. The resurrection overturned every one of those verdicts. Paul opens his letter to the Romans by saying that Jesus "was declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 1:4). The verb Paul uses means to appoint or mark out, to designate publicly, and in this sentence the designation is made by raising a condemned man. Easter is a ruling.

Karl Barth gave this its fullest form in *Church Dogmatics* IV/1 (1953), where the resurrection is the verdict of the Father on the obedience of the Son: on the cross the judge was judged in our place, and on the third day God pronounced that this judgment had done what it claimed. Without that verdict the cross is an assertion; with it, an accomplished act. Wolfhart Pannenberg, in *Jesus: God and Man* (1964; English 1968), added a historian's angle. Before Easter Jesus claimed an authority that made sense only if God stood behind it, forgiving sins in his own name and setting his word beside the law of Moses. The resurrection, Pannenberg argued, was God's confirmation of that claim and the end of history arriving ahead of time, the resurrection Israel hoped for at the close of the age happening to one man in the middle of it.

This is also a word about justice. If God overturned the sentence passed on this executed man, then no court on earth sits at the top of the appeals. The court that sentenced him was a real court with real reasons, and it was wrong, and God said so in public. Moltmann saw what this means for the crushed. The resurrection of the crucified, he argued, is God's contradiction of the way things are; it doesn't make believers content with the present but restless in it, because they have seen the future and the present doesn't match. The slave who sang to Christ before dawn, the widow the courts ignored and the child whose death the empire filed as ordinary have all heard that the last word on their lives has not yet been spoken, and that the one who will speak it was himself a victim of the courts.

But the Acts sermons refuse to let any reader stand safely outside the verdict. Peter is saying *you* to the people in front of him, the ones who shouted and the ones who stayed quiet. When they were cut to the heart and asked what to do, he didn't hand them a campaign against the guilty. He told them to repent and be baptized for the forgiveness of their sins (Acts 2:38). The resurrection is a verdict against injustice, and every one of us has been on the wrong side of some courtroom, so it is also a verdict against us. And the same Sunday that condemns the executioners offers them mercy. That's the only kind of justice that doesn't simply trade one set of victors for another.

Christians on both sides of our politics have found ways to blunt this. One habit makes the resurrection wholly private and future, a promise that my soul will be safe someday, which leaves the world's courts untouched and asks nothing of how I vote, hire, lend or treat a stranger. The other habit makes the resurrection a symbol of social progress, a spring festival for causes, which asks a great deal of the world and has nothing to say at the grave of a child. The first keeps the hope and drops the justice. The second keeps the justice and drops the hope. Both are smaller than an empty tomb, and most of us drift between them depending on the week.

## What your body is for, and the life that starts before you die

At a great many Christian funerals someone says, kindly, that the body in the casket is just a shell, that the person we loved is gone and only the container remains. The part of it that says the dead in Christ are with the Lord is true. But the rest of the sentence is closer to Plato than to Paul. The Christian hope was never that we'd finally be free of our bodies. It's that our bodies will be raised. The tomb in Jerusalem was empty because the body that had been laid in it was the body that walked out, changed beyond the reach of death.

Paul drew the ethical consequences in the same letter. Some in Corinth reasoned that since the body would perish anyway, what they did with it didn't touch the soul, so visiting a prostitute mattered no more than a meal. Paul answers, "The body is not meant for sexual immorality, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (1 Corinthians 6:13), and grounds it in Easter: "And God raised the Lord and will also raise us up by his power" (1 Corinthians 6:14). The argument is about ownership and destiny more than rules. The body is joined to Christ, indwelt by the Spirit and headed for resurrection, and so the chapter ends, "for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20). The Corinthian error, and ours, is to think that what we do with our bodies matters less than what we believe in our heads. Easter says the body is where belief becomes real.

Oliver O'Donovan built an entire ethics on this in *Resurrection and Moral Order* (1986). His argument, in paraphrase, is that the resurrection of Jesus is God's vindication of the world he made. God didn't abandon the created order when it fell into sin and death; in raising Jesus he reaffirmed it and set it on the way to its fulfillment. Christian morality, then, is neither arbitrary command nor cultural preference. It's the shape of creation itself, restored in Christ. The body, with its limits and its given meanings, is part of that order. It isn't raw material for the will to reshape at pleasure, and it isn't a prison for the soul to despise.

That cuts in more than one direction. It stands against a sexual culture, inside the church as well as outside it, that treats the body as a private instrument whose uses mean whatever we decide. It stands as firmly against the old Christian habit of ranking bodily suffering below spiritual things. If God intends to raise bodies, then the body of the addict, the disabled child, the refugee, the prisoner and the elderly woman in memory care is not a shell. Feeding a hungry body and sitting with a dying one are aimed straight at the future God has promised.

Resurrection life has also already begun in anyone joined to Christ. Paul explains baptism as a burial and a rising: "We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). Then he draws the practical conclusion: "So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus" (Romans 6:11). The patterns you're sure are simply who you are, the resentments that have hardened into personality, the compulsions you've quit fighting, aren't the final truth about you. They belong to a life that has, in the only sense that finally matters, already ended.

The same power is at work now. "If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you" (Romans 8:11). That's why Paul could write from prison that he wanted to know Christ "and the power of his resurrection, and may share his sufferings, becoming like him in his death" (Philippians 3:10). Resurrection power didn't lift Paul above suffering. It met him inside it, as the strength to stay faithful where faithfulness cost something, while he kept pressing on, as the following verses say, toward a resurrection he hadn't yet attained (Philippians 3:11–14).

## Grieving as people who have hope

The resurrection changes grief, but not in the way we are sometimes told. A church that has turned Easter into a doctrine to affirm will often use it to hurry mourners along, as if faith required a smile at the graveside and tears were a small failure of trust. Paul, writing to a young church in Thessalonica that had buried some of its members, said, "But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). He didn't tell them not to grieve. He told them to grieve differently. The difference rests on the next verse: because Jesus died and rose, God will bring the dead in Christ with him (1 Thessalonians 4:14).

Jesus himself set the pattern. Standing at the tomb of his friend Lazarus, knowing what he was about to do, "Jesus wept" (John 11:35). The order matters. The weeping was real, and then the tomb opened. Paul described the apostolic life with the same doubleness, "as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing" (2 Corinthians 6:10). Grief is what love does when it meets death. Hope is what love does when it hears that death has been beaten. Christians are allowed, and even required, to do both at once.

A second distinction keeps faith from curdling into cheerfulness. Optimism expects things to turn out well; it trusts the trend lines and is refuted by evidence, which is why it rarely survives the intensive care unit. Hope expects God to raise the dead, and it isn't defeated when the trend lines fail. It can look straight at the diagnosis, the grave, the marriage that ended and the years that came to nothing, and refuse to give any of them the last word, not because the pain is unreal but because it isn't final. Peter calls it "a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1 Peter 1:3). The hope is alive because the one it rests on is.

If you're reading this in a season when grief has begun to slide toward despair, when the thought of not going on has started to feel like relief, please don't carry that alone. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour, and a real person will answer. Tell a pastor, a friend or a doctor today. Hope of the kind Paul describes is almost never held by one person on their own. It is held by a church, and on the worst nights it is held for us by others until we can hold it again.

That's why the early church kept Easter in a calendar and not only in a creed. Wright complains, fairly, that churches spend forty days keeping Lent and then give Easter a single day of celebration, and he wants Easter week, at the very least, kept as an eight-day festival. For the first Christians every Sunday was a small Easter, the eighth day. The resurrection can't be sustained as a reality on an annual feeling. It has to be practiced week after week, in bread and wine, in baptism, in the gathered church singing to Christ, and in the stubborn habit of good work no one will see. Wendell Berry closed his poem "Manifesto: The Mad Farmer Liberation Front" (1973) with two words quoted so often they risk sounding like a slogan: practice resurrection. For the church it means living each ordinary day as people who know how the story ends.

None of this settles the question for a reader who doubts Easter happened. If the tomb stayed full, Paul was right about the stakes, Christians are to be pitied, and you can walk away with a clear conscience. But if it was empty, the resurrection can't be one belief among others. It's a verdict on the cross, on death, on every court that has condemned the innocent, and on each of us who has stood in one. It tells us what our bodies are for, what our work will come to, and how to stand at a grave.

Most of us will say the creed again this Sunday. We'll say we believe in the resurrection of the body and the life everlasting, and we'll mean it the way we mean most true things we've said for a long time. The question the empty tomb puts to us isn't whether we affirm it. We do. The question is whether anything in our week would look different if we didn't: the way we grieve, the way we spend our bodies, the way we treat the people the courts have sentenced, the faithfulness we keep when no one is counting. Paul wanted to know the power of Christ's resurrection, and he wanted it more than he wanted to be safe. I've had seasons when I would have settled for the doctrine. The tomb is still empty, and it's still asking for more than that.
