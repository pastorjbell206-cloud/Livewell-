Somewhere in the second year, many grieving people start apologizing. They apologize for crying at a birthday, for skipping the church picnic because it was his favorite, for still saying her name in the present tense. Somebody told them, or they absorbed from the air, that the first year is the hard one. When the second weighs what the first did, and sometimes more, they conclude that something has gone wrong inside them, that they grieved incorrectly or didn't trust God enough. The grief that doesn't go away becomes, on top of everything else, a verdict against the one who carries it.

This essay is written against that verdict. It isn't written against getting better, and it doesn't romanticize a sorrow that has stopped a life in its tracks, because that sorrow is real and deserves real help. It is written against a particular expectation: that grief is a set of stages ending in a clean room called acceptance, where the one you lost no longer hurts to remember. That expectation is younger than most people assume, and its history explains why so many faithful people feel like failures for loving someone who died.

## Where the stages came from

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross was a Swiss-born psychiatrist working in Chicago in the 1960s, and the book that made her famous, *On Death and Dying* (1969), was about the dying. It grew out of a seminar at the University of Chicago in which she interviewed patients with terminal illnesses, often in front of students, and asked them the question hospitals of that era had trained everyone to avoid: what is it like to know you are going to die? Out of those conversations she described five reactions that recurred among her patients as they faced their own deaths: denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance.

The book deserves more gratitude than its critics usually give it. Mid-century American medicine had become very good at keeping dying people out of sight and very bad at speaking with them. Kübler-Ross sat at the bedside and listened, and her book gave real momentum to the hospice and palliative care movements that have since spared millions a lonely, over-treated death. It was a work of mercy. (The site's longer treatment of dying itself is in [How to Die Well](/writing/how-to-die-well).)

What happened next is the problem. The stages traveled out of the hospital into the funeral home, the counseling office and the pew, and on the way they changed subjects. A description of how some terminally ill patients faced their own deaths became a map of how the bereaved ought to mourn someone else's. What had been offered as recurring reactions hardened in the popular mind into a sequence, then a schedule, then a test. If you were still angry, you were behind. If the sadness came back after some peace, you had slipped a stage.

Kübler-Ross herself resisted that reading. In *On Grief and Grieving* (2005), written with David Kessler and published after her death, the authors applied the stages to bereavement explicitly and insisted they were never meant as a tidy progression or a timetable. By then the popular version had a life of its own. When Paul Maciejewski and colleagues published an analysis from the Yale Bereavement Study in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* in 2007, following people whose loved ones had died of natural causes, they found that the negative states did tend to peak in roughly the predicted order, but that acceptance was the most commonly reported state from the earliest months onward, and that the dominant painful feeling was neither denial nor depression. It was yearning. Most of the bereaved accepted the fact of the death early, and went on missing the person anyway.

That finding matters. It means the thing that stays isn't a failure to accept. A widow who has signed the papers, cleared the closet and gone back to work still aches for her husband on an ordinary Tuesday in the third year. Nothing in her is stuck. She has accepted the death. What she hasn't done, and what no stage can ask of her, is stop loving the man.

## The older idea underneath: grief as work to be finished

The stages would not have spread so fast if the ground hadn't been prepared. Push the story back half a century. In "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917), Sigmund Freud described mourning as labor: the mind tests reality, finds that the loved person no longer exists, and slowly withdraws its attachment from every memory and hope bound up with that person. When the withdrawal is complete, Freud argued, the ego is free again to love someone new. Healthy grief, on this account, ends in detachment.

In 1942 the Cocoanut Grove nightclub fire in Boston killed nearly five hundred people, and the psychiatrist Erich Lindemann studied the bereaved. His paper "Symptomatology and Management of Acute Grief" (1944) popularized among clinicians the phrase that still shapes how we talk: grief work. Lindemann described the task as emancipation from bondage to the deceased, readjustment, and new relationships, and he was optimistic that an uncomplicated grief could be brought to resolution within weeks. He was a humane physician responding to a catastrophe, and much of what he observed was accurate. But the frame was Freud's. Grief was a job, the job had an end, and the end was a severed tie.

Meanwhile the customs that had once carried mourners through the long middle of grief fell away. The English anthropologist Geoffrey Gorer, in *Death, Grief, and Mourning in Contemporary Britain* (1965), documented how quickly the old public forms had vanished in his lifetime: the black clothes, the drawn blinds, the recognized year of mourning. The Victorians had overdone it; Queen Victoria wore black for the forty years between Prince Albert's death in 1861 and her own. But the collapse after the First World War left the bereaved with no script at all. Gorer found grief pushed indoors, treated as an embarrassment to be managed privately and finished promptly, and he argued that this left people lonelier than the old rituals had.

Put the pieces together and you have the modern expectation. Freud supplied the theory that healthy grief ends in letting go, Lindemann the language of work, a culture without mourning customs the pressure to be done, and the stages the checklist. The marketplace supplied the vocabulary we still reach for at gravesides: getting over it, moving on, closure. We close a deal. We move on to the next account. The language of finished transactions has been laid over the one human experience it fits least.

There is a quiet irony in the history. Freud's daughter Sophie died in the influenza pandemic in 1920. Nine years later, writing to his friend Ludwig Binswanger after the death of Binswanger's son, Freud admitted that the acute sorrow does subside, but that one remains inconsolable and never finds a substitute, and that this is how we keep alive a love we do not wish to give up. The theorist of detachment, grieving his own child, didn't detach. He described what his theory had called a failure as the form love takes.

## What the research found instead

By the 1990s researchers had begun to say out loud what bereaved people had long known. The landmark was a collection edited by Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman, *Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief* (1996). Its argument was simple and, for the field, disruptive. Across cultures and across most of history, the dead have not been let go. They have been remembered, spoken to, honored, woven into the identity of those who survive them. Bereaved parents keep a place for the child in the family story; children who lose a parent keep a sense of that parent's presence. The contributors found these bonds normal, often healthy, and frequently a source of strength rather than a symptom of being stuck. The demand that the bond be severed turned out to be a peculiarity of the twentieth-century West, a clinical theory mistaken for a law of nature.

The distinction here changes everything for a grieving person. Resolution means the relationship ends. Integration means it changes form and is carried. Freud and Lindemann assumed healthy grief ends in resolution; Klass and his colleagues described bereaved people moving toward integration instead, and found nothing pathological in it. The love finds a new address in the life of the one who remains.

George Bonanno's work complicates the picture from the other side, and it deserves an honest hearing because it cuts against any romance of endless sorrow. In *The Other Side of Sadness* (2009), Bonanno summarized years of studies following bereaved people over time and argued that the most common response to loss is resilience. Most people, even after the death of a spouse, continue to function, to work, to love the living and to laugh. Grief, he found, doesn't move in a straight line. It comes in waves, sadness alternating with moments of relief and even joy, and that oscillation seems to be part of how human beings bear it. He also argued that the absence of overwhelming distress is not usually hidden denial waiting to explode. Some who feel guilty for not grieving hard enough need to hear that as much as others need to hear that their grief is allowed to last.

Held together, these findings say something a Christian should find familiar. Most people bear loss and go on living, and many who go on living still carry the one they lost. Resilience and continuing love aren't opposites. The widower who has rebuilt his days and laughs with his grandchildren may still weep every year on the anniversary. He isn't failing to recover. He has recovered in the only sense that matters, and the love has come along with him.

## When lasting grief becomes something more

Honesty requires the harder distinction between grief that stays and grief that stops a life. The first is love carried. The second is love that has become a room with no door, and the people inside it deserve more than a reassuring essay.

In 2022 the American Psychiatric Association added prolonged grief disorder to the text revision of its diagnostic manual, the DSM-5-TR, after the World Health Organization had included a similar condition in its own classification a few years earlier. The decision was debated. Critics worried about turning ordinary sorrow into a disease that could be used to hurry mourners along, and those worries echo everything above. But the clinicians who pressed for it were responding to something real: a minority of bereaved people who, a year or more after the death (the manual's threshold for adults), remain in an intense, daily yearning for the one who died that doesn't soften and crowds out almost everything else. They can't find their footing in the world again. They may feel that part of themselves died too, or feel numb and cut off from people who love them. The grief exceeds what their own culture and faith would expect, and it keeps them from living.

I have to be careful here, because the purpose of naming this is not to hand anyone a diagnosis. Only someone trained, who has met you and heard your story, can say whether what you are carrying is prolonged grief or the ordinary long ache of love, and nothing in this essay is medical advice. A better question than "How long has it been?" is "Can I still live alongside this?" If you can work, eat, sleep most nights, and receive love and give some back while the loss hurts, you are likely carrying grief the way people always have. If the loss has swallowed the rest of your life and months have gone by without change, please tell your doctor and ask for a counselor trained specifically in grief. Therapies designed for prolonged grief exist and help many people. Seeking that help is not a lapse of faith. It is the same stewardship that sends a Christian to a surgeon.

And if the grief has turned into thoughts of ending your own life, or of joining the one you lost, please don't wait and don't carry that alone. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour; if you are in immediate danger, call 911. Tell a pastor, a friend, a family member tonight. The longing to be with the one you lost grows out of love, and there is no shame in saying out loud that you feel it. But the despair that says your life is finished is not telling you the truth, and it deserves to be answered by people who can sit with you, not by you alone in the dark.

The church has failed people on both sides of this line. We have told the ordinary long griever she needed more faith, and told the person drowning in grief she only needed to pray harder, when she needed a physician as well as the prayers. Both errors come from the same refusal to look closely at the person in front of us.

## The Christian suspicion of tears

It would be convenient to blame all of this on Freud. The truth is that the church has its own long habit of treating grief as a faith problem, older than psychiatry. It goes back to the philosophers the early church admired, the Stoics above all, who taught that excessive mourning is a failure of reason. Christians who believed in the resurrection could easily conclude that grief, for them, was not merely unreasonable but faithless.

Augustine felt that pull, and he left the record of how it broke. In Book IX of the *Confessions*, he tells of his mother Monica's death at Ostia in 387, days after the two of them had stood at a window and spoken together of eternal life. When she died, he forced his tears back, thinking it unfitting to mourn with weeping a woman who had died in faith, as if she were simply gone. When his son Adeodatus cried out, the others quieted the boy. Augustine went through the funeral dry-eyed while inwardly torn apart. He even went to the baths, having heard that bathing drives sorrow from the mind, and came out as sorrowful as he went in. Then he slept, woke, remembered a hymn of Ambrose, and finally let himself weep before God, for his mother and for himself. If anyone thinks it a sin, he writes, that he wept a small part of an hour for the mother who had wept for him for many years, let that reader not scorn him but weep for his sins instead.

That scene shows a great Christian mind learning in real time that faith in the resurrection doesn't require the suppression of grief. His instinct told him tears were a kind of unbelief. His experience told him otherwise, and he let the church see it.

Sixteen centuries later C. S. Lewis made the same discovery less gracefully, and more usefully for that. After his wife Joy Davidman died of cancer in 1960, he filled notebooks that became *A Grief Observed* (1961), published at first under a pseudonym. Its first sentence has stayed with readers ever since: "No one ever told me that grief felt so like fear." The book is shocking for a Christian classic. Lewis rages. He wonders whether God is not absent but cruel, and describes going to God in his need and finding what felt like a door slammed and bolted from the inside. He doesn't clean any of it up. Near the end he offers the image grieving people most often recognize: losing Joy was less like an illness that passes than like an amputation. The stump heals and a man learns to get about again, but he will never walk as he once did. Lewis also came to see bereavement not as the interruption of love but as one of its ordinary phases, following marriage as surely as marriage follows courtship. He did not stop grieving. He stopped treating his grief as proof that his faith had failed.

Nicholas Wolterstorff, a philosopher at Calvin College, lost his twenty-five-year-old son Eric in a climbing accident in Austria in 1983. *Lament for a Son* (1987), a short book of fragments, is among the most honest Christian books on loss of the last century. Wolterstorff refuses the comforters' explanations. He won't say the death was good or that it made sense, and he admits he doesn't know why God didn't prevent it. He writes that he will look at the world through tears from now on, and that perhaps he will see through them what dry eyes cannot. And he arrives where Christian faith and grief meet most deeply: the God of Scripture, as he comes to see him, does not stand outside our suffering explaining it but has entered it and suffers too. Wolterstorff did not expect the wound to close. He expected to carry it and be changed by carrying it.

Augustine, Lewis and Wolterstorff agree on something the Stoic in us resists. In each case faith was precisely what gave them permission to grieve honestly before God, instead of hiding their tears as though God would be embarrassed by them.

## Jesus at the tomb, and what Paul actually wrote

The shortest verse in the English Bible is the one the grieving most need. In John 11, Lazarus, a friend Jesus loved, has been in the tomb four days when Jesus reaches Bethany. Martha meets him on the road, and Jesus tells her, "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live" (John 11:25). He knows what he is about to do. Then Mary comes and falls at his feet weeping, and the mourners with her are weeping, and John records this: "When Jesus saw her weeping, and the Jews who had come with her also weeping, he was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled" (John 11:33). A moment later comes the verse itself: "Jesus wept" (John 11:35).

Sit with the order of events. The one who is the resurrection, minutes away from undoing this death, stands at the grave and weeps. He doesn't treat the coming joy as a cancellation of the present sorrow, and he doesn't rebuke Mary for crying when she should be trusting. The verb John uses for "deeply moved" carries a note of sternness elsewhere in the New Testament, and many interpreters hear in it indignation, anger at death itself, the enemy Paul named: "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26). Others read it as overwhelming distress, and the Greek will bear either reading. If a certain resurrection minutes away did not stop the Son of God from weeping, no Christian should be told that a resurrection promised at the end of the age requires him to stop.

The verse most often used against the grieving is 1 Thessalonians 4:13, and it is nearly always misquoted. What Paul wrote is this: "But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). Read the context. The young church in Thessalonica had lost members, and the survivors seem to have feared that those who died before Christ's return had missed it. Paul writes to correct the fear, not to forbid the tears. He grounds the answer in the resurrection: "For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so, through Jesus, God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep" (1 Thessalonians 4:14). And he closes the passage not with a command to stop mourning but with an instruction to the community: "Therefore encourage one another with these words" (1 Thessalonians 4:18).

Paul never tells the Thessalonians not to grieve. He tells them not to grieve "as others do who have no hope." The difference isn't the amount of grief. It is its horizon. Much of the pagan world around them buried its dead with a flat finality; one Latin epitaph formula common enough to be abbreviated on Roman tombstones ran, in effect, "I was not, I was, I am not, I care not." Christian grief is not smaller than that grief. It faces a different direction. The tears are the same salt. What changes is what the mourner is looking toward while he sheds them.

Paul knew this from the inside. Writing to the Philippians about his friend Epaphroditus, who had nearly died, he says God had mercy "not only on him but on me also, lest I should have sorrow upon sorrow" (Philippians 2:27). The apostle who wrote the great chapter on the resurrection did not imagine that his hope would make a friend's death painless. He expected sorrow, and he called its absence mercy.

The rest of Scripture keeps the long griefs in plain view. Jacob, believing Joseph dead, "refused to be comforted" (Genesis 37:35), and the narrator doesn't scold him. And there is Psalm 88, the darkest prayer in the Psalter, which begins in faith, "O LORD, God of my salvation, I cry out day and night before you" (Psalm 88:1), and never climbs out. It ends without a turn to praise: "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18). In Hebrew the last word of the psalm is darkness.

That psalm is in the Bible on purpose. Walter Brueggemann argued in "The Costly Loss of Lament" (1986) that a church which drops lament loses the prayer in which the suffering may speak honestly to God, and so quietly teaches the hurting that their pain has no place in the covenant. Psalm 88 tells the person in the tenth year of grief that she hasn't wandered outside the faith. She has wandered into the part of it the brochures leave out. (The site's essay on that forgotten prayer, [Is It Okay to Be Angry at God?](/writing/lament-the-prayer-the-church-forgot), takes up lament at length.)

## What the church owes the long grief

The skeptic has a fair question here. Freud argued in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927) that religion is a consolation human beings invent to bear what they cannot bear, and the reader who doesn't believe may suspect that everything above is an elaborate painkiller. Some of that suspicion has been earned. Christians have often waved the empty tomb at a fresh grave as though it should end the crying, and the grieving have felt the difference between being comforted and being shushed. But notice what the Christian story actually offers at the grave. It doesn't offer a God who explains the death, or a doctrine that makes it painless. It offers a God who weeps at the tomb, a psalm that ends in darkness, an apostle who calls a friend's survival mercy, and a promise that death is an enemy to be destroyed, not a friend to be accepted. As painkillers go, that is a strange one. It keeps the pain and denies the pain the last word.

The church's failure with the grieving is seldom a failure of doctrine. It is a failure of stamina. We are good at the first week: the casseroles, the cards, the full funeral. We in the church, pastors not least, are often too quick to hurry a funeral from the grave to Easter, as if the congregation needed reassurance before it had been allowed to mourn. Then the calendar moves on, and by the second Christmas the grieving person often faces the hardest day of the year alone, carrying both the loss and the discovery that the world has decided it should be over.

What the long grief needs from a church is mostly simple, and it costs almost nothing except memory. It needs someone to say the name of the dead out loud, on purpose, years later, because the grieving are starving to hear it and everyone around them fears it would only stir things up. The name was already stirred. It never stopped. It needs a note in March, when the meals stopped in January, that says only: I thought of him today. It needs the empty chair at Thanksgiving acknowledged rather than tiptoed around. Paul's instruction in Romans holds the whole ministry in one line: "Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15). The verse has no expiration date.

It also needs a church that sees the griefs no one brings casseroles for: the miscarriage never announced, the parent who was hard to love and is now hard to mourn, the loss that carries guilt as well as sorrow. Those griefs often last longest because they were never allowed to begin. (The site speaks to one of them in [Grief and Guilt After Abortion](/writing/grief-abortion-church).)

And the church already possesses, in its ancient creed, the thing the researchers rediscovered in 1996. Every time Christians say the Apostles' Creed, they confess "the communion of saints." The dead in Christ are not deleted from the family. They belong to the same body we do, and the bond with them is not a coping device but a theological fact. Christians have divided, sometimes sharply, over how far that communion reaches and whether it is right to pray for the dead; those are real questions between faithful churches, and this essay won't settle them. But every tradition that confesses the creed agrees on the floor beneath the disagreement: love for the faithful dead is not something to be severed and finished. It is held in Christ, and it waits.

That leaves one more scene, and it is the one I can't get past. When the risen Jesus appeared to his disciples, he still had his wounds. Thomas, who had refused to believe, was told, "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe" (John 20:27). The resurrection did not erase the marks of the cross. It kept them, and made them the way the disciples recognized him. The first body ever raised into the life of the world to come carried its scars into that life.

That is not a promise that your grief will lift by next spring. It may not. It is something stranger and harder to hold: the promise that what death did to you is not the last word about you, and that the one who will finally wipe away every tear has wounds of his own. Until then the weight stays. You will carry it into rooms where no one knows it is there, on Tuesdays no one else remembers, for as long as you love the one you lost. Christ doesn't ask you to set it down. He asks you to carry it toward his empty grave, and he carries it with you, and he has not told either of you how far it is.
