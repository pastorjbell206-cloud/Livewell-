Sooner or later most Christian parents go looking for a list. What should a four-year-old know about God? When is a child ready to pray on her own, to read the Bible by himself, to be baptized, to come to the Lord's Table, to be trusted with a real doubt? There's no shortage of answers: charts of spiritual milestones by age, laid out like the pediatrician's growth curve, with boxes for memory verses and a line for the date a child made a decision. Everything else about a modern childhood comes with a benchmark. It would be strange if faith didn't.

Some of that instinct is right. A four-year-old and a fourteen-year-old are different creatures, each season of childhood can carry some things and not others, and the church has always marked certain passages with rites that matter. I have five sons, and I've missed plenty of the windows the charts describe.

But a milestone and a rhythm are different kinds of thing, and the difference decides more than we think. A milestone is a point on a line that a child is supposed to reach. It is measured on the child, and once it's passed it stays behind him. A rhythm is a pattern the whole household returns to: a meal, a day, a week, a year. It isn't measured on anyone. It forms everyone inside it, the parent included, and it never falls behind you, because it comes around again. Scripture gives parents remarkably few milestones and a great many rhythms. It gave Israel a seventh day and a calendar of feasts, and it wrote the child's question into the rite. Faith passes between generations less through the checkpoints a child clears than through the time a household keeps, and many of us, the church's own families included, have handed our time to someone else and gone shopping for a checklist to make up the difference.

## The Calendar That Is Already Forming Your Children

Ask an American family what season it is and they'll likely answer in the school's vocabulary. The real year of most households runs from back-to-school in August through the fall sports season, a holiday break measured largely by retail, the testing weeks of spring, and the long release of summer. The week has its own shape: practices on weeknights, games and tournaments on weekends, and increasingly on Sunday mornings. The day is bookended by the phone, checked before anyone speaks and the last light in the room at night. Nobody sat down and adopted this calendar as a creed. It arrived one reasonable yes at a time. It teaches anyway.

James K. A. Smith gave the church a way to see this in *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009). His claim is that human beings are lovers before they are thinkers, and that our desires are trained by repeated bodily practices. He read the shopping mall as a liturgy, a place with its own pilgrimage, icons and picture of the good life, forming shoppers without ever making an argument. A family calendar is a liturgy in exactly that sense. It tells a child, week after week and without a word, what matters most, what the family will sacrifice for, and where a person's worth is found.

Read that way, the ordinary American calendar says some specific things. The travel team teaches that a child's future is built on weekends and the family goes wherever it is being developed. The school year teaches that time exists for advancement, that each year is a grade to be passed and left. The phone teaches that no moment is quite enough, since there is always another thing waiting behind the glass, and its feed is designed with no natural place to stop. It has no seventh day. None of these goods is evil. Sports teach discipline and the dignity of the body, school is a genuine gift, and a screen lets a grandmother see a grandson's face from four states away. The problem isn't that these things are present. It's that they have become the frame into which everything else must fit, including God, who is now scheduled in the gaps the frame allows.

We did not decide this, and the church has often helped it along. We moved youth events so they wouldn't compete with the season and taught ourselves to feel grateful that families came when they could. I'm inside that indictment with every pastor I know. Christian Smith and Amy Adamczyk, in *Handing Down the Faith* (2021), concluded from national surveys and interviews that parents are by far the most important influence on the religious lives of their children, more than congregations or youth ministers. If that's so, the household's time is not the setting of a child's formation. It is the curriculum.

This is where the milestone chart shows its limits. It doesn't challenge the calendar that already rules the house. It fits neatly inside it, one more benchmark beside the reading level and the batting average. A checklist can be completed in the margins of a life formed somewhere else. A rhythm can't, because a rhythm is the life.

## How Faith Came to Be Measured in Milestones

For most of Christian history, the church handed on the faith by keeping time. The Didache, a short manual of Christian practice from the late first or early second century, told believers to fast on Wednesdays and Fridays and to pray the Lord's Prayer three times a day. Around 155, Justin Martyr described to the emperor what Christians did on "the day called Sunday": they gathered from town and country, read the apostles and prophets, prayed, shared bread and wine, and gave for orphans and widows, meeting on the first day because God made the world on it and Jesus Christ rose from the dead on it. In 321 Constantine made Sunday a day of rest in Roman law, though he left farmers free to work it, and over the following centuries the church year grew up around the Easter feast: Lent before it, Pentecost after it, Advent and Christmas and Epiphany at the other pole. Benedict of Nursia, writing his Rule around 530, ordered the monk's whole day by the hours of common prayer. A medieval child, kept by parish bells and saints' days, learned the faith less from a lesson than from the year itself.

That arrangement had real failures. A faith absorbed from the calendar could be held without being understood, and the Reformers were right to be alarmed at how little many baptized people knew. But the modern turn away from time came in three other movements, and each left something in the chart on the refrigerator.

The first was revival. From the Great Awakening of the 1730s through Charles Finney's "new measures" a century later, American Protestants increasingly pictured conversion as a datable crisis, a before and an after that a person could name. Real grace moved through those meetings. But the pattern taught families to treat a child's faith as an event waiting to happen rather than a life being grown. Horace Bushnell, a Congregationalist pastor in Hartford, protested in *Discourses on Christian Nurture* (1847). He argued that a child raised in a Christian household should grow up a Christian and never know himself as being otherwise, that the ordinary life of the home was itself a channel of grace. His critics thought he had given up conversion. He thought the revival had given up the household.

The second movement was the classroom. Robert Raikes opened his Sunday schools in Gloucester in 1780 to teach reading and Scripture to poor working children, a real mercy. In America, as public schools sorted pupils into grades by age over the nineteenth century, the churches followed with graded lessons. Formation moved from the table to the classroom and from the household's shared time to an age-sorted program. The third movement gave those grades a science. Twentieth-century developmental psychology, from Jean Piaget to Erik Erikson and James Fowler, described childhood as a sequence of stages with tasks to be accomplished in order.

The revival gave us the dated event, the school the grade, and the psychologist the stage. Put them together and you have the milestone chart. Each saw something true. But together they trained us, my own Baptist people not least, to picture faith as something that happens to an individual child at identifiable points rather than something a people keeps together in time. We kept the benchmarks and lost the calendar.

## Sabbath, Feast and the Question Built Into the Rite

Israel's formation of its children begins with a commandment addressed to a household:

> Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days you shall labor, and do all your work, but the seventh day is a Sabbath to the LORD your God. On it you shall not do any work, you, or your son, or your daughter, your male servant, or your female servant, or your livestock, or the sojourner who is within your gates. For in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them, and rested on the seventh day. Therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and made it holy. (Exodus 20:8–11)

The son and the daughter are named in the commandment itself, alongside the servant, the animal and the foreigner. The Sabbath is something a whole house keeps, and a child keeps it long before he can explain it. Exodus grounds the day in creation; when Moses repeats the commandment in Deuteronomy, he grounds it in deliverance: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. Therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15). Slaves don't get a day off; the seventh day was weekly proof that Israel was no longer owned.

Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Sabbath* (1951), pointed out that the first thing Scripture calls holy is not a mountain or a shrine but a day: "So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy" (Genesis 2:3). He argued that Judaism is above all a religion of time, aimed at the sanctification of time, and he called the Sabbath "a palace in time." Walter Brueggemann, in *Sabbath as Resistance* (2014), read the commandment against its backdrop in Pharaoh's brickyards, where the quotas only ever rose (Exodus 5). The Sabbath, he argued, is the practice of a people who refuse the anxious logic of endless production because they are no longer defined by what they make. A child whose family sets the ball and the homework down one day a week and receives the day as a gift learns in his body what the tournament season will spend years trying to unteach him: that he is not what he produces.

The feasts extend the pattern across the year. Deuteronomy 16 gathers Israel's year into three pilgrim festivals, Passover, Weeks and Booths, each of them a household event. "You shall rejoice in your feast, you and your son and your daughter, your male servant and your female servant, the Levite, the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow who are within your towns" (Deuteronomy 16:14). Passover is kept "that all the days of your life you may remember the day when you came out of the land of Egypt" (16:3), and Booths is kept so "that you will be altogether joyful" (16:15). I was raised without a father, and I read that guest list slowly. The feast formed a child's sense of who belongs at the table. Year after year an Israelite boy watched the orphan, the widow and the foreigner eat beside him, and learned what his God was like from the seating chart.

Then there is the question. At the first Passover, before Israel has left Egypt, Moses anticipates a child at the meal: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?' you shall say, 'It is the sacrifice of the LORD's Passover, for he passed over the houses of the people of Israel in Egypt, when he struck the Egyptians but spared our houses'" (Exodus 12:26–27). A chapter later he does it again: "And when in time to come your son asks you, 'What does this mean?' you shall say to him, 'By a strong hand the LORD brought us out of Egypt, from the house of slavery'" (Exodus 13:14). The rite was built to be strange. Bread without yeast for a week, a lamb eaten in haste, bitter herbs: a child could not sit through it without wondering. And the answer is given in the first person. "You shall tell your son on that day, 'It is because of what the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt'" (13:8). The father who never saw Pharaoh says *for me*. The Mishnah, compiled around the year 200, kept that grammar alive by teaching that in every generation a person must regard himself as though he had come out of Egypt.

This is the difference between the two ways of raising a child. A milestone asks a child to produce an answer. A rhythm makes a child produce the question. The checklist wants to know whether he can recite the verse by seven; the feast sets a strange table every spring until he can't help asking what it means, and then hands him a story in which he is already included. No rite, no question.

Luke tells us that the Son of God grew up inside exactly this pattern. "Now his parents went to Jerusalem every year at the Feast of the Passover. And when he was twelve years old, they went up according to custom" (Luke 2:41–42). The incarnate Word had a family calendar, and the one scene we have from his boyhood happens because Joseph and Mary kept it. Luke closes the chapter by saying that "Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (2:52). Scripture isn't embarrassed by growth. It sets growth inside a household's faithfulness to the year.

## What the Stages Can See, and What They Can't

A parent who takes childhood seriously will eventually meet the developmental psychologists, and should. Erik Erikson, in *Childhood and Society* (1950), described human life as eight stages, each organized around a crisis: in infancy, basic trust against mistrust; in adolescence, identity against role confusion; and on through old age. James Fowler built on Erikson, on Jean Piaget and on Lawrence Kohlberg, and after hundreds of interviews published *Stages of Faith* (1981). Fowler defined faith broadly, as the way any person composes meaning in relation to whatever they take to be ultimate. The young child's faith, which he called intuitive-projective, lives in images, moods and imitation. The school-age child's mythic-literal faith takes the community's stories as true and literal and makes them his own. The adolescent's synthetic-conventional faith is held in conformity with the people who matter most, largely unexamined. Young adulthood can bring an individuative-reflective faith, in which a person steps back from the group and takes responsibility for commitments of his own. Fowler described further stages that few adults reach.

At its strongest this is genuinely useful, and a parent who refuses it in the name of the Bible is refusing a gift. A child's faith has a structure appropriate to his age, so a six-year-old who takes the story of Jonah very literally is not failing, and a fifteen-year-old whose faith is mostly the faith of his friends is not a hypocrite. The move into reflective faith often looks, from the parent's chair, like loss. And the map keeps us from demanding a grown man's reasons from a small boy or handing a seventeen-year-old the answers that satisfied him at seven.

But the map has limits. The first is that Fowler's faith is, by design, content-neutral. It describes how a person trusts anything ultimate, God or nation or self, and can chart the shape of a child's trust without saying whether its object is worthy of it. Christian faith is defined by its object: trust in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. A stage chart can tell you how your son believes; it cannot tell you whether he is coming to know Christ. The second limit is the direction of the arc. The higher stages prize critical distance from one's community and a self that authors its own commitments, and critics, sympathetic ones among them, have noticed how closely that trajectory resembles the modern ideal of the autonomous individual. Scripture has its own developmental image, and it ends somewhere else. "When I was a child, I spoke like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I gave up childish ways" (1 Corinthians 13:11). Paul's next sentence isn't about independence: "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (13:12). The goal of Christian maturity is to be known by God, and Jesus set a child in the middle of his disciples to say so: "unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:3). No stage chart puts childlike dependence at the top. The third limit is humbler. Stages describe; they don't prescribe, they can't be sped up by a diligent parent, and their ages are averages drawn from particular people in a particular culture. Use the map. Don't mistake it for the destination.

A rhythm serves every stage at once. The Passover table held the four-year-old and the grandfather together, and each received what he could carry. A milestone sorts children by age; a rhythm gathers them.

In the earliest years, roughly to age five, a child's faith is not yet content. It is the felt answer to Erikson's first question, whether the people over him can be trusted, laid down in being fed, held and returned to. Rhythm matters here because it is predictable: the same blessing at bedtime, the same prayer at the table, the same church on the same day. For a small child, reliability is the first sermon, and rhythm is the form reliability takes. In the school years, roughly six to ten, the child is a lover of story and a natural literalist, and the church year was made for him. Advent teaches him to wait, Lent to repent, Holy Week to grieve, Easter to rejoice. Memorized words go deep at this age, psalms and the Lord's Prayer and a catechism's answers, and the story should be left to do its work without a moral hurriedly attached.

Early adolescence, roughly eleven to fourteen, is when the ground shifts. Belonging to peers suddenly weighs more than belonging to family, and abstract thought brings abstract doubt. The rhythm will be tested now, sometimes resented, and this is the season to give the young person a real part in it: reading the Scripture at the table, leading the prayer, welcoming the guest, asking the question or answering it for a younger brother. Scrutinizing a practice his family actually keeps is very different from scrutinizing an idea he has never seen lived. In the later teens, roughly fifteen to eighteen, a parent's authority gives way to influence whether we accept it or not. The rhythms become more invitational, and the parent keeps them for his own soul whether or not the young adult joins in that week. The aim across all of it is release: a faith that can stand when we are no longer in the room. The ages are approximate to the point of being almost fictional. Watch the actual child in front of you; he will show you which season he is standing in.

## Baptism, Confirmation and First Communion Across the Traditions

Here Christians disagree in earnest. Baptism is a second-order question: it divides faithful churches without dividing the faith. The Nicene Creed confesses "one baptism for the forgiveness of sins" without settling who receives it or when. Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican and Methodist churches baptize the infants of believers. They read baptism as God's covenant sign given to a household, as circumcision was, a grace that goes before the child's response (Acts 2:39). The Orthodox give baptism, chrismation and communion to infants together; the Latin West separated them over centuries: confirmation was reserved to the bishop, the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 tied confession and communion to the years of discretion, and Pius X lowered the age of first communion to about seven in 1910. Lutheran churches, and many Reformed ones, made confirmation or a public profession of faith the culmination of years of catechesis. Baptists, from John Smyth's congregation in Amsterdam in 1609, and the Anabaptists in Zurich before them in 1525, baptize on a credible profession of faith at whatever age it comes, following the New Testament pattern of repentance and faith before baptism, and they mark infancy with a dedication in which parents and congregation make promises. The strongest objection to infant baptism is that every clear baptism in Acts follows a profession. The strongest objection to believer's baptism is that Acts also records whole households baptized (Acts 16:15, 33) and that the covenant has always included children. This essay is written from a Baptist church and won't settle the question here; faithful readers on either side can disagree and remain brothers and sisters.

What every tradition shares, pressed hard enough, is that its rite is a door into a rhythm and not a finish line. The baptized infant's parents promise to raise him in the faith, and those promises are kept in ten thousand Lord's Days; the Baptist church dedicates a child and then spends years teaching before the child asks for the water. Each tradition has its version of the same failure: confirmation treated as graduation from church, or a baptism recorded on a certificate and then followed by a teenager who quietly disappears. A rite without a rhythm becomes a milestone. A rite within a rhythm becomes a threshold.

## The Parent Who Fears Structure Will Become Legalism

Some parents hear all of this and flinch, and they deserve their strongest statement. Many grew up in homes where devotions were enforced, the routine measured the family's righteousness, and Sunday was a list of prohibitions. They have Scripture on their side. "Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:16–17). Paul wrote to the Galatians, "You observe days and months and seasons and years! I am afraid I may have labored over you in vain" (Galatians 4:10–11), and to the Romans that one person esteems one day above another while another esteems all days alike (Romans 14:5). Jesus's sharpest conflicts were with the calendar's most careful keepers, and he told them "the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). A rhythm imposed as a measure of the family's holiness can teach a child that God is a supervisor, and children who learn that often leave. All of this is true.

But three things are also true. First, no household lives without a rule. The choice is between a rule we choose and one chosen for us by the school, the league and the phone, and the second is at least as demanding as any Puritan Sabbath. Second, the tradition that gave the church its word for a rule understood the danger. The Latin *regula* meant a straight rod, a measuring stick. Yet Benedict, in the Prologue to his Rule, hoped to set down nothing harsh or burdensome, and near its end he called the whole thing a little rule for beginners. A rule in his sense was a support for people who could not pray well unaided, which describes most parents at six o'clock on a weeknight. Third, habit is how character is made. Aristotle argued in Book 2 of the *Nicomachean Ethics* that the virtues of character come about by habituation: we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave ones, as a builder becomes a builder by building. It makes no small difference, he said, what habits we form from our youth; it makes all the difference.

The Christian has to amend Aristotle at one point. He believed good habits make a good man. The gospel says grace makes a new one, and no family rhythm can manufacture faith in a child. What a rhythm can do is place the child, again and again, where God has promised to meet his people: the Word read, prayer offered, the table shared, the church gathered. And Mark 2:27 cuts both ways. If the Sabbath was made for man, it is a gift, and refusing a gift for fear of misusing it isn't freedom. A law measures you. A rhythm carries you. It keeps going on the night the parents are exhausted and the prayer at dinner is rote, and that night counts, because the child learns this is simply what we do, not what we do when we feel like it. Start with one practice, most likely the table, and let the rest grow over years.

## What the Household Keeps

This ends in repentance before it ends anywhere else. We gave our children's time away, one sensible yes after another, and asked the youth ministry to undo what the week had formed. The way back is not a better chart. It is a household that decides, humbly and imperfectly, whose calendar it will keep.

No rhythm guarantees a child's faith. "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6) is a proverb, wisdom about how life usually goes, not a contract. Some children raised at the feast walk away, and some raised without it come home. I came to faith from atheism; grace is not confined to families that got the calendar right. But the Christian week still begins on the day of resurrection, and every Lord's Day a family keeps is a small rehearsal of the morning the tomb was found empty. The rhythm points past itself: "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9).

The child's question in Exodus only comes when something in the house is strange enough to ask about. The question left for us isn't which milestones our children have reached. It is whether anything in the way we keep time would make them ask what it means, and whether, when they ask, we could answer honestly that the Lord did this for me.
