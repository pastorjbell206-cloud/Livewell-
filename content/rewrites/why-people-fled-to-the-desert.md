Most people can still name the seven deadly sins, even if they couldn't say where the list came from. Pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony, wrath and sloth have outlived the catechisms that taught them. Very few know that the list began as field notes on the human heart, kept by a monk in a hut in the Egyptian desert at the end of the fourth century, who had gone there to find out what was inside him once everything else was taken away.

That monk was Evagrius Ponticus, and he was one of thousands. Between the late third century and the fifth, men and women left the towns of Egypt, Palestine and Syria to live alone or in communities in the wilderness. We call them the desert fathers and mothers, from the titles *abba* and *amma*, and the Christians who walked out to find them wanted one thing: a word to live by, from someone who had earned the right to give it.

The usual explanation is that they fled the world. That's true as far as it goes, but it leaves out the most interesting part. They discovered, often within weeks, that the world had come with them. The greed, the anger and the hunger to be admired did not stay behind in Alexandria. They were waiting in the cell, and louder there, because nothing else competed for attention. The desert did not rescue these people from the world. It showed them where the world actually lived.

That is why they matter to more than monks. They were a protest against a church that had grown comfortable, and we are that church. They drew a distinction our culture has nearly lost, between a thought that arrives and a sin that is consented to. And the case against them, which is serious and gets a full hearing below, has not managed in sixteen centuries to make them go away.

## Antony and the church that grew comfortable

The founding story belongs to Antony, born in Egypt around 251. Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote *The Life of Antony* around 360, a few years after Antony's death in 356, and it became one of the most widely read books of the ancient church. It tells how the young Antony, recently orphaned and holding good farmland, heard Matthew 19:21 read in church around 270 and took it as spoken to him. The site's essay on [the eye of the needle](/writing/the-eye-of-the-needle-jesus-and-the-rich) tells that story and what it meant for the church's teaching on wealth. Antony moved to the edge of his village, then to the tombs, then across the Nile to an abandoned fort, where he lived alone for about twenty years. When friends broke down the door around 305, Athanasius says, they were amazed to find a man in balance, his body neither bloated nor wasted and his soul neither agitated nor dulled.

The *Life* is a portrait meant to form its readers, full of demonic combat told in the conventions of ancient holy biography, but its outline isn't seriously in doubt. The timing needs care. Antony's withdrawal began before Constantine, and the word the movement took for itself, *anachoresis*, withdrawal, already had a history in Egypt, where the papyri often use it for peasants fleeing to the desert to escape crushing taxes. So the desert wasn't invented as a protest against the imperial church. But it swelled into a mass movement after 313, when the Edict of Milan ended the persecutions, and it kept swelling as the church went from tolerated to favored to established. Constantine built basilicas and gathered the bishops at Nicaea in 325 at imperial expense, and in 380 the Edict of Thessalonica made the Nicene faith the religion of the empire. Being a Christian had gone from a risk to an advantage.

For three centuries the martyrs had shown what the faith cost. Now the arenas were closed, and the question pressed: what does it cost to follow Christ in a church the emperor pays for? During the last persecution, in 311, Antony had gone down to Alexandria to serve the confessors in the prisons and mines, half hoping to be arrested. When it ended he went back to his cell, and Athanasius says he became a martyr daily in his conscience. The ascetic life became the martyrs' successor, a slow death to self now that the swift one was no longer on offer.

It is easy to read that as a verdict on the fourth century and feel nothing. It's harder to hear the risen Christ's words to Laodicea and miss the address: "For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing, not realizing that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked" (Revelation 3:17). The American church has rarely been persecuted. It has been courted, funded and flattered. We are that church, and the question lands on us before it lands on anyone we might want to aim it at. The desert doesn't ask only whether we are orthodox; the monks cared fiercely about that. It asks what our faith costs us, and whether we would hear the answer "almost nothing" as a crisis.

One more detail keeps this from becoming a romance of escape. When the Arian controversy was tearing the church apart, Antony came down to Alexandria again to denounce the teaching that the Son was a creature. The man who fled the city returned to it twice, once to stand with the martyrs and once to stand for the deity of Christ. Withdrawal, for him, was never indifference. It was a way of getting free enough of the city's approval to tell it the truth.

## From the lonely cell to the common life

The solitary life had an obvious danger. A hermit answerable to no one can become very holy or very strange, and from outside it's hard to tell which. The man who did most to answer that danger was Pachomius, born around 292 to pagan parents in Upper Egypt. His *Life* records that as a young conscript held under guard at Thebes, he was astonished when local Christians came to feed the prisoners out of simple mercy. He was baptized around 313, trained under an old hermit named Palamon, and around 323 founded a community at Tabennesi on the Nile.

What Pachomius built was less a hermitage than a village. He called it a *koinonia*, the word Luke uses for the fellowship of the first church in Jerusalem, and the form of life it named is called cenobitic, from the Greek for common life. Monks ate, worked and prayed together at fixed hours and held everything in common. The rule demanded something remarkable in a peasant society: anyone who arrived unable to read was to be taught, and everyone was to learn large portions of Scripture by heart, at least the Psalms and the New Testament. His sister Mary led a community of women nearby. By his death in 346 the federation stretched along the river and numbered in the thousands, and in 404 Jerome's Latin translation of its rules carried the common life west.

The sharpest early criticism of the solitary life came from a man who admired the desert enough to tour it. Basil of Caesarea visited the monks of Egypt, Palestine and Syria in the 350s and went home to write the rules that still shape Eastern monasticism. In his *Longer Rules* he argued that the solitary life, whatever its gifts, cannot fulfill the command to love, because love needs someone to receive it. He put it in a question that hasn't lost its bite: if you live alone, whose feet will you wash? He had in mind Jesus kneeling with a towel in John 13.

Benedict of Nursia inherited all of this. His Rule, written around 530, calls monks who live in community under a rule and an abbot the strongest kind, honors hermits only after long training in community, and is scathing about wanderers who drift from house to house, never staying long enough to be changed. Benedict called it a little rule for beginners and sent readers who wanted more to the Lives of the Fathers, the Rule of Basil and the *Conferences* and *Institutes* of John Cassian, who had carried Egypt's wisdom to Marseille a century earlier. Western monasticism, which kept the hours of prayer through the collapse of Rome, runs back through Cassian to the Egyptian sand.

The movement's own development renders a verdict. It began with a man alone and matured by learning that the Christian life can't be lived alone for long. The cell wasn't abandoned. It was set inside a community, where someone could see what you couldn't see in yourself.

## The sayings, and the mothers of the desert

The desert survives most vividly in the *Apophthegmata Patrum*, the Sayings of the Desert Fathers, short stories and one-line answers passed down orally and compiled in Greek in the late fifth century, most likely in Palestine by heirs of monks who had left Scetis after it was raided. Benedicta Ward's translation of the alphabetical collection (1975) made them readable for a generation of English speakers. Thomas Merton's earlier *The Wisdom of the Desert* (1960), a small selection from the Latin version the medieval West read, argued that the fathers saw their society as a kind of shipwreck from which each person had to swim for his life, and that what they sought was not an escape from humanity but the true self hidden with Christ in God.

The sayings are dry and often funny. Antony tells of seeing the snares of the enemy spread over the whole earth and asking with a groan what could possibly get through them, and hearing a voice answer, "Humility." Elsewhere he puts the whole spiritual life in a sentence about the neighbor: our life and our death are with our brother, so that if we win our brother we have won God, and if we cause him to stumble we have sinned against Christ. That saying anchors Rowan Williams's *Silence and Honey Cakes* (2003), the best short modern reading of the desert. Williams argues that the monks' deepest concern was never getting away from people but learning to be truthful with them, refusing the habits by which we manage, judge and use each other. The cell was not a refuge from relationship. It was a school for it.

It's a common mistake to picture the desert as a male enterprise. Only three women have their own entries in the alphabetical collection, Syncletica, Sarah and Theodora, which says something about who kept the records, but the ammas were sought out by men and women alike. Syncletica of Alexandria compared the beginning of the life with God to lighting a fire: first smoke and streaming eyes, and only then the fire you wanted. She warned that a bird who keeps getting up off her eggs will never hatch them, and that a monk or nun who keeps moving from place to place grows cold. Sarah, the sayings report, was assailed by the spirit of fornication for thirteen years and never prayed for the fight to end, only for strength; when at last it told her she had won, she answered that the victory belonged to her master, Christ. She also said that if she prayed for everyone to think well of her, she would end up doing penance at every door, so she prayed instead that her own heart would be pure toward all. Some of her sayings meet, with a flash of irony, the assumption that women were the weaker sex. The ammas were holy inside their culture, not above it.

Theodora left a story that answers the commonest caricature of the desert before anyone raises it. A hermit who could cast out demons asked them what drove them away. Fasting? They said they don't eat. Vigils? They don't sleep. Living apart from the world? They live in the desert too. What, then? Only humility, they admitted, overcomes them. And there is Abba Joseph, who, when Abba Lot described his modest rule of prayer and fasting and asked what more he could do, stood and stretched his hands toward heaven so that his fingers became like ten lamps, and said that if Lot wished, he could become all flame. The point of the discipline was never the discipline. It was a person set on fire by the love of God.

Peter Brown showed how badly the wider world wanted what these people had. His article "The Rise and Function of the Holy Man in Late Antiquity" (1971) looked mainly at Syria, where Simeon the Stylite spent more than thirty years on a pillar. Brown argued that the holy man's power came from his detachment. Having cut the ties of family, property and patronage, he could be trusted as an arbiter where the powerful could not, and villagers brought him their quarrels over land and water, their sick children and their debts. The man who fled society became one of its hinges.

## Evagrius and the eight thoughts: where the seven deadly sins began

Evagrius came to the desert by a crooked road. Born around 345 in Pontus, he was ordained a deacon by Gregory of Nazianzus and became a celebrated debater against heresy in Constantinople around the council of 381, until, as Palladius later recorded, an entanglement with a married woman sent him out of the capital in a hurry. He reached Egypt around 383 and lived at Nitria and the Kellia until his death in 399. His speculative theology was later condemned, but his teaching on the heart passed into the bloodstream of the whole church.

In the *Praktikos* Evagrius set out eight *logismoi*, thoughts, through which the enemy makes war on the soul: gluttony, fornication, love of money, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory and pride. The order is deliberate. The first three work through bodily appetite, sadness and anger through the soul's capacity for grief and resentment. Acedia, the listless restlessness the monks called the noonday demon, attacks the whole person at once, and the site's essay on [silence and solitude](/writing/silence-and-solitude) describes it closely. Vainglory and pride come last because they feed on victory. The monk who has beaten gluttony and lust is exactly the one most likely to admire himself for it.

The most important thing about the list is the distinction underneath it. A *logismos* is not yet a sin. Evagrius says that whether these thoughts trouble us is not in our power, but whether they linger and stir up the passions is. The insult rehearsed in the shower, the flare of envy at a friend's good news: these arrive uninvited, in everyone. The spiritual life happens in the space between the thought's arrival and our consent. Jesus had already located evil at that depth: "For from within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts, sexual immorality, theft, murder," (Mark 7:21). Mark's word for the thoughts, *dialogismoi*, means the arguments a person carries on inside himself, and Paul describes the fight over them as a siege: "We destroy arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, and take every thought captive to obey Christ," (2 Corinthians 10:5).

The distinction cuts two ways. It frees the scrupulous person who despairs because tempting thoughts keep coming; they will come until you die, and they are not the measure of your holiness. And it strips the respectable person of the assumption that a life without scandal means a clean heart. The war has been going on inside him all along, unattended, and he has been losing it quietly.

Evagrius also gave the church a weapon. His *Antirrhetikos*, roughly "talking back," gathers verses of Scripture to be spoken against each thought as it comes. The model was Jesus in the wilderness, who answered every temptation from Deuteronomy, the book of Israel's forty years in the desert. To the first he said, "It is written, 'Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God'" (Matthew 4:4). The words come from Moses' account of why God led Israel through the wilderness at all: "And you shall remember the whole way that the LORD your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness, that he might humble you, testing you to know what was in your heart, whether you would keep his commandments or not" (Deuteronomy 8:2). That verse is the desert tradition in a sentence: the wilderness is where the heart is shown and where God humbles and feeds his people in the same stroke.

The list traveled. John Cassian, who had lived among the Egyptian monks, wrote the *Institutes* in Marseille around 420, giving eight of its twelve books to the eight principal vices in nearly Evagrius's order, though without naming him. Gregory the Great reworked the scheme in his *Moralia in Job*, finished in the 590s: he set pride apart as the root of the rest, added envy, and folded acedia into sadness. Later medieval teachers restored sloth and merged vainglory into pride, and in the early fourteenth century Dante built the terraces of his *Purgatorio* on the seven most people still know. The West called them capital sins, from *caput*, head, because they were the sources other sins flow from, not the worst sins a person could commit. But over the centuries a map of the heart hardened into a checklist of behavior, and the desert would have seen the danger at once. A checklist can be passed. A heart has to be watched.

## The case against the desert

The desert's critics deserve to be heard at full strength. Edward Gibbon, in *The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776–1789), above all in chapter 37, treated the monks as fanatics who drained the empire of useful citizens and mistook self-torture for virtue. Friedrich Nietzsche went deeper. The third essay of *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) asks what ascetic ideals mean and answers that they are life turned against itself: people unable to master the world take revenge on it by calling the body and its pleasures sin, and then call their resentment holiness. On that account the desert isn't heroism. It is a sickness of the will dressed as sanctity.

The desert supplied evidence for the charge. Some monks starved themselves into ruin and treated filth as virtue, and pilgrims flocked to the most extreme as spectacles. Flight also had costs that other people paid: some sayings praise monks who would not see their own grieving mothers, and a reader today may fairly call that coldness, not holiness. Many sayings speak of women mainly as a temptation, an attitude that did long damage to the church's teaching on marriage and the body. And Basil's question about washing feet stands against any spirituality that leaves the neighbor behind. Scripture had already named the danger. Paul warned the Colossians about rules against touching and tasting: "These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (Colossians 2:23). And against teachers who forbade marriage and foods he told Timothy, "For everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving," (1 Timothy 4:4). Any desert that despises creation stands under that verdict.

So the critics are right about a good deal. But they are describing the desert's disease, and the desert diagnosed it first. Theodora's demons, who neither eat nor sleep, are an ascetic's own warning that fasting and vigils save no one. Syncletica taught that there is an asceticism that comes from the enemy, and that the true kind can be known by its balance. Several sayings tell of monks who gladly broke their fast to eat with a visitor, because hospitality is the command of love and fasting was only their own resolve. These are people who had watched others hate the body and recognized the hatred as pride.

Peter Brown's *The Body and Society* (1988) makes the historical case: for the best of the desert ascetics, he argues, the body was not a prison to escape but part of the self to be remade, drawn back toward the freedom Adam lost. That is why Athanasius dwells on Antony's body when he came out of the fort and again in old age, his sight clear and his limbs sound. And the portrait's author spent his life defending the teaching that the eternal Son took real flesh; a man who staked everything on the Incarnation was not recommending contempt for it. Paul's word to the Corinthians was the desert's premise, not its enemy: "for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20).

The deeper answer turns on what the desert meant by the world. Scripture uses the word in two senses that can sound contradictory: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:16), and yet, "Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him" (1 John 2:15). The first world is the creation, which God loves to the point of death. The second is the order of human desire turned away from him, the economy of appetite, status and display. At its best the desert fled the second in order to love the first more truly. At its worst it confused them.

Nietzsche's charge needs one more answer. Were these simply the weak taking revenge on a world they couldn't master? The record doesn't fit. Antony left good land and Evagrius a brilliant career; many walked away from precisely the mastery Nietzsche prized. They wanted freedom from the need to be admired, and the sayings show them finding it with a humor resentment doesn't produce. A thoughtful unbeliever may still conclude they were wrong about God. It is much harder, after reading them, to conclude they were sick.

The critique also comes home to us, though not the way Gibbon meant it. Our temptation runs the other way, toward a faith that costs nothing, whether a religious nationalism that baptizes a political tribe's ambitions or a therapeutic spirituality that offers calm without repentance. The desert, for all its faults, refused comfort, and a church as comfortable as ours is in no position to be severe with people who erred in the opposite direction.

## What a household can take from the desert without leaving home

Almost no one reading this will move to Egypt, and the elders would discourage most people from trying. They knew the war the cell revealed follows a person anywhere, which means ordinary life can become a desert in the good sense, a place where the heart is shown and God feeds his people.

The first gift is the distinction between a thought and a sin. Most families have no words for what happens inside a person before it comes out as a slammed door. The eight thoughts supply some. A child who learns that the flare of envy at her brother's birthday present is a thought that arrived, not a verdict on who she is, and that she can name it and refuse it, has learned what many adults never do. A parent who can say at the end of a hard day that anger came to the door that afternoon and was let in, and who asks forgiveness for it, teaches the same lesson the only way children finally believe it. Talking back can be as simple as a family keeping a few verses by heart for when a familiar thought comes calling.

The second is stability. Syncletica's bird speaks to people who are always half packed, sure the next church or town or season of life will finally be where God can reach them. For most of us the cell is a particular kitchen, marriage and set of neighbors, and the itch to be elsewhere is often acedia in respectable clothes. One boundary must be said plainly. Stability never means enduring abuse, and nothing in Scripture or in the desert asks anyone to stay where they or their children are in danger. Reaching for safety is faithfulness. In the United States the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, and the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline answers by call or text at 988.

The third is Antony's word about the neighbor. A household is where our life and our death are with our brother, often literally. The desert's refusal to judge is Jesus' teaching practiced at close quarters: "Why do you see the speck that is in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:3). There is no harder school for truthful relationship than a family sharing one bathroom.

The fourth is a rhythm of quiet and rest. The desert was built on hours of prayer, work with the hands and a weekly gathering at the Lord's Table, and a household can keep a smaller version of each. The site's essays on [silence and solitude](/writing/silence-and-solitude) and [what the Sabbath is](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it) take up those practices. What the desert adds is the reason. The monks kept silence and rest because noise and busyness were how the heart hid from God, and they wanted to be found.

In the end the desert isn't first a set of practices. It is a place in the story of God. Israel learned in the wilderness what was in its heart, and learned that God fed it every morning. Through Hosea, God spoke of faithless Israel almost as a lover making plans: "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (Hosea 2:14). Then his Son was driven into the same wilderness, met the temptations Israel had failed and the monks would face, and did not fall. He went on to a garden where he sweated in prayer, to a cross outside the city and to a borrowed tomb, and on the third day the tomb was empty. The monks could walk into the wilderness because he had already walked through it and out the other side.

That is what the desert fathers and mothers leave on the kitchen table. They went looking for what remains of a person when everything propping him up is taken away, and they found two things at once: a heart more disordered than they had feared and a God more patient than they had hoped. Most of us have arranged our lives so we'll never have to find out what they found. We won't have to leave home to find it, but we will have to stop running from the part of home where the thoughts come to the door, and learn to answer them there, with Scripture, before God, among the people he has given us, for as long as it takes.
