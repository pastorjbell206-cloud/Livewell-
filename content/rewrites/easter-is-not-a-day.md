On one Sunday each spring the parking lot overflows. Folding chairs come out of the closet, the ushers run short of bulletins, and families who haven't shared a pew since Christmas share one. By midafternoon the ham is eaten and the plastic eggs are found, or most of them. The Sunday after, which English Christians once called Low Sunday with a certain honesty, the room looks the way it looks the rest of the year.

There's nothing contemptible in that picture. Some people come on Easter because a grandmother is holding on to that one thread for the family, and the thread matters. Those of us who pastor are tempted to treat the day as a number, the high-water mark we report and compare, and the temptation says more about us than about the visitors. But the shape of the day tells a story about what we think Easter is. It's an event we attend, and by dinner it's over.

The church didn't receive Easter in that shape. Its oldest festival began as a night of watching kept in the dark, broke into dawn with the baptism of new believers, and then ran for fifty days, during which the ancient church forbade fasting and even kneeling. **Easter can be a Sunday we attend, or it can be a season that keeps us, and only the second is long enough for a people to learn, slowly and together, how to believe that a crucified man is alive.** Whether the resurrection happened is weighed in [Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen), and what it changes for the body, grief and work in [Why the Resurrection Matters](/writing/what-the-resurrection-changes). This essay is about the feast itself.

## The Oldest Feast the Church Keeps

Easter is older than Christmas by well over a century; the first firm evidence for a feast of Christ's birth comes from Rome in the 330s. Its roots run further back than the church, into a night in Egypt.

Israel is told to eat that first Passover dressed for departure: "In this manner you shall eat it: with your belt fastened, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand. And you shall eat it in haste. It is the LORD's Passover" (Exodus 12:11, ESV). Before the night has even come, God makes it an inheritance: "This day shall be for you a memorial day, and you shall keep it as a feast to the LORD; throughout your generations, as a statute forever, you shall keep it as a feast" (Exodus 12:14, ESV). Generations who weren't there would keep it as though they had been. That is what a feast is in Scripture: not a commemoration held at a respectful distance, but the way a later people is placed inside the saving event.

Jesus died at Passover, and none of the Gospel writers treats that as a coincidence. Matthew, Mark and Luke make his last supper a Passover meal, and John sets the crucifixion on the day of Preparation, while the lambs were being made ready. Within about twenty-five years Paul could write to a largely Gentile church in Corinth that was tolerating open sexual sin, and reach for the Passover rule that every trace of leaven be swept from the house: "Cleanse out the old leaven that you may be a new lump, as you really are unleavened. For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7, ESV). Then comes the sentence that gives the Christian feast its warrant: "Let us therefore celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven, the leaven of malice and evil, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (1 Corinthians 5:8, ESV).

Whether Corinth already kept an annual Christian Pascha, nobody can say with confidence, and Paul's point doesn't depend on it. The lamb has been killed, so the festival has begun, and it is kept with the house swept clean. It is moral before it is liturgical. Even the name came from Israel: *Pascha* is the Greek form of the Aramaic word for Passover, the Hebrew *pesach*.

The oldest surviving Easter sermon shows what came next. Melito was bishop of Sardis in Asia Minor, and his homily *On Pascha*, usually dated between 160 and 170, was recovered from papyri only in the twentieth century. Preached after Exodus 12 was read aloud, it treats the Passover of Egypt the way an artist treats a model in clay or wax, precious while the real work is being made and set aside once the finished statue stands. In its closing movement Melito lets Christ himself address the congregation and name what he is for them: their forgiveness, the Pascha of their salvation, the lamb slain for them, their resurrection.

Honesty requires the other half. The same homily turns on Israel with savage rhetoric and accuses the people of the Passover of murdering God. It is among the earliest Christian texts to make that charge, and the charge had a long and bloody future; in medieval Europe, Holy Week too often became a season of danger for Jewish neighbors. The church received its greatest feast from Israel's hands and then used it to indict the people who gave it. We can't keep the Pascha honestly without confessing that sin as part of our own memory.

Melito kept the feast on the fourteenth of Nisan, the date of Passover itself, while Rome kept the following Sunday, a quarrel told in [What Is the Church Calendar?](/writing/why-the-church-has-a-calendar). Both sides agreed on what mattered most: the death and rising of Christ were one event, the Christian Passover, and every church kept it.

## The Night Before the Morning: The Triduum and the Easter Vigil

The first Passover was kept at night, and Exodus insists that the darkness itself belongs to the feast: "It was a night of watching by the LORD, to bring them out of the land of Egypt; so this same night is a night of watching kept to the LORD by all the people of Israel throughout their generations" (Exodus 12:42, ESV). The early church took the verse at its word. For centuries the Pascha was a vigil, a fast that ran into a night of Scripture and prayer and broke, near dawn, into the Eucharist. The Gospels give it its hour: "Now on the first day of the week Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early, while it was still dark, and saw that the stone had been taken away from the tomb" (John 20:1, ESV). The oldest way of keeping Easter reproduces that: you wait in the dark, you hear the long story of God's saving acts, and the news reaches you before sunrise.

That night became the church's great night for baptism. Around 200, Tertullian of Carthage wrote in *On Baptism* that the Pascha was the most solemn day for baptizing, because believers are baptized into the Lord's passion, and that the fifty days after it, which he called the most joyful stretch of time, came next. The logic is Paul's: "having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God, who raised him from the dead" (Colossians 2:12, ESV). If baptism is burial and rising with Christ, it belongs on the night between his burial and his rising.

The fullest early description of such a night comes from *The Apostolic Tradition*, traditionally credited to Hippolytus of Rome around 215. Author and date are now disputed; Paul Bradshaw, Maxwell Johnson and Edward Phillips argue in their commentary (2002) that it is a layered church order assembled over time, and the rite it describes is never said to take place at the Pascha. Placing it at the Easter Vigil is a reading, a likely one given Tertullian, but still a reading. The candidates fast on Friday and Saturday, keep the whole night hearing Scripture and instruction, and at cockcrow go down into the water. At their first Eucharist they receive, along with bread and cup, milk mixed with honey, the food of the promised land. They had crossed the sea.

By the late fourth century the whole week had taken shape. The pilgrim Egeria's diary from Jerusalem, kept around 381 to 384, describes Holy Week observed in the places it happened and the bishop teaching the newly baptized what had been done to them. Around 400 Augustine, writing to Januarius, spoke of the most sacred triduum of the one crucified, buried and raised: Friday, Saturday and Sunday, one movement from the cross through the grave into the morning. The vigil many Catholics, Anglicans and Lutherans still keep gathers this into one rite. A new fire is lit in the dark, a single candle is carried into an unlit church, and a deacon sings the ancient *Exsultet*, which keeps returning to the words "this is the night" and dares to call Adam's sin a happy fault for having won so great a Redeemer.

What most of us have lost is the waiting. The sunrise service, which Moravian tradition traces to young men who went before dawn to pray among the graves at Herrnhut on Easter morning 1732, still keeps the vigil's hour, but Holy Saturday passes like any errand day. The morning means little to people who haven't sat through the night, and a congregation that never waits in the dark will hear the Easter announcement as a pleasant word about spring and fresh starts rather than the specific news that the man they watched die has been raised.

## Fifty Days Kept as One Great Sunday

Israel counted fifty days from the first sheaf of the barley harvest to the Feast of Weeks at the wheat harvest, and the Greek word for fiftieth, *pentekostē*, gave that feast its Christian name. For the early church Pentecost named not a single day but the whole season, fifty days from the Pascha kept as one continuous feast.

The rules show how seriously. Tertullian, in *The Crown* (around 211), lists among the church's unwritten customs that no one fasts or kneels to pray on the Lord's day, and that the same freedom holds from the Pascha until Pentecost. At Nicaea in 325 the bishops made it a rule for the whole church; their twentieth canon notes that some were kneeling on the Lord's day and in the days of Pentecost, and directs that prayer in those times be offered standing. Basil of Caesarea explained in *On the Holy Spirit* (around 375) that the standing of the fifty days points to the resurrection still to come, in the age that has no evening. Athanasius, whose festal letters announced the date of the feast to the churches of Egypt nearly every year, closed them by bidding the churches add the seven holy weeks of Pentecost, one after another, rejoicing. The church came to speak of the whole season as a single great Sunday, and the phrase fits. Every Lord's day was a small Easter. The fifty days were that Sunday stretched across a week of weeks.

The proportion should embarrass us. Paul Bradshaw and Maxwell Johnson, in *The Origins of Feasts, Fasts and Seasons in Early Christianity* (2011), show that the fifty days are attested by the end of the second century, while the forty days of Lent don't clearly appear until the fourth. A short fast before the Pascha is very old, but the season of feasting is older than the season of fasting. Most of us have reversed that. We keep Lent seriously for forty days, as we should, and then keep one of the fifty. The reversal may be a confession. Sin is easy to believe in, because we meet it every day in the mirror; resurrection is hard, because none of us has seen the dead rise. So we keep the season that matches our experience, and quietly tell the truth about which half of the gospel we find credible.

The resurrection narratives show why the church needed seven weeks. Luke's Gospel seems to compress everything into one day, and then the same author, opening Acts, says the risen Jesus "presented himself alive to them after his suffering by many proofs, appearing to them during forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God" (Acts 1:3, ESV). The event happened in a morning. Understanding it took the better part of two months. At Emmaus two disciples hear the risen Christ open the Scriptures and still don't know him until he breaks bread at their table: "And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And he vanished from their sight" (Luke 24:31, ESV). That evening the eleven see him, and Luke's account of them is startlingly honest: "And while they still disbelieved for joy and were marveling, he said to them, 'Have you anything here to eat?'" (Luke 24:41, ESV). The news was too good to hold, and the risen Lord answered their bewilderment by asking for fish.

John slows it further. Thomas refuses his friends' testimony, and the story waits a week: "Eight days later, his disciples were inside again, and Thomas was with them. Although the doors were locked, Jesus came and stood among them and said, 'Peace be with you'" (John 20:26, ESV). The second Sunday is still Easter, and the doors are still locked. Then in Galilee Jesus waits beside a charcoal fire: "Jesus said to them, 'Come and have breakfast.' Now none of the disciples dared ask him, 'Who are you?' They knew it was the Lord" (John 21:12, ESV). John's word for that charcoal fire appears in only one other place in the New Testament, the fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter denied him. Beside the second fire Jesus asks Peter three times whether he loves him. The man who failed worst was restored later, over breakfast, at the pace a wounded friendship actually heals.

That's what the fifty days are for. A single Sunday can hold the announcement that Christ is risen. It can't hold the slow recognition at the table, the second Sunday behind locked doors, the breakfast where a denial is undone.

The strongest objection comes from the free-church tradition: every Sunday is already Easter. That's true, and a church that keeps every Sunday as resurrection day keeps something truer than one that decorates a season and treats its Sundays as routine. But the fifty days were never a rival to the Lord's day. They were the Lord's day enlarged, and they press one question: whether our joy in the resurrection can last longer than a morning.

## Why Easter Moves, and Why East and West Keep Different Sundays

Easter wanders across five weeks of spring, and in most years Orthodox Christians keep it on a different Sunday from their Catholic and Protestant neighbors. Both facts come from the feast's origin. Passover is dated by the moon, and the church never cut its great feast loose from that reckoning. It only disagreed about the arithmetic.

Nicaea's decision in 325 that every church keep the same Sunday survives in letters rather than canons, and the council supplied no method. The rule most of us learned, the Sunday after the first full moon on or after the equinox, fixed by the church at 21 March, came from later tables.

Reconciling lunar months with solar years was hard mathematics. Alexandria, heir to a long tradition of astronomy, became the recognized authority, and its bishop announced the date each year. That is why Athanasius wrote his festal letters, and one of them, for the Pascha of 367, contains the earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven books of our New Testament. The canon reached the churches of Egypt inside an Easter announcement. In Britain, Irish and Roman missionaries brought different reckonings to the same kingdoms, so that the king of Northumbria could be feasting on Easter while his queen was still keeping Lent, until at the Synod of Whitby in 664 King Oswiu chose the Roman method. Bede tells that story in his *Ecclesiastical History* (731), and he wrote the leading medieval textbook on the reckoning, *The Reckoning of Time* (725). The labor was worth it. A church that couldn't agree on the day of the resurrection could hardly show the world it was one body.

The present division came later. The Julian calendar runs about eleven minutes longer than the solar year, and by the sixteenth century its equinox had drifted some ten days from the real one. In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII issued a reformed calendar, which the West adopted over the next two centuries. The Orthodox churches did not adopt it for the Pascha; apart from the small Finnish Orthodox Church, even those that later revised the calendar for fixed feasts like Christmas kept the Julian reckoning for Easter. So the Orthodox Pascha sometimes coincides with the Western Easter and sometimes falls one, four or five weeks later.

Each side has reasons its own members would sign. The Western case is fidelity to what Nicaea intended: a feast tied to the real spring and the real moon, from which the Julian calendar had drifted. The Eastern case is fidelity to what the whole church had done for twelve centuries, wariness of a change issued by a Roman pope, and concern for Orthodox unity; when a revised calendar for fixed feasts was proposed in 1923, it split communities in Greece that remain divided.

This is not a creedal question. The creeds confess that Christ rose on the third day; they bind no one to a table of full moons. But it isn't trivial. In many towns, Christians who confess the same risen Lord celebrate his rising on different days, and the world is entitled to notice. In 2025, the seventeen hundredth anniversary of Nicaea, the two reckonings fell on the same Sunday, 20 April, and on 29 November Pope Leo XIV and Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew signed a joint declaration in Istanbul asking that the search for a common date continue. This essay won't pretend to settle the question. A divided Easter is a visible wound, and Christians on both sides should feel it as one.

## Eostre, Eggs and Rabbits: Is Easter Pagan?

Every spring someone posts that Easter is a pagan festival with a cross painted on it, sometimes a skeptic who thinks Christianity is borrowed mythology, sometimes a Christian who thinks the faithful shouldn't keep the holiday. Both assume the name and customs tell you what the feast is. The claim deserves a patient answer, because part of it is true.

Start with the name. In most languages of the Christian world the feast still bears Israel's word: *Pâques* in French, *Pascua* in Spanish, *Pasqua* in Italian, *Paskha* in Russian, *Pasg* in Welsh. A few name it for its greatness, as the Polish *Wielkanoc*, the Great Night, does. Only English and German say Easter and *Ostern*, and only their word is said to come from a goddess. Our single early witness to that origin is Bede, who says in chapter 15 of *The Reckoning of Time* (725) that the month roughly matching April was once named for a goddess called Eostre, whose feasts were held in it, and that the English kept her name for the paschal season, calling the joys of the new rite by the old word.

Scholars have argued about that sentence ever since, because no other early source names Eostre. Some trust Bede, a careful scholar writing about a century after Northumbria's king was baptized in 627; Philip Shaw, in *Pagan Goddesses in the Early Germanic World* (2011), argues she was probably real, though a local figure rather than a great goddess of spring. Others suspect Bede reasoned backward from a month name that may simply have meant the month of dawn, or of the east. The question is open.

What came later is less open. In 1835 Jacob Grimm, in his *Deutsche Mythologie*, reasoned from the German *Ostern* to a goddess he called Ostara, for whom there is no ancient evidence, and much of the modern picture of a spring goddess with hares and eggs grew from that reconstruction. In 1853 the Scottish minister Alexander Hislop published *The Two Babylons*, later expanded, arguing in earnest that Roman Catholic worship was the religion of ancient Babylon in disguise, and among its claims was that Easter is the Babylonian Ishtar. His method, joining words by their sound and gods by their resemblance, hasn't survived scrutiny; the names sound alike only in English, and no linguistic path joins them. The Ishtar claim that circulates every spring descends from a Protestant attack on Catholics, not from ancient history.

The steelman of the pagan-origin case is still worth stating, because it is partly right. The church did sometimes adopt local customs on purpose. In 601 Pope Gregory the Great advised the missionary Mellitus, in a letter Bede preserves, to cleanse the temples of the English for Christian worship rather than destroy them, and to let their sacrificial feasts give way to Christian festivals. Feasts gather customs the way rivers gather silt. A Christian who worries that the silt can bury the river isn't foolish.

But the particular customs have plainer histories than the posts suggest. The medieval Western Lent forbade eggs along with meat, so the eggs piled up, and Easter was the day they returned to the table, decorated and carried to church for a blessing. In the Eastern churches eggs are still dyed red for the blood of Christ. The earliest known description of a hare that brings eggs comes from a German physician, Georg Franck von Franckenau, who wrote about the custom in 1682, and it reached America with German settlers in Pennsylvania. The historian Ronald Hutton, no apologist for the church, argues in *The Stations of the Sun* (1996) that claims of unbroken pagan survival in seasonal customs usually outrun the evidence. The egg is more likely a Lenten leftover than a fertility rite. And the feast's own origin is not in doubt: it is dated by Passover, and it was kept in Sardis and Rome more than four hundred years before Augustine of Canterbury landed in Kent in 597.

Paul faced the question in another form. Much of the meat in Corinth's market had passed through pagan temples, and he told the church that an idol is nothing, quoting a psalm: "For 'the earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof'" (1 Corinthians 10:26, ESV). He also told the strong not to trample the conscience of the weak. A family that hunts eggs isn't dabbling in paganism, and a family that declines for conscience's sake shouldn't be mocked. The skeptic who calls the feast a spring rite in borrowed clothes has the chronology backwards, and the Christian who fears the eggs has granted him the premise that the clothes make the feast. If there's a real danger in the modern Easter, it isn't the rabbit's pedigree. It's what all of us, church people included, have let the holiday become: one pleasant Sunday of new clothes, candy and a family meal that asks nothing and is over by evening. That Easter has no pagan roots. We built it ourselves.

## A Day We Attend, or a Season That Keeps Us

Every year at the paschal service, Orthodox churches read a short sermon handed down under the name of John Chrysostom, who died in 407. Whether he wrote it is uncertain, but it may be the best sermon on the Easter crowd ever preached. It takes up Jesus' parable of the laborers in the vineyard, where those hired at the last hour are paid the same as those who worked since dawn, and turns it on the church. Those who came only at the eleventh hour are told not to be afraid, because the Lord receives the last as the first, and those who kept the fast and those who didn't are both told to rejoice. The table is full, and no one should go away hungry. No one should weep over sins, because forgiveness has risen from the tomb. Then the sermon taunts death and Hades, which took a body and found themselves face to face with God.

That sermon doesn't scold the eleventh-hour crowd or flatter the faithful. It seats them all at one table and tells them the same news. That is the difference between an Easter we attend and an Easter that keeps us. Attendance is something we give; it can be counted and reported, and it's finished when we walk out the door. A feast is something given to us, and the ancient church thought receiving it took fifty days. The pastor who measures Easter by the crowd and the visitor who treats it as an annual obligation make the same mistake from opposite ends of the pew. Both have made the resurrection an event to be present for, not a table to linger at.

The Orthodox keep the season with a sentence. From Pascha until the eve of the Ascension they greet one another with the announcement "Christ is risen" and the answer "Truly he is risen." It echoes what the Emmaus disciples heard when they ran back to Jerusalem: "The Lord has risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon!" (Luke 24:34, ESV). The greeting turns the news into something you say to a neighbor in the third week of May, when nothing about the day feels like Easter, until the saying shapes the one who says it. Alexander Schmemann argued in *Great Lent* (1969) that the fast makes sense only as a pilgrimage toward Pascha, from which the whole Christian life is lived. The fifty days are where the church practices that.

A congregation that has never kept them doesn't need a program. It can keep preaching the resurrection appearances until Pentecost, sing Easter hymns in May, baptize new believers into Christ's death and rising, and say the old greeting at the door. None of it is required, and none of it earns anything. It's what a people does when it believes the news is too large for one morning.

For most of my life I kept Easter as one Sunday, and I know what that yields: a morning of real gladness, then a Monday no different from the one before it. Anyone who comes to faith by weighing the evidence as a skeptic would is tempted to file the resurrection as settled, a conclusion reached rather than a world to live in. The early church would have found that strange. For them it was the beginning of a new creation, and nobody takes in a new creation in an hour.

The church stood for fifty days. It didn't forbid kneeling because repentance had become unnecessary; it would kneel again, and so will we. It stood because repentance had been answered. The one whose death our sins required had walked out of the grave and cooked breakfast for the man who denied him, and there was nothing left to do for seven weeks but stand up in the presence of that fact and learn to believe it. Most of us sit back down on Monday. The lilies will be brown by Wednesday, and they were never the measure. The measure is whether, on the forty-ninth day, anyone in our churches is still saying that Christ is risen, and whether the people saying it could tell you why they haven't stopped.
