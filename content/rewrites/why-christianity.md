The question usually comes from someone who has done more homework than the Christian trying to answer it. They work beside a Buddhist who is kinder than most churchgoers they know. They have watched a Muslim neighbor keep the Ramadan fast through long summer days without complaint, or read the Bhagavad Gita in college and found in it a seriousness about duty and death their youth group never touched. And they ask, fairly: why Christianity? Why this faith and not one of the others, when every one of them has saints, scriptures, prayer and a moral vision older than the country we live in? Spoken by that person, the Christian claim that one faith is true in a way the others are not sounds like the provincialism of people who have never met an alternative.

The question deserves an honest answer, and I mean something specific by honest. It should be an answer that could be read aloud to a Buddhist, a Muslim, a Hindu and a Jew without any of them stopping the reader to say, that isn't what we believe. Most Christian answers fail that test. They compress three thousand years of other people's devotion into a single sentence, usually the one that says every other religion is a ladder of human effort and only ours comes down from heaven. There is something true near that sentence, but as usually stated it isn't fair, and a skeptic notices unfairness long before he notices error. I came to faith from atheism, so I know how these answers sound from the outside. An argument that wins by misdescribing its rivals hasn't won anything.

**The honest case for Christianity doesn't begin by pretending the religions secretly agree, or by pretending the others are empty. It begins by admitting that they differ at the root, that the pluralist who says otherwise is making a religious claim of his own, and that what Christianity alone says, when its claims are held together (a God who is love within himself, a grace that God pays for, a Lord who was crucified, and a resurrection that could in principle be disproved) is specific enough to be weighed and, at one point, specific enough to be falsified.**

## Why the Question Is Newer, and Harder, Than It Looks

For most of Christian history, most Christians never faced this question as a live option. The faith they were baptized into was not something they chose from a shelf. It was the air. The sociologist Peter Berger gave the modern change its sharpest name in *The Heretical Imperative* (1979). The Greek word behind "heresy," *hairesis*, means choice. In a traditional society, Berger argued, religion comes to a person as fate, the way language and kinship do. Modernity dissolves that. It multiplies the options, weakens the institutions that made one option feel inevitable, and turns every believer into a chooser. Under modern conditions, in the root sense of the word, everyone becomes a heretic, because everyone has to pick.

Americans met that condition in stages, and some of the stages have dates. In September 1893 the World's Parliament of Religions opened in Chicago, alongside the Columbian Exposition, and the most celebrated speaker was not a Protestant bishop but a young Hindu monk, Swami Vivekananda, who told the assembly that the many religious paths are like streams rising in different places and flowing into one sea. Then in 1965 the Immigration and Nationality Act ended the national-origins quotas that had kept most of Asia out, and within a generation the Hindu temple, the Buddhist meditation center and the mosque had become features of American suburbs. Diana Eck of Harvard documented the change in *A New Religious America* (2001): the other religions are no longer in textbooks but at the soccer field and the next desk over. The question "why yours?" is now asked by people who can put a face to every alternative.

That's why the old answers feel thin. Christians who respond by pretending those traditions contain nothing of value are not being faithful. They're being defensive, and defensiveness is a poor foundation for anything.

So begin with the common ground, because it's real. The instinct that we should not do to others what we would hate done to ourselves appears in the *Analects* of Confucius, in the *Mahabharata*, and in the famous saying the Talmud attributes to the sage Hillel, who summed up the whole Torah as refusing to do to your neighbor what is hateful to you and called the rest commentary. C. S. Lewis gathered dozens of such maxims from across the ancient world in the appendix to *The Abolition of Man* (1943), and he took the overlap as evidence not that all religions are one but that there is a real moral order, which he called the Tao, that human beings did not invent and every culture in some measure recognizes.

The first distinction matters most. The world's religions converge most where they talk about how to treat a neighbor, and they diverge most where they talk about what is ultimately real, what has gone wrong with us, and what would put it right. Agreement on ethics is not agreement on God. Two doctors can agree completely that the patient should stop smoking while disagreeing about whether he has cancer. The question "why Christianity?" is not a question about the Golden Rule. It is a question about the diagnosis.

## The Pluralist Case at Full Strength

The strongest answer to "why Christianity?" is that the question is badly formed, and no one made that case with more care than the philosopher John Hick. His fullest statement came in *An Interpretation of Religion* (1989), the published form of his Gifford Lectures, and it grew out of his years at the University of Birmingham, where he taught from 1967 and came to think that his Muslim, Sikh and Hindu neighbors were doing in their own buildings what Christians do in church. It deserves to be stated as he would state it.

The argument runs on a distinction taken from Immanuel Kant: we never know anything purely as it is in itself, only as it reaches us through the forms our own minds supply. Hick applied that to God. Ultimate reality, which he called the Real so as not to decide in advance whether it is personal, lies beyond every human concept. What the religions meet is the Real as a particular culture and history receive it. Some meet it as a personal Lord: the God of Israel, Allah, Vishnu, the Holy Trinity. Others meet it as an impersonal absolute: Brahman beyond all attributes, or the emptiness of Buddhist teaching. None of these is the Real itself, and each is a genuine response to it. Hick also offered a test that favors no tradition. The great religions all aim at one transformation, a turning of human life from self-centeredness toward the Real, and as far as anyone can observe, its fruit in compassion and holiness shows up about as often in one faith as in another.

Behind the philosophy sit a moral argument and an intuition. The argument is that a God who loves every human being would not make knowing him depend on being born near the right tradition. The intuition is one nearly everyone has felt: born in Lahore or Kyoto, you would very likely hold that faith with exactly the confidence you now hold this one. Pluralism's favorite picture gathers it all up, the old story of the blind men and the elephant. One touches the trunk and calls the animal a snake, another the leg and calls it a tree, another the ear and calls it a fan. Each has part of the truth, and each is foolish to take his part for the whole.

Hick's is one of three Christian answers. In *Christians and Religious Pluralism* (1983) the English theologian Alan Race sorted them into exclusivism (salvation through explicit faith in Christ), inclusivism (Christ alone saves, but his grace may reach those who never heard his name) and pluralism (no tradition is the center), and his scheme has organized the debate ever since. How Christ's saving work reaches those who never heard is a real question on which faithful churches have disagreed, and it is taken up at length in [Is Jesus Really the Only Way?](/writing/is-jesus-really-the-only-way). This essay asks a different question. Not who will be saved, but which account of reality is true.

Grant the pluralist what is true in his case, because a good deal of it is. Holiness does appear outside the church, and Christians who deny it aren't looking. Birthplace does shape belief, and anyone who never asks what his own faith would be if he'd been raised elsewhere hasn't yet taken the question seriously. And the church has often carried its convictions as a weapon against the very neighbors Hick came to admire. The pluralist didn't invent those facts. We handed them to him.

But the position has a price, and Hick, to his credit, paid it in the open. A literal incarnation would make one tradition the center after all, so Hick came to read the incarnation as metaphor rather than fact. Pluralism, in other words, doesn't set Christianity on a shelf beside its equals. It reshapes Christianity until it fits the shelf, and it quietly does the same to every other faith.

## No One Stands Above the Elephant

Lesslie Newbigin knew this argument from the inside. He sailed for India in 1936 as a missionary, served there for most of the next four decades, became a bishop in the Church of South India, and in 1974 returned to England and settled in Birmingham, the city where Hick was teaching, among the same neighbors. The two men looked at the same streets and reached opposite conclusions. Newbigin's reply, set out in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989), begins with a single question about the elephant. Who is telling the story?

The story is told from the vantage of someone who isn't blind, someone who sees the whole elephant and can therefore tell that each of the others has only a part. Newbigin's point was that the pluralist quietly gives himself that role. To say that every religion grasps only a fragment of the Real, he has to claim a sight of the Real large enough to know that the fragments are fragments. That isn't humility. It is the claim to see everything, made by the one man in the room who says nobody can.

The irony deepens when you trace the parable home. Its oldest Buddhist telling, preserved in the Pali *Udana*, is not a plea for pluralism at all. The Buddha tells it about wandering teachers of rival schools who quarrel because each clings to one side of the truth, and the one telling the story is the Awakened One, who sees. In its own tradition the parable is a claim that there is a clear-sighted teacher and that his dharma is the way out of partial views. Vivekananda's streams flowing into one sea come from a home as well: the Vedanta he taught, in which the many gods and paths are finally expressions of one Brahman. Pluralism has sources, and they are religious sources. It is not the view from above the traditions. It is one more tradition, with a doctrine of ultimate reality that Christianity, Islam and much of Buddhism reject.

Newbigin also named the habit of mind that makes pluralism feel like the only polite position. Modern Western culture keeps two ledgers, one for facts, which are public and binding on everyone, and one for values, which are private and binding only on whoever chooses them. Religion was entered in the second ledger, so it is judged by sincerity rather than truth. You may believe in the resurrection the way you prefer jazz to country, but you may not say it happened in the way the Battle of Hastings happened. Newbigin thought the arrangement incoherent, because the gospel doesn't present itself as a value. It reports that certain things occurred in Judea under a named Roman governor and that they disclose who rules the world. That report is either true or false. It can't be true for me.

This is where the pluralist's generosity costs the religions their own voices. Because Hick's Real lies beyond personal and impersonal alike, it can be neither the God who spoke to Moses nor the emptiness the Buddha realized, and it can't, in itself, be said to love. Every tradition that says the ultimate reality loves is told it has described a human picture rather than the thing itself. The Catholic theologian Gavin D'Costa pressed the point in a 1996 article in *Religious Studies*, "The Impossibility of a Pluralist View of Religions." Every pluralist, he argued, needs criteria for deciding which religions count as valid responses, and those criteria come from somewhere, so pluralism turns out to be a form of exclusivism that won't admit it. In practice the pluralist informs the Muslim that the Qur'an is not literally God's speech, the Buddhist that nirvana is one cultural image of the Real, and the Christian that the incarnation is a metaphor, and calls the result tolerance.

The Christian who says "I believe the others are mistaken where they contradict what God has done in Christ" is at least taking them at their word. Being told you are wrong is a form of respect. Being told you didn't mean what you said is not.

## What the Religions Actually Say

Stephen Prothero, a scholar of religion at Boston University, wrote *God Is Not One* (2010) to puncture what he considered a well-meant fiction. The idea that all religions are different paths up the same mountain, he argued, is neither true nor respectful. It flattens what adherents actually care about into a lowest common denominator none of them would recognize. Prothero proposed a simple way to see the differences. Ask each religion four things: what is the human problem, what is the solution, what techniques move a person from one to the other, and who are the exemplars who show the way. Ask those questions, and the traditions stop looking like dialects of one faith.

For Buddhism, in Prothero's scheme, the problem is suffering and the solution is awakening. The Buddha's Four Noble Truths name the condition (life as we grasp it is marked by suffering and unsatisfactoriness), its cause (craving), its cessation (nirvana), and the Eightfold Path that leads there. The self that craves is not, on Buddhist teaching, a permanent soul at all, and no creator God stands behind the world as its answer. In most Buddhist schools, what a person needs is not forgiveness from a divine person but to see clearly and let go. For Islam, the problem is pride, the human forgetfulness that refuses to acknowledge the one God, and the solution is submission, which is what the word *islam* means. God is one, utterly without partner, and he is merciful: every chapter of the Qur'an but one opens by naming him the Compassionate, the Merciful. Jesus is honored as a prophet born of a virgin, and the Qur'an (4:157) denies that he was crucified. For Judaism, Prothero names the problem as exile and the solution as return: a covenant people called by God, given the Torah as the shape of a life lived before him, and waiting on his faithfulness. For much of Hindu thought, the problem is *samsara*, the round of rebirth driven by karma, and the solution is *moksha*, release. In the Advaita Vedanta of Shankara, the eighth-century teacher, release comes when one realizes that the deepest self, *atman*, is identical with Brahman. In the devotional traditions associated with Ramanuja and others, it comes through loving surrender to a personal Lord.

Those are not four descriptions of one elephant. One tradition says the ultimate reality is a personal God who speaks and judges; another says it is an impersonal ground in which the individual self is finally discovered to be no separate self at all; another says the question of a creator is beside the point. One says Jesus died on a cross; another says that he didn't. The theologian S. Mark Heim took these differences so seriously that in *Salvations* (1995) he argued the religions may not even be aiming at the same end. The Buddhist who seeks nirvana isn't trying and failing to reach the Christian heaven, and the Christian who hopes for communion with the triune God isn't grasping clumsily at nirvana. They want different things. Heim writes as a Christian, and he holds the Christian end to be the fullest. But he refuses to pretend the others are confused versions of it.

This is also where Christians must correct our own caricatures. A great deal of popular apologetics, the kind many of us learned first, says that every other religion is salvation by human achievement and only Christianity is grace. Put that way, it isn't accurate, and the adherents of other faiths would say so. Jews do not, and did not in Jesus' day, think they earned God's favor by keeping the law. E. P. Sanders showed in *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) that the Jewish literature of the period assumes Israel is in the covenant by God's gracious election, with obedience as the response and the sacrifices and repentance as God's provision for failure, a pattern he called covenantal nomism. (The debate over Sanders, and what Christians owe Judaism, is traced in [The Jewish Roots of Christianity](/writing/what-christians-can-learn-from-judaism).) Muslims believe deeds will be weighed at the judgment, but they speak constantly of God's mercy, and a well-known hadith in the collection of al-Bukhari has the Prophet saying that no one enters paradise by his deeds, not even himself, unless God covers him with his mercy. And in twelfth- and thirteenth-century Japan, Honen and his disciple Shinran taught a Pure Land Buddhism in which the believer gives up all reliance on his own power and trusts wholly in the vow of Amida Buddha to bring him to the Pure Land. The *Tannisho*, a record of Shinran's sayings, has him teaching that if even the good person is born in the Pure Land, how much more the wicked one, who has no illusions about his own merit.

Karl Barth, the most influential Protestant theologian of the twentieth century, knew about Shinran, and he didn't look away. In *Church Dogmatics* I/2 (1938), in §17, where he calls all religion, the Christian religion included, a form of human unbelief, he stopped in the closing part on "true religion" to discuss the Pure Land schools as the closest parallel he knew to the Reformation's teaching on grace. What makes Christianity true, he said in substance, is not that it possesses a doctrine of grace, since Japan had arrived at something strikingly similar without the gospel. It is the name of Jesus Christ alone. The distinctiveness does not lie in an idea. It lies in a person and in what that person did.

That is the honest ground on which the Christian answer has to be built. Not "we have grace and they don't," but "we say grace has a name, a date and a body." The difference between Christianity and its neighbors is not that it alone speaks of mercy. It is where it locates mercy and what it says the mercy cost.

## What Only Christianity Says

Four claims, held together, belong to Christianity alone, and the fourth needs a section of its own. The first is that God is love, not only toward us but in himself. "Anyone who does not love does not know God, because God is love" (1 John 4:8). Islam names God *al-Wadud*, the Loving, and Muslims would rightly resist any suggestion that their God is cold. But a God who is a single person has no one to love until he makes a world. His love is real, but it's something he does, not what he is before anything else exists. The Christian doctrine of the Trinity says that from eternity the Father loves the Son in the Spirit. Jesus prays to the Father, "you loved me before the foundation of the world" (John 17:24). Muslim theologians answer that God's attributes are eternal and need no eternal beloved, and that answer deserves respect. The Christian reply, put by Richard of St. Victor in the twelfth century, is that perfect charity is by its nature love for another, so a God who simply is love must have within himself one to love. Love is not a feature God adds to his relation with creatures. It's the shape of his own life, and he made us to be drawn into it.

The second is that grace has a cost and God pays it. Pure Land Buddhism teaches trust in Amida's vow, and in that tradition the vow was fulfilled through the bodhisattva Dharmakara's immeasurable practice on behalf of others, so the compassion there is real and even costly. But it is the compassion of one who became a Buddha, not of the creator taking on flesh and dying at the hands of his creatures. Judaism knows the covenant God's steadfast love, and Islam his mercy, but in neither does God himself bear the judgment. Paul's claim is stranger: "but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). The one who forgives is the one who suffers the offense. The fuller account is in [Christianity vs Islam](/writing/christianity-and-islam), and it is only fair to say that Muslims find this idea not merely unnecessary but unworthy of God.

The third is the crucifixion itself. The Buddha and the Prophet of Islam died in honor. The Buddha died in old age among his disciples, having reached final nirvana; Muhammad died in Medina in 632, a prophet and a statesman. Jesus died on a Roman cross, the death the empire reserved for slaves and rebels. Martin Hengel showed in *Crucifixion in the Ancient World* (1977) how unspeakable that death was to Roman ears. No one inventing a religion to win the Mediterranean world would have put a crucified man at its center, and Paul knew exactly how it sounded: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23). Jürgen Moltmann argued in *The Crucified God* (1972) that the cross must be the ground of everything Christians say about God, because there God enters the place of the godforsaken. Other traditions have a God who rescues the sufferer or a path that transcends suffering. Christianity has a God who suffered.

## A Faith That Could Be Proved False

In 1777 the German critic Gotthold Ephraim Lessing wrote that accidental truths of history can never become the proof of necessary truths of reason, and he confessed that he could not get across what he called the ugly broad ditch between the two. Much religion lives comfortably on the far side of that ditch. Buddhists teach that the dharma is discovered, not invented, true whenever and by whomever it's seen; a Buddhist can grant that the details of Siddhartha's life are uncertain without losing the path. Christianity has never had that option. It jumped into the ditch on purpose. Around the mid-50s, writing to Corinth, Paul set the terms himself: "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14). Three verses later he said it again: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). It is rare for a founding text to name the historical event whose failure would refute it. The evidence for that event, and the strongest skeptical replies, are weighed in [Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen).

That changes what kind of question "why Christianity?" is. It isn't, finally, which faith feels deepest or produces the kindest people. I have watched the gospel hold people in hospital rooms and at gravesides, but an imam and a Buddhist chaplain can say the same about their own, and they'd be telling the truth. The lived test shows that a faith can bear the weight of a life. It can't show which faith is true. Christianity has asked to be judged on something harder: whether a man executed outside Jerusalem around the year 30 was alive again on the third day.

The claim also takes away every ground Christians have for feeling superior. "What do you have that you did not receive?" Paul asked a church puffed up over its teachers and its own wisdom (1 Corinthians 4:7). If salvation is a gift, the one who has it can only hold it with gratitude. That's why the crusades, the forced baptisms and the contempt the church has so often shown other faiths are not merely bad manners. They contradict the message. Barth was right that the Christian religion stands under the same judgment as every other; we are not the exception to the diagnosis. We are the first patients.

So the honest answer to the skeptic is not that Christians are better, or that other traditions have nothing to teach, or that Christianity feels truer. It's that Christianity says something none of the others says, that God is love in himself and came in person to bear what we did, and then stakes everything on a morning in Jerusalem that either happened or didn't. You may weigh it and conclude it didn't, and nothing in this essay will call you foolish for that. What you can't say is that the faith hid from the question. It wrote down the terms of its own refutation and handed them to its enemies.

And if that morning did happen, the question comes back around, and it doesn't land on the skeptic first. It lands on those of us who say we believe it. A God who died for his enemies can't be carried into the world as a trophy of our side. If the tomb is empty, then the people who claim to have seen it have to explain why we have so often looked like the least likely people on earth to have seen anything at all.
