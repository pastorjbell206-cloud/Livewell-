The church in America has not been silenced. It has broadcast networks, publishing houses, schools and a political voice that the martyrs who built its first congregations could not have imagined. What it has lost is not the ability to speak but the standing to be heard, and that is a harder problem than silence. A church suppressed by force can keep the faith underground until the force relents, and it usually comes out with more authority than it went in with, because persecution proves what a community actually believes. A church that has lost the trust of the people it claims to serve has no such consolation. Nobody took its credibility. It spent it.

Gallup has asked Americans since the early 1970s how much confidence they have in a list of institutions, and one of them is "the church or organized religion." In 1975, 68 percent said they had a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in it, and for years the church sat at or near the top of the list. In 2022 the figure was 31 percent, the lowest Gallup had recorded, and in the years since it has stayed close to that floor. Within a single lifetime, the institution Americans trusted most became one that two in three of them do not trust much at all.

I came to faith from atheism, so I know how the church's file reads from the outside, and as a pastor I know how it reads from the inside. The file is not a conspiracy assembled by hostile journalists. It's a record, and most of it is true. This essay argues that credibility and truth are different things, that the church has confused them in both directions, and that the only road back is the one it keeps trying to route around: repentance, slow and costly and public, of a kind that cannot be staged.

## What the numbers measure, and what they don't

It helps to be exact about what Gallup is counting, because the figure is easy to misuse in both directions. The question does not ask whether people believe in God, whether they pray, or whether they think Jesus rose from the dead. It asks how much confidence they have in an institution. A person can believe every line of the Nicene Creed and still tell Gallup she has little confidence in organized religion. So the decline is not, by itself, a measure of unbelief. It measures trust in the people and structures that carry the faith, which is a different thing and in some ways a more painful one.

The strongest defense of the church deserves to be heard at full strength. Nearly every institution on Gallup's list has fallen over the same half century. Confidence in Congress, in newspapers, in big business and in the public schools has dropped too, and Congress has sunk far lower than religion. Something has happened to American trust in general, and the church is caught in a current it did not create. The defenders go further, and rightly. The long record of Christian witness includes the hospital and the university, the campaign against the Atlantic slave trade that William Wilberforce and his allies carried through Parliament in 1807, the Black churches that organized much of the civil rights movement, and the care of the sick and the hungry in every generation. Any account of the church that leaves that record out is as dishonest as one that leaves out the Crusades or the use of Scripture to defend slavery.

All of that is true, and none of it resolves the problem. Credibility is not assessed across centuries. It's assessed in the present, by particular people who needed the church to protect them and found it protecting itself. The general collapse of institutional trust explains why the fall was easy. It does not explain why so much of it was deserved. And the comparison with Congress is not the comfort it seems, because nobody expects Congress to be holy. The church is the one institution on the list that claims to speak for God, and it is judged, fairly, by the standard it announced.

The shape of the fall also tells against the general explanation. The church's numbers did not glide downward like a tide going out. They dropped in stages. Gallup's own analysts connected the slide of the late 1980s to the televangelist scandals of those years, and the sharpest single fall, from 60 percent in 2001 to 45 percent in 2002, came as the Boston revelations broke. A current can carry a boat downstream. It does not explain the holes in the hull.

## How the trust was spent

The first cause, and the heaviest, is abuse and its cover-up. In January 2002 the *Boston Globe* documented how the Archdiocese of Boston had moved priests accused of abusing children from parish to parish rather than removing them, and Protestants who read it as a Catholic story had their own reckoning coming, most visibly in the 2022 report on the Southern Baptist Convention's handling of abuse. That history is told in [Sexual Abuse in the Church and How Churches Should Respond](/writing/sexual-abuse-crisis-in-the-church). What matters here is the shape of the wound. People do not expect any human community to be free of predators. What broke their trust was the second wrong, the institution's decision, made again and again across traditions and decades, that its reputation was worth more than the child. If you are carrying that kind of harm, it was not your fault. RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline answers at 1-800-656-4673, and if a child is at risk now, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline answers at 1-800-422-4453. When a crime has been committed, report it to the police, not to the church's own leaders alone.

The second cause is political capture, and it runs in both directions, though not with equal weight. On the right, a large share of white American evangelicalism spent the decades after the founding of the Moral Majority in 1979 fusing its witness to one party's program until the two could hardly be told apart, a story traced in [Should Churches Have an American Flag in the Sanctuary?](/writing/the-flag-in-the-sanctuary) and [Why Did Christians Lose the Culture War?](/writing/why-the-church-lost-the-culture-war). Part of the exodus described in [Why Are People Leaving the Church?](/writing/why-people-are-leaving-the-church) was a reaction against exactly that fusion. On the left, the older mainline churches learned the same habit earlier and practiced it more politely, issuing statements that could be predicted from the other party's platform and quietly subtracting whatever in the faith their members found embarrassing. Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon made the uncomfortable point in *Resident Aliens* (1989) that liberal and conservative churches, for all their mutual contempt, shared one assumption: that the church's job was to help run America. They disagreed only about which America. When a church's moral positions line up with a party's in every particular, the watching world concludes, not unreasonably, that the politics came first and the theology was recruited to bless it.

The third cause is money. In 1987 the PTL ministry of Jim and Tammy Faye Bakker collapsed in scandal, and Jim Bakker was convicted of fraud in 1989 for selling lifetime memberships at a resort that could never honor them. In February 1988 Jimmy Swaggart, who had publicly denounced other preachers' sins, confessed his own in tears before his congregation. The ordinary cases did more lasting harm: the ministry with no independent board, the pastor's salary the congregation was not allowed to see, the building campaign that treated a widow's savings as a measure of her faith. The prosperity teaching discussed in [What Is the Prosperity Gospel, and Is It Biblical?](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel) made the whole enterprise look like a transaction to people who had watched someone they love pay into it and receive nothing back but the suggestion that their faith had been too small.

The fourth cause never makes the news and may matter most: the gap between what the church preaches and what it practices. The failures here are small, steady and visible to anyone who stands close enough, like the church that claims to love its neighborhood but appears there only to recruit, or the pastor whose private life contradicts his public words in the daily ways his family and staff cannot help seeing. Paul put the charge to his own people with a directness we would not dare: "you then who teach others, do you not teach yourself? While you preach against stealing, do you steal?" (Romans 2:21). He was writing to people who knew the law and were proud of knowing it, which is to say he was writing to us.

None of these causes is someone else's fault. I belong to the institution whose file this is, and I stand in its pulpit every week. None of us who preach is exempt from having been more certain about other people's politics than about our own hearts. The people who walked away were doing what honest people do when the evidence reaches the point where it requires a conclusion. The church does not get to call that conclusion persecution.

## The church has been here before

The church's first great credibility crisis came from its own failure under pressure. When the emperor Diocletian launched the Great Persecution in 303, the authorities demanded that Christians surrender their Scriptures to be burned. Many refused and died. Some clergy handed the books over, and the church in North Africa called them *traditores*, the ones who handed over. In 311 or 312 a new bishop of Carthage, Caecilian, was consecrated by a bishop accused of being one of them, and a rigorist party refused to accept him. They became known as Donatists, and their argument had real moral force. How could a church whose ministers had surrendered the Word under threat claim to speak for it? A sacrament performed by a compromised man, they said, was no sacrament at all.

The answer the wider church finally gave, most fully in Augustine's writings against the Donatists around 400, is one of the most important things it has ever said about its own credibility. The sacraments belong to Christ and not to the minister. Baptism is valid because Christ is its true giver, whatever the moral state of the man pouring the water. That is a merciful answer and a true one, since no congregation can be certain of its pastor's heart, and it means the grace of God is not held hostage to the worst of his servants. But an institution can turn that truth into an alibi: if the gospel is true whoever preaches it, perhaps the preachers' conduct doesn't finally matter. Augustine never meant that. Yet the story has a darker chapter. When argument failed to bring the Donatists back, Augustine came around, in letters written around 408, to defending the emperor's use of coercion against them. The church that had won the argument about truth reached for power to win the argument about loyalty, a pattern it would repeat for fifteen hundred years.

The church before Constantine had no such power to reach for, and that is why its credibility is instructive. Tertullian, writing his *Apology* in Carthage in 197, described a common fund, given monthly by those who wished and were able, and spent on feeding and burying the poor, on orphans, on the housebound elderly, the shipwrecked and believers condemned to the mines. It was these works of love, he says, that led some pagans to brand the Christians, and he reports what they said: see how they love one another, and how ready they are to die for one another. The remark carried as much suspicion as admiration. What the pagans noticed first was not the doctrine but the life, and the life was strange enough to require an explanation, as [Was the Early Church Communist?](/writing/early-church-practiced) describes.

The same thing happened under plague. When an epidemic swept the empire in the middle of the third century, Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote a letter that Eusebius preserved in his *Ecclesiastical History*, describing Christians who nursed the sick without thought for their own danger, many of them dying of it, while pagan neighbors pushed the sick away and fled. It is a bishop's testimony about his own people and should be read that way. But the sociologist Rodney Stark argued in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) that the pattern would have mattered in plain demographic terms: basic care, water and food for the bedridden, would have saved many lives, and the survivors would have remembered who stayed. The early church's credibility was built by people dying of plague in order to care for other people dying of plague.

The early Christians had no platform, no influence, no protection and no reputation to manage, only a way of life the watching world could test at close range. They did not ask to be trusted. They became, slowly and at great cost, people it was hard not to trust.

## A true message in discredited hands

The distinction this whole question turns on is between credibility and truth. Aristotle observed in the *Rhetoric*, written in the fourth century before Christ, that a speaker persuades partly by argument, partly by the feelings he stirs, and partly by the character his speech displays, which he called *ethos*, and that character comes close to being the most powerful of the three. Aristotle had the speech itself in view, but the point reaches further. Credibility is a fact about the relationship between speaker and hearer: whether the hearer has reason to believe this person. Truth is a fact about the claim. A liar can say something true, and an honest man can be mistaken. When the two come apart, both the skeptic and the church are tempted to make an error.

The skeptic's temptation is to treat the messenger's failure as a refutation of the message. It isn't, and the thoughtful skeptic knows it. A fraudulent doctor does not disprove medicine. But the skeptic is right about something more modest and more important: he is entitled to weigh the messengers. When someone tells you something you cannot check for yourself, his character is most of your evidence, and a church that asks to be believed about the unseen while lying about the seen has given the listener every reason to stop listening. The philosopher Onora O'Neill, in her BBC Reith Lectures of 2002, published as *A Question of Trust*, argued that the fashionable cures for distrust, more transparency, more audits, more carefully managed communication, rarely restore it. People need a way to place their trust intelligently, which means an institution's real task is to become trustworthy rather than to demand more trust. The skeptic who withholds his trust from the church is doing exactly what she says a sensible person should do.

The church's temptation runs the other way, and it is more dangerous because it can quote Scripture. Jesus himself separated the truth of a teaching from the credibility of its teachers. Of the scribes and Pharisees who sat in Moses' seat he told the crowds, "so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice" (Matthew 23:3). The Word of God does not become false because its preachers are hypocrites; that is Augustine's point, and it is true. But read the rest of Matthew 23. The chapter that tells the crowds to keep what the Pharisees taught is the fiercest indictment of religious leaders in the Gospels. Jesus says the message stands, and then he pronounces woe after woe on the men who made it hard to hear. The truth of the message is never an excuse for the messenger. It is the ground of his judgment.

Paul holds the two together in a single image. Defending his ministry to a suspicious church in Corinth, he wrote, "But we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Corinthians 4:7). The treasure is the gospel of the glory of Christ, and the jar is the frail human messenger, as ordinary as household pottery. The image refuses both errors. The container cannot be what persuades anyone; the power is God's. But it does not follow that the jar may be filthy. A few verses earlier Paul describes how he has tried to carry the treasure: "But we have renounced disgraceful, underhanded ways. We refuse to practice cunning or to tamper with God's word, but by the open statement of the truth we would commend ourselves to everyone's conscience in the sight of God" (2 Corinthians 4:2). He had no reputation to protect. He had a conscience, and the conscience of every hearer, and he placed himself before both. The jar is not the message. But a jar that has held something rotten will make people refuse to drink from it, and they will not be wrong to refuse.

The sharpest sentence on this subject is one Paul borrowed from the prophets. "For, as it is written, 'The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you'" (Romans 2:24). The line comes from Isaiah and echoes Ezekiel, and its setting is exile. Israel had been scattered for its unfaithfulness, and the nations, seeing a defeated people, concluded that their God was weak. Ezekiel records the Lord's answer: "Therefore say to the house of Israel, Thus says the Lord GOD: It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for the sake of my holy name, which you have profaned among the nations to which you came" (Ezekiel 36:22). Notice who is blamed: not the nations for mocking, but God's own people for giving the mockery its material. Paul turns the text on those who carried the law and took pride in it. When outsiders speak with contempt of Christ because of what they have watched Christians do, Scripture does not tell us to resent the outsiders. Judgment, Peter wrote, begins "at the household of God" (1 Peter 4:17).

So the gospel's truth does not depend on our credibility. Its hearing does. God is able to speak through broken jars, and he has done it in every century. But we are not permitted to presume on that. A church that says "the message is true whatever we do" has quietly stopped believing the message, because the message is about a God who makes his people holy.

## Reputation management and repentance

Every institution that loses trust reaches first for the same tools. It hires communications consultants, issues a statement regretting that anyone was hurt, announces a review conducted by people it chose, and finds a younger face for the platform. Some of this is harmless and some is necessary. Together it amounts to reputation management, and reputation management is the opposite of what Scripture calls repentance, even when it borrows repentance's words.

The Bible draws the line in two kings who said the same sentence. When Samuel confronted Saul for disobeying the Lord's command, Saul admitted it, and then asked for something: "I have sinned; yet honor me now before the elders of my people and before Israel, and return with me, that I may bow before the LORD your God" (1 Samuel 15:30). Even while confessing, Saul's concern was how it would look. That is reputation management at prayer. When Nathan confronted David over Bathsheba and Uriah, the words were almost the same and utterly different: "David said to Nathan, 'I have sinned against the LORD.'" (2 Samuel 12:13). He asked for nothing. The psalm the church has long associated with that moment goes further: "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight, so that you may be justified in your words and blameless in your judgment" (Psalm 51:4). David wanted God to be proved right, even at his own expense. Saul wanted to be seen as right, even at God's.

Paul gives the distinction a name. "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Worldly grief is sorrow at the consequences: at being caught, at the lawsuit, at the donors leaving. It changes nothing, because what it grieves is lost standing. Godly grief grieves the wrong itself and the people it harmed, and so it will lose standing if telling the truth requires it. You can tell which one an institution feels by what it is willing to lose.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw the same counterfeit in the German church of the 1930s. In *Discipleship* (1937) he called it cheap grace: forgiveness preached without repentance, absolution without confession, grace held as a principle rather than received as the costly call of Christ. An institution that apologizes in general terms while protecting the people who did the harm is practicing cheap grace on itself. Bonhoeffer's own life shows the other thing. In the summer of 1939 he was safe in New York, where friends had arranged work for him. Within weeks he sailed back to Germany, convinced he could have no part in rebuilding the church after the war if he did not share its trials during it. He was hanged at Flossenbürg in April 1945. What gave his theology its weight was that he paid what it cost.

Repentance, when it is real, has a recognizable shape. When Zacchaeus met Jesus, he did not issue a statement. "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8). He named the wrong, repaid it with interest, and did it where the people he had cheated could see. For a church, that means telling the whole truth without defensive qualification, not "mistakes were made" but "we did this." It means handing investigations of abuse to people it does not control and publishing what they find, reporting crimes to the police, and paying for the care of those it harmed without making that help conditional on their silence. It means opening the books. It means rebuking its own political allies as readily as its opponents, with the people in the room. And it means accepting that trust will come back slowly, if at all, and that the church has no right to set the timetable.

Most congregations will never face a national scandal. What they face is the smaller gap between preaching and practice, closed in small ways over long years: a church present in its neighborhood, materially and consistently, long before it asks the neighborhood inside; a pastor whose household would tell you he is the same man on Monday as on Sunday; a congregation that treats the outsider's objections as worth answering. Lesslie Newbigin argued in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) that the only interpretation of the gospel a skeptical culture can finally read is a congregation that believes it and lives by it, an argument taken up in [The End of Home-Field Advantage](/writing/the-end-of-home-field-advantage). Credibility of that kind cannot be manufactured, because it takes too long to produce and costs too much to fake. That is why it is believed when it appears.

## What the church carries now

A word first to the reader who does not believe and is reading this with arms folded. You were right to notice. The file you keep on the church is not paranoia, and most of it was written by us. Nothing here is meant to talk you out of what you saw; if anything, it asks you to hold the church to the standard it announced, which is higher than the one you would have set. It asks only that you keep one distinction in view: the failure of the messengers is a real reason to distrust the messengers, and it is not yet a verdict on the one they were sent to speak about. You are not wrong to want them to earn the hearing first.

To the church, and to pastors especially, the word is harder. Many of us have inherited a debt we did not personally incur. A pastor in a small town did not move abusers from parish to parish in Boston or sell the lifetime memberships. But he speaks from inside the institution those things discredited, and the people in his town cannot know, until they have watched him for years, whether he is different. He cannot pay that debt with a statement. He can only pay it the way it has always been paid, one week at a time, before people watching to see whether he means it. And he has to begin by admitting that he is not as far from the file as he would like to think, because the logic of reputation management is available to every church of every size.

There is one more thing to say, and it changes the whole question. The gospel was discredited from the start, and Paul said so plainly: "but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23). The central event of the faith was a public execution, the most shameful death Rome could devise, reserved for slaves and rebels. By every measure of ethos Aristotle would have recognized, the message began with no credibility at all. Its founder died condemned by the religious authorities of his people and the political authority of the empire, abandoned by his friends, mocked by passersby. He lost his standing completely. The difference is that he lost it for sins that were not his. We have lost ours for sins that are.

That is where any recovery has to start, because it is the only place the church has ever found a credibility that lasts. The church is not asked to restore its reputation. It is asked to follow a Lord who gave his up, to tell the truth about itself when the truth costs members and money and friends in high places, and to be present at cost among people who owe it nothing. The Christians who nursed their neighbors through the plague did not know whether anyone would believe them afterward. They did it anyway, because they believed a crucified man had been raised, and a people who believe that can afford to lose everything except him.

Whether trust will return is not ours to decide, and it should not be. A church that repents in order to be trusted again has not yet repented. The treasure is still in the jars, and the power still belongs to God. What remains with us is the question the watching world is right to keep asking, which no statement will ever answer for us: whether the people who carry the name of Christ in this country are willing to become people whose lives make the name believable, and to begin while no one is yet inclined to believe us.
