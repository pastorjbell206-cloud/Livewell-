Start with a person, because the argument can wait. Picture a fourteen-year-old, not any one child but the kind of young person parents and pastors across the country now describe, who can't explain why their own body feels wrong to live in. They only know that the feeling didn't lift when they were told to pray harder, didn't lift when they were told it was a phase, and has been there so long they can't remember life without it. Then picture a mother in a church parking lot with the engine off, not going in yet. She loves her child and she loves her church, and she has started to fear that she can't keep hold of both, and that whichever one she lets go of, she'll lose. The country argues about this in statehouses; the cost gets paid in that car.

So the first thing has to be said plainly, and it ought to cost something for readers on my own side to hear it. The distress is real. Gender dysphoria, the persistent and often consuming anguish of being at odds with one's own sexed body, is recognized in clinical medicine, and for most of the people who carry it, it was never chosen. A church that waves it away because the subject is uncomfortable has put its comfort ahead of a suffering person. I'm not a physician, and nothing in this essay is medical advice. But any pastor who has sat with people in real despair learns the difference between someone performing a crisis and someone going under, and the first thing the church owes a person going under is to stop arguing about the water and get in.

We haven't done that. Much of the time we've done the reverse. We took one of the smallest and most frightened populations in the country and turned it into a plank in a platform, a thing to be against, a line in a fundraising letter. That has to be named as sin before anything else is said, and I have to name it as mine. I've been in rooms where a transgender person was discussed the way you'd discuss a policy, and I didn't always stop it, and my silence was a kind of consent. If a suffering person can't walk through a church door without becoming somebody's political point, we've failed them before the doctrine of the body ever gets a hearing.

What follows states the affirming case and the historic Christian account of the body, each at full strength, lands with the historic account, and then names what the church owes right now, whichever way a reader lands.

## How the question got here

The ancient world had no modern concept of gender identity, but it knew people whose bodies and social roles didn't fit its categories. Scripture speaks of eunuchs, some castrated for royal service and some born that way, and both sides of this debate read those passages closely. The modern question begins elsewhere. In 1919 the physician Magnus Hirschfeld opened the Institute for Sexual Science in Berlin, where some of the earliest surgical sex reassignments were attempted; in 1933 the Nazis ransacked it and burned its library. In December 1952 Christine Jorgensen, a former American soldier, made front pages after surgery in Copenhagen. The endocrinologist Harry Benjamin gave the condition its mid-century clinical shape in *The Transsexual Phenomenon* (1966), the same year Johns Hopkins opened the first American university clinic to offer surgery. Hopkins closed that program in 1979 amid a dispute over whether surgery had actually improved patients' lives, and that dispute has never quite ended.

In 2013 the American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5 replaced "gender identity disorder" with "gender dysphoria," moving the clinical focus from the identity to the distress that can accompany it, and the World Health Organization's ICD-11, adopted in 2019, moved "gender incongruence" out of its chapter on mental disorders altogether. Out of that history come distinctions honest conversation can't do without. Sex names the body as it develops. Gender identity names a person's inner sense of being a man or a woman, or neither. Dysphoria names the suffering when the two are at war. Intersex conditions, or differences of sex development, in which the body itself develops atypically, are a separate matter again. A child can suffer dysphoria without claiming a new identity, and a person with an intersex condition may have no dysphoria at all.

Beneath the clinical history runs a longer one about the self. In *Habits of the Heart* (1985), Robert Bellah and his colleagues described expressive individualism, the conviction that each person carries a unique core of feeling and intuition and becomes truly an individual only by expressing it. Carl Trueman, a traditional Protestant historian, opens *The Rise and Triumph of the Modern Self* (2020) with his curiosity about a single sentence, "I am a woman trapped in a man's body," and why so many people now take it to be meaningful. He traces the answer through Rousseau, the Romantics, Freud and the thinkers who followed them to a self understood first as a psychological interior and only second as a body. It's a serious argument, but it explains the readiness of a culture, not the pain of a person. Nobody's dysphoria was caused by reading Rousseau, and a teenager who has felt this way since early childhood isn't a philosophical trend.

The last turn was political, and it's the one the church bears most responsibility for. The 2010s brought a sharp rise in referrals to youth gender clinics in Britain and elsewhere, with a marked shift from mostly younger children registered male at birth toward mostly adolescents registered female at birth, a change that still isn't well understood. At almost the same moment, after the Supreme Court's 2015 decision on same-sex marriage, the energy of the American culture war needed somewhere to go, and it came here. North Carolina's bathroom bill of 2016, and the waves of legislation that followed on both sides, turned a small population of suffering people into a front line. Some of it reflected sincere concern for children, and some of it sincere concern for a despised minority. But the fundraising letters didn't need the people to be real. They needed them to be a threat or a cause. We learned to talk about transgender people the way we talk about a tax rate, and many of us never noticed we'd done it.

## The affirming case, as its holders would state it

Before any verdict, the affirming Christian case deserves the form its most serious holders would sign.

It begins with Jesus and the person in front of him. A woman who had been bleeding for twelve years pushed through a crowd to touch his clothes, though the law counted her unclean (Mark 5:25–34). He didn't recoil. He stopped, looked for her, called her "daughter," and sent her home in peace. The Gospels keep finding him on the wrong side of respectability, and the people he confronted hardest were never the suffering. They were the ones who used holiness as a wall. The affirming Christian asks the church to begin where Jesus began, with the particular person in pain rather than the rule about a category.

Then it asks the church to take seriously what many transgender people report. A person whose distress has been crushing finds that it eases, that sleep returns and the will to live comes back, when they're allowed to live as the man or woman they experience themselves to be. That isn't rebellion. It's relief, and many adults who have transitioned say plainly that it saved their lives. To refuse that relief in the name of a principle, when the alternative might be a person who doesn't survive their own body, looks to the affirming Christian like loving an abstraction more than a neighbor.

It reads Scripture carefully, too. Austen Hartke, a transgender Christian writer, argues in *Transforming: The Bible and the Lives of Transgender Christians* (2018) that the Bible's nearest kin to transgender people are its eunuchs, whose bodies and social roles fell outside the expected pattern of husband and wife. The law of Deuteronomy barred a man with damaged genitals from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:1). Yet Isaiah promises the eunuchs who hold fast to the covenant a monument and a name in God's house, better than sons and daughters (Isaiah 56:3–5). And when Jesus speaks about marriage, he names a category the reader might have forgotten.

> For there are eunuchs who have been so from birth, and there are eunuchs who have been made eunuchs by men, and there are eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven. Let the one who is able to receive this receive it. (Matthew 19:12, ESV)

In Acts 8, among the first people outside Judea and Samaria whose conversion the book describes is an Ethiopian eunuch, a high official of a foreign queen, riding home from worship in Jerusalem, where the law would have kept him at a distance. He is reading Isaiah. When Philip explains it, the eunuch sees water by the road and asks the question the whole passage has been building toward.

> And as they were going along the road they came to some water, and the eunuch said, "See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" (Acts 8:36, ESV)

Nothing does. For the affirming reader, that is the direction the canon moves, from a body that excluded to a body that is welcomed, and Paul's sentence to the Galatians seems to seal it.

> There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus. (Galatians 3:28, ESV)

At their most serious, affirming Christians aren't trying to abolish male and female, and many of them confess the creeds without reservation. They're trying to keep a child breathing and to make the church a place where a transgender person can worship without hiding. And they put to the church a question it has been very slow to answer honestly: what is your certainty worth if it's paid for with their lives? That question deserves to land, and this essay won't pretend it away.

## The historic conviction, as its holders would state it

The historic Christian conviction deserves the same refusal to caricature, because it isn't what its critics say it is either. At its best it is neither disgust with a theological vocabulary nor a project of control. It is a claim about what a human being is, and it begins in Genesis.

> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them. (Genesis 1:27, ESV)

The historic reading hears something load-bearing there. Being made male and female isn't an accident for us to correct, or a social arrangement layered over neutral flesh. It's part of what God spoke over the world before anything went wrong, and he called it good with the rest. Genesis 2 says it from the ground up.

> then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature. (Genesis 2:7, ESV)

Dust and breath together make a living creature. On this account the body isn't a costume the real self wears. It's part of you, given rather than chosen, and the givenness is a mercy, not a prison. The Anglican moral theologian Oliver O'Donovan, taking up the question of marriage in *Transsexualism and Christian Marriage* (1982), argued that a person's sex belongs to what is given with the body, received rather than decided, so that surgery can change how a body appears without changing the given itself. His argument concerned what the body is, not anyone's guilt for suffering in it.

Then comes the fall, and here the historic view is more tender than it's usually given credit for. It doesn't say the anguish of dysphoria is fake. It says the anguish is real and belongs to the same wrongness that runs through the whole creation since Genesis 3, the wrongness that puts cancer in healthy cells and grief inside good love.

> And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies. (Romans 8:23, ESV)

Paul expects the body's redemption, not release from it. The answer to a body at war with itself, on this view, is not finally to remake the body to fit the mind, because the mind is no more infallible than the body; both are fallen, and both are waiting. Augustine, in the twenty-second book of *The City of God*, asked whether women would rise as women, and argued that they would, since a woman's sex is not a defect but part of the nature God made. Resurrection doesn't erase the body. It heals and raises it, and Christ "will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body, by the power that enables him even to subject all things to himself" (Philippians 3:21, ESV).

The historic view reads the eunuch passages in context, and just as seriously. In Matthew 19 the saying about eunuchs closes a conversation about divorce in which Jesus has just grounded marriage in Genesis: "Have you not read that he who created them from the beginning made them male and female" (Matthew 19:4, ESV). The eunuchs he names are people who won't marry, and he honors them as they are, without renaming them. The Ethiopian in Acts 8 is baptized as a eunuch, not reclassified. And when Paul says there is "no male and female" in Christ, he's arguing about who inherits the promise, in wording that echoes Genesis 1:27. Nobody's sex adds to or subtracts from their standing in Christ. But Paul goes on addressing men and women as men and women, which suggests the distinction lost its power to rank people without being dissolved.

There's a second thing in this conviction that the culture war never lets anyone hear. A person's worth has nothing to do with how well they perform the local script for masculinity or femininity. The gentle boy who hates what boys are supposed to love isn't less a man, and the church that told him so was preaching a culture, not Christ. Much of what a dysphoric teenager carries was loaded on by a world, and sometimes a church, that allowed one narrow way to be a boy or a girl. Held rightly, the historic view doesn't add to that load. It lifts it.

> I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Wonderful are your works; my soul knows it very well. (Psalm 139:14, ESV)

The body you have is not a mistake you must escape in order to be loved. That's meant to be good news, and too often we've preached it as a threat.

## What medicine knows, and what it doesn't yet

Nothing that follows is medical advice, and decisions about anyone's care belong with that person, their family, and clinicians who can examine them. What an essay can offer is an honest account of where the evidence stands, and the honest account is that serious bodies of medical opinion disagree.

On one side stand the professional bodies that shaped American practice. The World Professional Association for Transgender Health published version 8 of its Standards of Care in 2022, treating gender-affirming medical care as medically necessary for many transgender people and supporting access for some adolescents after thorough assessment. The American Academy of Pediatrics set out its support for a gender-affirming model of care in 2018 and reaffirmed it in 2023 while commissioning a systematic review of the evidence, and the Endocrine Society and the American Medical Association have also supported access. The clinicians who hold this position aren't reckless. Many have watched young people in acute distress and believe that withholding treatment carries grave risks of its own.

On the other side, several European health systems that were early to offer this care have grown markedly more cautious about it for minors. In 2020 Finland's Council for Choices in Health Care made psychosocial support the first response for young people with gender dysphoria. In 2022 Sweden's National Board of Health and Welfare concluded that for adolescents the risks of puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones currently outweighed the likely benefits. In April 2024 the independent review led by the pediatrician Hilary Cass for England's health service published its final report; drawing on systematic reviews from the University of York, it found the evidence base for puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones in young people to be of low quality, with little good evidence on long-term outcomes, and urged a far broader assessment of each young person's needs. Cass was explicit that her report did not try to define what it means to be transgender or to question anyone's identity. Critics answered, among them a group of researchers, several of them at Yale, who argued that the review held this field to a stricter standard of evidence than comparable pediatric care. In June 2025 the United States Supreme Court, in *United States v. Skrmetti*, upheld Tennessee's restrictions on this care for minors, deciding a constitutional question rather than a medical one.

The evidence on long-term outcomes, on mental health and on regret, is contested, and anyone who hands a frightened family one confident number in either direction is claiming more than the research can bear. Many adults who have transitioned say it was the right choice; others who later stopped or reversed a transition say it was not. Both deserve to be heard as people, not deployed as props.

Mark Yarhouse, a Christian psychologist, offers in *Understanding Gender Dysphoria* (2015) a distinction that has helped many churches. He describes three lenses. The integrity lens treats the difference between male and female as sacred, woven into creation, so that cross-sex identification violates something God made. The disability lens treats dysphoria as a nonmoral effect of a fallen world, to be met with compassion like any other suffering of body or mind rather than as a sin to be repented of. The diversity lens treats transgender identity as something to be recognized and celebrated, an identity and a community. Yarhouse writes from within the traditional view, and he argues that the church needs what is true in all three: the integrity lens's conviction about creation, the disability lens's refusal to moralize suffering, and the diversity lens's insistence that people need a place to belong. A church that holds only the first has doctrine without mercy.

## First things, second things, and two shortcuts

The church sorts its questions badly, and on this subject bad sorting costs lives. There is a first-order center that the creeds do bind: the human being is God's good creation, made in his image, body and soul together, fallen in every part, and destined for resurrection, and the Son of God took a human body, rose in it, and ascended in it. But the pastoral and medical particulars, what compassion requires in a given life, at a given age, with a given history, are hard and genuinely disputed among people who share every word of that faith. Christians who agree that the body is a good gift still disagree, sometimes sharply, about what faithfulness looks like across a desk from a fifteen-year-old in agony. Whoever tells you that part is simple is offering the comfort of a tribe, and it doesn't matter which tribe.

So both shortcuts have to be refused with the same hand. The church's shortcut is cruelty dressed as conviction: the joke from the pulpit at the expense of people the preacher has never sat with, the youth group where the effeminate boy learns he's a punch line. It turns a doctrine of the body's goodness into a weapon against particular bodies, and you can't preach that a person is fearfully and wonderfully made and treat them as a joke in the same breath. My own side produced that sneer, often excused it, and called the excusing courage. I have to own that.

The culture's shortcut is the pretense that none of this is hard: the confidence that every question is settled, that there is no cost or grief anywhere, and that the only possible reason for hesitation is hatred. That isn't true, and it isn't kind, because it hands a frightened family a certainty the research and the long human record don't yet support, with the same untroubled ease the church shows on the other side. The European turns toward caution weren't the work of preachers; they came from health authorities reviewing the evidence.

The two shortcuts look like opposites, but they share a grammar. Both spare the speaker the weight of the question and hand it to the suffering person. Both need the transgender teenager to be a symbol, of decline or of liberation, rather than a fourteen-year-old who can't sleep. And both let us, the people doing the talking, stay at a safe distance, confident about someone else's body and untroubled in our own.

## Where this essay lands, and what the church owes

No one should have to guess where this essay stands. It holds the historic Christian reading, and so do I. The body is not a cage or a costume the real self wears. It is part of the given self, and being made male or female is a created good, not a mistake to be corrected. The deepest healing a suffering person is finally promised is not the remaking of the body to match the mind, since the mind is no more infallible than the body; it is the resurrection, when the whole self is made whole. That makes me genuinely cautious about irreversible medical paths, most of all for the young, and I won't soften that to sound kinder than I am.

What that does not mean matters just as much, because this is where cruelty likes to hide. It doesn't mean ruling on your child's medicine from a website. I don't know your child, their body, their history or the edge they're standing on, and a man who issues a verdict without knowing those things isn't being brave; he's being reckless with a life that isn't his. Those decisions belong to the person living them, the family that loves them, doctors who can examine them, and a pastor who has earned a place in the room by staying in it. It doesn't mean the distress is invented or chosen. And it doesn't mean the church may say its piece before it has repented of its contempt.

There's also an objection this essay hasn't answered, and it's the heaviest. I don't have a clean, safe, obvious pastoral path for the fifteen-year-old in genuine agony, and anyone who claims one, in either direction, is selling the comfort of certainty in place of the care of a person. The affirming Christian's question, what certainty is worth if it's paid for with a life, still stands over this essay. I hold the historic teaching, and I mean to hold it kneeling next to the people it costs, not standing over them.

Underneath what can't yet be resolved, some debts are plain. The church owes presence before position: a transgender person, or a parent in that parking lot, ought to be known by name and face long before they're known by this, and loved in a way that doesn't depend on the argument coming out as we'd prefer. It owes honesty about its failures, the boy we shamed for being gentle and the family we let walk out alone. It owes a refusal to lie in either direction, neither pretending the questions cost nothing nor pretending a hurting person is a threat. Parents thinking about how to talk with their own children about the body and identity will find that ground covered in [How Christian Parents Should Talk to Kids About Sex and Gender](/writing/parenting-talking-about-sex-and-identity).

And it owes a path to real help, kept always in view: a pastor who will stay, a counselor trained for this weight, and medical care from people who actually know you. If you've reached the place where you aren't sure you want to be here at all, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, tonight. If you're an LGBTQ young person, The Trevor Project answers at 1-866-488-7386, any hour. If you're in immediate danger, call 911. Reaching for help is one of the most faithful things a person can do, and for some reader this paragraph is the only one that matters.

Go back, last of all, to the road in Acts 8. The Ethiopian official was riding home from a temple whose assembly the law had closed to him, reading Isaiah's song of a suffering servant. A few lines before the place where he was reading, the same song says this.

> He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief; and as one from whom men hide their faces he was despised, and we esteemed him not. (Isaiah 53:3, ESV)

A man whose body had put him outside the assembly found himself reading about a man everyone had turned away from, and asked who it was.

> Then Philip opened his mouth, and beginning with this Scripture he told him the good news about Jesus. (Acts 8:35, ESV)

Christians will keep disagreeing, in good faith, about what that road means for the questions in this essay. But they can agree about the one the passage was describing. When his disciples saw a man born blind and asked whose sin had caused it, he refused to let suffering become a verdict.

> Jesus answered, "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him." (John 9:3, ESV)

He didn't hold a debate about the man. He went to him. Every Christian, whatever they conclude about the body, is waiting for the redemption of our bodies, and none of us gets there by standing over anyone else. So if you're the teenager who can't bear your own skin, hear at least this: you aren't a category, you aren't a mistake, and you aren't as alone in the dark as it feels. And if you're the mother still sitting in the car, go in, not because the church has figured this out, but because you shouldn't have to carry your child alone. That puts the weight on the rest of us, the ones already inside. Whether she finds a place worth coming into depends on what she meets at the door.
