A woman stands at a kitchen sink at six in the morning, washing the dishes she washed last night, and somewhere under the steam she wonders whether any of this counts. She has simply absorbed, the way we all absorb the air, a quiet verdict that the real work of God happens somewhere else, in a pulpit or on a mission field, and that what she does with her hands is the part of life you get through so the meaningful part can begin. Men carry the same suspicion into the cab of a truck or a cubicle they can't wait to leave. Forty hours a week, fifty weeks a year, for forty years, comes to eighty thousand hours. The question under the dread is whether those hours carry any weight before God, or whether they're simply the price of being alive.

The church's word for this is vocation, and we have nearly ruined it. In common speech a vocation has become a career you happen to enjoy, which sounds generous and is actually cruel, because it tells the woman at the sink that she has no vocation at all. Robert Bellah and his colleagues heard the thinning in *Habits of the Heart* (1985). They distinguished three ways Americans hold their work: as a job, a way of making money; as a career, a ladder of achievement; and as a calling, work morally inseparable from a person's life that ties him to a community whose good it serves. Their worry was that the third had grown faint: the people they interviewed could explain their work readily as income or advancement and found it far harder to explain as a calling. We kept the work and lost the reason for it.

The Christian doctrine of vocation is both higher and humbler than the career religion that replaced it. It's higher because Scripture puts work in the garden before the fall and treats ordinary labor as a way God loves the world. It's humbler because the same Scripture refuses to let work tell you who you are or make you right with God. Everything turns on a distinction our culture has collapsed, between work as a calling and work as an identity or a salvation. A calling is received from Someone and offered for someone. An identity built out of work has to be earned again every morning, and it's never finally earned. Your work matters. It was never going to save you.

## Work Before the Curse

The oldest thing the Bible says about work comes before anything has gone wrong. God is introduced as a worker, speaking, separating, naming and making, and "on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done" (Genesis 2:2). The creature made in his image is handed a share of the labor: "Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it" (Genesis 1:28). The world was made good and unfinished, and human beings were made to carry it further. Then Genesis narrows to a garden: "The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it" (Genesis 2:15). There's no sweat yet and no thorn, and there's already a job.

The two verbs mean to work or serve and to keep or guard, and the same pair later describes the Levites' service at the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7–8). Many commentators conclude that Eden is pictured as a first sanctuary with the first human as its keeper. If so, the line we draw between sacred service and secular work didn't exist in the beginning; tending the ground and serving God were one act described twice. Jesus assumed that continuity when critics objected to his healing on the Sabbath: "My Father is working until now, and I am working" (John 5:17).

That changes what the fall did. The curse in Genesis 3 falls on neither work nor the man but on the ground: "cursed is the ground because of you; in pain you shall eat of it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you... By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:17–19). Something already good was frustrated, and the last line is the hardest: at the end of all the sweat, dust. The job that exhausts you is good work under the conditions of a broken world. Redemption doesn't rescue us from work. It rescues work.

No book feels that last line more than Ecclesiastes, and its second chapter reads like the memoir of a king trying to build his way back into Eden. He planted vineyards and made himself "gardens and parks" (Ecclesiastes 2:5), and the Hebrew word for parks is a Persian loanword that reached English, through Greek, as *paradise*. It failed him: "Then I considered all that my hands had done and the toil I had expended in doing it, and behold, all was vanity and a striving after wind, and there was nothing to be gained under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). Worse, he can't keep it: "I hated all my toil in which I toil under the sun, seeing that I must leave it to the man who will come after me" (Ecclesiastes 2:18). And then a sentence that could hang over every sleepless professional in America: "Even in the night his heart does not rest. This also is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 2:23).

Yet the Preacher doesn't end in contempt. "There is nothing better for a person than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil. This also, I saw, is from the hand of God" (Ecclesiastes 2:24). Enjoyment is possible only as a gift received, never as a yield extracted. So the oldest theology of work in the world holds three things together. The work is good, because God gave it before the fall. The work is broken, because it pushes back and ends in the grave. And nowhere is the work offered as what justifies the worker. The one economy in the Bible built on the opposite creed was Egypt, where a man was his brick count and Pharaoh heard a request to worship as sloth: "You are idle, you are idle" (Exodus 5:17). God brought his people out of it by force. Its theology of the quota didn't drown in the Red Sea, though. Most of us commute to it.

## The Ladder Luther Broke

For centuries that unified picture was divided in two. Early in the fourth century Eusebius of Caesarea described, in his *Demonstration of the Gospel*, two ways of life: one above nature, given wholly to God without marriage or property, and a humbler one that let a Christian marry, farm and trade while keeping a secondary grade of piety. Later, Thomas Aquinas, reading Mary and Martha in Luke 10 as types of the two lives, judged the contemplative life, in itself, more excellent than the active (*Summa Theologiae*, II-II, q. 182). Upstairs lived the monks, nuns and priests, the people with a *vocatio*. Downstairs lived the farmer, the cobbler and the mother.

The monastic tradition deserves better than the caricature Protestants usually give it. The sixth-century *Rule of Saint Benedict* calls idleness the enemy of the soul and orders the brothers' days around prayer and manual labor together. The defenders of the two lives weren't despising work. They were insisting that one thing is necessary, and a life given wholly to prayer still testifies to that. But the ranking taught ordinary Christians that the second floor was the nearest they could come to God, and that their daily labor was, at best, spiritually neutral.

Martin Luther took an axe to the staircase in 1520. In *To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation* he called the division of Christians into a spiritual estate and a temporal estate an invention. Every baptized Christian belongs to the spiritual estate, he argued, and Christians differ only in office: the cobbler, the smith and the farmer each has the work of his trade, and each is a consecrated priest. Later that year, in *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church*, he went further. The works of monks and priests, however holy and arduous, don't differ in God's sight from the work of a farmer in his field or a woman keeping her house, because all works are measured before God by faith alone.

His Bible translation carried the conviction into the language. Paul told the Corinthians, "Each one should remain in the condition in which he was called" (1 Corinthians 7:20), and through Luther's German Bible the word *Beruf* came to mean both an occupation and a summons from God. Max Weber later argued that this sense of the word was new, a product of the Reformation's Bible translations. Whatever the linguists decide, the theology was plain. Vocation isn't a rank you reach by leaving the world. It's the place in the world where God has put you, and the neighbor he has put in front of you.

That is what separates vocation from self-expression. In the *Small Catechism* (1529) Luther explained the petition for daily bread by listing what it covers: food and drink, house and home, a faithful spouse, good government, good weather, good neighbors. You pray for bread, and God feeds you through a farmer, a miller, a trucker and a cashier, most of whom have no idea they're part of an answered prayer. In his exposition of Psalm 147 (1532) Luther called such ordinary callings the masks of God, behind which he stays hidden while he gives his gifts. Gustaf Wingren's *Luther on Vocation* (1942) gave the teaching its classic modern form, and the tradition has attached to Luther memorable images of it, God milking the cows through the milkmaid, the cobbler serving God by making a good shoe rather than by stitching crosses onto it. They're better treated as the tradition's summaries than as quotations, but they're faithful to him.

The New Testament had said as much, and said it to the people with the least reason to believe it. Paul's word on work in Colossians is addressed to slaves: "Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men, knowing that from the Lord you will receive the inheritance as your reward. You are serving the Lord Christ" (Colossians 3:23–24). In Roman law a slave owned nothing in his own right, and Paul promises him the inheritance. A few lines later he turns to the owners: "Masters, treat your bondservants justly and fairly, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). To a church in Thessalonica where some seem to have quit working and leaned on others, he wrote, directly after praising their love for one another (1 Thessalonians 4:9–10), that they should "aspire to live quietly, and to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands, as we instructed you, so that you may walk properly before outsiders and be dependent on no one" (1 Thessalonians 4:11–12). Quiet, competent work was a form of that love, since it spared others a burden, and a witness to the watching city, from a tentmaker (Acts 18:3) who served a Lord his neighbors knew first as "the carpenter" (Mark 6:3).

## How Calling Became Career

The English Puritans gave Luther's recovery a system. William Perkins, the Cambridge theologian who died in 1602, left behind *A Treatise of the Vocations or Callings of Men*, published soon after. He defined a calling as a kind of life God appoints for the common good, and drew a distinction that is still the best tool we have. Every Christian has a general calling, to be a Christian at all, and a particular calling, the specific work God gives him to do. The particular must always sit inside the general and answer to it. His measure of a calling was never its profit. It was whether the work served the common good and was done in faith.

That tradition is what Max Weber had in view in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905). Weber argued that the Puritan calling produced the habits modern capitalism needed: steady labor, reinvestment instead of luxury, time treated as something you answer to God for. He found the spirit already secularized in Benjamin Franklin's advice to a young tradesman that time is money. His darker conclusion was that the calling outlived the One who called. The discipline hardened into what Talcott Parsons's 1930 translation called an iron cage, and near the close of the book Weber pictured its inhabitants as "specialists without spirit, sensualists without heart."

His causal story has been argued over for a century. R. H. Tawney, in *Religion and the Rise of Capitalism* (1926), accepted part of it but insisted the influence ran both ways. Others pointed out that capitalism predated the Reformation: the Medici were banking in Catholic Florence a century before Luther, and the Catholic Fugger house helped finance the very indulgence campaign that provoked the Ninety-Five Theses. On the history, Weber may have overreached. On the diagnosis, he saw clearly. The seriousness Puritans brought to work because God had called them survived the loss of the One who called, and a seriousness with no one to answer to has nowhere to go but back into the work.

You can hear the decline in two words. Vocation comes from the Latin *vocare*, to call; it assumes a voice, and a voice assumes someone calling. Career came into English from a French word for a racecourse; it assumes a track, and a track assumes rivals and a finish line. The question vocation existed to answer (whom does my work serve?) was exchanged for the only question a career knows how to ask: what does my work make of me? In 2019 Derek Thompson named the result in *The Atlantic*: workism, the belief that work is the center of a person's identity and purpose, which he argued had become a kind of religion that fails to deliver what it promises. It keeps all of a religion's demands and offers none of its absolution.

Burnout is what that religion does to its devout. The psychologist Herbert Freudenberger gave the word its clinical currency in 1974, describing what he saw in himself and the idealistic volunteers of a free clinic in New York. The first to burn out were the people who believed most in the work. Byung-Chul Han argued in *The Burnout Society* (2010) that the modern worker no longer needs an overseer. In what Han calls the achievement society, the worker drives himself, freely and without limit, so that exploiter and exploited are the same person. Egypt's quota has moved inside. (This isn't medical advice. If exhaustion has turned into despair, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.)

The right and the left keep their own liturgies for this. One preaches hustle and, in its prosperity-gospel form, promises that God rewards output. The other preaches "do what you love," the career as the chief site of self-realization, and quietly consigns everyone whose job can't be loved to a life without meaning. For all their quarrels they agree on the one thing that matters: in both, work is asked to tell you who you are. I haven't escaped that agreement either. I'm a pastor, which is supposed to be immune to this, and it isn't. I have measured myself by the size of the room, the count in the seats and the reach of the name, which is only the career disease in a clerical collar. I have told a congregation that their worth is not their work, then gone home and priced my own by mine.

## Your Work Was Never Going to Save You

The gospel refuses to reverse one order. "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:8–10). The passage doesn't say work is beneath you now. It says you are the workmanship before you are ever the worker, and that the good works were prepared for you like a road, not demanded from you like a ransom. The gospel leaves the labor in place and moves it out of the courtroom, where you were working toward a verdict, into the field, where the verdict has already come in.

That is the distinction our culture has collapsed, and it has three terms. Work as a calling is received from God and offered to the neighbor, and it asks what the neighbor needs. Work as an identity asks the job to tell you who you are. Work as a salvation asks the job to justify your existence. The second and third are counterfeits of the first, and they deceive because they resemble it. The difference is which way the work faces. A calling faces outward, toward the neighbor, and it can rest because someone else holds the verdict. An identity or a salvation faces inward, and it can never rest, because the verdict is always pending.

A summary of Luther often credited to Wingren puts the logic in a sentence: God doesn't need our good works, but our neighbor does. Once the verdict comes by grace, work no longer has to climb toward God, so it can turn and go where it was needed all along. Timothy Keller, with Katherine Leary Alsdorf in *Every Good Endeavor* (2012), argued that work cut loose from the gospel becomes either an idol, asked for a worth only God can give, or a mere means that pays for the life we actually want. So the workaholic and the idler aren't opposites. Both have agreed the work is about the self.

The wage is where the gospel picks its fight. Jesus told of a vineyard owner who hired laborers at dawn, hired more through the day and a last crew an hour before dark, and paid them all the same. The dawn workers were furious, and every honest reader is furious with them, because by every rule we live under they're right. The owner's answer dismantles the ledger: "Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me? Or do you begrudge my generosity?" (Matthew 20:15). The kingdom runs on gift rather than wages, and gift offends the wage earner in us more deeply than theft would, because theft at least honors the ledger. Grace ignores it.

A hired man works one way, watching the clock, the boss and his own back. A son works another way in a field his father owns. The field and the sweat are the same, and the universe is different. "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Jesus said that to people whose bodies hurt at the end of the day, and the invitation wasn't to stop working. It was to stop carrying. What he lifts is the verdict we strapped to the work, the absolution we keep expecting the next accomplishment to pronounce. It never pronounces it. It only extends the term.

## The Jobless, the Disabled and the Work Nobody Wants

A doctrine of vocation that works only for the employed and the able isn't Christian, so the skeptic deserves a hearing at full strength. Calling, he says, is a comforting story religion tells to dignify drudgery, keeping people content with arrangements that mostly serve someone else. He isn't wrong about the history; a great deal of vocation talk has been a sedative handed to the tired so they won't ask who profits from their tiredness. His best witness is Studs Terkel, who opened *Working* (1974) by saying that a book about work is, by its nature, about violence to the spirit as well as the body. The steelworker who speaks first in the book, Mike LeFevre, wishes a great building like the Empire State carried, from top to bottom, the names of every bricklayer and electrician who raised it. That is the cry of people made for vocation and handed only employment.

Miroslav Volf pressed the objection from inside the church. In *Work in the Spirit* (1991), engaging Marx's account of alienated labor, Volf argued that Luther's doctrine was built for a static society of fixed stations, and that it can ennoble dehumanizing work by telling the man on the line his station is holy and beyond question. He proposed grounding work in the gifts of the Spirit instead, so that alienating work can be named for what it is. On the ESV's reading of a much-debated phrase, Paul grants the point himself. Right after telling the Corinthians to remain where they were called, he adds: "Were you a bondservant when called? Do not be concerned about it. (But if you can gain your freedom, avail yourself of the opportunity.)" (1 Corinthians 7:21).

The answer to the sedative isn't to abandon vocation. It's to recover the half of it the sedative left out. If God loves the neighbor through human hands, an arrangement that crushes the worker violates something holy. "Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:4). John Paul II argued in *Laborem Exercens* (1981) that work draws its dignity first from the person doing it, so that work exists for the human being and never the human being for work. The doctrine that dignifies the laborer is the doctrine that condemns his exploitation. Sometimes the faithful response to a degrading job is to leave it, or to press for its reform, or, if you set other people's hours, to change it. (That's a moral claim, not financial or career advice. Whether and when to leave a job depends on your income, your obligations and your prospects, and it's worth deciding with people who know them.)

Then there are those with no job at all. In 1933 Marie Jahoda, Paul Lazarsfeld and Hans Zeisel published their study of Marienthal, an Austrian village whose textile mill had closed. Where one might have expected revolt, they found resignation; for the men without work, time itself lost its shape. The church has sometimes added shame to that resignation by misusing Paul: "If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" (2 Thessalonians 3:10). The verse concerns people who could work and wouldn't, not the man who has sent out forty applications and heard nothing. And a calling is wider than a paycheck. The garden work of Genesis 2 was unpaid, and so is much of the work that holds the world together: the parent at home, the grown child caring for a failing mother. If you're out of work, your particular calling has been interrupted. Your general calling hasn't, and it doesn't wait on an employer.

And there are people who will never hold a job, because of a body or a mind that can't. A culture that measures worth by output has no place for them, the surest sign it has measured wrongly. Scripture's picture here is a table. When David found Jonathan's son Mephibosheth, who was "lame in both his feet" (2 Samuel 9:13), he didn't hand him work to earn his keep: "So Mephibosheth ate at David's table, like one of the king's sons" (2 Samuel 9:11). He sat there because of a covenant made before he could contribute anything. That is every one of us before God, and the disabled Christian isn't a lesser case of it but the clearest one; the rest of us have only hidden it behind our productivity. A church that can't see this has confused the kingdom with a labor market.

## What the Resurrection Does to a Day's Work

If vocation is real, the church has mostly failed to equip people for it. Lesslie Newbigin argued in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) that the church's priesthood is exercised mainly out in the world, in the daily work of its members, and that a faithful congregation prepares and sustains its people for it. Dorothy Sayers made a sharper charge in her wartime address "Why Work?" (1942): the church had failed the worker by caring more about his conduct off the job than about the quality of what he made, when the worker's first duty is to serve the work and make the thing well. It still indicts most of our preaching.

But the Preacher's question is still open. What lasts? He planted his parks, knew he would leave them to a man who might be a fool, and called it vanity. Isaiah answers him in his vision of the new heavens and the new earth: "They shall build houses and inhabit them; they shall plant vineyards and eat their fruit. They shall not build and another inhabit; they shall not plant and another eat; for like the days of a tree shall the days of my people be, and my chosen shall long enjoy the work of their hands" (Isaiah 65:21–22). At the end of the canon the kings of the earth bring "the glory and the honor of the nations" into the holy city (Revelation 21:26). Volf's phrase for this is *transformatio mundi*, the world transformed rather than annihilated. If God means to make all things new, then honest work done in faith isn't scaffolding thrown away once a soul is saved for somewhere else. Something of it, purified, may be carried through, though Scripture doesn't tell us how.

Paul spends the whole of 1 Corinthians 15 on the bodily resurrection of Jesus and of those who belong to him, and at the very end he turns from the empty tomb to the labor of the living: "Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58). The work of the Lord he has in view is first the building up of the church (the same phrase describes Timothy's ministry in 16:10), and it would stretch the verse to make it a promise about every job. But the ground he gives is a resurrection of bodies in a creation made new, and that ground is wide enough to hold the woman at the sink. Under the sun, the Preacher said, all is vanity. In the Lord, Paul answers, your labor is not in vain. Between those two sentences lies an empty grave.

So come back to the woman at the sink. She isn't waiting for her real calling; her hands are full of it. She is one of the masks behind which God answers someone's prayer for daily bread. She needs the other half too, and so do I. Her worth isn't in the dishes. If her hands failed tomorrow, if the job ended or the body gave out, she would still be seated at the King's table like one of his children, because the seat was never earned by the work. Grace and the wage look alike from across the room. One is earned, and it runs out. The other is given, and it can't.

Tomorrow the alarm will go off, and the eighty thousand hours will be one day shorter. The curse promised that the sweat would end in dust, and the resurrection has answered that dust isn't the last word. What you take with you into the day is a question no one can answer for you: whether you're going to work tomorrow to serve your neighbor or to save yourself. The work can bear the first. It will break under the second, and it will take you with it.
