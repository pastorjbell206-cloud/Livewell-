People rarely come to the words "sexual abuse in the church" out of idle curiosity. Some are parents who have just received a letter about a staff member placed on leave and are counting the years their child spent in his program. Some are church members wondering why their pastor asked everyone to pray for "all involved" and said nothing more. And some are survivors checking, once again, whether what was done to them, and what was done after they told, has happened to anyone else. It has, and that is not the comforting part of what this essay has to say.

If you are one of the people this essay is about, several things belong here first. If a child is in danger right now, or you are, call 911. If you know or suspect that a child is being abused, report it to the police or to your state's child protective services; the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline, 1-800-422-4453, can help you think through what to do. If you were sexually assaulted, at any age and by anyone, including a pastor, a priest, a youth leader or a volunteer, the RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline answers confidentially at 1-800-656-4673. If the weight of it has turned toward thoughts of ending your life, call or text 988. Sexual abuse by a church leader is a crime before it is anything else, and it belongs with the police, not only with his church. This is a pastor's essay, not legal advice. And if you once told someone in a church and weren't believed, or were told to forgive and keep quiet, hear this plainly: the failure was theirs, not yours.

Spiritual abuse and religious trauma have their own essay on this site. This one is about the sexual abuse of children and adults by people who held spiritual trust, and about the second wrong that so often followed the first, the cover-up. For a quarter of a century the reports have come in, and their agreement is itself the finding: the crimes differ by place and polity, but what institutions did afterward is almost the same everywhere. I'm a Baptist pastor. Like most Protestants, I watched the Catholic story from what felt like a safe distance, and the part of this essay I least wanted to write is the part about my own house.

The argument is this. The abuse crisis is a crisis of shepherding, not of church structure: the same logic, protecting the institution in the name of protecting the mission, produced the same silence in hierarchical, congregational and established churches alike. Scripture names that logic and judges it most severely in the household of God. And the church's most common defense, that Christians are called to forgive, rests on a confusion between forgiveness and immunity that the Bible never makes.

## What twenty-five years of reports found

In January 2002 the Boston Globe's Spotlight team began publishing what it had found in the records of the Archdiocese of Boston. The first story concerned one priest, John Geoghan, accused by more than a hundred people over some thirty years, and showed that Cardinal Bernard Law and other officials had known of accusations against him and moved him from parish to parish anyway. The stories that followed, gathered that year into the book *Betrayal*, showed that Geoghan was an instance, not an exception. Complaints were handled privately, settlements carried confidentiality clauses, and accused men were reassigned to places where no one knew what the archdiocese knew. Law resigned in December 2002.

That June the American bishops adopted, in Dallas, the Charter for the Protection of Children and Young People: zero tolerance for any priest who had abused a minor, background checks, safe-environment training and annual audits. It changed practice across thousands of parishes. But it governed priests, not bishops, and the men who had done the concealing mostly stood outside it. That gap came due in 2018, when the Vatican removed the retired cardinal Theodore McCarrick from ministry after an accusation that he had abused a minor was found credible, and it became clear how many people in authority had heard warnings about him while he rose. That August the Pennsylvania attorney general released the report of a statewide grand jury that had spent two years in the files of six dioceses. It named more than three hundred priests, identified more than a thousand children over roughly seventy years, and said the real number of victims was likely in the thousands. Its most lasting contribution was not a number. FBI analysts who reviewed the evidence for the grand jury identified practices that recurred in file after file, and the report likened them to "a playbook for concealing the truth": soft words in place of the name of the crime, inquiries run by fellow clergy instead of trained investigators, an evaluation at a church-run treatment center, no explanation to the parish when a priest was removed, a transfer to a place where no one knew, and above all no call to the police.

Many Protestants watched with a quiet relief. The explanation, we told ourselves, was celibacy, or hierarchy, or a secrecy peculiar to Rome. Southern Baptists have married pastors, no bishops, and congregations that govern themselves. In February 2019 the Houston Chronicle and the San Antonio Express-News published "Abuse of Faith," which found that since 1998 about 380 leaders and volunteers in Southern Baptist churches had faced allegations of sexual misconduct, with more than 700 victims, and that some of the accused had moved to other churches with no warning following them. In 2021 the convention's messengers voted to commission an independent investigation of the Executive Committee, and Guidepost Solutions reported in May 2022. It found that for close to two decades survivors and advocates had brought reports to the committee and been met with resistance, stonewalling and at times open hostility, and that its leaders had been guided above all by fear of legal liability. The committee had long said Baptist polity made a database of abusers impossible, while a staff member privately kept a list of accused ministers. In an internal email the report quoted, one of the committee's longtime lawyers, a former senior staff member, described the work of survivor advocates, among them Christa Brown and Rachael Denhollander, as a satanic scheme to distract Southern Baptists from evangelism. Brown, abused as a teenager in a Texas Baptist church, had pressed the convention for years and been treated as an adversary rather than heard.

The Church of England supplies the third case, and it is especially hard for evangelicals to read. John Smyth was a barrister who chaired the Iwerne Trust, which ran Christian holiday camps for boys from leading English schools and formed a generation of evangelical Anglican leaders. In 1982 an internal inquiry documented his abuse of boys and young men drawn in through that ministry, and no one took it to the police. Smyth left for Zimbabwe in 1984, later moved to South Africa, and abused again. He died in 2018 without facing a court. Keith Makin's independent review, published in November 2024, concluded that Smyth had abused well over a hundred boys and young men in Britain and southern Africa, that people in the church had known and kept silent for decades, and that he could have been brought to justice in England long before. It found that the matter was known at the highest level of the Church of England from 2013, the year Justin Welby became Archbishop of Canterbury, and that Welby could and should have personally made sure it was reported to the authorities. Within a week Welby announced his resignation.

Set the three side by side and the lesson flatters no one. One church is hierarchical, one congregational, one established by law. One concealed through bishops, one through a committee that insisted it had no authority, and one through a network of evangelical friends who trusted each other more than they trusted the police. Each polity supplied its own shield. The pattern was the same.

## The first wrong and the second

Two wrongs run through every report, and they have to be kept apart. The first is the abuse, committed by a person. The second is what the institution did when it learned: the disbelief, the transfer, the confidential settlement, the statement asking for prayer, the long silence toward the police. The psychologists Carly Parnitzke Smith and Jennifer Freyd named the second wrong institutional betrayal in a 2014 paper of that title, meaning harm done by an institution to people who depend on it, including its failure to prevent abuse or to respond well when abuse is disclosed. The research they drew on linked such betrayal to worse trauma symptoms after an assault, over and above the harm of the assault itself. For many survivors it is the wrong that told them what they were worth.

Judith Herman saw why the second wrong comes so easily. In *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) she observed that the perpetrator asks the bystander only to do nothing, appealing to the ordinary wish to see, hear and speak no evil, while the victim asks the bystander to share the burden of pain. She also traced the order of the perpetrator's defense: secrecy first, and when secrecy fails, an attack on the victim's credibility. Freyd had argued in *Betrayal Trauma* (1996) that abuse by someone a person depends on is especially hard for the victim to see and name, because seeing it threatens a bond she needs. A year later she named the accused person's characteristic reply DARVO: deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender. Read the reports with those descriptions in hand and the institutions stop looking mysterious: again and again the church took the bystander's easier side and then helped the accused reverse the roles.

The instincts behind the cover-ups deserve their strongest form, because the men who acted on them were rarely monsters, and most of us would have felt the same pull. False accusations do happen, and removing a pastor on a rumor is its own injustice. The accused has a family who did nothing wrong. A public scandal wounds believers who had nothing to do with it. And no one is beyond redemption. Each of those holds something true. The trouble is what they share. Each puts the institution, the accused or the faith's reputation on one side of a scale and a child or a survivor on the other, and in report after report the survivor was found lighter. What makes the church's version worse than a university's or a sports federation's is that the church could baptize the calculation. Protecting the ministry sounded like protecting the gospel. Silence sounded like discretion. Pressuring a survivor sounded like calling her to forgive.

Diane Langberg, a psychologist who has spent decades treating survivors of trauma, makes the diagnosis theological in *Redeeming Power* (2020). Power, she argues, is not evil in itself but a gift held in trust. Abuse is power turned from serving the vulnerable to using them, and it survives by deception, first in the abuser and then in the system around him, which learns to guard its own name and call that guarding the name of God. A church that has made its reputation an idol will sacrifice to it whatever it asks. Langberg keeps returning to the incarnation, in which the one who held all power used it to serve. A shepherd's authority is given for the sheep. Used on them, the office has been turned inside out.

Pastors should be honest about why these instincts come so easily to us. We usually know the accused; we have eaten at his table. The person bringing the accusation is often young, or new, or someone the church has already filed away as difficult. Our salaries and our sense of our own faithfulness are bound up with the institution's good name. Reading the reports doesn't make any of us safe from the calculation. The men whose emails fill the Guidepost report had lived through the Boston revelations like everyone else.

## What Scripture says to shepherds

Ezekiel prophesied among the exiles in Babylon, and his oracle against the shepherds follows the news that Jerusalem had fallen in 587 BC. Explaining the catastrophe, he starts with Israel's own leaders:

> "Son of man, prophesy against the shepherds of Israel; prophesy, and say to them, even to the shepherds, Thus says the Lord GOD: Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" (Ezekiel 34:2, ESV)

The indictment is specific. They ate the fat and wore the wool. "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4, ESV). Neglect of the wounded is itself the sin. And God does not answer by reforming procedures. He declares himself their opponent: "Behold, I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand" (Ezekiel 34:10, ESV). Then he takes the flock himself: "I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out" (Ezekiel 34:11, ESV). The promise runs forward to the one who would say, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11, ESV). In Ezekiel's grammar, a leader who protects himself at the flock's expense has put himself on the other side from God.

In Matthew 18 the disciples have just asked who is greatest in the kingdom. Jesus calls a child, sets him in the middle of them, and turns their question about rank into a warning: "whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea" (Matthew 18:6, ESV). Then: "See that you do not despise one of these little ones" (Matthew 18:10, ESV). Then the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to find the one. Only after all that comes the familiar instruction about going to a brother who sins against you, alone, then with witnesses, then before the church (Matthew 18:15–17). Churches have reached for those verses to keep abuse inside. Read in order, the chapter forbids that use. The process in verse 15 is for two brothers who can face each other. The chapter begins with a child standing among men arguing about which of them was greatest.

Luke sets the same saying beside something that seems at first to pull the other way:

> And he said to his disciples, "Temptations to sin are sure to come, but woe to the one through whom they come! It would be better for him if a millstone were hung around his neck and he were cast into the sea than that he should cause one of these little ones to sin. Pay attention to yourselves! If your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him." (Luke 17:1–3, ESV)

The millstone and forgiveness come in one breath. Jesus does not treat them as rivals, and neither may we: the command to forgive does not cancel the woe, and the woe does not cancel the command. Before either comes the instruction that governs both, "Pay attention to yourselves," spoken to the church's future leaders before anyone else.

The Old Testament tells the story of a cover-up inside a royal household, without softening it. In 2 Samuel 13, Tamar, the king's daughter, is assaulted by her half-brother Amnon, the king's eldest son. She does what survivors are so often told not to do: she tears her robe and goes away crying aloud. Her brother Absalom answers her as institutions have answered ever since: "Now hold your peace, my sister. He is your brother; do not take this to heart." The narrator's next words are the verdict on the whole house: "So Tamar lived, a desolate woman, in her brother Absalom's house" (2 Samuel 13:20, ESV). And the king? "When King David heard of all these things, he was very angry" (2 Samuel 13:21, ESV). In the Hebrew text that has come down to us, that is all. The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation, and a Hebrew manuscript of Samuel found at Qumran preserve a longer reading: he would not punish his son Amnon, because he loved him, since he was his firstborn. Some translations, such as the NRSV, print it in the text; the ESV gives it in a footnote. Either way the narrative's point stands: the king was angry and did nothing. The silence did not even save the house. Absalom took his own revenge two years later, and the kingdom came apart.

The text most often invoked to shield accused leaders is 1 Timothy 5:19: "Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses" (ESV). It deserves its strongest reading. Paul is protecting leaders from malicious accusation, a real danger in any congregation, and applying the rule of Deuteronomy 19:15 that no one is condemned on one person's word. But Paul goes on: "As for those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear. In the presence of God and of Christ Jesus and of the elect angels I charge you to keep these rules without prejudging, doing nothing from partiality" (1 Timothy 5:20–21, ESV). The passage that protects an elder from a false charge requires that a guilty one be exposed in public, precisely because he leads, and forbids the partiality that shields a man for who he is. Then: "Do not be hasty in the laying on of hands, nor take part in the sins of others" (1 Timothy 5:22, ESV). A church that quietly passes a known abuser to another congregation has done both. And Paul is describing how a church judges its own officers, not whether a crime may be reported; the police don't need two witnesses to begin. The law of Moses itself gives a betrothed young woman assaulted in the open country the benefit of the doubt, "because he met her in the open country, and though the betrothed young woman cried for help there was no one to rescue her" (Deuteronomy 22:27, ESV). Scripture knows that abuse happens where there are no witnesses. The church has too often pretended not to.

## Forgiveness is not immunity

On January 24, 2018, in a Lansing, Michigan, courtroom, Rachael Denhollander was the last of more than 150 women and girls to address Larry Nassar, the former Michigan State University and USA Gymnastics physician who had abused them. She had been the first to accuse him publicly, in 2016. Denhollander, an attorney and an evangelical Christian, spoke to Nassar of the gospel, told him she prayed he would come to real repentance and receive forgiveness from God, which he needed far more than hers, and said she extended her own forgiveness as well. In the same statement she asked the judge for the maximum sentence, and asked, "How much is a little girl worth?" The institutions that failed Nassar's victims were secular; the pattern is human before it is ecclesiastical. Yet in an interview with Christianity Today soon afterward, Denhollander said that the church is one of the worst places to go for help with abuse, and her advocacy for survivors in Christian institutions has cost her.

Her statement held together two things the church had pulled apart. Forgiveness, in the New Testament, is the release of my own claim to repay: "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19, ESV). Paul's next paragraph (the chapter break came in the Middle Ages) turns to the governing authority, which "is God's servant for your good" and "does not bear the sword in vain," being "an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4, ESV). The Christian lays down private vengeance not because wrong doesn't matter but because God has not left it unanswered; he will judge at the last day, and meanwhile he has given the sword to the magistrate. A survivor who forgives and then testifies in court is doing exactly what Romans 12 and 13 describe.

Nor does forgiveness remove consequences. When Nathan confronted David, David said, "I have sinned against the LORD," and Nathan answered, "The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die" (2 Samuel 12:13, ESV). The same prophet had just told him, "Now therefore the sword shall never depart from your house" (2 Samuel 12:10, ESV). God forgave David and did not undo what David had set in motion, and the very next chapter is Tamar's. Forgiveness is not reconciliation, which requires restored trust and may never be safe. It is not restoration to office, since an elder must be "above reproach" (1 Timothy 3:2, ESV), and a man who has used his office to abuse has shown he cannot hold it, however sincere his repentance. And it is not a legal defense.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer gave the church a name for what it did instead. In *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937) he called it cheap grace: forgiveness preached without repentance, absolution without confession, a grace that justifies the sin rather than the sinner. The abuse crisis turned his warning inside out. The church demanded costly forgiveness from the weakest people in it and handed cheap grace to the strongest: survivors were told to forgive quickly and stop talking, and offenders were told they were forgiven and sent to another pulpit. Jesus does command forgiveness. But it is God's work in a wounded person, often slow and never on another's timetable, and no church may make it the price of being believed, cared for or allowed to speak. It never requires a survivor to keep quiet, to meet, or to have any contact with the person who harmed her.

## Why a church must never investigate a crime

Every state requires certain people to report suspected child abuse. Many name clergy, some require every adult to report, and the rules about confession and pastoral conversations vary. Church leaders should know the law where they serve; nothing here is legal advice. But a church's own rule should be simpler and stricter than any state's. When there is reason to suspect a child is being abused, the church calls the police or child protective services immediately, before anything else. It doesn't interview the child, confront the accused or wait to be sure. Being sure is the investigators' job.

The reasons are practical first. Children are interviewed by trained forensic interviewers, often at child advocacy centers, because the way a question is asked can shape the answer, and well-meaning elders can spoil the one account that matters most. Confronting the accused warns him. And a church examining its own staff has every conflict of interest there is: it knows the man, employs him, fears the lawsuit and loves its name. It cannot compel testimony or imprison anyone. The state can, and Romans 13 says it bears the sword for this.

The strongest objection comes from 1 Corinthians 6:1: "When one of you has a grievance against another, does he dare go to law before the unrighteous instead of the saints?" (ESV). The instinct to settle disputes inside the church has an honorable root there, but Paul is rebuking lawsuits between believers over what he calls trivial cases, and a chapter earlier, faced with sexual sin in the congregation, he demanded public judgment, not private management (1 Corinthians 5). The apostle who wrote 1 Corinthians 6 also wrote Romans 13, and no reading that uses the first to keep a predator from the second can be right.

An accusation of abuse in a church touches three jurisdictions. The crime belongs to the civil authorities, and the church's part is to report it and cooperate fully. The institution's own conduct, what its leaders knew and did, belongs to a genuinely independent review with full access and freedom to publish. No institution can investigate itself, and in every report above the truth arrived from outside. The accused man's membership and fitness for office belong to church discipline, which comes alongside the civil process or after it, never instead of it. Nearly every scandal began when a church used the third to replace the first and refused the second.

With an adult survivor, the survivor decides whether to go to the police, though states also protect some dependent or vulnerable adults with reporting laws of their own, and leaders should know those too. The church should tell her she may, help her if she chooses to, and never discourage it or make her silence the price of its care. When a pastor sexually exploits someone who came to him for counsel, the church should call it an abuse of power, not an affair between equals; some states treat it as a crime. On an ordinary Tuesday, faithfulness looks unremarkable: background checks, no adult alone and out of sight with a child, trained volunteers, a written policy saying anyone may call the police, an accused staff member placed on leave while the authorities work, the congregation told the truth, and survivors offered care without being asked to sign away their voice. It needs only a decision, made in advance, that the church's name will never again be weighed against a child.

## What the church carries now

Peter told scattered churches that "it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (1 Peter 4:17, ESV). That judgment has come, mostly by outside hands: reporters, grand juries, independent investigators, and survivors told for years that speaking would hurt the church. To the reader who doesn't believe and takes these reports as proof of what you suspected: about these institutions you are right, and the church should say so first. But the verdict you pass on these shepherds is Ezekiel's, and the millstone was Jesus's word before it was yours. That doesn't prove the faith true. It does mean the scandal is not all Christianity has to say about itself.

The logic of every cover-up in these reports was once spoken aloud, in the council that decided what to do about Jesus: "If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation" (John 11:48, ESV). Caiaphas supplied the answer: "it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish" (John 11:50, ESV). Protect the place and let one person bear the cost: that is the calculation the reports describe, and the calculation that crucified the Good Shepherd. No church can hold that logic and follow him.

We pastors are inside this, and I am one of them. Repentance is not a statement. It is the slow labor of telling the truth, making restitution without silencing anyone, removing from office men who should never hold it again, and building churches where the next child who tells is believed the first time. And to survivors who have read this far: nothing here asks you to come back, to forgive on anyone's schedule, or to explain yourself. Ezekiel's promise concerns the scattered sheep, not the shepherds: "I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak" (Ezekiel 34:16, ESV). If anyone is in danger now, call 911. If a child is being harmed, report it to the police or child protective services, and the Childhelp hotline, 1-800-422-4453, can help you decide what to do. If you need someone tonight, RAINN answers confidentially at 1-800-656-4673, and if you are thinking of ending your life, call or text 988.

The church has no reputation left to protect here, and it never had the right to protect one at a child's expense. The millstone is still in the text. So is the promise to bind up the injured. We will be judged by which one we believed.
