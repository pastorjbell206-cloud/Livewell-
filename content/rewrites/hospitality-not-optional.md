Most Christians feel a small flush of guilt at the word hospitality, and the guilt is usually about the house. The kitchen isn't finished. The couch is stained. The children are loud, the calendar is full, and the last time anyone came over the whole Saturday went to cleaning for it. So hospitality gets filed where we file the things we admire and don't do: a gift some people have, a personality type, a ministry for the retired couple with the big dining room. Church websites list it beside the greeting team. It feels optional, and for most of us it has quietly become optional.

Some of that guilt deserves a hearing before it gets corrected. Modern life really is hard on an open door, and a sermon that answers two incomes, long commutes and a full calendar with "just have people over" hasn't listened. It's also true that a beautiful table is a good thing. A family that loves to feed the people it loves is doing something human and good.

But that is only part of the story, because what most of us feel guilty about isn't what the New Testament commands. We feel guilty about entertaining, and the Bible has almost nothing to say about entertaining. What it commands has an older and harder name. The Greek word is *philoxenia*, and it means love of the stranger. That distinction changes the whole question. Entertaining is a practice among people who can repay each other, and its natural anxiety is performance: will the house and the food be good enough. *Philoxenia* is a practice toward people who may never repay, and its natural anxiety is cost: what will this guest ask of me. We have made hospitality hard in the wrong place. We made it expensive in presentation and cheap in welcome, when Scripture makes it cheap in presentation and expensive in welcome.

This essay makes a claim with a long history behind it. Hospitality in the Bible isn't a spiritual gift reserved for a few or a skill in hosting. It is the ordinary obligation of every household that has itself been taken in by God, and its test is the guest who can't pay you back. The command runs from Abraham's tent to the tables of Jesus and the house churches of the first centuries. The church has lost it and recovered it before, and it can again, though not cheaply.

## The word the apostles chose

The Greek noun *xenos* faces both ways. It means a stranger or foreigner, someone from outside. It also means a guest. And in at least one place in the New Testament it means the host: when Paul closes his letter to Rome, he sends greetings from "Gaius, who is host to me and to the whole church" (Romans 16:23), and the word behind "host" is *xenos*. The ancient world knew what we forget: stranger, guest and host are one relationship, and the roles can turn over in a single evening.

The apostles joined that word to love. *Philoxenia* appears in Hebrews and Romans, and its adjective, *philoxenos*, in Peter's letter and in both of Paul's lists of qualifications for church leaders. "Therefore an overseer must be above reproach, the husband of one wife, sober-minded, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach" (1 Timothy 3:2). Titus 1:8 repeats it. The early church wouldn't put a man in charge of a congregation whose house was closed. Sitting beside sobriety and sound teaching, hospitality wasn't treated as a temperament. It was evidence of character, the kind of thing a church could check.

Paul's phrasing in Romans is sharper still. Describing what genuine love looks like, he writes, "Contribute to the needs of the saints and seek to show hospitality" (Romans 12:13). The verb behind "seek" is *diōkō*, which means to pursue or chase. The very next verse uses the same verb for a different kind of pursuer: "Bless those who persecute you; bless and do not curse them" (Romans 12:14). The echo is hard to miss in Greek. The Christian is to chase the stranger with something like the energy a persecutor spends chasing the Christian. Hospitality in Paul isn't a door left ajar in case someone wanders in. It goes looking.

The setting sharpens it further. Paul wrote around 57 to house congregations (Romans 16:5 greets "the church in their house") of Jewish and Gentile believers with a painful history, since Claudius had expelled the Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2), usually dated to 49. Chapters 14 and 15 are about who eats what with whom, and the argument lands on a sentence that grounds this whole essay: "Therefore welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God" (Romans 15:7). The welcome Paul asks for is measured against the welcome the reader has already received.

Peter adds the cost. Writing to scattered believers he calls "elect exiles" (1 Peter 1:1), he says, "Show hospitality to one another without grumbling" (1 Peter 4:9). A command that has to forbid grumbling assumes there will be something to grumble about. Peter didn't pretend hospitality would be easy for poor, suspect people short on space. He simply didn't treat difficulty as an exemption.

All of this landed in a world that already had a hospitality code. The Greeks called it *xenia*, guest-friendship, and held it sacred under Zeus. In the sixth book of the *Iliad*, two warriors meet in battle, discover that their grandfathers had been guest and host, and trade armor instead of blows. The Romans wove their version, *hospitium*, into a patronage system where every dinner was also a ledger. It was a real moral achievement, and it was reciprocal at heart: the guest-friend was someone of your own standing whose house would one day open to you. The ancient world knew how to honor a guest who could return the honor. What it had no category for was a table set on purpose for people who could return nothing.

## A tent in the heat of the day

The Bible's first great scene of hospitality is set at the worst hour for it. "And the LORD appeared to him by the oaks of Mamre, as he sat at the door of his tent in the heat of the day" (Genesis 18:1). Midday is the hour for rest, not visitors, and it's the hour Abraham is interrupted. "He lifted up his eyes and looked, and behold, three men were standing in front of him. When he saw them, he ran from the tent door to meet them and bowed himself to the earth" (Genesis 18:2). An old man in the heat, running.

Abraham offers "a morsel of bread" (Genesis 18:5), then has Sarah bake with a great quantity of fine flour, runs to the herd for a calf "tender and good" (Genesis 18:7), and sets curds, milk and meat before strangers whose names he doesn't know. Then comes a detail that says more than the menu: "he stood by them under the tree while they ate" (Genesis 18:8). The host takes the posture of a servant. One of the guests is the LORD, and over that food the promise of a son is spoken to a couple long past hope (Genesis 18:10). The covenant's future is announced at a meal Abraham served before he knew whom he was serving.

The narrator sets another scene beside it on purpose. The two remaining visitors go down to Sodom, where Lot takes them in, and the men of the city surround the house and demand the strangers be handed over (Genesis 19:1-5). Whatever else is argued about that chapter, the reader sees two answers to the stranger: one tent that runs to feed him, one city that gathers to seize him. The prophets read Sodom's sin more widely than most modern readers do. "Behold, this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy" (Ezekiel 16:49). A city with too much food and no room.

The chapter carries a second warning that gets less attention. Lot, trying to protect his guests, offers the mob his own daughters (Genesis 19:8). Nothing in Scripture commends him. It's a picture of a hospitality code honored as an absolute, the guest's safety purchased with the safety of the most vulnerable people already in the house, and we will need that warning later.

Israel's law makes Mamre the ordinary shape of a nation. "You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 19:34). The ground of the command isn't the stranger's merit or the host's generosity. It is Israel's memory and God's character. Deuteronomy describes the LORD as one who "loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing" (Deuteronomy 10:18), and then tells Israel to love the sojourner too, because they were sojourners themselves. The gleaning laws and the third-year tithe (Leviticus 19:9-10; Deuteronomy 14:29) turned that love into provision. That legal tradition gets its own treatment on this site in [What Does the Bible Say About Widows, Orphans and Strangers?](/writing/widow-orphan-stranger) and, where it meets the border and the ballot, in [What Does the Bible Say About Immigration and Refugees?](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-immigrants-and-refugees-in-depth). The law turned one man's welcome into a people's habit, and it did so by making each household remember that it had once been the stranger.

Centuries later, the letter to the Hebrews reaches back to the tent. "Let brotherly love continue. Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" (Hebrews 13:1-2). The writer is most likely thinking of Abraham and Lot, and perhaps of Gideon and Manoah (Judges 6; 13). It's easy to hear the verse as a lottery, as though the odds of hosting an angel made hospitality a good bet. The word that matters is "unawares." Abraham didn't run because he had assessed his guests and found them important. He ran before he knew. The command takes away the host's right to decide by estimate who is worth the trouble, and the very next verse extends the same reflex to the people least likely to be mistaken for angels: "Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body" (Hebrews 13:3).

## The guest who became the host

When God came among us in the flesh, he came, for most of his ministry, as a guest. "Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Luke 9:58). He slept in other people's houses, and women among his followers provided for Jesus and the Twelve out of their means (Luke 8:1-3). When he sent his followers out ahead of him, he sent them to depend on hospitality the way he did: "And remain in the same house, eating and drinking what they provide, for the laborer deserves his wages" (Luke 10:7). The Son of God let himself be received. Before he ever set a table, he sat at ours.

Luke's Gospel is full of meals, and much of its controversy happens at them. Jesus eats at Levi's house with tax collectors (Luke 5:29), at the homes of Pharisees (Luke 7:36; 11:37; 14:1), at Martha's table in Bethany (Luke 10:38). The charge that followed him was a charge about the guest list: "And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them'" (Luke 15:2). Where sharing a table meant sharing standing, Jesus's meals were a public claim about who could be received by God, and his critics understood that better than many of us do.

The clearest case comes near the end of the road to Jerusalem. Zacchaeus is a chief tax collector in Jericho, rich and despised, and he climbs a tree to see a man he has no standing to meet. "And when Jesus came to the place, he looked up and said to him, 'Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for I must stay at your house today'" (Luke 19:5). The guest invites himself, which is exactly how the gospel works. The crowd reacts the way crowds do: "And when they saw it, they all grumbled, 'He has gone in to be the guest of a man who is a sinner'" (Luke 19:7). The word Luke uses for their grumbling comes from the same family as the one Peter uses when he tells the churches to show hospitality without grumbling. The complaint that dogged Jesus's table is the complaint Peter tells his readers to refuse at theirs.

Then the host is changed by his guest. Zacchaeus promises half his goods to the poor and fourfold repayment to anyone he has cheated (Luke 19:8), the penalty the law set for a stolen sheep (Exodus 22:1) and far beyond the added fifth it asked of a thief who confessed (Numbers 5:7). "And Jesus said to him, 'Today salvation has come to this house, since he also is a son of Abraham'" (Luke 19:9). A son of Abraham, the man who ran from his tent in the heat of the day. The meal that scandalized Jericho ended with the host making room for the poor.

At a Pharisee's dinner Jesus turns from guest to instructor of the host, and cuts straight through the ancient system of guest-friendship and our version of it. "When you give a dinner or a banquet, do not invite your friends or your brothers or your relatives or rich neighbors, lest they also invite you in return and you be repaid" (Luke 14:12). The problem isn't friendship. It's a table run as an exchange, where every invitation creates a debt paid in kind, until the circle of people who can repay closes around itself. "But when you give a feast, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind, and you will be blessed, because they cannot repay you" (Luke 14:13-14). Then comes the clause that makes the whole command intelligible: "For you will be repaid at the resurrection of the just" (Luke 14:14).

That clause is the theology underneath the practice. Hospitality to those who can't repay only makes sense if someone else keeps the accounts and settles them at the resurrection. A table set for the poor is a small wager on the last day. In the parable that follows, the invited guests make excuses and the master sends his servant out: "Go out quickly to the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor and crippled and blind and lame" (Luke 14:21). This is the feast Isaiah saw, where "the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food" (Isaiah 25:6). Every Christian table set for people who can't repay is a rehearsal of that one.

After the resurrection, Luke's first meal turns the roles over one more time. On the road to Emmaus, two disciples walk with a stranger they don't recognize, and at evening "they urged him strongly, saying, 'Stay with us, for it is toward evening and the day is now far spent.' So he went in to stay with them" (Luke 24:29). They are the hosts. Then, at their table, the stranger acts as host: "When he was at table with them, he took the bread and blessed and broke it and gave it to them" (Luke 24:30). "And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him" (Luke 24:31). The risen Christ was known in the breaking of bread, at a table two tired men had opened to a stranger. The Bible's last book keeps the image: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me" (Revelation 3:20).

Hospitality isn't one Christian virtue among many. The whole gospel has its shape. We were the strangers, far off and without claim, and God received us at his own cost and seated us at his own table. Every time the church gathers at the Lord's Supper, it remembers a host who is also the meal. A Christian who has understood that can't treat the open door as an optional extra, any more than a forgiven man can treat forgiveness as a hobby.

## What the early church built, and what it lost

The first Christians had no buildings, so hospitality wasn't a ministry of the church. It was the church. Believers were "breaking bread in their homes" (Acts 2:46), Lydia's first act after baptism was to insist that Paul and his companions stay at her house (Acts 16:15), and the whole missionary enterprise of the first century moved along a chain of open doors. John's third letter commends Gaius for receiving traveling believers, "strangers as they are" (3 John 5), and rebukes Diotrephes for refusing them (3 John 10). The second letter, though, forbids receiving a teacher who denies the truth about Christ (2 John 10). The house that fed a teacher sponsored his message, so from the beginning the open door came with discernment attached.

The *Didache*, a short church manual from the late first or early second century, shows what that discernment looked like in practice. Its eleventh and twelfth chapters tell congregations to receive a traveling apostle as they would the Lord, but to expect him to move on after a day or two; one who stays three days, or asks for money, is a false prophet. Anyone who comes in the Lord's name is to be welcomed, then tested, and a traveler helped as far as the church can manage for two or three days. One who wants to settle and has a trade should work for his bread. One with no trade should be given a way to live that isn't idle, and one who refuses work is, in the manual's blunt coinage, trading on Christ. That is hospitality without naivety: welcome and wisdom in the same breath.

As the church grew after Constantine, hospitality took institutional form. Christians built the *xenodocheion*, literally a house for receiving strangers, and these guesthouses cared for travelers, the sick and the destitute. By 362 the emperor Julian, trying to revive paganism, was urging his priests to build such houses in every city because the Christians were feeding the pagans' poor along with their own, a story this site tells more fully in [What Does the Bible Say About Poverty, and Is It Political?](/writing/is-poverty-political-the-bibles-answer). Basil of Caesarea built a whole complex of shelter and care outside his city. The hospital and the hospice owe much of their lineage to that work, among the church's great gifts to the world.

Something was lost along the way, and Christine Pohl's *Making Room* (1999) is the classic account of what it was. Pohl argues that as hospitality moved into institutions it gained scale and competence but slowly lost the household table, where host and guest share the same food and the same room and the guest is recognized as a person rather than processed as a case. Over the centuries the word itself shrank, until by the modern period "hospitality" meant mostly entertaining family and friends, while the care of strangers belonged to agencies. Pohl doesn't argue against institutions. She argues that the church can't hand them the whole practice without losing the small-scale welcome in which a person of no standing is given a place. John Chrysostom saw the danger around the year 400, urging the households of his congregation, in one of his homilies on Acts, to keep a room of their own for strangers instead of leaving that work to the church's common house.

The Rule of Saint Benedict, written around 530, kept both halves together. Its fifty-third chapter begins, *Omnes supervenientes hospites tamquam Christus suscipiantur*, which, rendered plainly here, says that all guests who arrive are to be received as Christ, and it grounds the command in Matthew 25:35: "I was a stranger and you welcomed me." The abbot is to pour water on the guests' hands, he and the whole community are to wash their feet, and the greatest care is to be shown to the poor and to pilgrims, because in them Christ is more especially received. The same chapter sets a separate kitchen for guests, so that visitors arriving at odd hours won't disturb the brothers, and appoints particular monks to attend them. Welcome and boundary were written into one rule.

## Why it is hard now, and what is really at risk

Our own loss is recent. When Levittown opened on Long Island in 1947, it offered returning veterans something genuinely good, a house of their own, but the pattern it set turned American homes away from the street. Kenneth Jackson's *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985) traces how the front porch gave way to the attached garage and the private backyard, and how a life organized around the car turned the household inward, away from the street and the neighbors on it. Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone* (2000) found that Americans reported having friends to their homes markedly less often at the end of the century than in the 1970s. Add two working parents, children's activities every night, and a generation raised on warnings about strangers, and the closed door becomes the default setting of a whole way of life.

The fears deserve a straight answer, because some of them are right. A woman living alone shouldn't be told that faithfulness requires her to open her door to any man who knocks. Parents are responsible before God for their children, and predators have used the language of hospitality to get close to them, sometimes inside churches. Some guests bring addiction, violence or deception into a home. None of that is paranoia, and the tradition itself refuses the idea that hospitality means having no walls. The *Didache* set time limits, Benedict built a separate kitchen, and Lot's story warns against sacrificing the vulnerable people already in the house to a code of welcome. Pohl, who studied communities that practice hospitality to strangers, insists that a household needs boundaries in order to have a place to welcome anyone into at all. Henri Nouwen, in *Reaching Out* (1975), described hospitality as the creation of a free space where a stranger can enter and become a friend instead of an enemy, and he saw that only a host who is at home in himself, settled enough not to need anything from the guest, can make that space. A house with no boundaries isn't more hospitable. It simply has nothing left to give. No one is required to keep an open door to someone who has been violent, and a household in danger should call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233.

But the risks shape how hospitality is practiced. They don't cancel the command. Most strangers aren't dangerous at all. They are the new family in the neighborhood, the international student with nowhere to go at Thanksgiving, the widower eating alone, the coworker whose marriage just ended. We rarely keep the door shut because of the dangerous stranger. We keep it shut because of the ordinary one, who is inconvenient and whose visit wouldn't be repaid.

And here the indictment has to include every side of the church. Some of us welcome the stranger in our politics and never at our tables; we advocate for refugees at the level of policy and have never had one to dinner. Others fear the stranger in our politics and confirm it at our tables; the door that stays closed on principle stays closed in practice. One side hands hospitality to the state and the other hands the household to its security system, and both have moved the practice out of the house where the New Testament put it. I have stood in the lobby of a church and counted us welcoming because we had greeters at the door, while people walked in alone and walked out alone. A greeter acknowledges your arrival. A host notices you're standing by yourself and asks you home. We built systems for the first and left the second to chance.

## Households first

If hospitality is recovered, it will be recovered in houses and apartments before programs. Rosaria Butterfield, whose own conversion began at the dinner table of a pastor and his wife (a story told in [How to Talk About Your Faith Without Being Weird or Pushy](/writing/how-to-talk-about-faith)), put the case in three words taken from the subtitle of *The Gospel Comes with a House Key* (2018): "radically ordinary hospitality." The weight falls on the middle word. Hospitality that depends on a clean house, a free weekend and a special menu will happen twice a year. Hospitality that means one more plate at a Tuesday supper of soup and bread can happen every week. Karen Mains made the older distinction in *Open Heart, Open Home* (1976): entertaining puts the host and the house on display, while hospitality puts both at the service of the guest. The guest who most needs a place at the table rarely cares about the unfinished kitchen.

This belongs to every kind of household. The single Christian in a small apartment can host, and children who grow up passing bread to people unlike them learn something about the gospel no curriculum can teach. And the table is not a trap. Nouwen was clear that hospitality isn't a method for changing people or winning them to our side; it offers them space where change can take place. Whatever change comes is God's to give, not ours to engineer, and a guest can always tell the difference between being fed and being worked on.

In the end, all of it rests on a fact about us. "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). Every Christian was once the stranger at the door, and every one of us was let in by a host who paid for our place with his own body. We didn't earn our seat, and we will never repay it. That is exactly what makes us fit to set a table for people who can't repay us, and exactly what makes it so strange when we don't.

Jesus promised that the host of the unrepaid table would be repaid at the resurrection of the just. Somewhere near your house tonight is a person who would take that seat if it were offered, and who has no way to return the favor. The Lord who invited himself to a sinner's house already knows the name. The only open question is whose table it will be.
