In some churches the word is spoken the way an earlier generation said "liberal," quietly and with a glance toward the door. A youth leader mentions that a former student is "deconstructing," and everyone in the room understands that someone has been lost. Online the same word carries the opposite charge. It's said with relief, sometimes with pride, by people describing the moment they finally let themselves ask what they'd been told not to ask. Search for what faith deconstruction is and both reactions turn up within a few results of each other: one warning that it's the first step out of the faith, the other describing it as the first honest thing a person ever did with their faith.

Both reactions are answering a real question, and both answer it too quickly. I'm a pastor, and pastors are the people this word frightens most, so I have to be careful here. I also came to faith from atheism, so I can't treat hard questions as the enemy of faith. The alarm in the churches isn't foolish. Some people who begin taking their faith apart do end with nothing, and a pastor who has watched it happen isn't paranoid to fear it. The relief online isn't foolish either. Much of what people are taking apart was never the faith of the apostles. It was an American church culture with its own politics, its own sexual anxieties and its own ways of protecting the powerful, handed down under the name of Jesus for so long that many of us could no longer tell the two apart.

That's the distinction this essay turns on. To deconstruct an inherited church culture is one thing; to abandon the creed is another. The first can be an act of faithfulness, and the church's own history is full of it. The second is a different decision, one that deconstruction doesn't require, though a person can drift into it when nobody is willing to say where the foundation is. There's a useful name for the first kind of work: excavation. Demolition clears a site. Excavation digs down through the layers to find what the house was built on, and it keeps digging until it finds out whether anything is there.

### Where the word "deconstruction" came from

The word has a lineage, and the popular sense has kept only part of it.

In 1927 Martin Heidegger published *Being and Time*, and near its opening he called for a *Destruktion* of the history of Western philosophy's account of being. He was careful about what he meant. The aim, he said, was not to shake off the tradition but to loosen the hardened layers of inherited concepts, so that the original experiences they had grown from could be seen again. The word sounded violent, and the project was, in its own way, an attempt at recovery.

Four decades later Jacques Derrida, a French philosopher born in Algeria in 1930, took up that word in French as *déconstruction*. In 1967 he published three books, among them *Of Grammatology*, which argued that Western thought had long privileged speech over writing and presence over absence, as though meaning could be anchored in something fully present and beyond question. Derrida read texts slowly and closely to show how their governing oppositions depended on the very terms they pushed to the margin, and how a text's foundations were less stable than the text claimed. The year before, in October 1966, he had lectured at a conference at Johns Hopkins University that introduced his work to American scholars, and through the 1970s deconstruction became a school of literary criticism, most famously at Yale.

Derrida resisted every tidy definition of the word. In a 1983 letter to the Japanese scholar Toshihiko Izutsu, who had asked how it might be translated, he explained that he had been trying to translate and adapt Heidegger's terms, and he insisted that deconstruction was not a method, not a critique and not a simple negative operation of tearing down. Whatever Christians make of Derrida, and they have made very different things of him, he wasn't describing a person leaving church. He was describing a way of reading that refuses to let any text rest on a foundation it hasn't examined.

Somewhere between the seminar room and the podcast, the word changed. In its common religious use today, deconstruction means something closer to a personal audit. A person examines the beliefs and practices they inherited, asks which ones are true and which were simply what their family or church happened to believe, and tries to sort one from the other. It often begins with a single thread that won't stop unraveling: a question about hell, a leader's hypocrisy exposed, a passage of Scripture that seemed to say something different from what the pulpit said it said. It's usually painful, often lonely, and far more emotional than philosophical. Very few people who say they're deconstructing have read Derrida, and they needn't.

The lineage still leaves a trace in the popular word, and the trace is worth naming. Derrida's deconstruction was suspicious of foundations as such. It worked by showing that whatever a text treats as bedrock rests on something else. When that instinct passes into a person's faith, it can do two very different things. It can expose a false foundation: the political loyalty, the celebrity pastor or the purity pledge that had been standing in the place where Christ belonged. Or it can train a person to believe there's no foundation anywhere, that every floor is simply another layer to be pried up. The first is excavation. The second never ends, because it has decided in advance that there's nothing at the bottom to find. Most people in the middle of deconstruction honestly don't know which of these they're doing. That uncertainty isn't a failure on their part. It's the real question, and the church has mostly refused to help them answer it.

### What "exvangelical" means, and why so many left

In 2016 the writer and podcaster Blake Chastain began using the hashtag #exvangelical on Twitter. It wasn't a manifesto. It was a name for something a great many people had been living without a word for: they had been formed by American evangelicalism, usually from childhood, and they were no longer inside it. The word names what was left, not where anyone went. Some exvangelicals still follow Jesus in Anglican, Catholic, Orthodox or mainline Protestant churches. Some believe in God but belong nowhere. Some no longer believe at all. What they share is a history and a verdict on it.

The timing wasn't an accident. Exit polls in November 2016 reported that roughly eight in ten white evangelical voters had supported Donald Trump. For many who had been uneasy for years, that number landed less as a political preference than as a revelation of what the movement had actually been trusting. A generation raised on sermons about the necessity of character in public life watched some of the same leaders decide that character was negotiable when power was at stake. Faithful Christians have voted in good conscience in more than one direction, and the grievance was never finally about one candidate. It was about how completely the faith had been fused to a political identity, so that doubting the party began to feel like doubting God.

The movement's grievances deserve to be stated at their strongest, in the form those who left would sign, and three of them carry most of the weight.

The first is purity culture. In 1993 the True Love Waits campaign, launched with Southern Baptist backing, began asking teenagers to sign pledge cards promising sexual abstinence until marriage. In 1997 Joshua Harris, then in his early twenties, published *I Kissed Dating Goodbye*, which became one of the defining books of evangelical youth culture for a decade. The stated goal was holiness, and the biblical call to sexual faithfulness is real and good. But what many young people absorbed was something else: that their bodies were a danger, that sexual sin was the one sin that permanently marked a person, that girls were responsible for the thoughts of boys, and that a pure past would buy a marriage spared the struggles other marriages face. Linda Kay Klein's *Pure* (2018) gathered the testimony of women raised in that world who spent years separating shame from their own sexuality. In 2018 Harris asked his publisher to stop printing his book, and in 2019 he announced that he no longer considered himself a Christian. For many exvangelicals, the author's departure felt like confirmation.

The second is political capture, and it didn't begin in 2016. It reaches back at least to the founding of the Moral Majority in 1979 and to the decades in which evangelical identity in America became, for outsiders and many insiders alike, a voting bloc first and a church second. The complaint is that political convictions came to set the terms of belonging, so that a person could hold every line of the Apostles' Creed and still be treated as suspect for voting wrong.

The third, and the heaviest, is abuse and the protection of abusers. In February 2019 the *Houston Chronicle* and the *San Antonio Express-News* published "Abuse of Faith," an investigation documenting hundreds of Southern Baptist leaders and volunteers accused of sexual misconduct over two decades. In 2021 an investigation commissioned by the ministry of the late apologist Ravi Zacharias found that he had committed sexual abuse over many years. In May 2022 a report by Guidepost Solutions, commissioned by the Southern Baptist Convention itself, found that leaders of its Executive Committee had for years resisted reforms and treated survivors as threats to the institution. These weren't fringe figures. They were the voices many of us trusted to defend the faith. When the institutions that told teenagers to guard their purity turned out to be guarding their own leaders, the moral authority of everything else they taught went down with them. The reasoning is simple and not unfair: if they lied about this, what else did they lie about?

If you're carrying that kind of harm, from a church or from a home, you don't have to carry it alone or in silence. The abuse of a child should be reported to law enforcement, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) is there for anyone who is being hurt now.

Scripture doesn't take the institution's side in any of this. Ezekiel indicts Israel's leaders for exactly this failure: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them" (Ezekiel 34:4, ESV). Jesus says of the religious teachers of his own day, "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger" (Matthew 23:4, ESV). Many who left evangelicalism left with some version of those verses in their mouths, and they were quoting the Bible they'd been taught.

The church can't hear this from a safe distance. We built the rallies, sold the books, cheered the candidates and trusted the celebrities, and pastors are inside that "we," this one included. The honest first response to the exvangelical movement is confession, and a rebuttal can wait. A great many people didn't leave because they stopped taking the faith seriously. They left because they took it more seriously than the institutions that taught it to them.

### The church has taken itself apart before

The church's panic about deconstruction has a short memory. Taking apart an inherited religious culture in order to recover the faith underneath it is one of the oldest things the people of God do, and Scripture does it long before the church does.

The prophets did it at the door of the sanctuary. Jeremiah stood in the gate of the temple and told the worshipers streaming past him, "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (Jeremiah 7:4, ESV). The building was holy, and the people had turned its holiness into a guarantee that exempted them from justice. Jeremiah dismantled their confidence in the institution for the sake of the God who dwelt there. The book of Job does something similar with a theology instead of a building. Job's friends defend a tidy moral system in which suffering always tracks sin, and Job, sitting in the ash, refuses to pretend it fits what has happened to him. At the end the Lord says to Eliphaz, "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7, ESV). The man who protested is vindicated, and the defenders of the system are rebuked. Job's protest, it matters, was addressed to God rather than away from him.

Jesus spent much of his ministry examining an inherited religious culture. When Pharisees and scribes challenged his disciples for eating with unwashed hands, he answered, "You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8, ESV), and a few lines later, "thus making void the word of God by your tradition that you have handed down" (Mark 7:13, ESV). He wasn't attacking tradition as such. He kept the Scriptures, the synagogue and the feasts. He was exposing a tradition that had grown over the commandment until it could be used to cancel it. The distinction between the word of God and the human customs wrapped around it is healthy deconstruction in a single sentence, and it came from Jesus.

Then there is 1517. On 31 October of that year an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg named Martin Luther published ninety-five theses for academic debate on the sale of indulgences. The first of them says, in substance, that when Jesus called people to repent, he meant the whole life of the believer to be one of repentance. Luther didn't set out to start a new church. He set out to call his own church back to its gospel, and he argued inside it for more than three years, until Pope Leo X excommunicated him in January 1521 and he refused to recant before the Diet of Worms that April. Whatever one's tradition, the shape of his first move is instructive. He examined an inherited practice, concluded that it had grown over the gospel, and appealed from the practice to the Scriptures and the early church.

That appeal had a slogan, and it came from the humanist scholars before it came from the reformers: *ad fontes*, back to the sources. In 1516 Erasmus of Rotterdam published a Greek New Testament, and a generation of readers began to meet the apostles in their own language rather than only through centuries of commentary. Erasmus never left the Roman church, and Luther didn't leave it by his own first choice. Both were excavating. They dug beneath the medieval layers to find the ground the church had been built on, and both believed that ground was still there.

Honesty requires the other half of the story. The Reformation also fractured Western Christianity, and the religious wars that followed didn't end until the Peace of Westphalia in 1648. Catholic readers tell this history differently, and they have a case the Protestant telling often skips: that reform pursued outside the church's unity tends to keep dividing, and that the right to examine everything soon becomes the right of every person to be his own pope. Protestants don't have to concede the whole argument to feel its weight. Reform, even right reform, can tear what it can't mend, and those who begin it rarely control where it ends. That's the warning folded inside the precedent, and anyone deconstructing today should hear both.

The early church left a test for telling the faith from its accretions. Vincent of Lérins, a monk writing around 434, proposed that the faith is what has been believed everywhere, always and by all. It's a blunt instrument, and it can't settle every question, but it points in the right direction. The Nicene Creed, set down in 325 and expanded at Constantinople in 381, is confessed by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches alike. The purity pledge card isn't. Neither is a voter guide, a dating rule or a worship style. The prophet gives the same direction in a sentence: "Thus says the LORD: 'Stand by the roads, and look, and ask for the ancient paths, where the good way is; and walk in it, and find rest for your souls'" (Jeremiah 6:16, ESV). Reform in Scripture always moves backward and down, toward what is older than the corruption. It never moves merely away.

### Suspicion, and what lies past it

The philosopher who best described the inner life of deconstruction was a French Reformed Protestant. In *Freud and Philosophy* (1965), Paul Ricoeur named Marx, Nietzsche and Freud the masters of a school of suspicion. Each taught modern people to read consciousness as a mask and to ask what a stated reason was hiding: class interest, the will to power, repressed desire. Ricoeur didn't dismiss them. He thought suspicion could do real work, even religious work, because it burns away idols, and he argued that interpretation lives under a double motive, a readiness to suspect and a readiness to listen. His readers turned his phrase into "the hermeneutics of suspicion," and it describes the deconstructing believer almost exactly. Why did my youth pastor teach that? Whose interests did that doctrine protect? Those aren't impious questions. Sometimes the honest answer is that it protected someone.

But Ricoeur also saw what suspicion can't do by itself. Five years earlier, in *The Symbolism of Evil* (1960), he had described a first naïveté, the immediate belief of a person who receives the symbols of faith without questioning them, and he said that modern people can't simply return to it. Criticism has happened, and we can't unknow it. Yet he didn't think criticism was the last word. Through patient interpretation, he argued, a person can come to a second naïveté, a belief that has passed through criticism and hears the old words again on the far side of the questions. His image was a desert: criticism is a wilderness we have to cross, and beyond it we long to be addressed once more. In Ricoeur's account that longing isn't nostalgia. It's the reason for the crossing.

John Henry Newman supplied a distinction a century earlier that saves a great deal of grief. In his *Apologia Pro Vita Sua* (1864) he wrote that "ten thousand difficulties do not make one doubt." A difficulty is a question a believer can't yet answer. A doubt is the withdrawal of assent itself. A person can carry many difficulties, about suffering, about hard passages in Scripture, about the church's record, and still believe, the way a scientist can hold anomalies without abandoning a well-tested theory. Much of the anguish of deconstruction comes from a church culture that taught people every difficulty was a doubt, so that the first unanswered question felt like the first crack in the dam.

Charles Taylor explains why all of this feels so sudden now. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he describes a world in which belief in God is one live option among many, even for believers, and he calls the modern person cross-pressured: pulled toward transcendence, pulled toward a world closed in on itself, and never quite free of either pull. The presence of so many serious alternatives makes every position more fragile, unbelief included. Evangelical subculture, with its own music, schools, publishers and radio, promised a kind of insulation from that pressure. When the insulation failed, often at nineteen in a dormitory or at thirty in a divorce, the cross-pressure arrived all at once, and it felt like the collapse of the faith itself. Taylor's account implicates the rest of us too. The pastor who panics at the word is cross-pressured as well, and some of the church's alarm is simply our own fragility, projected onto the young.

### Two maps for the road: McLaren and Childers

Two recent books offer maps for the person in the middle, and each deserves to be read as its admirers would recognize it.

Brian McLaren, a former pastor and a leading voice of the emerging church of the 2000s, published *Faith After Doubt* in 2021. He describes four stages: Simplicity, where the world is sorted into right and wrong and authorities are trusted; Complexity, where faith becomes a set of skills for growth; Perplexity, where the questions break the system and the authorities are exposed; and Harmony, a mature faith that gathers up the earlier stages and centers on love. His pastoral point is that doubt is a threshold, not a disease, and that most churches only have room for the first two stages. That's true, and it's a mercy to the perplexed to hear it. The difficulty is in the last stage. McLaren had already questioned much traditional doctrine in earlier books, and Harmony is defined chiefly by love rather than by any confession. A map like that can leave a person with Jesus as the name for their own highest ideals. McLaren would answer that love is the content, and John does say that God is love. But the same letter defines the word from outside us: "In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins" (1 John 4:10, ESV). The love of the Christian creed has a cross in it.

Alisa Childers, a former member of the Christian pop group ZOEgirl, published *Another Gospel?* in 2020. She describes a church class that unsettled her faith and a slow rebuilding through church history, the evidence for the resurrection and the historic creeds. Her argument is that progressive Christianity, where much deconstruction lands, isn't a variation on the historic faith but a different religion using the same vocabulary, and she tests it against Scripture's authority, the atonement and the bodily resurrection. She's right that words can outlive their meanings, and right that the creed has content. Her critics, many of them exvangelicals, answer that the book gives more attention to doctrinal drift than to the wounds that set people moving, and that it can make every question sound like a step toward another gospel. A woman who left because a church protected her abuser doesn't need an argument about atonement first. Childers would reply, fairly, that she doesn't deny the wounds.

Each map sees what the other can miss. McLaren sees the person; Childers sees the creed. The church needs both, and it needs a third thing neither book supplies on its own: the discipline of sorting convictions by weight. Some things are first-order, the Trinity, the full deity and humanity of Christ, his bodily resurrection, salvation by grace through the cross, and the authority of Scripture. Some are second-order, dividing faithful churches but not the faith: baptism, church government, women in office, the millennium. And a great many are cultural, the pledge cards, the dating rules, the party loyalties, the worship style. Most of what exvangelicals left belongs to the third category, and some to the second. The tragedy is that the church taught all three at the same volume. The progressive exit can repeat the error in reverse, building its own culture with its own pledges and fusing the faith to the other party with the same confidence. The same instrument measures both.

### Excavation, not demolition

"For no one can lay a foundation other than that which is laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 3:11, ESV). Paul goes on to warn the Corinthians that whatever is built on that foundation, gold and silver or wood, hay and straw, will be tested on the Day by fire. The fire burns the building; it doesn't burn the foundation. A good deal of American church culture was hay and straw, and some of it is already burning. A person standing in that fire may mistake it for the end of the faith. It may be the fire Paul promised.

Paul also says what the foundation holds. "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4, ESV). That's the bedrock excavation is looking for: a claim about a man executed under Pontius Pilate and raised from the dead, which the skeptic is free to reject but can't honestly reject by pointing at purity rallies. When Jeremiah was called, he was set over nations "to pluck up and to break down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (Jeremiah 1:10, ESV). Four of the six verbs are for tearing down. The commission wasn't finished until the last two.

For the church, repentance comes first. We have to stop teaching a pledge card with the same authority as the empty tomb, report abuse to the authorities instead of managing it, make room for the person in perplexity without treating them as contagious, and refuse to lend the name of Christ to any party. For the person in the middle, the instruction is older than any of us: "test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21, ESV). The verse has two halves, and deconstruction as a mood keeps only the first. Suspicion is a good servant and a poor master. Turn it on the inheritance, and then turn it on the new certainties too, the ones that arrived with the freedom, because they're inherited as well.

The letter to the Hebrews says that God shakes the created order so that what can be shaken is removed, "in order that the things that cannot be shaken may remain" (Hebrews 12:27, ESV). The American church is being shaken, and some of us will lose things we had mistaken for the faith. That loss is real, and grief is the right response to it. What remains when the shaking stops is a crucified and risen Lord and a kingdom that can't be shaken. The question each of us carries out of the rubble isn't whether to dig. It's whether we'll keep digging until we reach him, and whether, when we get there, we'll let him bear our weight.
