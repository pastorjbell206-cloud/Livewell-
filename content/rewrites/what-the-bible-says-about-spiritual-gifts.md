Tongues, prophecy, healing, words of knowledge: the vocabulary of spiritual gifts sounds thrilling or alarming depending on which church you walked into first, and the Christian world seems divided into two rooms. In one, people expect God to speak and heal on a Sunday morning. In the other, people believe God now speaks through the Scripture he has finished giving, and they grow uneasy with anyone who claims more. The rooms rarely talk to each other, and when they do, they mostly talk about each other.

A Christian who asks whether the gifts in Acts still happen is asking whether the God who acted then acts now, or whether he has gone quiet on purpose. Pastors who pray at hospital bedsides know the moment when the words have been said and the monitor does not change. Many of us have also heard, from people with no appetite for theater, of a recovery their doctors could not explain. I came to faith out of atheism, so the miraculous is not a doctrine I inherited and stopped examining. It is the seam where my old life and my new one still rub against each other.

**Spiritual gifts are not achievements for an elite tier of Christians. They are grace in usable form, handed out by the Spirit so that ordinary believers can build up the church, and every one of them is measured by love. Whether prophecy, tongues and healing continue today is a real second-order disagreement among people who agree on far more than they dispute, and the New Testament binds both sides to the same pair of commands: do not quench the Spirit, and test everything.**

## Grace in a usable form

The New Testament word is *charismata*, built on *charis*, grace. The root carries the doctrine. A gift is not a wage, a rank, or a proof of spiritual seniority. It is grace taking a form that can be used, and used for someone else. Paul puts the governing question to the Corinthians: "What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you boast as if you did not receive it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7, ESV).

Four passages carry most of the teaching, and no two give the same list. Romans 12 names prophecy, service, teaching, exhortation, giving, leadership and mercy, under one instruction: "Having gifts that differ according to the grace given to us, let us use them" (Romans 12:6). First Corinthians 12 names wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, the distinguishing of spirits, tongues and interpretation (12:8–10), then adds apostles, teachers, helping and administrating (12:28). Ephesians 4 names people rather than abilities: "And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ" (Ephesians 4:11–12). Peter, writing to scattered churches under slander, reduces it all to speaking and serving: "As each has received a gift, use it to serve one another, as good stewards of God's varied grace" (1 Peter 4:10), so that "in everything God may be glorified through Jesus Christ" (4:11).

The lists overlap, differ, and never claim to be complete. Paul isn't publishing a catalog for Christians to sort themselves into; he reaches for examples of what Peter calls God's "varied grace," and much of it is ordinary. Generosity is a spiritual gift. Administration sits in the same sentence as miracles.

Paul's governing sentence comes early in 1 Corinthians 12: "To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good" (12:7). Then he adds the fact that disarms pride and envy together. The Spirit "apportions to each one individually as he wills" (12:11). You can't be proud of what you didn't choose, and you have no grounds to envy what the same Spirit gave someone else on purpose.

The setting explains why Paul pressed so hard. Corinth had been refounded as a Roman colony in 44 BC, a city of new money and social climbing, and the church had brought the city's hunger for rank inside. They ranked their teachers, they ranked each other at the Lord's Supper, where the well-off ate while the poor went hungry (11:21), and they ranked gifts, with tongues at the top. Gordon Fee suggested in *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* (1987) that they may have taken tongues for the speech of angels (13:1), a sign in their eyes that they had already begun to share a heavenly kind of existence. Paul's image of the body, where the eye cannot tell the hand it has no need of it (12:21), is aimed straight at the ranking. Then he closes the door on a two-tier church: "Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Do all work miracles? Do all possess gifts of healing? Do all speak with tongues? Do all interpret?" (12:29–30). The Greek form of each question expects the answer no.

So the deepest distinction in this subject, deeper than the one between cessationists and continuationists, lies between a gift received as equipment for building up others and a gift displayed as evidence of one's own standing. The first is what the New Testament means by a *charisma*. The second is what Corinth made of it, and every church on either side of the modern argument has been Corinth at some point. Jesus warned of people who will plead on the last day that they prophesied and did mighty works in his name, and hear, "I never knew you" (Matthew 7:22–23). A gift can be real while the one using it does not know the Giver.

## The more excellent way

Paul ends chapter 12 with a turn: "But earnestly desire the higher gifts. And I will show you a still more excellent way" (1 Corinthians 12:31). Then comes chapter 13, read at weddings and never about weddings. "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal. And if I have prophetic powers, and understand all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but have not love, I am nothing" (13:1–2). Every gift the Corinthians prized is named and reduced to noise or nothing without love. Chapter 13 is not a digression between two chapters on gifts. It is the measure Paul sets in the middle of the argument so that the argument can't be won without it.

The same chapter holds the verses the modern debate fights over most. "Love never ends. As for prophecies, they will pass away; as for tongues, they will cease; as for knowledge, it will pass away. For we know in part and we prophesy in part, but when the perfect comes, the partial will pass away" (13:8–10). Some cessationists, especially in the last century, read "the perfect" as the completed New Testament. The difficulty is the sentence that follows: "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (13:12). Seeing face to face is the language of Christ's return, and interpreters on both sides read it that way, including Richard Gaffin, whose case for cessation is among the most careful, who argues that Paul is contrasting this age's partial knowledge with the next age's full knowledge rather than setting a timetable. So the passage doesn't prove the gifts ceased when the Bible was finished, though it does prove that every gift is temporary. Prophecy and tongues are scaffolding. "So now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; but the greatest of these is love" (13:13), because love will still be doing its work when the scaffolding comes down.

Chapter 14 gives the practice, and Paul is neither cheerleader nor prosecutor. He speaks in tongues more than any of them, he says, yet "in church I would rather speak five words with my mind in order to instruct others, than ten thousand words in a tongue" (14:19). He ranks prophecy above uninterpreted tongues because it can be understood and so can build others up. His rule for the whole chapter is one clause: "Let all things be done for building up" (14:26). He limits the speakers, requires interpretation, and makes prophecy answerable to the congregation: "Let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh what is said" (14:29). He closes with both halves in one breath: "So, my brothers, earnestly desire to prophesy, and do not forbid speaking in tongues. But all things should be done decently and in order" (14:39–40). The eager quote the first sentence and the wary the second. Neither gets to quote only one.

## What the church remembers

The history is thinner and more interesting than either side's slogans. The Didache, a church manual probably from the late first or early second century, assumes traveling prophets still circulate and gives rules for spotting false ones by their conduct: one who overstays or asks for money is a false prophet, and not everyone who speaks in the Spirit is a prophet, only the one who has the ways of the Lord. The earliest instinct after the apostles was neither credulity nor denial. It was testing, and the tests were character and money.

Justin Martyr, around 160, told the Jewish teacher Trypho that prophetic gifts remained among Christians. Around 180, Irenaeus of Lyons, answering the Gnostics in *Against Heresies*, reported that believers in the church prophesied, spoke through the Spirit in many languages, drove out demons and healed the sick by laying on hands, and he stressed that the church ministered freely, as it had freely received, unlike the heretical teachers who worked wonders by magic and deceit. The gifts did not vanish the day the last apostle died.

In the same decades the church met its first crisis of prophecy. In Phrygia, Montanus and two prophetesses, Priscilla and Maximilla, prophesied in ecstatic states, claiming that the Paraclete promised in John's Gospel was giving the church new rules through them. The churches of Asia Minor weighed them and rejected them for how and what they prophesied, and over the following centuries the gifts grow faint in the record. Preaching on 1 Corinthians 12 in the late fourth century, John Chrysostom told his hearers the passage was obscure to them because the things it described used to happen and no longer did. Augustine, in his earlier writings, said the apostolic miracles had not been allowed to continue into his day, lest people grow dependent on what can be seen. Late in life he changed his emphasis. In the final book of *The City of God*, finished around 426, he recorded healings in his own diocese of Hippo, many of them at a shrine holding relics of Stephen, and had accounts of them written down and read to the congregation. The fathers give neither side a clean witness. They give a church that expected God to act, grew cautious about the forms his action took, and never stopped praying for the sick.

Through the Middle Ages the miraculous migrated toward shrines and relics, and the Reformers set their faces against that system. When Roman critics demanded miracles, John Calvin answered in the *Institutes* that he preached no new gospel needing fresh signs, and in discussing the anointing of the sick he argued that the gift of healing, like the other miracles given for a season, had passed away so that the new preaching of the gospel would be marvelous forever. Protestant cessationism was born partly as a doctrine of Scripture's sufficiency and partly as a polemic against Rome's shrines, and both parents still show in the child.

B. B. Warfield of Princeton gathered the whole Protestant case in *Counterfeit Miracles* (1918), examining the claims of Rome, of Edward Irving's London followers after 1831, of the faith healers and of Christian Science, and concluding that none survived honest scrutiny. But the Azusa Street revival of 1906 had already launched the Pentecostal movement, charismatic renewal reached the mainline churches and Catholics in the 1960s, and John Wimber's Vineyard carried it into evangelical churches in the 1980s. In *The Next Christendom* (2002), Philip Jenkins described a Christianity whose center of gravity had moved to Africa, Latin America and Asia, and whose southern churches largely read the healings and exorcisms of the Gospels as descriptions of ordinary church life. Pentecostal and charismatic Christians are now counted in the hundreds of millions. The question is no longer a quarrel among Western seminaries. It concerns how much of the world's church actually prays.

## Three answers, each in its strongest voice

The creed of 381 confesses that the Spirit has spoken through the prophets; it says nothing about whether prophecy continues, and no ecumenical creed does. The question divides churches, because a congregation must decide what happens when it gathers: whether anyone may stand and speak a word, whether tongues are welcome, how the elders pray for the sick. It does not divide the faith. This is a second-order question, and orthodox Christians who love the Scriptures have stood on each side. Each position deserves to be heard as its own defenders would state it.

The cessationist begins with what the miraculous gifts were for. In Scripture, signs cluster around moments of new revelation: Moses and the exodus, Elijah and Elisha in Israel's apostasy, Christ and the apostles at the founding of the church. The New Testament ties them to the apostolic message, which those who heard the Lord passed on "while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (Hebrews 2:4). Paul calls them credentials: "The signs of a true apostle were performed among you with utmost patience, with signs and wonders and mighty works" (2 Corinthians 12:12). Warfield argued that the extraordinary gifts were part of the apostles' credentials as God's authorized agents in founding the church, conferred through the apostles' own hands, and that they passed away with the apostolic age.

Richard Gaffin gave the argument its strongest modern form, in *Perspectives on Pentecost* (1979) and as the cessationist voice in *Are Miraculous Gifts for Today? Four Views* (1996), edited by Wayne Grudem. His case rests on Ephesians 2:20, where the church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone." A foundation is laid once and then built upon. Prophecy and tongues, Gaffin argues, are word gifts that bring revelation, and if they belonged to the foundation, they did their work once the apostolic witness was complete and written. Keeping them running leaves the foundation permanently open to new words that compete with the one the church already has. None of this means the Spirit has gone quiet. He speaks through the Scripture he has finished giving and works with full power in conviction, new birth, sanctification and answered prayer. Cessationist churches still call the elders to pray over the sick (James 5:14). What they deny is a continuing gift of healing, never a God who heals.

Beneath the argument lies a pastoral fear. The cessationist has watched what happens when people hunger for a fresh word more than they trust the finished one: the healer who blames the sick man's faith, the prophecy that flatters the crowd, the Bible closed on the lap while someone waits for a whisper. He has concluded that the sufficiency of Scripture is a wall worth defending. And he can ask the historical question continuationists must answer: if the gifts were meant to be the church's normal equipment until Christ returns, why are they so faint in the record of so many centuries? To those who hold it, this view is reverence for a God who has spoken finally in his Son (Hebrews 1:1–2).

The continuationist begins with the text as it stands. Nowhere does the New Testament put an expiration date on any gift. The chapters that treat the gifts most fully read like instructions for an ongoing life, and their commands are present tense: "Pursue love, and earnestly desire the spiritual gifts, especially that you may prophesy" (1 Corinthians 14:1), and "do not forbid speaking in tongues" (14:39). It is hard to obey a command to earnestly desire something God has withdrawn. D. A. Carson, whose *Showing the Spirit* (1987) is a patient exposition of 1 Corinthians 12 through 14 and no charismatic manifesto, concluded that the text gives no warrant for declaring the gifts ended. Gordon Fee's *God's Empowering Presence* (1994) agreed.

Wayne Grudem's *The Gift of Prophecy in the New Testament and Today* (1988) answered the foundation argument directly. The New Testament counterpart to the Old Testament prophet, Grudem argues, is the apostle, whose words carried God's own authority and became Scripture. Congregational prophecy in Corinth and Thessalonica was something else: a human report of something God brought to mind, with real but lesser authority, which is why Paul told the churches to weigh it (1 Corinthians 14:29) and test it (1 Thessalonians 5:21). No one weighs Scripture to keep the good parts. On this reading the "apostles and prophets" of Ephesians 2:20 are one group rather than two, the apostles who were also prophets, and the everyday congregational gift can continue without competing with the canon.

Sam Storms, a former cessationist who wrote the "third wave" chapter in *Four Views*, presses the pastoral point: Scripture is sufficient, and it is Scripture that tells the church to desire the gifts. Craig Keener's two-volume *Miracles* (2011) presses the historical one. Keener gathered a very large body of contemporary eyewitness accounts of healing, many from the Global South and some with medical documentation, and argued that David Hume's premise of a uniform human experience against miracles only looks true if your sample is the modern West. Eyewitness claims don't prove miracles, Keener grants, but they mean the question can't be settled by assuming nobody sees such things anymore.

Between them stands a posture that deserves to be named honestly rather than used as a hiding place. Robert Saucy argued it as the "open but cautious" voice in the same volume. Finding no text that shuts the miraculous gifts off, he would not say God no longer gives them, but he read the New Testament as describing a special concentration of signs around the church's founding, and he saw no warrant for making them the expected norm of every congregation or for building a church's life around seeking them. Some call it a dodge, but it may be the most exact reading of a Bible that refuses both to end the gifts and to let them run wild. Its weakness is that it can drift into practical cessationism: a door held open in principle that nobody ever walks through.

## When gifts become weapons

Each tradition has a temptation it would rather not name, and honesty requires naming both with the same instrument. I have to start with myself, because I have leaned both ways in the same week.

The continuationist temptation is credulity: theater that mistakes intensity for presence, the prophet who is never wrong because no one may check him, a hunger for the next sign louder than the hunger for the God who has already spoken. Its most damaging form is gift-shaming. Where tongues are taught as the necessary evidence of the Spirit's baptism, believers who have never spoken in tongues can be treated as second-class, as though Paul had never asked, "Do all speak with tongues?" Where healing is promised to faith, the sick are told in effect that their illness is their own failure. The New Testament never licenses that cruelty. Paul left Trophimus ill at Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20) and carried his own thorn after asking three times for its removal, hearing instead, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Praying for healing is obedience, and it never replaces medical care; nothing here is medical advice, and no prophecy should persuade anyone to stop treatment. Then there is manipulation: the "word from the Lord" that happens to confirm the leader's plan, the pressure to give, or the romantic hopes of the one speaking.

False prophecy has also been public. Before the 2020 American election, a number of prominent charismatic prophets declared that Donald Trump would win a second term. When he didn't, some, Jeremiah Johnson among them, publicly apologized, and in 2021 a group of charismatic leaders issued a statement of prophetic standards calling for accountability when predictions fail. That self-correction came from inside the movement and deserves credit. But the episode showed what happens when prophecy fuses with a political tribe: the word from God begins to sound exactly like the hopes of the people hearing it. Deuteronomy had already given the test: "when a prophet speaks in the name of the LORD, if the word does not come to pass or come true, that is a word that the LORD has not spoken" (Deuteronomy 18:22). The same grammar appears at the other end of the spectrum, when a progressive church announces that the Spirit is doing a new thing and the new thing retires whatever apostolic teaching the culture finds hardest. A fresh word that outranks the finished one is the same move with a flag or without one.

The cessationist temptation is quieter and wears better clothes. It is a functional deism that believes God could act and would be startled if he did. It prays without expecting an answer it would recognize and calls its low expectations maturity. It has its own shaming too: the raised eyebrow that greets a believer who reports God's nearness, the contempt that treats every charismatic as a fool. The serious cessationist critique is something else, and it deserves to be heard as such. John MacArthur's *Strange Fire* (2013) documented real abuses, above all among prosperity and faith-healing preachers, and argued that they grow from the claim of continuing revelation itself rather than from a few bad actors. But it drew its line around nearly the whole charismatic movement, and continuationists who shared much of his theology could not recognize their own churches in the portrait. I know this temptation from the inside. The old atheist in me finds it the most comfortable room in the house. It asks nothing of me. It lets the monitor stay silent and calls the silence theology.

The New Testament's answer to both temptations is not a middle position. It is a practice. "Do not quench the Spirit. Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:19–21). The text hands the eager a warning and the cautious a rebuke in the same breath. "Let the others weigh what is said" (1 Corinthians 14:29) makes every prophet answerable to the gathered church and the Scriptures its elders guard. John adds a doctrinal test: "test the spirits to see whether they are from God" (1 John 4:1). The Didache added character and money. A serious church asks of any claimed word whether it agrees with Scripture, whether it builds up the body rather than the speaker, whether it has been weighed by others rather than announced over them, and whether the speaker's life bears the marks of the Lord. A word that fails those tests binds no one's conscience, and a word from God has never needed protection from examination.

## Where this essay lands

This essay's conclusion is the one its sources reached, and it is modest. It will not say the gifts have ceased, because no text sets that date, and the passage most often used to set it points past the canon to the face of Christ. It will not tell anyone to go chasing them either, because the chapters that permit them bind them to order, testing and love, and because the church's memory is full of people who wanted a sign more than they wanted God. I have felt in my own chest how easily that happens. That places the essay nearest the open but cautious posture: the door held open, and the one standing at it charged to test everything that comes through.

The strongest objection comes from the continuationist, and this essay hasn't fully answered it. What Paul commanded about the gifts was eager desire rather than caution: "earnestly desire the spiritual gifts" (1 Corinthians 14:1). A posture that holds the door open and never walks through it may be obeying "test everything" while quietly disobeying "do not quench the Spirit," and calling the disobedience wisdom. The cessationist presses just as hard from the other side: if prophecy today is fallible and must be weighed, is it still the gift the New Testament describes, or a different thing wearing its name? Neither objection has a clean answer here. You can close this page a cessationist and remain a brother or sister in Christ, or close it a continuationist and remain one. None of us gets to treat the other as a fool for standing where the church has let faithful people stand.

What you can't opt out of is the part that isn't in dispute. You have a gift. "To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good" (1 Corinthians 12:7). Far more Christians fail here by neglect than by excess, letting the argument become a reason to bury what the Spirit gave them for the people beside them. A gift comes into view in the doing, confirmed by the people you served, the way a hand learns it is a hand by being put to use.

Paul wrote the love chapter to a church that lacked no gift and was still immature (1 Corinthians 3:1–3), and he had told them what he came to preach: "For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Corinthians 2:2). That is the center the gifts were given to serve. They flow to a body whose head was broken for it, and they are meant to be spent as he spent himself, for others. A gift used for display has forgotten where it came from.

A day is coming when prophecy will pass away because the One it spoke of is present, and partial knowledge will give way to seeing face to face. Until then the church stands at the bedside with the words said and the monitor unchanged, not knowing whether God will act as he did in Acts or hold his peace. He is quieter than some continuationists promise and louder than some cessationists allow, and he has not told us which to expect on any given afternoon. What remains is the posture Paul left to a proud and gifted church: hands open and eyes open, praying with expectation and weighing with care, loving the person in the bed more than the answer our theology would prefer. On the far side of that door, faith becomes sight. Love will still be there, and it is the one thing every Christian is commanded to pursue before any gift.
