## Why the doctrine offends

Most Christian doctrines can survive a dinner table. Grace, forgiveness, even the resurrection will get a hearing, sometimes a warm one. Hell is different. Say the word and the believers at the table study their plates, while the unbeliever leans back with the look of someone who has just been handed the evidence he was waiting for. Here, he thinks, is what the whole enterprise was about all along: obedience bought with fear, a God who is gentle in the brochure and keeps a furnace in the basement. Pastors aren't exempt from the discomfort. Many of us, asked privately, would name this as the teaching we would most like to discover we had misread.

I understand the unbeliever's reaction, because I came to faith from atheism and once shared it. The objection at full strength has three parts, and each one is a moral argument rather than a cheap shot. The first concerns proportion. A few decades of ordinary failure, much of it committed half-aware, seem to be answered with a sentence that never ends, and we would call any human judge a monster who sentenced a shoplifter to endless pain. The second concerns the character of God. A being who makes persons while knowing that many will suffer without end looks, on its face, worse than any tyrant history has produced. The third concerns what the doctrine does to the people who hold it. Anyone who could be content in paradise while others are in agony seems to have lost something essential to being human, and a religion that asks for that contentment seems to be asking for moral damage.

People who press these objections aren't being shallow. They are protesting on behalf of justice and mercy, and a Christian ought to hear that the protest is reaching for something true. We should also admit that the picture under protest was largely built by us. Dante's *Divine Comedy*, finished around 1320, gave the West in its *Inferno* a map of hell with descending circles and torments fitted to each sin, and a good deal of later preaching treated the poet's imagination as though it were revelation. The revival tent added its own heat. Whole generations learned the doctrine chiefly as a threat aimed at other people, delivered with a relish no reader of the Gospels should be able to find in Jesus. If a God who enjoys the screaming were what the church actually teaches, the recoil would be the healthiest thing in the room.

But the recoil proves more than the objector may have noticed. You can't protest cruelty without appealing to justice, and you can't appeal to justice while insisting that nothing is ever finally answered. The conscience that rejects a torturing God also rejects a God who looks at the trafficking of children and shrugs. We want wrongs to meet a reply, and in the Bible's vocabulary that reply is called judgment. So the real question isn't whether a good God judges. It is what his judgment is, and the whole of this essay turns on a distinction between two things that are easy to confuse. Judgment can mean God's refusal to call evil good: his insistence that the truth about every life will be told, that no wrong will be quietly filed away, that the victim's case will be heard. Or it can mean cruelty, suffering inflicted for its own sake by a power that is gratified by it. Scripture teaches the first without apology. It never teaches the second, and a great deal of the confusion about hell, among believers and skeptics alike, comes from reading the one as though it were the other.

## What the Bible actually says about judgment

Start with the word itself. When Jesus speaks of hell in the Gospels, the term he uses most is Gehenna, and it names a real place. The Valley of the Son of Hinnom runs along the south side of Jerusalem, and under the kings of Judah it held Topheth, where children were burned as offerings. Kings records that Ahaz burned his son as an offering (2 Kings 16:3) and that Manasseh did the same (2 Kings 21:6), and Chronicles sets both in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom (2 Chronicles 28:3; 33:6). Josiah, in his reform late in the seventh century BC, "defiled Topheth, which is in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, that no one might burn his son or his daughter as an offering to Molech" (2 Kings 23:10). Jeremiah's indictment of the place is among the most terrible sentences in the prophets: "And they have built the high places of Topheth, which is in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom, to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire, which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind" (Jeremiah 7:31). Popular preaching often adds that in Jesus' day the valley was a smoldering city garbage dump, but the earliest known source for that detail is a medieval commentator, Rabbi David Kimhi, writing around 1200, and recent scholars doubt it. The history we can trust is heavier. Gehenna was where Israel had given its own children to a false god, a valley that stood in the nation's memory for idolatry at its most murderous and for the judgment that followed it.

That is the word Jesus chose, and it matters to whom he said it. In Mark 9 the disciples have just argued over which of them is greatest and have tried to stop a stranger who was casting out demons in Jesus' name. He answers them: "And if your hand causes you to sin, cut it off. It is better for you to enter life crippled than with two hands to go to hell, to the unquenchable fire" (Mark 9:43). A few lines on he closes with an image borrowed from the last verse of Isaiah: "'where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched'" (Mark 9:48). Read in its setting, the warning falls on insiders, men who had left everything to follow him and were already competing for rank. Jesus isn't inventing a torture chamber to frighten outsiders. He is telling his friends where the road into that valley has always led.

Matthew 25 gives judgment the form of a scene. The Son of Man comes in glory, the nations are gathered before him, and he separates them as a shepherd divides sheep from goats. What decides the division is how each person treated the hungry, the stranger, the sick and the prisoner, and the most unsettling detail is that neither group knew whom they had been serving or neglecting. The king explains it simply: "Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40). Then comes the verse that has carried more weight than any other in the history of the doctrine: "And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46). Whatever else the passage settles, it won't let judgment shrink into a quiz on correct opinions. It exposes how a life has treated the people it found easiest to overlook.

Luke 16 tells of a rich man who feasted every day while a sick beggar named Lazarus lay at his gate. Both die, and their places are reversed. Most interpreters read it as a parable, and its flame and thirst shouldn't be pressed into a floor plan of the afterlife, but its moral logic is plain. Even in torment the rich man speaks of Lazarus as someone to be sent on errands, first with water for his tongue and then as a messenger to his brothers. He still can't see the man. Abraham tells him that a gulf now lies between them: "And besides all this, between us and you a great chasm has been fixed, in order that those who would pass from here to you may not be able, and none may cross from there to us" (Luke 16:26). In context the chasm looks less like a wall God raised at the last moment than the final shape of a distance the rich man kept every day of his life.

Paul gives the doctrine its most careful statement in Romans, and he aims it first at the religious reader who has just nodded through the catalogue of pagan sins in chapter 1. "But because of your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath when God's righteous judgment will be revealed" (Romans 2:5). The standard is the same for Jew and Gentile: "For God shows no partiality" (Romans 2:11). And the judgment reaches beneath conduct to what no human court can see, on the day "when, according to my gospel, God judges the secrets of men by Christ Jesus" (Romans 2:16). Paul counts the judgment as part of his gospel, not an embarrassment to be hidden behind it.

The canon's last scene of judgment is the great white throne. John sees the dead, great and small, standing before it, and the books are opened. "And the dead were judged by what was written in the books, according to what they had done" (Revelation 20:12). Another book lies open beside them, the book of life. Then death itself and the realm of the dead are thrown into the lake of fire, which John calls the second death (Revelation 20:14), and the scene closes on its hardest line: "And if anyone's name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire" (Revelation 20:15). Revelation speaks in symbols, and its lake is not an architect's drawing. But what the symbols carry is not symbolic. Every life is brought into the light, the powerful and the forgotten stand on the same floor, death, the tyrant that has ruled every chapter of human history, is itself destroyed, and whether a person is found in the book of life is the question that remains. Read in order, these passages correct the cartoon twice over: judgment is more serious than the sentimentalist allows, and its sharpest edge is turned toward the insider rather than toward the outsider the street preacher had in mind.

## The day the wronged have been waiting for

The church wrote this conviction into its earliest confessions. The Apostles' Creed, which grew out of the baptismal questions of the early Roman church, says that Christ "will come again to judge the living and the dead," and the Nicene Creed of 325, expanded at Constantinople in 381, confesses the same. That places final judgment on the confessional floor. It is first-order, held by Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant Christians alike. When Paul stood before the council in Athens, he tied it to the one event on which the whole faith stands: God "has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed; and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead" (Acts 17:31). The resurrection is God's public notice that history is headed for a verdict and not a fade.

Whether that sounds like good news depends a great deal on where you are standing. To someone who has lived safe, fed and unwronged, judgment sounds like an audit to be avoided. To the enslaved woman who died in chains without ever seeing her owner face a consequence, or the child worked to death whose employer was buried rich and honored, it sounds like the only hope that her story isn't finished. The comfortable tend to hear judgment as a threat. The crushed have usually heard it as a promise.

No modern theologian has seen this more clearly than Miroslav Volf. A Croatian, he wrote *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996) while the former Yugoslavia, the land of his birth, was tearing itself apart and villages were being emptied by force. He set out to argue for the embrace of the enemy, and he found he couldn't honestly ask victims to lay down vengeance unless there was a God who would not let the atrocities pass. We tend to assume that belief in divine judgment breeds human violence and that letting go of it makes people gentle. Volf argues the reverse: that a refusal to take up the sword ourselves depends on trusting that God will one day judge, and that the notion of a God who never judges tends to be born in the quiet of comfortable places, far from the burning villages. He was pressing on something Paul had already said. "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19). Peter says the same of Jesus under abuse: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23). A world without a judge wouldn't be gentler. It would leave every victim to choose between despair and getting even.

I once held the view that there is no such court. As an atheist I thought it the braver position, and I didn't notice how little it could hold. On that account the sentence "that was unjust" names nothing outside the speaker's feelings, and a tyrant and the villagers he murdered end in the same silence, set apart only by who enjoyed the years before it. Almost no one can live inside that for long. People who describe morality as evolved preference still read of an abused child and an acquitted abuser and feel something that refuses to be called a preference, something absolute that their account of the world has no room for. The Christian claim is that the outrage is not a glitch. It corresponds to something real.

Here the distinction between judgment and cruelty earns its keep. When Abraham pleaded for Sodom, his appeal rested on a question: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). Isaiah pronounced his woe on "those who call evil good and good evil" (Isaiah 5:20). Judgment in Scripture is first of all God's refusal to join that confusion, his refusal to let the lie stand. Cruelty is another thing entirely, because cruelty takes pleasure in pain, and Scripture denies that pleasure to God in so many words. "As I live, declares the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). The poet of Lamentations, sitting in the ruins of a Jerusalem that God himself had judged, says of him: "for he does not afflict from his heart or grieve the children of men" (Lamentations 3:33). The God of the Bible judges with a grief the tent preacher rarely let into his voice.

That same day will also turn on us. Jesus said, "I tell you, on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak" (Matthew 12:36). The account reaches past the crimes the news reports to the remark made to wound and then forgotten by the speaker, the contempt passed off as candor, the lie told for convenience. The hearing the victims long for is the same hearing at which I'll be examined, and no honest believer can pray for the one while hoping to skip the other.

## Three answers to what hell is

Moving from the fact of judgment to the nature of hell means crossing from a first-order question to a second-order one. The creeds of Nicaea and Constantinople bind Christians to the coming judgment, but they don't define the duration or character of the punishment. (Traditionalists fairly point out that the later Athanasian Creed, long used in the Western church, does speak of everlasting fire.) Faithful Christians who share the whole confessional floor have read the texts in three ways. Each deserves to be stated as its own defenders would state it.

The first view, eternal conscious punishment, has been the majority position through most of the church's history. Its classic defense is the twenty-first book of Augustine's *The City of God*, completed around 426. Augustine was answering fellow Christians who hoped that the punishment of the lost would one day end, and his central argument came from Matthew 25:46: the same word, eternal, governs both the punishment and the life, so that if one is limited the other must be as well. Defenders add the smoke of Revelation: "And the smoke of their torment goes up forever and ever, and they have no rest, day or night, these worshipers of the beast and its image, and whoever receives the mark of its name" (Revelation 14:11). This is the view of Aquinas, of the Reformers, and of the great Protestant confessions. In its most thoughtful modern form it doesn't picture God holding the lost in a fire they are begging to leave. It pictures creatures who go on refusing him, whose separation from him is the continuation of the refusal. Its strength is that it takes the parallel in Matthew 25 at its word and doesn't let the warnings lose their edge.

The second view, conditional immortality, is sometimes called annihilationism. Edward Fudge's *The Fire That Consumes* (1982) is its most thorough evangelical defense. Fudge argued that the Bible's most frequent vocabulary for the fate of the lost is death, destruction and perishing, and that immortality is God's gift rather than a native property of every soul, since Paul calls God the one "who alone has immortality" (1 Timothy 6:16). Jesus warns, "Rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28). Paul writes that the lost "will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might" (2 Thessalonians 1:9), and conditionalists read eternal there as describing a result that is never reversed, the way Hebrews speaks of eternal redemption. They note, too, that the undying worm of Isaiah 66:24 feeds on corpses. John Stott, in his exchange with David Edwards published as *Essentials* (1988; in the United States, *Evangelical Essentials*), wrote that Scripture seemed to him to point toward this reading while insisting he held it tentatively, confessed that he found the traditional view emotionally unbearable while insisting that feeling can't settle the matter, and asked that the view be given a fair place in evangelical discussion rather than dismissed as heresy. This is not a soft view, since the loss it describes is total and final.

The third view, universalism, holds that God's love will finally reconcile every creature, so that hell is real but remedial. Origen, in *On First Principles* (around 230), speculated about a restoration of all rational beings. Gregory of Nyssa, in *On the Soul and the Resurrection* (around 380), pictured judgment as a purifying fire that ends when evil has no place left and God is all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28). Its advocates point to texts of wide scope, such as God, "who desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4), and Paul's hope that through Christ God would reconcile all things to himself (Colossians 1:20). Hans Urs von Balthasar, in *Dare We Hope "That All Men Be Saved"?* (1986; English 1988), rejected it as a doctrine and held it only as a hope and a prayer. Robin Parry, writing as Gregory MacDonald in *The Evangelical Universalist* (2006), argued it from Scripture within an evangelical frame. David Bentley Hart, in *That All Shall Be Saved* (2019), argues it not as a hope but as a certainty, contending that a God who creates freely out of nothing would bear responsibility for any creature's endless ruin. It should be said plainly that this view stands outside the historic mainstream. Propositions drawn from Origenist teaching, including the claim that the punishment of the wicked will end, were condemned in an edict of the emperor Justinian in 543, and fifteen anathemas against Origenism are associated with the Second Council of Constantinople in 553, though historians debate whether the council itself formally adopted them. Gregory of Nyssa was never condemned, and the church has honored him as a saint. But no major Christian tradition has taught universal salvation as doctrine, and the warnings of the New Testament press hard against any presumption of it.

This essay's conclusion is narrower than either camp might like. The judgment is certain, and the loss it brings to those who finally refuse God is real, grave and never reversed. Between the first two views, both held by Christians who share the whole confessional floor and both reading the severe texts severely, it doesn't choose. Universalism it does not hold. At most a Christian may pray for all people, as Paul urged (1 Timothy 2:1), without presuming on an answer God hasn't given. The strongest objection this position leaves unanswered cuts from both sides. The conditionalist has to face the parallel in Matthew 25:46, where the life that is eternal is plainly conscious. The traditionalist has to face the objection of proportion. Neither is easily dismissed, and a Christian on the other side of this question should be treated as a brother, not a suspect.

## How could a loving God allow it?

The ache under the original question remains. On proportion, the objection assumes that sin is a small thing answered with a large penalty. Anselm of Canterbury, in *Cur Deus Homo* (1098), reasoned that sin must be weighed against the honor of the God it dishonors, so that a wrong against the source of all goodness can't be weighed like a wrong against a stranger. That argument doesn't prove anything about duration, and conditionalists accept it as readily as traditionalists do. But it does undo the picture of a cosmic overreaction to trivia. It is also worth asking where our scale of proportion came from. The historian Tom Holland argues in *Dominion* (2019) that the West's conviction that the weak matter and that suffering demands an accounting descends in large part from Christianity. The protest against hell is often voiced in a moral dialect the church taught the West to speak. That settles nothing by itself, but it complicates any claim that the objection stands on neutral ground.

C. S. Lewis faced the question in *The Problem of Pain* (1940), and his posture is the right one. He said there was no teaching he would more gladly remove from Christianity if it were in his power, and that he kept it only because Scripture, the words of Jesus, the consistent teaching of the church and reason itself all supported it. His best insight was that the doors of hell are locked from the inside. The lost aren't pounding on a gate God refuses to open; they are rebels who have made a self that wants no master, and God, having made them free, finally lets that freedom have what it insisted on. Tim Keller, in *The Reason for God* (2008), put the idea in modern terms: hell is a self freely chosen apart from God, carried forward without end. Jerry Walls, in *Hell: The Logic of Damnation* (1992), set the test any account must pass, that a perfectly loving God could permit it without ceasing to be perfectly loving. The cartoon fails that test at once. Lewis's picture at least attempts it, because a love that permitted no refusal would be something other than love, and if our refusal could be canceled at the last moment, our consent would have been a formality all along.

The skeptic can fairly answer that real people are mixtures, and that few of us are pure refusers. That is true, and it is exactly why judgment belongs to God and not to us. We are poor readers of hearts, quick to give up on people God hasn't given up on and quick to excuse what he won't. Nearly every time the New Testament speaks of judgment, it turns the warning back on the reader. As for the charge that heaven would require the saved to stop caring, the Christian hope runs the other way, toward a people whose love has been enlarged rather than anesthetized. Scripture doesn't show us how perfect justice and perfect mercy will be reconciled over a single lost soul, and I won't pretend to. The picture of the saved gloating over the lost belongs to the caricature, and the New Testament gives it no room.

## The judge who was judged

There is one fact the objection nearly always leaves out, and it changes the register of everything else. Jesus said, "For the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son" (John 5:22). The one who will judge on that day is the one who stood before Pilate in a rigged trial, was condemned by the religious leaders of his own people and executed under Roman law. The judge has been judged. On the cross the one with every right to condemn took the condemnation into his own body, and the darkness at noon, the cry of abandonment and the borrowed grave were God entering the far country of judgment himself rather than watching it from outside. Whatever hell is, it is not a place God knows nothing about. Tyrants send others to die for their anger. This judge went himself.

None of this makes the reckoning lighter. It tells us who will conduct it. On the third day God raised the condemned man, and Paul told the Athenians that the emptied grave was his pledge that the court will sit. So the Christian faces judgment from a strange position, unable to plead his own record, which can't bear the weight, and unable to despair, because the verdict has already fallen once on someone else. Those the world ground down will have their case heard, and the truth about every life, mine first, will come out.

Lewis's locked door is only secondarily a statement about the dead. Its first readers are the living, for whom the latch is still within reach. The rich man had years to open his gate before any chasm was fixed, and each of us has a gate with someone lying at it, and a careless word we could still go back and answer for. The books John saw opened are being written now, a line a day, and the one who will read them aloud carries the scars.
