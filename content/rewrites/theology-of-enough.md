Most households can remember a raise. For a few weeks it felt like arrival: the bill hanging over the kitchen table got paid, the car making the noise got fixed, and there was a small margin at the end of the month. Then, without anyone deciding anything, the margin was gone. The new income had become the ordinary income, and the low hum of not-quite-enough came back at its old pitch. Ask almost anyone what would finally be enough and they'll name a figure a little above what they make now. Ask again after they reach it, and the figure has moved.

Some of the usual explanations are true. Prices rise, children grow, and some households really are short; it would be an insult to tell a family choosing between the electric bill and the prescription that its problem is spiritual. But the moving figure shows up at every income, in people who lack nothing. Scripture's word for what we're missing is contentment, and it treats contentment not as a temperament some people are born with but as something learned, slowly, against the grain of everything that has taught us to want.

**The claim of this essay is that "enough" in Scripture is not a quantity a household finally reaches but a trust it learns, one day's bread at a time. A quantity can always be revised upward. A trust can't be bought. Our economy is very good at forming people who never learn the difference, and the church has taught it poorly, sometimes by baptizing the wanting and sometimes by despising the goods.**

Companion essays cover [what the Bible says about money and wealth](/writing/what-the-bible-actually-says-about-money-and-wealth) and [Jesus and the rich young man](/writing/the-eye-of-the-needle-jesus-and-the-rich); this one asks the narrower question of how much is enough. Nothing here is financial advice; for debts, budgets and investments, talk with a qualified professional.

## Bread that would not keep

The Bible's first long lesson in enough takes place in a desert, a month out of Egypt, among people who were hungry and frightened and remembered slavery as a time when at least there had been food: "Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full" (Exodus 16:3, ESV). The complaint deserves more sympathy than it gets. Slavery had been cruel, but it had been predictable, and Pharaoh's economy had taught its slaves the lesson every scarcity economy teaches: whoever controls tomorrow's supply is safe.

God answered with bread that had a test built into it. "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I am about to rain bread from heaven for you, and the people shall go out and gather a day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not'" (Exodus 16:4, ESV). The portion was set by need, an omer for each person in the tent, and the measuring produced something close to a miracle of arithmetic: "They gathered, some more, some less. But when they measured it with an omer, whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack" (Exodus 16:17–18, ESV). Then came the prohibition: "Let no one leave any of it over till the morning" (Exodus 16:19, ESV). Some kept it anyway, and it "bred worms and stank" (Exodus 16:20, ESV). Only on the sixth day did a double portion keep, because the Sabbath was part of the lesson too, a subject taken up in [what the Sabbath is and why you need it](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it).

The people who stored the manna weren't being greedy in any dramatic sense. They were being prudent in exactly the way slavery had taught them to be prudent. What spoiled overnight was not only bread but a whole account of where safety comes from. Moses later named what the forty years had been for: "And he humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna, which you did not know, nor did your fathers know, that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but man lives by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 8:3, ESV). The omer was a sign pointing past itself to the One who kept filling it.

Paul later carried the manna out of the desert. Raising money from the Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem, he told the Corinthians he wasn't asking them to be burdened so others could be eased, but that one church's surplus should meet another's need, as a matter of fairness. Then he quoted Exodus: "As it is written, 'Whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack'" (2 Corinthians 8:15, ESV). In the wilderness the leveling happened by God's hand. In Corinth it would happen through the hands of Christians who gave. Enough, it turns out, is not only a private matter between a household and God. One family's enough is measured, in part, against another family's lack.

## Why the figure keeps moving

Ecclesiastes saw the moving figure long before anyone measured it: "He who loves money will not be satisfied with money, nor he who loves wealth with his income; this also is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 5:10, ESV). The Preacher adds that wealth can't buy the rest it seems to promise: "Sweet is the sleep of a laborer, whether he eats little or much, but the full stomach of the rich will not let him sleep" (Ecclesiastes 5:12, ESV).

Researchers have since tried to measure what he saw, and their findings are often stated carelessly. In 1971 the psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell proposed what came to be called the hedonic treadmill: people adapt to improved circumstances, so each gain lifts happiness for a while and then becomes the baseline for the next. Later work softened the claim. Reviewing the evidence in 2006, Ed Diener, Richard Lucas and Christie Napa Scollon concluded that adaptation is real but not total; people differ in how far they adapt, and some changes, such as long unemployment, leave lasting marks. In 1974 the economist Richard Easterlin reported that richer Americans were happier than poorer ones at any given moment, yet average reported happiness hadn't risen across postwar decades of substantial income growth. That became the Easterlin paradox, and it's still argued over. In 2008 Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers found that richer people and richer countries do report higher life satisfaction when income is measured proportionally, and questioned whether the evidence over time really showed a paradox; Easterlin has kept defending his finding over the long run. Anyone who says the research proved money doesn't matter is overstating it.

In 2010 Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton, drawing on some 450,000 responses to a daily Gallup survey, distinguished two things we usually lump together. People's evaluations of their lives as a whole rose steadily with income. Their day-to-day emotional experience rose only to about $75,000 a year and then leveled off. In 2021 Matthew Killingsworth, sampling the moment-to-moment feelings of tens of thousands of working Americans through a phone app, found no such plateau. Then the rivals worked together, and in a joint 2023 paper Killingsworth, Kahneman and Barbara Mellers reported that for most people, feelings kept improving with income well past $75,000, while for the unhappiest fifth, gains flattened around $100,000; beyond that, they suggested, the misery that remained was the kind money doesn't reach. In every version the relationship runs on a proportional scale, so each further step takes something like a doubling of income.

None of this is Scripture, and it shouldn't be baptized as if it were, or dismissed for its source. Read fairly, the research gets the symptom right. Money matters, sometimes a great deal, especially to people without enough of it. Gains are absorbed. A certain unhappiness is untouched by income at all. What a survey can't supply is an account of why the figure moves. Scripture's answer is that human desire was made for more than any income can give, so it attaches its infinite hunger to whatever finite thing is nearest. The treadmill isn't a design flaw in the creature. It is a good appetite, made for God, running on the wrong fuel. The laborer in Ecclesiastes sleeps not because he has less but because he isn't asking his wages to do what wages were never meant to do.

## How we were taught to want

Some of this is simply human nature; the tenth commandment wouldn't exist if coveting were a modern invention. But the story of how a civilization came to organize itself around the moving figure has dates, and what was built can be seen for what it is.

For most of Christian history the church assumed that desire had an end. The medieval moralists counted avarice among the capital vices precisely because it has no natural stopping point, and they measured a person's needs by his station and calling. That order had its own injustices, but it took for granted that enough was a real thing that could be known. The modern economy began from a different premise. In 1932 Lionel Robbins gave economics the definition most students still learn: the study of human behavior as a relationship between ends and scarce means that have alternative uses. As a method, that's reasonable. Taken as an anthropology, it treats wants as open-ended, so scarcity becomes permanent however much is produced.

Earlier witnesses had already seen where that leads. In 1899 Thorstein Veblen, in *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, coined "conspicuous consumption" for spending meant to display standing, and a good bought for display can never be enough, because its value lies in someone else having less. In 1930 John Maynard Keynes, in "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren," predicted that within a century the economic problem might be solved, or at least within sight of solution, for the prosperous nations, whose people might work something like fifteen hours a week. He distinguished absolute needs, felt whatever our neighbors have, from relative needs, felt only because satisfying them lifts us above others. He granted that the second kind might be insatiable, yet hoped that once the first was met, the love of money as a possession would come to be seen as a kind of sickness. His arithmetic about productivity proved roughly right. His hope about desire did not.

Juliet Schor traced the next stage. In *The Overspent American* (1998) she described a new consumerism in which middle-class families had begun measuring themselves against people far above them, seen at work and on television, and she gave attention to a minority she called downshifters, who had chosen to earn and spend less in exchange for time. In *Born to Buy* (2004) she turned to children, documenting how marketers learned to address them directly, as consumers and as lobbyists inside the family, and arguing that deeper immersion in consumer culture did them no good. The advertising a child absorbs is instruction in what a good life looks like, delivered over and over before the child can argue back.

The theologian William Cavanaugh, in *Being Consumed* (2008), gives the most searching Christian account of what this forms in us. Consumer culture, he argues, is less attachment to things than restlessness, pleasure found more in acquiring than in having. He presses on the premise of scarcity too: an economy that begins from scarcity trains people to experience the world as never having enough to go around. Against it he sets the Lord's Supper, where a small portion of bread and wine is not diminished by being shared, and where the usual direction of consumption is reversed. At that table the one who eats is taken up into the body of Christ. The consumer is consumed, and there is enough because the gift is a Person, and a Person doesn't run out.

It's easy to read Veblen and think of the neighbor's truck, or Schor and think of other people's children. But this formation is the water most of us grew up in, pastors included. I have lived in that territory, and I recognize it from the inside: it presents itself not as greed but as reasonableness. Churches have absorbed the same formation, measuring health by growth and treating congregations as audiences whose preferences must be met. We aren't observers of consumer culture. We're among its most faithful members, and we have to learn enough the way Israel did, one morning at a time.

## Neither poverty nor riches

The Bible's clearest prayer about enough comes from a man we know almost nothing about. Agur son of Jakeh appears only in Proverbs 30, and he asks: "give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me" (Proverbs 30:8, ESV). The Hebrew is close to "the bread of my portion," and it's hard not to hear the manna in it. He fears both ends of the scale, "lest I be full and deny you and say, 'Who is the LORD?' or lest I be poor and steal and profane the name of my God" (Proverbs 30:9, ESV). Riches tempt a person to forget God, and poverty tempts a person to disgrace him. Agur asks to be kept where dependence stays visible, and Jesus taught his disciples to pray for the same portion: "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matthew 6:11, ESV).

Paul's longest treatment of contentment, in 1 Timothy 6, answers teachers "imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (1 Timothy 6:5, ESV) by turning their word around: "But godliness with contentment is great gain" (1 Timothy 6:6, ESV). His measure is startlingly low: "But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content" (1 Timothy 6:8, ESV). And his diagnosis falls on the wanting, not only the having: "For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils. It is through this craving that some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs" (1 Timothy 6:10, ESV). The craving wounds the one who craves.

The same short letter confronts the opposite error. Two chapters earlier Paul warns against teachers who forbid marriage and certain foods, and answers them: "For everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4, ESV). One letter, two errors, judged by the same standard. The prosperity gospel, examined in [what the prosperity gospel is and whether it is biblical](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel), is right that God cares about bodies and bread; the manna proves it. But it keeps the moving figure and gives it a divine guarantee, so the treadmill becomes a mark of faith. The opposite error is quieter and more respectable: a suspicion of beauty and comfort, a Christianity that can speak of material things only as temptations, sometimes a performance of simplicity that looks down on the neighbor with the nicer house. Paul says such rules "have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (Colossians 2:23, ESV). Contempt for things is still preoccupation with things. The prosperity preacher grasps and the anxious ascetic recoils, and neither has learned to receive with thanksgiving, the only posture in which enough can be recognized.

The strongest objections to a theology of enough come from elsewhere, and they deserve their best form. The defender of markets says discontent built the modern world: the desire for more drove the innovation that ended famine across much of the globe and lifted hundreds of millions out of extreme poverty, and preaching contentment to the poor is a luxury of people who aren't. The critic from the other side has watched the powerful preach contentment to the powerless for centuries; tell the worker to be content with his wages and you've spared the owner the trouble of raising them.

Both have hold of something true. Agur prays against poverty as earnestly as against riches, Paul's collection for Jerusalem moved real money toward real need, and the prophets reserve some of their hardest words for the comfortable who deny the poor justice in the gate (Amos 5:11–12). What answers both objections is a distinction between desire that has a finish line and desire that doesn't. Wanting enough to feed your children, to get out of debt, to give to someone in need is desire aimed at an end, and it can be satisfied. Wanting more as such has no end and can't be satisfied at any income. The market's defender is right that the first kind has done immense good, and the critic is right that contentment can be abused as a sedative. But the contentment Scripture commends is neither a brake on honest work nor a gag on the poor. It is the freedom of a person whose wanting has been given a limit by something other than his bank balance.

## The secret Paul learned in chains

The most quoted verse on contentment is almost never quoted as a verse about contentment. "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13, ESV) is printed on gym walls and athletic gear, where it has come to mean that with God's help I can win. Where Paul put it, it means something close to the opposite.

He wrote Philippians as a prisoner (Philippians 1:13), and by the fourth chapter he is thanking the church for a gift it had sent by Epaphroditus (Philippians 4:18). He's glad of it, and at once makes sure they don't mistake his gladness for need: "Not that I am speaking of being in need, for I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content" (Philippians 4:11, ESV). Then he says what he learned: "I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need" (Philippians 4:12, ESV). Only then comes the verse on the gym wall. The "all things" are the things he just listed, going hungry and having plenty. The strength is the strength to be content in a cell, with or without a gift from Philippi. We took a prisoner's contentment and made it a slogan for ambition.

Two words carry more than they seem to. The word for "content" in verse 11, *autarkēs*, belongs to the family behind the "contentment" of 1 Timothy 6:6, and it could mean simply having enough; but Stoic and Cynic moralists had made it a favorite for self-sufficiency, the wise man's independence from circumstance. Many interpreters hear Paul keeping the word and moving its center: his sufficiency comes "through him who strengthens me." And "I have learned the secret" in verse 12 uses a verb drawn from the language of the mystery cults, where it meant being initiated, though by Paul's day it could also mean learning something thoroughly. Either way, contentment was something Paul was brought into, in plenty and in hunger, not something he achieved by willpower.

In 1648, in the middle of the English Civil War, a book appeared from sermons on Philippians 4:11 by a Puritan pastor who had died two years earlier. Jeremiah Burroughs had preached at Stepney, east of the City of London, and sat in the Westminster Assembly, and the sermons behind *The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment* were preached in a decade that gave their hearers every reason to be anxious. He defined the virtue in one long sentence: "Christian contentment is that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, freely submitting to and taking complacency in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." It is inward, so outward calm over inward seething doesn't count. It is gracious, a work of grace rather than temperament. And what it takes complacency in, a word that then meant delight rather than smugness, is a Father's "disposal," his ordering of the portion.

Burroughs also called contentment a mystery, and part of what he meant is the paradox at the center of Philippians. The Christian, as he describes it, is at once the most contented person in the world and the most unsatisfied: content with whatever earthly portion God gives, never satisfied with less than God himself. That is the answer to the treadmill. The appetite that keeps moving the figure is meant to be turned, not killed, toward the one object that can bear its whole weight. A person who wants God without limit can afford to want bread modestly. "Keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you have, for he has said, 'I will never leave you nor forsake you'" (Hebrews 13:5, ESV). The ground of contentment is not the quantity. It is the presence.

## Enough at the kitchen table

A theology of enough is tested where the bills are paid. It comes with no formula, but a household that wants to learn it will find the lesson arriving in a few ordinary places.

The first is the budget, which is a tool and also a confession. Where the money goes each month tells the truth about what a household trusts and fears, often more honestly than anything said on Sunday. A useful question for a couple isn't how to afford the next thing but what enough would look like for them, asked before the next raise rather than after. A figure named in advance is a finish line; a figure discovered afterward is usually just the new baseline. Saving for real needs isn't faithlessness, since Joseph stored grain against a famine and Proverbs praises the ant, and the difference between the ant and the rich fool of Luke 12:16–21 lies in what the barn is for. Again, this isn't financial advice; for a real plan, especially around debt, see a qualified professional or a reputable nonprofit credit counselor.

The second place is envy, which is where the Ten Commandments end: "You shall not covet your neighbor's house; you shall not covet your neighbor's wife, or his male servant, or his female servant, or his ox, or his donkey, or anything that is your neighbor's" (Exodus 20:17, ESV). The last commandment governs a desire rather than a deed, and it names the neighbor. Keynes's relative needs are the tenth commandment restated by an economist. What has changed is the size of the neighborhood. Most of us now carry a stream of other people's kitchens, vacations and children in our pockets, chosen because they are better than ordinary life. In the comfortable, envy can pass for ambition, and in the struggling it can pass for justice. In both it is sorrow at someone else's portion, and it makes enough impossible to recognize, because enough is always measured against someone who has more.

The third place is the children, whom an entire industry is teaching the opposite of enough at an age no parent would choose. A family can't wall that out. What it can do is give the children a second formation alongside the first. Children notice whether their parents talk about what they're grateful for or only about what they lack, whether the family ever says no to something it could afford and why, and whether there's a day each week when the buying and scrolling stop. A child who has watched her parents decide that something was enough has learned a thing no advertisement easily unteaches.

Some readers aren't wrestling with the moving figure at all. They are genuinely short, and talk of contentment can sound like one more person telling them to be quiet about it. Agur's prayer is yours too: he asked not to be poor and wasn't ashamed to ask. If money trouble has pushed you toward despair, tell someone, and if you are in danger of harming yourself, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Asking for help isn't a failure of trust. It's often how the manna arrives.

## The bread that came down

The day after Jesus fed thousands near the Sea of Galilee, the crowd found him and asked for a sign, with the wilderness in mind: "Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, 'He gave them bread from heaven to eat'" (John 6:31, ESV). Bread every morning is exactly what anxious people want. Jesus redirected their hunger: "Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst'" (John 6:35, ESV). Then the contrast: "Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died" (John 6:49, ESV). The manna was enough for the day, and it couldn't do the one thing human hunger finally asks.

Beneath the moving figure is a creature who knows he will die and has been trying to store up enough to hold that knowledge off. The rich fool's barns and the Israelites' spoiled leftovers were walls against the same night. Jesus doesn't answer that fear with more bread. He gives himself as bread, on a cross outside Jerusalem, and walks out of a tomb that could not keep him. Paul could be content in a cell because the question beneath all his wants had been answered there. Contentment, then, is less a technique for wanting less than the shape wanting takes after the deepest want has been met. The church rehearses it at the Lord's table, where the portion is almost comically small and no one leaves full, a meal received rather than stored and repeated because the lesson doesn't keep.

One detail in Exodus 16 is easy to miss. After all the warnings, God commanded that a single omer of manna be kept in a jar before the LORD, so that generations who never saw the wilderness could see the bread he had fed their fathers with (Exodus 16:32–34). It was the only manna Israel was ever allowed to keep for good, and it wasn't kept for security. It was kept for remembrance.

Most of us are keeping something against the future, and much of it is prudent and right. The question the jar asks is what the keeping is for. Tomorrow the figure will move again. An advertisement will explain what you lack, someone's photograph will show you a better kitchen, and the raise, if it comes, will be absorbed by spring. None of it will announce itself as a spiritual matter. It will feel like ordinary life, which is exactly where Israel learned the lesson and where most of us keep failing to. Whether you can call today's bread enough was never a question about your income. It's a question about whether you believe the One who gave it will come again in the morning.
