Nearly every American Christian has heard the line, usually from someone who wants it to sting. In April 1960, on NBC's *Meet the Press*, Martin Luther King Jr. called it one of the shameful tragedies of the nation that eleven o'clock on Sunday morning was one of the most segregated hours in Christian America. He said versions of it more than once in those years, and he never said it as an outsider sneering at the church. He said it as a Baptist preacher, the son and grandson of Baptist preachers, about the institution he loved and served. That's what made it hurt, and it's why the sentence has outlived the decade that produced it.

More than sixty years later the sentence is still mostly true, and most of us who pastor know it. We also know the usual response. A church notices that its neighborhood has changed and its pews have not. Someone in a leadership meeting says the congregation ought to look more like the community, and everyone agrees, because everyone means it. A committee forms. A worship leader of color is hired, a special service celebrates the cultures represented in the congregation, the photographs on the website change. A few years later the church looks much as it did, or it looks different in the photographs and the same in every room where decisions are made. The families who came and left rarely say why. The people who stayed usually conclude that the program failed.

The program was rarely the problem. The diagnosis was. This essay argues that the American church has mostly confused two things that share a vocabulary: diversity, which is a matter of who is present, and reconciliation, which in the New Testament is a matter of shared life and shared power under the cross. You can have the first without a trace of the second. A church can be photographed as multiethnic and still be, in every way that counts, a church in which one culture sets the terms and everyone else is welcome to adapt. That is an indictment, and as a white pastor I stand inside it, because the default culture of my tradition is the one I have most often mistaken for the gospel itself. None of what follows is written from outside.

## Why Sunday morning is still segregated

Start further back than the civil rights era, because the segregated hour was not an accident of neighborhoods. The separate Black church in America was born of exclusion. From the late eighteenth century forward, Black Christians who had been seated in balconies and, in one famous account, pulled from their knees at prayer in white churches built congregations and then denominations of their own, and those churches became the one institution in Black life that white power did not own. That history deserves its own telling, and it gets one elsewhere on this site. White Protestants, for their part, divided over whether a slaveholder could serve as a missionary. The Southern Baptist Convention was organized in 1845 over exactly that question, a founding the convention itself named and repented of in its 1995 resolution on racial reconciliation. The segregated hour is the residue of decisions, made by Christians, over generations.

The most painful chapter is the one where the wall came down and was rebuilt. In April 1906, in a converted building on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, a revival broke out under William Seymour, a Black holiness preacher whose parents had been enslaved. What scandalized the Los Angeles press was not only the tongues but the mixing: Black and white and Mexican worshipers praying together under a Black man's leadership in the first decade of Jim Crow. Frank Bartleman, a white participant, wrote in his later account that the color line had been washed away in the blood. It didn't stay washed. In 1914 a largely white group of ministers, some of whom had held ministerial credentials under the name of Charles Harrison Mason's predominantly Black Church of God in Christ, met in Hot Springs, Arkansas, and organized the Assemblies of God. No council voted against Ephesians. The old pattern simply reasserted itself, the way water finds its level, and within a decade one of the most integrated revivals in American history had sorted itself by race.

Then, in the middle of the twentieth century, the pattern acquired a theory. Donald McGavran, a missionary born in India to missionary parents, watched the gospel move through castes and clans rather than across them, and in *The Bridges of God* (1955) and *Understanding Church Growth* (1970) he argued that people prefer to become Christians without crossing barriers of race, language or class. The observation became the homogeneous unit principle, taught at Fuller Theological Seminary and applied to American church planting, and a generation of American church planters learned to design congregations around one kind of person. The segregated hour, which King had called a tragedy, became for some a strategy.

What does the hour look like now? The most careful answer comes from the sociologist Michael Emerson and his colleagues. In *United by Faith* (2003), written with Curtiss Paul DeYoung, George Yancey and Karen Chai Kim, and then in *People of the Dream* (2006), written with the pastor Rodney Woo, Emerson defined a multiracial congregation as one in which no single racial group makes up 80 percent or more of the people. The line has a logic that organizational sociologists had described long before: below a certain share, a minority tends to be present without being able to shape much. Rosabeth Moss Kanter's *Men and Women of the Corporation* (1977) showed how people who make up a small fraction of a heavily skewed group become tokens, highly visible as symbols of their category and pressed into the roles the majority expects of them. By Emerson's count, fewer than one American congregation in ten met even that modest standard at the end of the 1990s. Later waves of the National Congregations Study, the national survey of American congregations directed by Mark Chaves, show the share growing in the two decades since, though multiracial congregations remain a minority. The growth is real and worth being grateful for. But a count of who is in the pews can't tell you who holds the pen, and that's the question the numbers leave open.

## The strongest case for the homogeneous church

Before any verdict, the other side deserves its strongest voice, and it has a strong one. McGavran was not a segregationist, and the homogeneous unit principle was not born in an American suburb. It was born in the villages of central India, where McGavran watched missionaries require converts to leave their families, their caste and their customs as the price of baptism, and watched the gospel stall. His insight was that the missionary had added a stumbling block the gospel did not require. A person should be asked to repent of sin, not of being Indian, or Korean, or working class. People come to faith across the bridges of kinship and friendship, and when you demand that they burn those bridges first, most of them won't come.

The defender of that principle can cite the apostles. The Jerusalem council of Acts 15 is precisely a refusal to require Gentiles to become culturally Jewish before they could belong: "Therefore my judgment is that we should not trouble those of the Gentiles who turn to God" (Acts 15:19, ESV). Paul describes his own method in the same key: "To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews" (1 Corinthians 9:20, ESV). Add to this the plain goods of culturally specific churches. A Korean immigrant congregation that worships in Korean is a place where grandparents can pray in the language they dream in, and nothing about that is prejudice. A Spanish-language congregation is where a newly arrived family can be pastored in words they understand. And the historically Black church was never simply a product of white refusal. It became a school of leadership, a house of lament, a prophetic institution and a place of rest from the constant labor of being a minority in white spaces. Many Black pastors have watched the multiracial church movement with a reasonable suspicion: that it would drain their congregations of gifted young members and leaders to fill the seats of white-led churches, and call the transfer reconciliation. That objection deserves to be taken as seriously as any other in this essay.

All of that is true, and a church that dismisses it hasn't understood the question. But the evangelical world had this argument once already, and it's worth hearing how it went. In 1977 the Lausanne movement convened a consultation on the homogeneous unit principle in Pasadena, with McGavran's colleagues at the table and John Stott among the participants. The Ecuadorian theologian René Padilla, who had been one of the sharpest voices at Lausanne in 1974, pressed the decisive point in a 1982 essay, "The Unity of the Church and the Homogeneous Unit Principle." In the New Testament, he argued, the unity of Jew and Gentile was not a later stage of discipleship to be reached after evangelism had done its work in homogeneous groups. It belonged to the gospel from the beginning. The apostles did not plant a Jewish church and a Gentile church and hope they might someday share a meal. They insisted, at great cost, that the two eat at one table.

The distinction that settles the matter is the one between description and prescription. As a description of how people usually come to faith, McGavran was largely right, and pastors ignore him at their peril. As a prescription for what the church should be once they arrive, the principle quietly canonizes the very walls the cross came to break. Acts 15 did free Gentiles from having to become Jews. It did not free Jews and Gentiles from each other. The council's whole purpose was to make one church possible, not to license two. A language congregation that belongs to a larger body, shares its leaders and its table, and knows itself to be one expression of a single people is doing something very different from a congregation designed, on market research, so that its members will never have to worship beside anyone unlike themselves.

## The wall, the table and the widows

Paul's letter to the Ephesians supplies the controlling image, and it's worth staying with long enough to feel its weight. He reminds Gentile believers of what they had been: "separated from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12, ESV). Then comes the turn: "But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ. For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility by abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances, that he might create in himself one new man in place of the two, so making peace, and might reconcile us both to God in one body through the cross, thereby killing the hostility" (Ephesians 2:13–16, ESV).

Many interpreters hear a literal wall behind the image. The Jerusalem temple had a low barrier between its outer court and the inner courts, and along it, Josephus tells us, stood inscriptions in Greek and Latin warning foreigners that anyone who went further would be responsible for his own death. One of those stones was found in 1871 and can still be seen. Paul knew that barrier personally. According to Acts 21, he was mobbed in the temple because the crowd assumed he had brought "Trophimus the Ephesian" past it (Acts 21:29, ESV). A letter to the church at Ephesus, then, from a man arrested over an Ephesian and a wall, says that Christ has torn the wall down in his own flesh. Whether or not Paul had those stones in mind, his point doesn't depend on them. Christ did not make Gentiles into Jews, and he did not leave them a separate people either. He made "one new man in place of the two," a humanity that belongs to neither side's former arrangement. And he did it "through the cross." The unity of the church is not a social achievement laid on top of the atonement. It is one of the things the atonement accomplished.

The earliest church found this very hard to live, and the New Testament is honest about the failure. At Antioch, the first great mixed congregation, Peter ate freely with Gentile believers until "certain men came from James." Then "he drew back and separated himself, fearing the circumcision party," and the other Jewish believers followed, "so that even Barnabas was led astray by their hypocrisy" (Galatians 2:12–13, ESV). Paul opposed him to his face, because "their conduct was not in step with the truth of the gospel," and said before everyone: "If you, though a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you force the Gentiles to live like Jews?" (Galatians 2:14, ESV). Notice what Paul says Peter was doing. Peter never commanded anyone to be circumcised. He simply withdrew to the table where the powerful were comfortable, and in doing so he told the Gentiles, without a word, what the price of full fellowship would be. That is how assimilation works. The majority doesn't have to issue a demand. It only has to decide where it will sit.

This is the setting of Paul's most quoted sentence on the subject, which comes a chapter later: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, ESV). The verse does not erase difference. The Greek still spoke Greek, and the slave was still, in Roman law, a slave. What the verse declares is that none of those differences determines standing, belonging or voice in the household of God. It is a sentence about who sits at the table and on what terms, written to churches that had just watched an apostle get up and leave one.

Luke gives the positive pattern, and it concerns power. In Acts 6 the Jerusalem church is growing, and "a complaint by the Hellenists arose against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution" (Acts 6:1, ESV). The Greek-speaking minority was being overlooked by the Aramaic-speaking majority. The apostles didn't tell the Hellenists they were imagining it, and they didn't appeal for unity over division. They asked the church to choose seven men to oversee the distribution, and the names Luke lists are all Greek: "Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit, and Philip, and Prochorus, and Nicanor, and Timon, and Parmenas, and Nicolaus, a proselyte of Antioch" (Acts 6:5, ESV). Greek names alone don't prove anyone's background, but most commentators take the seven to be Hellenists, and the last of them was a Gentile convert to Judaism. The church handed the remedy to the people who had been wronged. Luke's next sentence reports what followed: "And the word of God continued to increase, and the number of the disciples multiplied greatly in Jerusalem, and a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith" (Acts 6:7, ESV). Two of the seven, Stephen and Philip, carry the story through the chapters that come next.

By Acts 13 the pattern had reached the top of a congregation. "Now there were in the church at Antioch prophets and teachers, Barnabas, Simeon who was called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen a lifelong friend of Herod the tetrarch, and Saul" (Acts 13:1, ESV). A Levite from Cyprus, a man whose Latin nickname probably meant "black," a North African from Cyrene, a man brought up alongside a Herod, and a former Pharisee from Tarsus: that was the leadership of the church from which the Gentile mission was launched. Antioch did not have a diverse congregation and a homogeneous eldership. The difference reached the place where decisions were made.

## When the diverse church is still a white church

If Emerson's research gave the multiethnic church movement its hope, the sociologist Korie Edwards gave it its hardest question. In *The Elusive Dream* (2008), Edwards studied interracial congregations, one Midwestern church at close range and others through national data, and argued that such churches very often remain white churches in everything but their photographs. Their worship, their leadership styles, their expectations about how long a service runs and how a sermon should sound, their sense of what counts as reasonable or divisive, tend to follow white evangelical norms. The Black members she studied were not pushed out; they were welcomed. But they stayed by accommodating, and the churches held together to the degree that their white members remained comfortable. Her conclusion was not that interracial churches are worthless. It was that proximity does not undo power, and that a congregation can mix its pews thoroughly while leaving the question of whose culture counts as normal entirely unasked.

Edwards is the key witness against the easy version of this project, because she isn't dismissing the dream her title names. She is reporting what happens when a church pursues diversity as presence rather than reconciliation as shared power, and her account explains the familiar failures better than any single story could. A church hires a worship leader of color, and within a year or two the songs have drifted back toward what the majority prefers, because the majority's discomfort is treated as a pastoral concern and the minority's is treated as a matter of taste. A church forms a diversity committee that can recommend but not decide. It invites a guest preacher from another tradition for a Sunday in February and then returns to its regular programming. It adds faces to the stage while the elder board, the budget and the search for the next senior pastor stay exactly where they were. Kanter's token appears here too: the lone Black elder who is asked to speak for an entire people in every conversation about race, and whose disagreement on any other question is quietly filed as not being a team player. None of this requires ill will. That's precisely why good intentions don't fix it.

The whole question turns on the difference between those two words. Diversity, as most churches use the word, is about who is present. Reconciliation, as the New Testament uses it, is about what the people who are present share: a table, a common life, and the authority to shape the community they belong to. Diversity asks whether the room looks different. Reconciliation asks whose room it is. A church can pursue the first while guarding against the second, and many do, because the first costs a marketing budget and the second costs the majority its unexamined sense that its preferences are simply how church is done.

The theologian Willie James Jennings, in *The Christian Imagination* (2010), gives the deepest account of why that sense runs so deep. He argues that Western Christianity, over the colonial centuries, forgot that it was a Gentile faith, a people grafted into Israel's story by grace, the ones who were once far off. Having forgotten that it had been the guest, it began to act as the host of the world, measuring every other people against its own image and sorting bodies by race rather than joining them. A white church that thinks of diversity as welcoming others into its house has, on Jennings's reading, forgotten that it was itself brought near. Every Gentile believer in Ephesus was a guest at Israel's table before he was anyone's host.

## The same instrument, right and left

The failure runs in two directions, and it has to be named with the same instrument in both. The instrument is a single question: does this arrangement leave one culture at the unexamined center and its power untouched?

On the right, the usual form is color-blindness offered as faithfulness. We just preach the gospel, the argument goes; we don't see race; talk of structure and power is a political import. There's something true here. The gospel really is the ground of unity, and partisan politics really does try to colonize the church. But a church that refuses to name its own center hasn't escaped having one. It has only made its center invisible to itself, while everyone who doesn't share that center can see it perfectly well. The church growth version belongs on this side too: a strategy that knows exactly which demographic it is built for and calls the result neutral. When a Christian community treats any mention of the segregated hour as suspect ideology, it makes King's observation more true, not less.

On the left, the usual form is diversity as optics. The language and methods of corporate diversity programs come into the church more or less whole. Representation is counted, the website is checked for the right mix of faces, and after a national crisis a statement goes out within the week. There's something true here as well, because representation matters and silence after injustice is its own kind of speech. But a church can count faces the way a company does and share power no more than a company does. The mainline Protestant denominations, which have produced some of the most thoughtful statements on race in American religion, remain among the whitest bodies in American religion: in the Pew Research Center's large 2014 survey of American religious life, several of the largest mainline denominations ranked among the least racially diverse religious groups in the country. And the progressive instinct can make a theological error of its own: it can treat race as the most determining fact about a person, when the whole claim of Galatians 3:28 is that in Christ it is not. A church that sorts its members into identity groups for the sake of representation has kept the very categories the cross relativized.

Both are forms of assimilation. The first invites people in on the condition that nobody mentions the terms. The second invites people in and puts them in the brochure. In both, the majority keeps the pen. The grief ought to be the same in both directions, too, because most of us who lead churches have practiced both, the color-blind sermon when we were comfortable and the program when we were stung, and neither moved the authority in our congregations an inch.

## Excavation, not construction

What would change it? The honest answer begins with a reversal. We tend to think of the multiethnic church as something we build, assembled through the right hires, the right music and the right outreach. But Ephesians doesn't say Christ made unity possible. It says he "has made us both one" and has "broken down" the wall (Ephesians 2:14, ESV), and that Jew and Gentile together "are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:22, ESV). The new humanity is his accomplishment before it is anyone's project. Our part is less like construction and more like excavation: clearing away the rubble of habit, preference and inherited power that buries a unity Christ has already made.

Excavation is still work, and it is costly work. John Perkins, the Mississippi preacher who was beaten nearly to death in the Brandon jail in 1970 and gave the decades that followed to this question, set out in *With Justice for All* (1982) the three commitments that became the core of the Christian community development movement: relocation, reconciliation and redistribution. He put the costly word last and meant it. Reconciliation that never touches who holds resources and authority is, on his account, sentiment. In a local church, redistribution is concrete. It means asking who sits on the elder board and who chooses the next pastor, whose theological tradition shapes the teaching and whose is treated as a supplement, whose discomfort is addressed when a sermon causes friction and whose is simply absorbed. It means minority members holding real authority, including the authority to make decisions the majority would not have made. It means the majority accepting, as the Jerusalem church did in Acts 6, that the people who were overlooked may be the ones best placed to repair what was overlooked. And it means patience, because the distrust many Christians of color carry toward white institutions was not formed in a planning cycle and won't be dissolved in one.

There is at least one American picture of what that repentance can look like. In October 1994, in Memphis, the Pentecostal Fellowship of North America, a body that had been white since its founding, voted to dissolve itself so that a new interracial fellowship could take its place, with a bishop of the Church of God in Christ as its first chairman. In the course of that gathering a white Assemblies of God pastor knelt and washed the feet of a Black Church of God in Christ bishop. Eighty years after Hot Springs, the heirs of the men who had left did more than issue a statement. They gave up an institution. That's the difference between an apology and repentance: repentance changes where the power sits.

The end of the story has already been shown to the church. "After this I looked, and behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands" (Revelation 7:9, ESV). The nations are still nations there, and their languages are not erased. They are gathered around one throne, and the one at the center is the Lamb who was slain. No culture is the host at that gathering. Every people there is a guest, and every people there was brought near by blood.

That crowd is where the church is already headed, whether or not our strategies hold, and every congregation on earth is either rehearsing it or resisting it. None of us who lead churches can say with confidence that ours is rehearsing it well, and the question doesn't close neatly for any congregation, large or small. But the question to carry out of this essay is plain enough, because the Hellenist widows asked it without words twenty centuries ago. When the minority is overlooked, who is given the power to fix it? The photograph won't answer that. The ledger of who decides will, and the Lamb at the center of that crowd died so that the answer could be ours to give.
