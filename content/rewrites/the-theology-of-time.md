It's twenty minutes to seven on a Tuesday, and the family calendar on the refrigerator has run out of room. One child has practice across town at seven, another needs poster board before morning, dinner is something eaten standing up, and the phone on the counter keeps lighting with messages that all seem to want an answer tonight. Nobody in the house is lazy. Everyone is tired. And if you asked either parent what the trouble is, the answer would come quickly, because it's the answer the whole culture gives: there isn't enough time.

Most of what we're offered for that trouble accepts the diagnosis and sells a cure. Nearly every productivity system assumes the problem is scarcity and the answer is efficiency, so that if we can fit more into the hours we have, the hours will finally be enough. Oliver Burkeman, a secular journalist who spent years writing about productivity, came to doubt the whole enterprise. In *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021) he observed that a life of eighty years is only about four thousand weeks long, and he argued that efficiency tends to feed the very problem it promises to solve, since the faster we clear the inbox, the faster it fills again. He's right about the symptom, and he gets further than a good many Christian books on the subject.

But Scripture reads the trouble at a different depth. The productivity system assumes we don't have enough time to do everything. The Bible assumes we don't have enough wisdom to know what our time is for. Those aren't the same problem, and until the second is faced, the first can only be managed, with growing efficiency and growing emptiness. Hurry, in the end, is a question about trust: whether the days belong to us or are lent to us, and whether anyone receives what we do with them. The Sabbath, the church's calendar and the phone each have their own essay on this site. The subject here is time itself, and the question every household is already answering, whether it means to or not, in the squares on the refrigerator door.

## How the Clock Came to Rule the House

The feeling that time is a scarce commodity has a history, and the history is stranger than we'd guess, because it begins in prayer. Around 530, Benedict of Nursia wrote a Rule for monks that ordered each day around what he called the Work of God, the common prayer of the psalms sung at fixed hours. He took his cue from the Psalter: "Seven times a day I praise you for your righteous rules" (Psalm 119:164, ESV), and "At midnight I rise to praise you" (Psalm 119:62, ESV). So the monastery kept a night office and seven offices of the day, from Lauds at dawn to Compline at nightfall, with manual labor and sacred reading fitted into the spaces between. The horarium, as the monastic timetable came to be called, was demanding, and it was also merciful. Benedict insisted that nothing be preferred to the Work of God, and he warned that idleness is the enemy of the soul, so the day held prayer and labor together without letting either swallow the other. Time in the monastery wasn't money. It was the frame of a life offered back to God in measured portions.

The monks needed to know when to pray, and that need, Lewis Mumford argued in *Technics and Civilization* (1934), is where the master machine of the modern world was born. Mumford traced the mechanical clock, which appeared in Europe toward the end of the thirteenth century, back to the regular bells of the monastery, and he made the provocative claim that the clock, rather than the steam engine, was the decisive machine of the industrial age. The steam engine supplied power. The clock supplied the discipline that let power be organized, synchronized and sold, because it pulled time loose from the events of human life and turned it into a uniform stream of measurable units. Later historians have qualified the monastic half of the story, pointing to the public clocks that fourteenth-century towns raised over their markets and town halls. But the irony survives the qualification. A device that grew up partly to call people to prayer became the device that would call them away from it.

The decisive turn came with the factory. E. P. Thompson's essay "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism" (1967) describes the change as a move from task-orientation to timed labor. The farmer, the fisherman and the craftsman had worked by the task: the harvest when the grain was ripe, the boat when the tide was right, long bursts of labor followed by slack. Work and the rest of life mingled, and a day was measured by what it held. The factory couldn't run that way. It needed workers present at the same moment, for the same span, under a clock the owner controlled, and Thompson traces how bells, fines, time-sheets, schoolroom drills and sermons against idleness trained a population into the new rhythm, while older habits such as keeping "Saint Monday," the unofficial day off after Sunday, were slowly broken. What emerged was a new moral sense of time itself. When labor is bought by the hour, time becomes currency, and Thompson's own compression of the change is that time came to be spent rather than passed. Whatever isn't spent productively is wasted.

The rest of the story is acceleration. On November 18, 1883, the American railroads replaced dozens of local times with standard zones, so that noon in a given town was no longer set by the sun overhead but by a timetable drawn up somewhere else. The telegraph, the telephone, email and the smartphone followed, each sold as a way to save time. The German sociologist Hartmut Rosa, in *Beschleunigung* (2005), translated as *Social Acceleration* (2013), names the paradox that results. Modern societies accelerate their technology, accelerate the pace of social change and accelerate the pace of ordinary life, and the first doesn't relieve the third. Machines save time on each task, but the number of tasks grows faster than the savings, which is how a society full of time-saving devices ends up chronically short of time. In Rosa's account, standing still in such a society means falling behind, so everyone runs simply to stay where they are.

This is the air the Tuesday kitchen breathes. The family isn't failing a private test of discipline; it's living inside a structure that treats every hour as inventory and every unfilled hour as loss. That matters, because it means hurry is not only a personal fault to be repented of but also a social order to be understood. It doesn't let any of us off, though, because we have baptized the structure. Christians who still keep the Lord's Day have often been as fluent as anyone in the language of spent and wasted time, and we have carried that language into our churches, our families and our prayers. I have done this myself. I have used a full schedule as evidence of my importance and my exhaustion as proof of my commitment, and I have called it sacrifice when the more accurate word was vanity.

## What Chronos and Kairos Can and Cannot Carry

Christian writing on time almost always reaches for two Greek words. *Chronos*, we're told, is clock time, the steady tick of minutes; *kairos* is the charged moment, the appointed hour when something is at stake. Chronos is quantity and kairos is quality, and the gospel calls us out of the first into the second. The distinction has carried thousands of sermons, including earlier writing on this site, and it isn't simply wrong. But it's overdrawn, and honesty requires saying so before building anything on it.

The Scottish scholar James Barr made the case in *Biblical Words for Time* (1962). His target was a generation of biblical theology that had tried to read a distinctively Hebrew or Christian view of time straight out of vocabulary. Oscar Cullmann's *Christ and Time* (1946) had argued that *kairos* in the New Testament names the decisive points in God's saving plan, and others pressed the contrast between the two words much further. Barr's objection was that words don't carry theology on their backs. The biblical writers use *chronos* and *kairos* with considerable overlap, and a difference in vocabulary can't bear the weight of a worldview. The New Testament makes the point on its own. When Jesus opens his preaching in Mark, he says, "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ESV), and the word is *kairos*. But when Paul writes, "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4, ESV), the word is *chronos*, the supposedly flat and quantitative word, used for the most charged moment in history.

So the distinction survives, but it moves. What makes a moment weighty in Scripture isn't the noun used for it. It's the God who acts in it. The New Testament does teach that time has a shape, that it's heading somewhere, that certain hours are appointed and can be missed. Jesus tells his brothers, "My time has not yet come, but your time is always here" (John 7:6, ESV), a quiet rebuke to people for whom every moment is interchangeable. That conviction comes from the story Scripture tells: a creation with a beginning and a promised end, a covenant with a history, a Messiah who arrives in the fullness of time. It doesn't come from a dictionary. Sermons that make *kairos* a magic word for meaningful moments usually end up sanctifying our own sense of intensity, and intensity is exactly what the hurried already have in excess.

Read that way, the verse most often quoted on this subject becomes more interesting, not less.

> "Look carefully then how you walk, not as unwise but as wise, making the best use of the time, because the days are evil." (Ephesians 5:15–16, ESV)

Older translations said "redeeming the time." Paul's participle is *exagorazomenoi*, built on *agora*, the word for the marketplace, and it carries the sense of buying something up or buying it out. The image is commercial, and Paul doesn't reject the language of value. He asks what the time is worth and who is setting the price. The same phrase turns up in Colossians: "Walk in wisdom toward outsiders, making the best use of the time" (Colossians 4:5, ESV), where the setting is the believer's conduct before unbelieving neighbors.

The context in Ephesians decides the meaning. Paul isn't offering a productivity tip. The sentences around the verse set wisdom against folly and drunkenness against the filling of the Spirit, and they lead straight into singing, thanksgiving and the ordering of a household (Ephesians 5:18–6:4). "Because the days are evil" isn't there to raise the pressure. It's the reason attentiveness is needed: the current of the age runs toward whatever empties a life, and a person who simply drifts with it will spend his days on what doesn't matter and miss what does. To make the best use of the time, in Paul's sense, is to be awake to what the moment is actually for. That means time can be wasted by laziness, as every grandparent warned. It also means time can be wasted by efficiency. A household can fill every hour with motion and reach Saturday having done almost nothing the week actually required.

## Teach Us to Number Our Days

If Ephesians tells us to be awake to the time, Psalm 90 teaches us how. It's the only psalm headed "A Prayer of Moses, the man of God," and it may be the oldest sustained meditation on time in Scripture. It begins with God before the mountains were brought forth and sets the human span against that eternity with unsparing plainness: "For a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past, or as a watch in the night" (Psalm 90:4, ESV). Then comes the arithmetic of our lives: "The years of our life are seventy, or even by reason of strength eighty; yet their span is but toil and trouble; they are soon gone, and we fly away" (Psalm 90:10, ESV). Only after that does the psalm ask for anything.

> "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom." (Psalm 90:12, ESV)

Notice what the prayer asks and what it doesn't. It doesn't ask for more days, and it doesn't ask for a method to get more out of the ones we have. It asks to be taught to count them, and it expects the counting to yield wisdom. The numbering in Psalm 90 runs the opposite way from the scheduler's arithmetic. The scheduler counts hours in order to fill them; the psalmist counts days in order to be sobered by how few they are, and the sobering becomes a heart that knows what they're for. The psalm ends where that wisdom leads, asking God to "establish the work of our hands" (Psalm 90:17, ESV). The work matters. It lasts only as God establishes it.

Burkeman arrives at something close to the first half of this. His four thousand weeks are Psalm 90:10 in a secular key, and his counsel is to accept finitude rather than fight it: to admit that we'll never get everything done, to choose deliberately what we'll leave undone, and to stop treating the present as preparation for a future in which we'll finally have caught up. That's wise, and many Christians would be healthier for reading it. But notice where it stops. For Burkeman, finitude is a fact to be accepted with as much equanimity as we can gather. For the psalmist, finitude is a fact to be prayed about, addressed to the God who is "from everlasting to everlasting" (Psalm 90:2, ESV), and the prayer goes on: "Satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love, that we may rejoice and be glad all our days" (Psalm 90:14, ESV). Secular wisdom about time names the symptom well. It can't say whether anyone receives the days we give. Psalm 90 can, and that decides whether our limits are a sentence or a calling.

Augustine took the question further than anyone in the ancient church. Book XI of the *Confessions*, written around 400, begins as a reflection on the first verse of Genesis and becomes a long wrestling with what time actually is. He admits, in a line that has become famous, that as long as no one asks him what time is he knows, and as soon as he tries to explain it to someone who asks, he doesn't. His tentative answer is that past, present and future exist for us only in the mind's present: the past as memory, the present as attention, the future as expectation. Time, he proposes, is a kind of stretching or distension of the soul, the *distentio animi*. Then the philosophy turns into confession. Near the end of the book Augustine describes himself as scattered among times whose order he doesn't understand, his thoughts torn apart by the tumult of change, and he prays to be gathered into the One, following Paul's "one thing I do: forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead" (Philippians 3:13, ESV).

This is the most modern thing in that ancient book. Augustine's picture of a self stretched across its demands, never wholly present to any of them, could stand as a description of the Tuesday kitchen. And his remedy isn't a better arrangement of the demands. It's to be recollected, gathered back into one before God. Jesus said something close to it to a woman who was, in Luke's words, "distracted with much serving" (Luke 10:40, ESV), and it's worth remembering that what distracted Martha was hospitality to Jesus himself. "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary" (Luke 10:41–42, ESV). Her work wasn't bad. The many things had scattered her, and she wanted her sister scattered too.

## A Time for Every Matter Under Heaven

Ecclesiastes 3 is usually read at funerals and heard as comfort, but in context it's closer to a confrontation. "For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven" (Ecclesiastes 3:1, ESV). Fourteen pairs follow, and the Hebrew word for time sounds twenty-eight times in seven verses: birth and death, planting and uprooting, weeping and laughing, embracing and refraining, war and peace. The poem is beautiful, and its first effect is to take control out of our hands. We don't choose most of our seasons. The time to mourn arrives on its own schedule, and so did the time to be born. In the Preacher's world, times are given before they are managed.

Then he returns to the question he has been asking since his first chapter: "What gain has the worker from his toil?" (Ecclesiastes 3:9, ESV). His answer cuts two ways. "I have seen the business that God has given to the children of man to be busy with" (Ecclesiastes 3:10, ESV). The busyness is given, and it isn't the enemy. But the next verse sets it inside a frame no schedule can hold: "He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also, he has put eternity into man's heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end" (Ecclesiastes 3:11, ESV). We are creatures of the season with eternity in our hearts, and we can't see the whole. That's the human condition in a sentence, and it explains the peculiar misery of productivity culture, which asks creatures who can't see the whole to optimize it anyway.

Across the book the Preacher tries everything a person can spend a life on, work and pleasure and wisdom and accumulation, and returns again and again to the word *hebel*, vapor or breath. The things aren't worthless. They simply can't answer the question of what a life is for, and his conclusion doesn't come from a better allocation of hours: "The end of the matter; all has been heard. Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man" (Ecclesiastes 12:13, ESV). The answer to hurry, in Ecclesiastes, is orientation before it is ever efficiency.

James carries the same wisdom into the marketplace, and his target is recognizably us. "Come now, you who say, 'Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a town and spend a year there and trade and make a profit'" (James 4:13, ESV). The merchant's plan is sensible: a destination, a timeframe, a business model. James doesn't condemn planning. He condemns planning as though tomorrow belonged to the planner.

> "yet you do not know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes." (James 4:14, ESV)

And he gives the alternative: "Instead you ought to say, 'If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that'" (James 4:15, ESV).

That sentence can become a pious qualifier tacked onto a plan made without reference to God. James means more. To say "if the Lord wills" honestly is to hold the calendar with an open hand: to plan the year, sign the children up for the season and think about the next job, while knowing all along that the squares on the page are provisional, lent rather than owned. The man in James 4 isn't wicked because he makes a plan. He's foolish because he has forgotten he is mist. A family calendar with no room left for interruption, for the neighbor's emergency, for the child who needs to talk at bedtime, for the possibility that the Lord's will differs from ours, has quietly made the same mistake.

## Hurry and the Unhurried Christ

John Ortberg tells a story in *The Life You've Always Wanted* (1997) about asking a wise friend, the philosopher Dallas Willard, what he needed to do to be spiritually healthy. As Ortberg recounts it, Willard answered that he must ruthlessly eliminate hurry from his life, and, pressed for what else, said there was nothing else. The line has traveled far, and it gave John Mark Comer the title of *The Ruthless Elimination of Hurry* (2019), which argues that hurry and love can't live together, because love takes time, and which commends old practices: silence and solitude, Sabbath, simplicity, a slower tempo in small things.

The distinction underneath is worth stating precisely. Busyness is a condition of the calendar. Hurry is a condition of the soul, the inability to be where you are because part of you is already at the next thing. A mother of small children can be very busy and not hurried, wholly present to the child in front of her while the list stays long. A retired man with an empty week can be hurried, unable to sit through dinner without checking his phone. The Preacher calls busyness a gift. What Scripture refuses is the scattered heart that can't hear "one thing is necessary" because it's already reaching for the many.

Jesus shows that the two can come apart. His days were crowded; in Mark's first chapter the whole town gathers at the door. Yet "rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed" (Mark 1:35, ESV). Told that everyone was looking for him, he went on to other towns instead, "for that is why I came out" (Mark 1:38, ESV). When the crowds left the disciples no leisure even to eat, he said, "Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while" (Mark 6:31, ESV). Hearing that Lazarus was ill, "he stayed two days longer in the place where he was" (John 11:6, ESV). He slept in a boat in a storm, and he left towns with sick people still in them. This is the Incarnation worked out in a daily schedule. The eternal Son took a body that needed sleep, one place at a time, days of twenty-four hours, and he didn't apologize for those limits. They were the shape of his obedience.

The strongest objection deserves its full weight. Suffering doesn't wait for our rhythms, and a theology of limits can become an alibi; the priest and the Levite passed by on the other side (Luke 10:31–32), perhaps with religious reasons. Sharper still, a skeptic might say that counsel to eliminate hurry is a luxury good. The single mother working two jobs doesn't need a book telling her to slow down. She needs a living wage and a schedule she can predict. If Rosa is right that acceleration is structural, preaching slowness as private discipline turns an injustice into a personal failing and hands the exhausted one more reason for guilt.

Concede it. Where hurry is imposed by an economy that treats workers' hours as disposable, the church's word isn't "slow down" but the prophets' word about justice. The Sabbath in Deuteronomy was given to freed slaves and extended to servants, animals and the stranger (Deuteronomy 5:14–15), so biblical rest was always a matter of justice as well as devotion. But the objection proves less than it claims. The nurse who never sleeps begins to make fatal errors, and the choice is finally between sustainable presence and a collapse that helps no one. And most of us aren't the single mother. We're people with some options, choosing hurry because it makes us feel necessary, and the objection that protects the truly pressed can't be borrowed to protect us.

## The Calendar Is a Confession

Every household keeps a calendar, and every calendar is a theological document. It records what we actually believe matters, not in the words we say on Sunday but in the hours we give and withhold: what can be sacrificed and what never is, which relationships get the first fruits and which get the leftovers. Read honestly, it's the truest account of a family's working theology, and for most of us, me included, the account is uncomfortable.

The lesson of Benedict's horarium isn't that households should pray seven times a day. It's that a life ordered to God needs a few fixed points the rest of the week bends around, rather than the other way round: the Lord's Day and its worship, a table the family shares on most nights, prayer that doesn't depend on how the day went. Fixed points work the way riverbanks do, giving water a direction instead of letting it spread into a marsh. The same wisdom at the scale of the week is [the Sabbath](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it), at the scale of the year [the church's calendar](/writing/why-the-church-has-a-calendar), and the phone that has become the household's second clock has [its own essay](/writing/digital-discipleship). Keeping any of them will usually mean subtraction, and subtraction in an accelerating culture feels like falling behind. By the culture's measure it is.

Yet the deepest word Scripture speaks about time isn't counsel. The verb Paul uses for making the best use of the time is the one he uses in Galatians for what Christ has done: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13, ESV), and God sent his Son "to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons" (Galatians 4:5, ESV). Barr's warning applies here too, and a single verb shouldn't carry more than it can. But Paul's first readers might be forgiven for noticing that before they were asked to buy up the time, they had been bought. Whatever our use of the time is, it isn't the anxious effort of people trying to purchase a life worth having. That purchase was made on an afternoon outside Jerusalem, when the one who never hurried said, "It is finished" (John 19:30, ESV), and on the third day an empty tomb announced that time is headed toward a morning rather than toward exhaustion.

None of that takes the clock away. The practice is still at seven and the weeks still go by at the rate they always have. Psalm 90 doesn't promise otherwise. It asks us to count the days honestly, to feel how few they are, and to ask God to establish what our hands have done, because we can't. The squares on the refrigerator door are filling again, and they'll say what we believe whether we read them or not. The question the psalm leaves in the kitchen is whether we'll read them before the week is spent, and whether, having read them, we'll hand back to God one of the hours we were sure we couldn't spare.
