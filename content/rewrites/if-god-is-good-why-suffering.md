The question comes in two places, and it uses the same words in both. In a philosophy seminar, or in the head of a sixteen-year-old lying awake after a documentary about famine, it runs like this: if God is wholly good and all-powerful, why is there so much suffering? That's an argument, and its premises can be tested. In a hospital corridor at two in the morning the same words aren't an argument at all. They're a cry from someone whose child is on the other side of the door. The first is the philosopher's problem. The second is the sufferer's problem. Much of the harm Christians have done on this subject comes from confusing the two, answering the cry with a syllogism or treating the argument as though it could be met only with tears.

This was the argument I couldn't answer when I was an atheist, and it didn't go away when I became a Christian. So what follows tries to do two things that belong together and must never be mixed. It answers the philosopher's problem as honestly as the best work on it allows, which is further than most skeptics have been told and not as far as most Christians would like. And it refuses to hand that answer to the person in the corridor, because the Bible itself refuses, and because the Christian reply to the sufferer is not finally a reason. It's an event: God entering the suffering he is accused of permitting, and promising to end it.

## The oldest argument against God

The best-known form of the argument survives because a Christian copied it down in order to answer it. Early in the fourth century, in chapter 13 of *On the Anger of God*, Lactantius attributed to Epicurus a dilemma. If God wants to remove evils and can't, he's weak. If he can and won't, he's envious. If he neither wants to nor can, he's both. And if he both can and will, which alone is worthy of God, where do evils come from, and why doesn't he take them away? The attribution is disputed. Nothing in the surviving writings of Epicurus contains the argument, and many scholars think Lactantius was passing on a later skeptic's formulation under a famous name. But he stated the argument at full strength, and the church has lived with it ever since. In Part 10 of Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779) the skeptic Philo calls these Epicurus's old questions, still unanswered, and in Part 11 he argues that no one looking at this world without prior belief would infer from it a God both almighty and good.

In 1955 J. L. Mackie tightened the argument into logic in "Evil and Omnipotence." Three propositions, he argued, can't all be true together: God is omnipotent, God is wholly good, and evil exists. Religious belief, Mackie concluded, wasn't merely unsupported but positively irrational. He anticipated the obvious Christian reply, too. If God could make free creatures who sometimes choose the good, why couldn't he make free creatures who always do?

This is serious thought from serious people, and it deserves to be met in the form its defenders would sign. The argument also has a moral form, stronger than the logical one, and Dostoevsky gave it its most devastating statement in *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880). In the chapter called "Rebellion," Ivan Karamazov tells his brother Alyosha, a novice monk, about children tortured by the adults responsible for them: a little girl shut all night in a freezing outhouse by her parents, a serf boy torn apart by a general's hounds before his mother's eyes. Ivan doesn't deny God. He grants that there may be a final harmony in which every sufferer is reconciled, and he wants no part of it. If the harmony costs the tears of one tortured child, the price is too high, and he respectfully hands back his ticket. Ivan's complaint isn't that the sums fail to add up. It's that some things should never be entered into a sum at all.

## What the free will defense proved, and what it didn't

In 1974 Alvin Plantinga's short book *God, Freedom, and Evil* changed the discussion, and it's worth stating exactly what he claimed, because believers and skeptics both overstate it. He began with a distinction. A theodicy tries to explain God's actual reasons for permitting evil. A defense only tries to show that God and evil are logically consistent, and for that it needs a possible story, not a true one. Plantinga offered a defense. A world with creatures significantly free to do good and evil is more valuable, other things being equal, than a world without them, and God can't cause free creatures to choose only the good, because a caused choice wouldn't be free. Then came the move that answered Mackie. It's possible, Plantinga argued, that every free creature God could have made would go wrong at least once in any world God could bring about, a condition he called transworld depravity. If that's even possible, then it's possible that God couldn't create a world containing moral good without also permitting moral evil, and Mackie's three propositions aren't contradictory after all.

Mackie himself, in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982), conceded that the problem of evil doesn't show the central doctrines of theism to be logically inconsistent with one another, though he doubted that this amounted to a real solution. William Rowe, an atheist, remarked in 1979 that no one, in his judgment, had succeeded in showing God and evil to be logically incompatible. Not every philosopher agrees, but many on both sides now regard the logical problem as largely answered.

That settles less than it sounds like. The defense speaks most naturally to moral evil, the suffering human beings inflict on one another, and much less to the tsunami or the bone cancer in a six-year-old. Plantinga noted, as a bare logical possibility, that such evils might come from the free choices of nonhuman spirits; as logic it works, and almost no one finds it satisfying. Others argue that a world of stable natural laws must be one where fire burns and tectonic plates shift. That has force, but it doesn't tell a mother why the plates shifted under her daughter's school.

Christians often add that calling a child's torture evil, not merely unfortunate, appeals to a moral standard a purely material universe struggles to supply; Timothy Keller pressed the point in *Walking with God through Pain and Suffering* (2013). Honesty requires the reply: the argument from evil is internal, testing the believer's own claims against the world, and needs no moral realism from the skeptic. What remains is smaller. The skeptic who is genuinely outraged, and not merely testing consistency, is standing on a floor his own account of the world still has to explain.

## The fawn in the forest

If the logical problem has largely been answered, the evidential problem hasn't, and a Christian who pretends otherwise has stopped telling the truth. Its classic statement came in Rowe's 1979 paper in the *American Philosophical Quarterly*, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism." Rowe didn't claim that evil makes God impossible, only improbable. In a distant forest lightning strikes a dead tree, the woods catch fire, and a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in agony for several days before it dies. No one sees it, and no soul is made by it. What greater good, Rowe asked, did that suffering make possible that an all-powerful God couldn't have secured some other way? So far as we can tell, none. And a wholly good God would prevent intense suffering that served no greater good. So, probably, there is no such God.

Rowe was no scoffer. In the same paper he defended what he called friendly atheism, the view that believers can be rational in their belief even though, as he was convinced, they are wrong. The fawn also wasn't his last example. The philosopher Bruce Russell had pressed a case from the news, the murder of a five-year-old girl in 1986, and in "Evil and Theodicy" (1988) Rowe set it beside the fawn, labeling the two E1 and E2. One is suffering that no human caused and no one witnessed; the other is a human horror done to a child. His claim about both was the same: no good we know of would justify a perfectly good God in permitting either.

The most serious reply goes by the name skeptical theism, and its most influential early form came from Stephen Wykstra in 1984. Wykstra pointed to a hidden step in Rowe's argument, the move from "I can see no good reason for this" to "there is no good reason for this." That move holds only if we'd reasonably expect to see the reason if there were one, a condition Wykstra called CORNEA, the condition of reasonable epistemic access. If God exists, the gap between his understanding and ours is at least as wide as the gap between a parent and an infant, and an infant has no idea why it's being held still for a vaccination.

The reply is real, but it carries a cost. Critics press that if we can never judge a horror pointless, we seem to lose the ground for preventing horrors ourselves. Skeptical theists answer that our duty to pull a child from the fire rests on God's command and the child's need, not on guesses about providence. A deeper difficulty remains. Skeptical theism can show that Rowe's argument proves less than it claims. It can't give anyone a reason to trust that the God who permitted the fawn's death is good. That reason has to come from somewhere else.

Marilyn McCord Adams, a philosopher and Episcopal priest, saw this most clearly. In *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God* (1999) she defined horrendous evils as evils so severe that taking part in them, as victim or perpetrator, gives a person reason to doubt that their life could be a great good to them on the whole. Her examples included torture, the abuse of children and the death camps. The standard defenses, she argued, fail exactly here. A God who makes a good world overall, while some lives are simply spent as its cost, hasn't been good to those persons. If God is good, he must be good to each one, defeating the horror within each life that suffered it. And she argued, against the habit of her discipline, that a generic deity can't supply the resources for that. For her, incarnation, cross and resurrection were no ornaments on the answer; they were where she found it.

## The reasons the church has given

Augustine spent nearly a decade of his youth among the Manichees, who made evil a power as real as God. Book VII of his *Confessions*, written around 400, records his escape. Evil, he came to see, isn't a thing God made. It is a privation, the lack or corruption of a good that ought to be there, the way blindness is the absence of sight. Evil is parasitic, then, and never God's rival. But that tells us what evil is, not why a good God permits it. Augustine's fuller answer, in chapter 27 of the *Enchiridion* (around 421), was that God judged it better to bring good out of evil than to permit no evil at all. That's true as far as it goes, and it leaves Ivan standing where he was.

John Hick, in *Evil and the God of Love* (1966), set against this Augustinian line what he called the Irenaean one, tracing it somewhat loosely to the second-century bishop Irenaeus. Humanity, on Hick's account, wasn't created finished and then ruined. It was created immature, at an epistemic distance from God, in a world hard enough to make real virtue possible: courage requires danger, forgiveness an actual wrong. Borrowing a phrase from John Keats, Hick called the world a vale of soul-making, and anyone who has watched grief make a person larger has seen the truth in that. But Hick admitted that much suffering is dysteleological, his word for suffering that crushes rather than builds, and his final appeal was to a life beyond this one where the process would be completed. The idea is older and sturdier than Hick's later theology, which left the creeds behind, but it can't carry the worst cases alone.

Behind both sits the greater-good theodicy: God permits each evil because it serves a larger good we can't yet see. Here Ivan lands hardest. Pressed to its end, that answer makes the suffering child a means, a cost paid toward someone else's benefit, and agrees with Ivan that the ticket was bought with a child. I don't believe God balances his books that way.

The sharpest living critic of these answers is Bart Ehrman, and his case should be put the way he'd put it. A New Testament scholar and former evangelical, he explains in *God's Problem* (2008) that what finally took his faith was suffering, more than his work on the manuscripts. He doesn't claim the Bible ignores the problem; he argues that it gives answers that don't agree: suffering as punishment in the prophets, as redemptive, as a test in the prose frame of Job, as a mystery not to be questioned in Job's poetry, as the work of cosmic powers soon to be overthrown in the apocalyptic books. Their disagreement, on his reading, leaves the Bible without a single answer, and none of its answers satisfies him. The biblical voice he treats with most sympathy is the sober realism of Ecclesiastes.

There's more to say in reply. But Ehrman is right about something Christians should admit first. We've often used these answers not to help the suffering but to protect ourselves from them. I have preached versions of them. I've handed a grieving person an explanation because it spared me the silence and my own helplessness at the bedside, and I've mistaken the closing of a face for peace. Used that way, the theodicies comfort the person who isn't suffering. They're what the well say to the sick so the well can leave the room.

## What the Bible does with the question

The Bible doesn't speak about suffering in one flat voice. But the most important thing it does with the question is to put it into the mouths of its own saints, and to judge the people who answered too quickly.

Job has to be read whole, because its meaning lives in the arc. It opens on a man "blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil" (Job 1:1, ESV). In a scene in heaven Job never sees, the accuser suggests his piety has been bought, and God permits a test. In a single day Job loses his herds, his servants and all ten of his children; then his body goes. His friends come, and they do the one thing in the book anyone does right: "they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great" (Job 2:13, ESV).

Then Job curses the day he was born, the friends open their mouths, and through round after round of speeches, with a younger man, Elihu, joining them near the end, they explain. They aren't villains. They're orthodox, and what they say is the respectable theology of their day and much of ours: God is just, so Job must have sinned, and if he'll repent the suffering will lift. The reader knows from the first chapter that they're wrong. Job knows only that they're lying about him, and he turns to God with a complaint that comes close to blasphemy and demands a hearing.

When it comes, "the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1, ESV): "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding" (Job 38:4, ESV). For four chapters God leads Job through a creation too wild for him to manage. He never mentions the scene in heaven or tells Job why. He does rebuke him. The first words are "Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge?" (Job 38:2, ESV), and later God asks, "Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" (Job 40:8, ESV). Job had overreached. But God never charges him with the hidden sin the friends insisted on, and in that silence their theory falls. Job answers, "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5, ESV). The next line, "therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6, ESV), is notoriously hard in the Hebrew; it may mean that Job withdraws his case and is consoled. Either way, what changes Job isn't an explanation. It's an encounter.

Then comes the verdict the church has often skipped. God says to Eliphaz, "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7, ESV). The men who defended God are rebuked. The man who protested is vindicated, and he's told to pray for them. Ehrman's objection to the ending deserves its hearing: Job receives double his livestock and ten new children, as if children could be replaced. An old reading notices that the text doubles everything except the children, as though the first ten weren't lost for good, but that is a hope, not a proof, and the objection should be left standing as a wound in the text. What the book establishes is simpler and harder. God preferred the honest complaint to the tidy defense.

Job isn't alone. Psalm 88 is the darkest prayer in Scripture. It begins, "O LORD, God of my salvation, I cry out day and night before you" (Psalm 88:1, ESV), and ends without any turn toward praise: "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18, ESV). Israel kept that in its hymnbook. Walter Brueggemann argued in "The Costly Loss of Lament" (1986) that when worship loses such prayer, the covenant becomes one-sided: the weaker partner loses the right to speak back, and the question of justice goes quiet. A church that can only praise has lost the ability to tell God the truth.

Habakkuk opens the same way: "O LORD, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear?" (Habakkuk 1:2, ESV). When God answers that judgment is coming through the Babylonians, the complaint only sharpens, because now the problem is God's own character. The book never resolves that into a theory. It ends in a trust that has already counted its losses, the fig tree without blossom and the stalls without a herd: "yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation" (Habakkuk 3:18, ESV).

And Jesus, told of the eighteen killed by a falling tower in Siloam, refused the friends' equation: "do you think that they were worse offenders than all the others who lived in Jerusalem?" (Luke 13:4, ESV). His answer was no, and then he turned the question on the askers: "No, I tell you; but unless you repent, you will all likewise perish" (Luke 13:5, ESV). He refused to read the dead as sinners and refused to let the living feel safe. Suffering isn't a receipt for sin.

## The God who does not stay out of it

If Christianity were one more theodicy, it would deserve Ivan's ticket. At its center is a claim about where God was.

At the ninth hour on the day he died, "Jesus cried with a loud voice, 'Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?' which means, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Mark 15:34, ESV). He was praying the first line of Psalm 22, a lament. In the hour on which the whole faith rests, the one Christians confess as God the Son isn't explaining suffering from above it. He's inside it, praying Israel's words of abandonment.

Jürgen Moltmann, who survived the 1943 Hamburg firestorm as a teenager, built a theology on that cry. In *The Crucified God* (1972) he argued that the cross must be the ground of everything Christians say about God, and that at the cross the Son suffers dying while the Father suffers the death of the Son. God, on this reading, doesn't permit suffering from a safe height. He takes it into his own life. Dietrich Bonhoeffer had reached toward the same insight in a letter from a Nazi prison on 16 July 1944, where he wrote that the Bible directs us to God's powerlessness and suffering, and that "only the suffering God can help."

Accuracy matters here, because this is contested ground among orthodox Christians. The historic church, from the Fathers through Aquinas to the Westminster Confession, which describes God as "without body, parts, or passions," has taught that the divine nature is impassible. Impassibility doesn't mean God is cold. It means he isn't overwhelmed or changed by his creatures, and its best defenders argue that this is what makes his love complete rather than reactive. The same tradition insists, as the Second Council of Constantinople confessed in 553, that the one crucified in the flesh is true God and one of the Holy Trinity. God the Son truly suffered and died in the human nature he made his own. Moltmann went further and placed suffering within the divine life itself. Defenders of the classical view answer him. Thomas Weinandy argues in *Does God Suffer?* (2000) that God's love is already fully in act and can't be enlarged by suffering, that the Son truly suffers as man because the incarnation is real, and that a God who suffered in his divine nature as we do would be caught in evil rather than able to overcome it. That debate is unsettled among faithful theologians, and this essay doesn't pretend to close it. Both sides confess what matters most. The one who hung on the cross and prayed Psalm 22 was not less than God.

One caution belongs beside that. In *Night*, his memoir of the camps, Elie Wiesel describes the hanging of a boy too light to die quickly. Someone behind him asks where God is, and Wiesel hears a voice within himself answer that God is there, hanging on the gallows. Moltmann set that scene inside his argument. Christians should be slow to follow. Wiesel was describing the death of his childhood faith, not a doctrine of the cross, and a Jewish child killed by a regime too many baptized people served can't be drafted into an illustration of our theology. A Christian can only let that page stand, grieve, and repent of the history that made it possible.

What the cross does give is the reason skeptical theism couldn't supply. We don't know why the fawn burned, or why any particular child suffers what no child should. But the Christian claim is that we know who God is, because we've seen what he did when evil came for him: rather than standing at a distance and permitting, he entered. At the grave of Lazarus, about to call his friend out of it, "Jesus wept" (John 11:35, ESV). He didn't hurry to the good ending.

And the cross isn't the end of the story, which keeps it from being merely shared misery. Paul wrote, "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us" (Romans 8:18, ESV). He doesn't call the sufferings small. The creation, he says, "was subjected to futility, not willingly," but "in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption" (Romans 8:20-21, ESV). It "has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now," and we ourselves "groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:22-23, ESV). Paul names the groan and then locates it inside a labor that is going somewhere, because "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV). That is Adams's answer to her own question: a horror is defeated not when it's explained but when the life it tried to ruin is raised and made whole. The last promise of Scripture is a hand, not a theory: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:4, ESV).

Ivan could reply that no future tenderness makes a present torture acceptable, and Christianity doesn't claim it does. Alyosha's answer in the novel is no argument; he reminds his brother that there is one who can forgive everything, because he gave his own innocent blood for all. In a letter of August 1879 to Konstantin Pobedonostsev, Dostoevsky wrote that he meant the next book of the novel, "The Russian Monk," the life and teaching of the elder Zosima, as his answer to the negative side Ivan had voiced, and he confessed his fear that it would not be answer enough. What he offered was a portrait of love rather than a proof. Ivan returned his ticket to a harmony purchased with children. The Christian hope is something else: a God who paid the price in his own flesh and who will not let one child's tears be forgotten or wasted.

## For the one in the corridor

To the philosopher, then, the honest answer runs this far. There's no contradiction in believing that a good and all-powerful God permits evil. The evidential argument is real, and no one can see God's reasons for the fawn or the child, but the Christian's confidence that they are good rests on the cross and the empty tomb. The older answers explain much about this world. They don't explain every horror in it, and a Christian who says they do is lying for God, which Job 42:7 suggests God doesn't want.

That answer belongs in the seminar. It doesn't belong at the bedside. Job's friends weren't wrong to believe God is just. They were wrong to use what they believed on a man sitting in ashes. The sufferer is asking something other than whether the propositions are consistent: whether anyone is there, and whether he can be trusted with this. You don't answer that with a defense. You answer it the way the friends did in their first seven days, by sitting down in the ashes and saying nothing that protects yourself, and the way the psalms do, by teaching a person to say the worst thing to God's face and call it prayer.

If you're the one in the corridor, you're allowed to pray Psalm 88 and say what Job said. Your anger doesn't put you outside God's reach; he kept Job's complaint and rebuked the men who tried to silence it. But the dark isn't telling you the truth when it says your life is finished. If it has pressed you anywhere near that edge, call or text 988, or go to the nearest emergency room, tonight. Reaching for help is no failure of faith, and staying close enough to keep someone alive in the ashes is exactly what friends are for.

For the rest of us the weight lands elsewhere. We've wanted the answer mostly for ourselves, to make the ward a thing that happens to other people for reasons. So did Job's friends, and God was angry with them. The repentance this subject asks of Christians is to keep confessing that God is good while we stop defending him in ways that require someone else's child to be a lesson. And the skeptic who still holds Ivan's ticket isn't asked to pretend the world is fine. He's asked only to consider that the God he accuses may already have come down into the ward, taken the worst of it into himself, and promised a morning when every tear will be wiped away by a hand with scars in it. Until that morning the child is still in the ward and the question is still in the corridor. The ones who have helped there, in every century, are the ones who stayed.
