Most arguments about worship happen in the car on the way home. One spouse found the service moving and the other found it loud. A teenager says the old prayers are boring and nobody means them; a grandmother says the new songs are all about how the singer feels and never say anything about God. A young couple new in town ranks churches the way they'd rank restaurants, by atmosphere and by whether they "got something out of it." Underneath sits a question almost nobody asks out loud: what is worship for, and what is it doing to us?

In one church the congregation stands, sits and kneels through an order older than anyone in the room: confession and pardon, several Scripture readings, a sermon, the Nicene Creed, prayers for the world, the Lord's Supper, a blessing. Down the road a band leads half an hour of singing, the pastor preaches a long message, there is an invitation to respond, and a final song sends everyone out. Both are Christian worship. But they come out of different histories, and over years they make different kinds of Christians.

I have a preference, and I'm going to try to keep it out of this for as long as I can, because the question isn't finally one of preference. A fifth-century theologian, Prosper of Aquitaine, gave the church the principle usually shortened to *lex orandi, lex credendi*: the law of praying establishes the law of believing. What a church prays, week after week, becomes what it believes, whether or not it ever intends to teach it. That is the lens here: what each tradition gained and lost, and why the choice most families actually face, between two styles, is the wrong place to fight.

The distinction that carries the argument is between style and shape. Style is the idiom: organ or guitar, robes or jeans, hymnal or screen, old words or new ones. Shape is the sequence of what a congregation actually does and the story that sequence rehearses: whether it hears Scripture at length, confesses its sin, receives a word of pardon, confesses its faith, prays for the world, comes to the Table, and is sent. The worship wars were fought almost entirely over style. Formation happens mostly through shape.

## What the Church Did Before Anyone Argued About It

The word *liturgy* is usually glossed as "the work of the people," and the gloss is slightly off in a way that matters. In classical Athens a *leitourgia* was a public service performed on the people's behalf, often at a wealthy citizen's expense: outfitting a warship, funding a festival. It was work done *for* the people before it was work done *by* them. The Letter to the Hebrews calls the risen Jesus "a minister in the holy places, in the true tent that the Lord set up, not man" (Hebrews 8:2, ESV), and the word translated *minister* is *leitourgos*. The first liturgist of the church is Christ, offering himself to the Father on our behalf. That turns out to be the key to the whole quarrel.

After Peter's sermon at Pentecost, Luke compresses the church's earliest worship into one verse:

> And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers. (Acts 2:42, ESV)

Luke had already given the pattern a story. On the road to Emmaus the risen Jesus opens the Scriptures to two disciples, and they know him only when he breaks the bread at their table (Luke 24:13-35). Word, then Table. Luke wasn't writing a rubric, but the church's oldest Sunday pattern follows the same road.

Around 155, Justin Martyr, a philosopher converted to Christ and later executed in Rome, wrote to the emperor to explain what Christians actually did when they gathered. In chapter 67 of his *First Apology* he describes Sunday. Believers from city and countryside gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read for as long as time allows. The presider urges the people to imitate what they have heard, and all stand to pray. Bread, wine and water are brought, the presider gives thanks as best he is able, the people answer Amen, and the deacons carry the gifts to those who are absent. Those with means give what they choose, and the money goes to widows and orphans, the sick, prisoners and strangers. Sunday, Justin says, is the day God began the creation and the day Jesus rose from the dead.

That paragraph contains the bones of nearly every historic Christian liturgy since. In *The Shape of the Liturgy* (1945) the Anglican monk Gregory Dix argued that beneath the variety of eucharistic rites lay a fourfold action taken from Jesus' own hands at the Last Supper: he took the bread, gave thanks, broke it and gave it. Scholars have argued with his history, but the insight has held: Christian worship has a shape before it has a style, and the shape is older than any of our denominations.

Prosper's principle belongs here, and its setting is worth knowing. He came from Aquitaine in Gaul and spent his life defending Augustine against those who held that human beings could take the first step toward God on their own. In the *Indiculus*, a digest of the Roman church's teaching on grace usually dated between 435 and 442, he pointed to the intercessions the church's ministers offered everywhere, received from the apostles: prayers that unbelievers would be given faith, that heretics and schismatics would return, that the fallen would repent. Why would the church ask God to give these things if they were not God's to give? The church's prayer already confessed that even the beginning of faith is grace. His sentence was that the law of supplication should establish the law of believing; *lex orandi, lex credendi* is the later shorthand. It was born as an argument about grace, and it has held as a broader truth: the way the church prays teaches theology whether anyone means it to or not. Every service teaches by what it says, what it repeats and what it leaves out.

## How the Old Shape Failed, and What the Reformers Did About It

Anyone who defends the historic liturgy has to reckon honestly with what it became. By the late Middle Ages in the Latin West, the shape Justin described was intact on paper and largely lost in practice. The Mass was said in a language most worshipers could not understand, at an altar at some distance from them, and for many the high point was not receiving the bread but seeing it lifted at the elevation. So few people actually took Communion that the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 had to require the faithful to receive at least once a year, at Easter. The form had survived, and the people had been moved outside it.

The Reformers answered with a principle the contemporary movement would later rediscover without always knowing its source: worship must be understood by the people who offer it. Paul had laid down the rule while correcting the Corinthians' uninterpreted tongues:

> Nevertheless, in church I would rather speak five words with my mind in order to instruct others, than ten thousand words in a tongue. (1 Corinthians 14:19, ESV)

Luther published his German Mass in 1526 for ordinary people who knew no Latin. Thomas Cranmer's first Book of Common Prayer appeared in English in 1549, its preface lamenting that the old services had so broken up the reading of Scripture that the Bible was never heard through, and its daily prayers arranged so that the Psalter would be read every month. In Geneva, Calvin gave the congregation the Psalms in French meter to sing, and he wanted the Lord's Supper every Sunday; the city council allowed it four times a year, and he lived with a practice he believed was wrong.

Notice what the Reformers did not do. They translated the service, gave the singing back to the congregation, restored preaching and brought the people back to the Table, but for the most part they kept the shape. The vernacular principle was a demand that the whole congregation understand and take part in the church's inherited worship, not a license to reinvent it every week.

The fight over new songs is old too. Every hymn the grandmother loves was once new, and when Isaac Watts published *Hymns and Spiritual Songs* in 1707, many churches that sang only the metrical Psalms treated his hymns as human words presumptuously set beside the words of God.

The free-church tradition carries a costlier objection to set forms, and it didn't begin with guitars. When the Act of Uniformity in 1662 required every English minister to use the Book of Common Prayer, some two thousand who would not conform lost their pulpits. John Bunyan, jailed in Bedford for preaching outside the established church, wrote in those years that true prayer comes from the Spirit and the heart, not from a form composed by others and imposed by law. His quarrel was with a book made the condition of prayer, and with a government presuming to dictate the words in which a Christian pours out his soul. Those who distrust written prayers as "canned" are, whether they know it or not, heirs of a conviction people once went to prison for.

## From the Camp Meeting to the Worship Set

The contemporary service most Americans know is not simply the free-church tradition with better sound equipment. Its more direct ancestor was born on the frontier. In August 1801 thousands gathered for days at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, for a communion season that became one of the most famous revivals in American history, and the camp meeting spread across the new country with a new way of ordering worship. James F. White, in *Protestant Worship: Traditions in Transition* (1989), called it the frontier tradition and argued that it became the most widespread pattern of American Protestant worship. Its shape was simple: singing to prepare the hearers, a sermon aimed at conversion, a call to respond.

Charles Finney gave that pattern a theory. In his *Lectures on Revivals of Religion* (1835) he argued that a revival was not a miracle but the result of using the right means, and he defended his "new measures," the protracted meeting and the anxious bench, against critics who found them manipulative. His deeper claim was that God had prescribed no fixed form of worship, so the church should adopt whatever measures proved effective. Once worship is justified by results, every element can be judged the way a business judges a process: does it draw, does it produce the response we want? Finney was a sincere man who saw many come to faith, and an outspoken opponent of slavery. But he bequeathed the American church a pragmatic principle that outlived his revivals.

The contemporary service as we know it came together after 1965 from more than one root. Lester Ruth and Swee Hong Lim, its most careful historians, tell the story in *Lovin' on Jesus: A Concise History of Contemporary Worship* (2017) and the fuller *A History of Contemporary Praise & Worship* (2021), and they trace two streams. One was pragmatic and evangelistic: update the church's music and manner so that a new generation could hear the gospel. Much of its early energy came from the Jesus Movement of the late 1960s and early 1970s. Chuck Smith, pastor of Calvary Chapel in Costa Mesa from 1965, opened his church to young converts from the counterculture, and in 1971 Maranatha Music was founded out of that circle to spread the simple, singable songs its musicians were writing. The other stream, which they call praise and worship, was Pentecostal, carried especially through the Latter Rain movement of the late 1940s: a theology of praise that read Psalm 22:3, "Yet you are holy, enthroned on the praises of Israel" (ESV), as a promise that God makes his presence known when his people praise him. In the early 1980s John Wimber and the Vineyard added a stress on intimacy, songs sung to God rather than about him. And in 1975 Bill Hybels founded Willow Creek Community Church outside Chicago, beginning from a canvass of the neighborhood about why people stayed away from church and building a "seeker service" to remove the obstacles they named. Ruth and Lim show how the pragmatic stream and the praise stream merged into what most churches now simply call worship.

The gains were real, and the grandmother in the car owes them a fair hearing. Contemporary worship brought millions of people into churches they would never otherwise have entered, and many came to Christ there. It recovered what the stiffest liturgical churches had let die, the conviction that worship involves the whole person, body and heart as well as mind. Jonathan Edwards, no friend of empty enthusiasm, argued in *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* (1746) that true religion lies to a great extent in the affections, in the heart actually moved by what it knows of God. Tears and raised hands are not embarrassments to be managed; they are what happens when people believe what they sing. The movement honored the Reformation's vernacular principle by singing the gospel in the musical language of the people in the room, and it kept writing new songs, which the Psalms themselves celebrate:

> He put a new song in my mouth, a song of praise to our God. Many will see and fear, and put their trust in the LORD. (Psalm 40:3, ESV)

The second half of that verse is the movement's best instinct. A new song is not only for the singer; others see, and fear, and trust. The people who built these services wanted the stranger in the door to hear the gospel in words he could understand, a missionary instinct as old as Pentecost, when the crowd heard the apostles in their own languages.

## The Return to the Old Words

The historic liturgy never went away, and in the last fifty years it has drawn people back from surprising directions. That story is no simple verdict against contemporary worship, because the churches that kept the old forms were declining in the same decades the new services were filling. Correct liturgy did not save the American mainline. The form carried the content but could not supply the faith.

Still, something was happening. In 1963 the Second Vatican Council's constitution on the liturgy, *Sacrosanctum Concilium*, called for the full, conscious and active participation of the faithful and opened the way for the Mass in the languages of the people. Rome took up the Reformation's vernacular principle four centuries late. Among evangelicals the pivotal figure was Robert Webber, a Wheaton College theologian raised in fundamentalism who found his way into the Episcopal Church. His *Evangelicals on the Canterbury Trail* (1985) gathered the stories of evangelicals drawn, as he had been, toward the sacraments, the church year and the historic prayers. In *Ancient-Future Faith* (1999) he argued that the way forward in a postmodern culture was not a newer technique but the recovery of the early church's faith and worship. Webber was no enemy of contemporary music; his point was that the church's future lay in remembering.

The Lutheran theologian Marva Dawn sharpened the case in *Reaching Out Without Dumbing Down* (1995). Worship, she argued, has God as both its subject and its object, and the first question to ask of any service is not whether it attracts people but whether it forms them in the character of Christ. She drew on Neil Postman, whose *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985) argued that the form of a medium shapes what can be said in it, and that television turns every subject it touches, religion included, into entertainment. A church that borrows the forms of entertainment to reach people, Dawn warned, will find those forms quietly rewriting its message. She wanted churches to reach out without assuming that reaching out required making the faith smaller.

James K. A. Smith gave the argument its fullest philosophical form. In *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) he argued that human beings are not first of all thinking things but loving things, and that our loves are shaped less by ideas than by practices, repeated bodily routines that train the heart before the mind catches up. He called these routines liturgies, and the secular world is full of them. His best-known example is the shopping mall, with its icons of the good life and its promise of redemption through acquisition, working on us whether or not we believe a word of it. The point is uncomfortable for both sides. If Smith is right, no church can choose whether its worship will form people, only what it will form them into. A service built around the singer's feelings is a liturgy, and so is one that runs like a lecture or a concert. The question is never liturgy or no liturgy. It is which liturgy, and toward what kingdom.

Here, then, is the liturgical case as its defenders would put it. The historic shape carries the whole story of Scripture every year, from Advent through the cross and resurrection to Pentecost, so that no congregation is left to one preacher's preferences. It puts true words in people's mouths until they know them by heart, and those words are still there when the heart has nothing of its own to say. It binds a small congregation to the church across centuries, so that the creed said in a rented room is the creed of 381. And it trusts repetition, the way a parent trusts bedtime prayers, to do slowly what no single moving service can do.

The psalmist held both halves of the argument together. In one of the Bible's rawest laments, a man far from Jerusalem steadies his cast-down soul by remembering the liturgy:

> These things I remember, as I pour out my soul: how I would go with the throng and lead them in procession to the house of God with glad shouts and songs of praise, a multitude keeping festival. (Psalm 42:4, ESV)

He pours out his soul through the memory of a procession. Feeling and form are not rivals in that verse. The form is what keeps the feeling from drowning.

## How Each Side Fails, and the Failure They Share

Both sides also have a characteristic way of going wrong, and the prophets were not partial about which they condemned. When Jesus confronted the religious experts of his day, he reached for Isaiah:

> "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me;" (Matthew 15:8, ESV)

That is the liturgical temptation named precisely. The words, the order and the responses are right, and the heart has gone somewhere else. A liturgy can be recited the way a child recites the Pledge of Allegiance, perfectly and without a thought. There is a subtler version among lovers of the old forms: liturgy as a matter of taste, a way of feeling more serious than the people down the road with their fog machines. Choosing a church for its beautiful prayers because they suit one's sensibility can be as much a consumer decision as choosing one for its band. The aesthete and the consumer are closer relatives than either would like to admit.

The contemporary temptation is the one Finney bequeathed. When worship is justified by what it produces, the service becomes an instrument and the congregation an audience whose response must be managed. The lights dim, the key rises, the drums build, and the room is carried to a place the planning meeting decided in advance it ought to reach. Much of that is done with good hearts. But engineered emotion can be mistaken for the presence of God, and people who learn to measure worship by how intensely they felt it will conclude, on the Sundays they feel nothing, that God was absent. The content narrows too. Songs built for immediate singability tend to circle God's love, God's greatness and the singer's response, and leave much of the canon unsung: lament, judgment, the long story of Israel, the world to come. A congregation that sings only what it already feels will not be taught to feel what it has not yet known. The pragmatic principle has at least been tested by its own measure. To its credit, Willow Creek published a self-study in 2007, *Reveal*, which found that its many programs did not by themselves produce the spiritual growth it had hoped for, and the church said publicly that it had to rethink.

Amos adds a warning neither side can escape. His target was the well-ordered, well-attended festivals of the northern kingdom:

> Take away from me the noise of your songs; to the melody of your harps I will not listen. (Amos 5:23, ESV)

> But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. (Amos 5:24, ESV)

Israel's songs were fine. Its life was not. Worship that does not form people who deal justly with the poor, the widow and the stranger has failed whatever its style, and Justin's Sunday, remember, ended with a collection for exactly those people.

Beneath the two failures lies a shared one, which the Scottish theologian James B. Torrance named in *Worship, Community and the Triune God of Grace* (1996) by distinguishing two views of worship. In the first, which he called unitarian in practice even among orthodox Trinitarians, worship is something we do: we gather, sing, pray and offer our best, and the whole weight rests on our performance, whether our correctness or our sincerity. In the second, worship is the gift of sharing, by the Spirit, in the Son's own worship of the Father. Christ is the true worshiper and priest, the *leitourgos* of Hebrews, and our prayers and songs are taken up into his. The liturgical church that trusts its correct order to please God and the contemporary church that trusts its sincere intensity to reach him have made the same mistake from opposite directions. Both have made worship our work. The gospel says it is first his.

The Lord's Supper is where the argument usually turns sharpest, and it belongs to the second order of questions, the kind that divide faithful churches without dividing the faith. Catholics, Orthodox, Lutherans and many Reformed Christians hold, in different ways, that Christ is truly present in the meal; many Baptists and free churches hold that it is chiefly a memorial and proclamation. Some keep it weekly, as in Justin's day and as Calvin wanted; many keep it monthly or quarterly. This essay won't settle those questions; the site's essay on [why faith uses physical things](/writing/why-faith-uses-physical-things) sets out the views in full. Every tradition agrees that the Table belongs to Christ and proclaims his death, and a church that lets it drift to the edges of its life has lost something the first Christians would not have understood losing.

## What Your Sunday Is Teaching Your Household

Most people reading this will never plan a worship service. They will choose a church, or stay in one, and bring a family into it for years. Moses gave Israel a picture of formation much like Smith's account of liturgy, except that it happens at the kitchen table:

> And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. (Deuteronomy 6:6, ESV)

> You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise. (Deuteronomy 6:7, ESV)

Every family already keeps liturgies of its own, the evening screen, the weekend tournament schedule, the way the house goes quiet or loud when someone is angry, and they form our children whether or not we name them. Sunday is one liturgy among many. Paul treats the church's singing as a form of teaching:

> Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God. (Colossians 3:16, ESV)

What a church sings, it learns, and the songs our children sing on Sunday are the theology they will carry out of the house. So it's worth asking of any church, whatever its style, whether over a year your family will hear the whole story of Scripture and not only the parts that suit a mood, confess its sins together and hear that they are forgiven, learn to lament as well as celebrate, pray for the world and not only for itself, say what the church has always believed in words older than the pastor, and find the Table near the center rather than at the edge. None of that requires an organ or forbids a guitar. A church with a band can do every one of those things, and a church with a prayer book can do all of them without meaning a word. And a household can supply what Sunday leaves thin: Advent kept at home, the Apostles' Creed at dinner, a psalm before bed.

Any parent can put the question simply. When our children are old, when their minds are going or their hearts are breaking or they're sitting by a hospital bed at three in the morning, what words will be in them? Not the arguments they won or the services that moved them once, but what they have by heart, which will be whatever they have said and sung ten thousand times. That is how *lex orandi* works on a household, this week.

The deepest reason worship matters is that it is not finally ours. Paul told the Corinthians what they were doing every time they came to the Table:

> For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes. (1 Corinthians 11:26, ESV)

Every service, plain or elaborate, stands between two events it cannot produce: a death on a Friday outside Jerusalem and a coming no one can schedule. We don't make God present by the right order or the right song. The one who died and rose is already the minister in the true sanctuary, and on the Lord's Day we are gathered into worship that was going on before we arrived:

> But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable angels in festal gathering, (Hebrews 12:22, ESV)

If that is true, the worship wars were fought over the wrong thing, and most of us fought them, if only in the car on the way home. We treated the prayer book as a matter of taste and the band as a matter of taste, and we sorted ourselves into churches that confirmed us. Repentance, for most of us, doesn't mean switching styles. It means asking a harder question of the style we already love: what it has taught us to expect from God, what it has left unsaid so long that we no longer notice the silence, and whether we have come to worship Christ or to enjoy ourselves in his vicinity. When a Samaritan woman wanted to argue about which mountain was the right place to worship, Jesus told her the hour was coming when true worshipers would worship the Father in spirit and truth. She asked a question about place. He answered with one about people. That question is still open on every street with two churches on it, and it will be answered next Sunday, and the Sunday after, in words our children are learning without knowing they are learning them.
