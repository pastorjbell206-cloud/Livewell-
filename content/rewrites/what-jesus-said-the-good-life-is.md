The good life is sold by the shelf in every airport bookstore in America. One cover promises calm and the next promises money; another offers a better body, or a morning routine that will finally put the day under control. Underneath the variety sits a single promise: there is a kind of person who has arrived, and for the price of a hardcover you can learn to become one. We walk into any room already sorting its people into those who have the life we're supposed to want and those who don't.

The better books on that shelf deserve a fair hearing. Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz, who lead the Harvard Study of Adult Development, called their 2023 book simply *The Good Life*. It rests on a study that has followed hundreds of lives since 1938, and its headline finding cuts against most of the shelf: good relationships, far more than the money and achievement most of us chase, are what kept the study's participants healthier and happier into old age. That is a real finding and a humbling one. It still pictures the good life as something a person builds and tends, and it has much less to say about the life in which the building has been knocked down: the widow, the prisoner, the woman who did everything right and lost it anyway.

Jesus began his most famous sermon on that side of the room. His first words were not a command, a warning or a program. They were a list of people who already have the good life, and by every measure the ancient world used, and every measure we use now, it was the wrong list.

**The claim of this essay is that the Beatitudes are neither a ladder of spiritual achievements nor soft words for hard days. They are Jesus' verdict on who is actually flourishing, spoken by the king whose arrival makes the verdict true. They overturn the good life Aristotle described and the one our culture sells, and they fulfill the longing underneath both, but only by way of a man who became poor himself.**

## A word that won't sit still

The trouble starts with one word. By the time "blessed" reached us it had been worn smooth. We say it about a parking space and a sunny vacation, and it has become a polite religious way of saying "lucky." A word that small can't carry what Jesus put on it.

The Greek word is *makarios*, and it arrived with a history. In Homer the gods are the *makares*, the ones untouched by the toil and death that mark human life, so for the Greek ear the word first named an existence beyond the reach of misfortune. The Gospels, though, heard it through Israel's Scriptures. When Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Bible into Greek before Christ, they used *makarios* for the Hebrew *'ashrê*, and *'ashrê* is how the Psalter begins: "Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked, nor stands in the way of sinners, nor sits in the seat of scoffers" (Psalm 1:1, ESV). Wisdom uses it the same way: "Blessed is the one who finds wisdom, and the one who gets understanding" (Proverbs 3:13, ESV).

English hides a distinction here, and the whole passage turns on it. Hebrew has a second word that English also renders "blessed": *barukh*, from the verb *barak*, the word of benediction. The priestly blessing, "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Numbers 6:24, ESV), uses that verb. *Barukh* gives something; it is a blessing pronounced. *'Ashrê* observes something; it is a verdict on the shape of a life. You can bless God with *barukh*, but *'ashrê* is never said of God, because it describes a creature whose life has come out right. The Greek translators kept the two apart, using *eulogētos* for the first and *makarios* for the second, and the Beatitudes use *makarios* every time.

Jonathan Pennington builds his reading of the whole sermon on that distinction. In *The Sermon on the Mount and Human Flourishing* (2017) he argues that a macarism, as scholars call these sayings, is a wisdom statement, not a benediction. It holds up a way of living and says that this is what a flourishing human life looks like. Pennington thinks "flourishing" comes nearer than any other English word, and he reads the sermon as Jesus taking up the question every ancient school of wisdom asked, Greek and Jewish alike: what is a good human life, and how does anyone come to have one?

Translators have never settled on an English word. Some modern versions, the Good News Bible among them, began each line with "happy," which fits the older sense of the word and misleads the modern ear, since "happy" now names a feeling. "How fortunate" catches the exclamation in *'ashrê* but suggests luck. "Blessed" keeps the reverence but invites us to hear a reward for good behavior, or else the caption under a photograph. "Flourishing" comes nearest, yet in our century it can sound like the vocabulary of a wellness app. What every option struggles to hold is an objective judgment about a whole life, spoken from outside the person's feelings about it. When Jesus calls the mourners *makarios*, he isn't claiming they feel wonderful. He is claiming that their lives are going the way human lives are meant to go, and that heaven sees it even when no one else does.

## Mountain and plain: Matthew 5 beside Luke 6

Matthew sets the scene with care. Just before the sermon he tells how Jesus' fame spread through all Syria, and how people brought him everyone who was sick, in pain, possessed, seized by fits or paralyzed, and he healed them, while great crowds followed from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem and Judea (Matthew 4:24–25). Only then: "Seeing the crowds, he went up on the mountain, and when he sat down, his disciples came to him" (Matthew 5:1, ESV). The people within earshot when he said "blessed are those who mourn" were the sick and the families who had carried them for miles. A teacher on a mountain would have put any Jewish reader in mind of Moses, but what comes down from this one begins with good news.

Matthew gives eight sayings in the third person, from "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:3, ESV) to "Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:10, ESV), and then turns to address his listeners directly. The first and eighth promises are identical and in the present tense: theirs *is* the kingdom. The six between them are future. The kingdom already belongs to these people, and its full possession is still ahead of them.

Luke tells a closely related sermon in a harder key. Jesus comes down and stands "on a level place" with a great crowd (Luke 6:17, ESV), and speaks four blessings in the second person: "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God" (Luke 6:20, ESV). His hungry and weeping are declared *makarios* "now." Then Luke does what Matthew doesn't. He turns the list over. "But woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation" (Luke 6:24, ESV). Woes follow for the full, for those who laugh now, and for those of whom everyone speaks well (Luke 6:25–26).

The word Luke uses for the rich man's consolation is *paraklēsis*, and he has used it once before. Early in his Gospel old Simeon waits in the temple for "the consolation of Israel" (Luke 2:25, ESV), the comfort God promised through the prophets. The rich, Jesus says, have their consolation already. The present order has paid them in full and they have nothing left to wait for. That is why the woes are nearer the prophets' cry of lament than a curse. The comfortable are being warned that comfort can close the one door through which God's comfort comes.

Whether "poor" or "poor in spirit" comes first has long been argued, and both sides have a case. The spiritual reading has a long pedigree and deserves to be put as its defenders would put it: "poor in spirit" names a posture before God that anyone can have, rich or poor, the recognition that one brings nothing and needs everything. The social reading answers that Luke's version refuses to be spiritualized, that the crowds in both Gospels were materially desperate, and that Luke opened his Gospel with Mary singing that God "has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich he has sent away empty" (Luke 1:53, ESV). In her song the reversal is not a metaphor. It is what God is doing to the world.

Israel's Scriptures show why the two readings belong together. The Psalms speak again and again of the *'anawim*, the poor and afflicted, a word that came to mean both the materially poor and the humble who have no one to lean on but God, because in Israel's experience those were usually the same people. "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (Psalm 34:18, ESV). The third beatitude comes almost word for word from that stream: "But the meek shall inherit the land and delight themselves in abundant peace" (Psalm 37:11, ESV). Matthew didn't invent a spiritual category to soften Luke. He named the posture that poverty had pressed onto the faithful poor of Israel, and Luke refuses to let the posture float free of the bodies that carried it. Behind both stands Isaiah 61, where the anointed one is sent "to bring good news to the poor" and "to comfort all who mourn" (Isaiah 61:1–2, ESV). Luke has Jesus read that passage in the synagogue at Nazareth and then say, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21, ESV). The Beatitudes are that announcement spoken to the faces of the people it was promised to.

I have preached the Beatitudes as a comfort for people in hard seasons, and that isn't wrong. It is incomplete, and the woes show why. By any global measure I am rich. I have never had a meal I couldn't afford or a night I didn't know where I would sleep, and my children have never gone hungry. By the standard of the crowd on that hillside, I am among those who have received their consolation, and I can't read Luke 6 from a safe distance. The question it puts to me is whether the comfort I've been given has made the Beatitudes good news or quietly made them a threat, and I suspect the honest answer is some of both.

## What Aristotle would have made of the list

Set the list beside the most careful account of the good life the ancient world produced. Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, from the 330s BC, calls the highest human good *eudaimonia*, usually translated happiness and better rendered flourishing. It is the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue across a complete life, since one good day no more makes a person happy than one swallow makes a spring. Flourishing is not a mood. It is a whole life lived well.

Aristotle was too honest to pretend virtue is enough by itself. Flourishing also needs external goods, he argues: some wealth, friends, good birth, children. Someone lowborn, alone or childless can hardly be called happy, still less someone whose children or friends are worthless, or were good and have died. He points to Priam, the king of Troy who lived well and then saw his sons killed and his city burned. Nobody, Aristotle says, calls a man with Priam's end happy. The poor, the grieving and the persecuted are exactly the people whose circumstances, on his account, have put flourishing out of reach. The virtues collide too. His ideal, the great-souled man, rightly thinks himself worthy of great honors, is ashamed to receive favors, and walks with a slow step and a deep voice. He would have found "poor in spirit" an embarrassing thing to say about anyone. Humility isn't on Aristotle's list of virtues at all; the man who claims less than he is worth is, for him, small-souled, and that is a vice.

The Stoics offered a different consolation, and it sounds closer to Jesus than it is. For them virtue alone sufficed for happiness, and poverty, exile and loss were matters of indifference to the wise. Epictetus, born a slave, advises in his *Enchiridion* that when you kiss your child you should remind yourself that you are kissing a mortal, so that you won't be disturbed when the child dies. The Stoic could call the poor man happy, but only by teaching him not to care what he had lost. So three positions stand side by side. Aristotle says the mourner cannot flourish. The Stoic says he can, if he stops mourning. Jesus says the mourner is flourishing now, inside his grief, and that he will be comforted, which means the loss was real, the grief was right and neither gets the last word.

The Beatitudes overturn the classical good life, then, without simply discarding it. What they overturn is the measure: the conviction that flourishing is secured by good circumstances and admirable qualities and so belongs to the fortunate and the strong. What they fulfill is the question. Aristotle was right that human beings are made for an end, and that flourishing concerns a whole life and the kind of person one becomes. He even praised a virtue called *praotēs*, mildness under provocation, from the same root as Matthew's word for "the meek." And in the last book of the *Ethics* he concluded that the highest happiness is contemplation, the activity that makes a human being most like the gods, though he assumed it belonged to the few with leisure for philosophy. Jesus promises that "the pure in heart" shall "see God" (Matthew 5:8, ESV). The vision Aristotle reserved for the leisured philosopher is offered to anyone whose heart is undivided, and it is the vision of God himself.

Augustine lived inside this argument before he ever preached on the sermon. In November 386, a few months after his conversion, he spent his birthday at a country house at Cassiciacum talking with friends and his mother, Monica, about what makes a life happy, and wrote it up as *On the Happy Life*. They concluded that the happy person is the one who has God. Thomas Aquinas built the medieval synthesis on that foundation, arguing in the *Summa Theologiae* with Aristotle that every human being acts for a final end, and against him that the end can only be the vision of God's own being, begun in this life and completed in the next. The classical tradition had asked the right question. It had assumed the answer was something a person could achieve.

## Twenty centuries of reading eight sentences

Every generation of the church has had to decide what kind of speech the Beatitudes are, and each major answer saw something real.

Augustine wrote *Our Lord's Sermon on the Mount* around 394, as a newly ordained presbyter at Hippo, and opened by saying that anyone who reads the sermon devoutly will find in it the complete pattern of the Christian life. He counted seven beatitudes, treating the eighth as a return to the first since both promise the kingdom, and he read them as steps of one ascent, matched to the sevenfold Spirit of Isaiah 11 (the Greek and Latin Bibles add piety to the six gifts of the Hebrew). Isaiah's list descends from wisdom to "the fear of the LORD" (Isaiah 11:2, ESV). Augustine climbed it the other way, from the poor in spirit, who begin in the fear of the Lord, up to the peacemakers, who reach wisdom. Its strength is that it treats the list as one life whose every step the Spirit supplies. Its cost is that a verdict on the unlikely becomes a curriculum for the advanced, and the poor in spirit become beginners whose task is to move on.

The medieval church kept the ladder and added a distinction. Thomas Aquinas gave the beatitudes a question of their own in the *Summa* (I-II q.69), treating them as acts that flow from the virtues and the gifts of the Spirit and keeping Augustine's pairing of the two lists. Elsewhere (I-II q.108) he distinguished precepts binding on every Christian from the evangelical counsels of poverty, chastity and obedience, offered to those seeking perfection. Aquinas didn't simply file the sermon under the counsels, but in the church's practice its hardest words drifted toward the cloister. The distinction deserves more respect than Protestants usually give it, because it built whole ways of life around words most of us have learned to admire from a distance. Its cost was a church in which the hardest beatitudes belonged to the professed religious and everyone else kept the gentler ones. (The site's essay on [the camel and the eye of the needle](/writing/the-eye-of-the-needle-jesus-and-the-rich) follows that division further.)

Martin Luther rejected the two tiers. In sermons on Matthew 5 through 7 preached from 1530 and published in 1532, he attacked the scholastics for turning Christ's teaching into counsels for the few, and in the same breath attacked the radicals who said a Christian could never hold office, swear an oath or bear the sword. His answer drew on the distinction he had set out in *Temporal Authority* in 1523. As a person answering for himself, the Christian must be meek, bear insult and refuse revenge. As a father, judge or prince holding an office for others, he must protect the neighbor, sometimes by punishing the wrongdoer. That is a real moral insight: a judge who turns the other cheek on a victim's behalf has abandoned the victim. Its cost showed over the following centuries, as the Beatitudes became the ethic of the private heart while office, market and state ran on other rules with a clear conscience. (The site's essay on [render unto Caesar](/writing/two-kingdoms-faith-and-state) traces that line.)

The radicals had their own case, and they paid for it. On 24 February 1527 Swiss and South German Anabaptists adopted a short confession at Schleitheim, largely the work of the former Benedictine prior Michael Sattler, committing them to a church separated from the world. They refused the oath because Christ forbade it in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:34), and they refused the sword because, while God had ordained it to punish evil in the world, it stood outside the perfection of Christ, who would not be made a king and told his followers to learn from his meekness. Within three months Sattler was burned at Rottenburg. Their reading is the plainest one: Jesus meant what he said, and the church is a visible community called to live it now. Few traditions have lived the last beatitude more literally. The strongest objection is Luther's: a community that withdraws from office hasn't ended the use of force, only left it to others, and the neighbor still needs protecting.

A later answer spread through American evangelical churches. The Scofield Reference Bible of 1909, and Lewis Sperry Chafer after it, taught that the Sermon on the Mount was the constitution of a kingdom Israel had refused and that God had postponed, its primary application belonging to Christ's future reign on earth, with a moral application to Christians now. It is easy to caricature and harder to answer. It takes "they shall inherit the earth" as a concrete promise about this world, and it guards against reading the sermon's demands as law that crowds out grace. Its cost is a sermon exiled to the future, and later dispensational scholars have moved toward a kingdom already begun in Christ.

Lay these readings side by side and a shared assumption shows. Each treated the Beatitudes as demands and argued over who had to meet them. Far fewer asked whether the list was a demand in the first place.

## An announcement, not an entrance exam

Most of us still read the list as a to-do list with the rewards printed beside it: become poorer in spirit, mourn more sincerely, work up some meekness, and collect the reward. Read that way it is crushing, because no honest person reliably has these qualifications, and anyone who thinks he has them has already failed the first.

Dallas Willard took that reading apart in *The Divine Conspiracy* (1998). The Beatitudes, he argued, are not conditions for receiving God's favor or instructions for becoming the kind of person God favors. Nobody becomes poor in spirit as a technique for getting in. The list is an announcement. Jesus is looking at the people everyone, the religious authorities included, assumed were beyond blessing, and telling them the kingdom of God is available to them now, through him. The scandal is in the casting. (What that kingdom is, present and not yet complete, is the subject of the site's essay on [the kingdom of God](/writing/what-is-the-kingdom-of-god-and-why-it-changes-everything).)

Willard's reading fits the first half of the list most naturally. The poor in spirit, the mourners, the meek and the hungry are defined by what they lack. The second half reads differently: the merciful, the pure in heart and the peacemakers are described by their character. Here Pennington's reading adds something. If a macarism describes the shape of a flourishing life, the list also portrays what people become once they have come in. The chapter's closing command, "You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew 5:48, ESV), uses *teleios*, which means whole or complete more than flawless: an undivided life.

The distinction that settles much of the argument is between a condition and a verdict. An entrance requirement is a condition; meet it and you're admitted. A macarism declares what is already true about a life in the light of what God is doing, before the person feels it or earns it. The poor in spirit are not flourishing because spiritual poverty is a virtue they mastered. They are flourishing because the king has arrived and the kingdom belongs to people who have stopped pretending they can manage without it. And the grace that announces the kingdom to empty hands goes on to form the hands. The first half of the list is the door; the second half is the house.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer read the list from inside a church under pressure. *Discipleship* appeared in 1937, the year the Gestapo closed the Confessing Church seminary at Finkenwalde that he directed. For him the blessing doesn't rest on poverty or grief as achievements. It rests on the people whom Jesus' call has made poor and set at odds with the world; their renunciation is the consequence of the call, not its price. That is why the persecution beatitude is no afterthought. He was hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945.

This essay lands with Willard on the first question and with Pennington on the second: the Beatitudes are an announcement of grace to the least likely, and a portrait of the character grace produces. That Jesus is Lord and salvation is by grace is the confessional floor; how to read this list, as ladder, counsel, private ethic, future constitution or announcement, is second-order, and faithful Christians have disagreed about it and remained brothers and sisters. The announcement reading has not fully answered its strongest objection, which sits inside the list: "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy" (Matthew 5:7, ESV). A chapter later Jesus warns that the Father will not forgive those who will not forgive others (Matthew 6:15). The reader who hears a real condition there has heard something that is actually in the text.

## The good life for sale, and the one who became poor

Alasdair MacIntyre argued in *After Virtue* (1981) that modern moral language is made of fragments of a tradition that once agreed human beings have an end. Take away the end and the vocabulary of flourishing survives only as preference, which is why a hundred incompatible versions of the good life can share one shelf: none of them has to be true. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton found the religious form of this in *Soul Searching* (2005). The working faith of many of the American teenagers they studied was what they called moralistic therapeutic deism, in which the central goal of life is to be happy and feel good about oneself. The teenagers learned it from us. The church sells its own editions. The prosperity preacher promises the very riches and fullness Luke's woes fall on. The therapeutic church treats the mourners as a problem to fix. The nationalist on the right can't abide a list where the meek inherit the earth; the activist on the left can turn it into a program the righteous run for the poor. Each edits the list in the same place, making the good life something we secure. I recognize the pull in myself. I would rather be commended for my strength than met at the end of it.

What breaks the shelf's hold is not a better theory of flourishing. It is a person. Read the list again and it describes Jesus before anyone else. He uses Matthew's word for the meek of himself: "for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls" (Matthew 11:29, ESV). He enters Jerusalem "humble, and mounted on a donkey" (Matthew 21:5, ESV), the same word again, and "when he drew near and saw the city, he wept over it" (Luke 19:41, ESV). He was reviled and had every kind of evil spoken against him falsely. Paul puts it in one sentence: "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). Bonhoeffer drew the conclusion: if you ask where on earth the community of the Beatitudes can be found, the answer is the cross, where the poorest and meekest of all was hanging.

Aristotle took seriously Solon's old saying that you can't call a life happy until you see how it ends, and he offered Priam as the man whose end cancelled everything before it. By that measure Jesus ended worse than Priam: stripped, mocked, abandoned and executed outside the city. Any honest observer that Friday would have said the list had been tested on the man who spoke it, and had failed. The resurrection is God's verdict on that life. On the third day the Father declared that the poor man on the cross was the one whose life had come out entirely right, and that everyone joined to him shares the verdict. The Beatitudes aren't true because poverty and grief are good. They are true because he went down into poverty and grief and did not stay there.

That leaves the list pointed where he pointed it. The people you have quietly pitied may stand nearer the kingdom than the people you have quietly envied, and the life you have spent building may be what keeps the door from opening. Luke has a woe for everyone whose consolation has already arrived, and most of us who buy books in airports have been paid. The door stands open to anyone who comes with empty hands. It is the one qualification the whole shelf was written to help us avoid.
