There is a kind of child every church knows and most Christian parents quietly hope to raise. She can find Habakkuk without the table of contents. Ask her a question in Sunday school and the answer arrives quickly, in the vocabulary the adults use, and the adults glance at one another with relief. Then, somewhere between sixteen and twenty-two, the answers stop. There is rarely an announcement; they simply fall out of use, the way a language fades in a country where no one speaks it. The parents are bewildered, because she knew everything. That was the problem. She knew it the way you know a script.

The other child is harder to love in the moment. He asks at dinner why God let his friend's father die, or who made God, or why people in the other church are going to hell if they love Jesus too. He asks at bedtime, when you are already half out of the room, in a tone that sounds like accusation, because at fourteen every question sounds like accusation. It is easy to hear him as the child in danger and her as the child who is safe. Very often it is the other way around.

I have five sons, and I am a pastor, which gives me two separate reasons to answer too fast. The failure I know best in myself is the good answer delivered too soon: the child asks something hard, I know something true about it, and I say the true thing completely and immediately and feel the relief of having handled it. I had not handled it. I had ended it. **The distinction this essay turns on is between a child who performs answers and a child who owns questions. The first can recite the faith without ever having been asked to trust it, and the recitation will be discarded the first time it costs something. The second has been allowed to ask inside the house of faith, and Scripture, far from fearing that child, set a place for him at the table.**

### The Question Written Into the Liturgy

Most Christians know Deuteronomy 6 by its opening. "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might. And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:4–7). We read this as a mandate for instruction, and it is. But look at where the instruction happens: at the table, on the road, at bedtime, at breakfast, on the doorposts a child walks past every day (6:9). The faith is handed down in the ordinary hours, which means it is handed down in conversation, and a conversation has two voices.

The second voice arrives a few verses later, and Moses expects it. "When your son asks you in time to come, 'What is the meaning of the testimonies and the statutes and the rules that the LORD our God has commanded you?' then you shall say to your son, 'We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt. And the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand'" (Deuteronomy 6:20–21). The text says *when*, not *if*. The child's question is not treated as a sign that the teaching failed. It is anticipated as part of how the covenant passes from one generation to the next.

The same pattern runs through the Passover itself: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?' you shall say, 'It is the sacrifice of the LORD's Passover, for he passed over the houses of the people of Israel in Egypt, when he struck the Egyptians but spared our houses'" (Exodus 12:26–27). When Joshua sets up twelve stones at the Jordan, it is for the day "when your children ask in time to come, 'What do those stones mean to you?'" (Joshua 4:6). Israel built memorials designed to provoke questions. The Mishnah, compiled around the year 200, kept faith with this in its instructions for the Passover meal: at a set point the son asks his father, and if the son does not yet know how to ask, the father teaches him. A child's question was so necessary that if the child could not produce one, the parent supplied it.

Notice, too, what kind of answer Moses gives. It is testimony in the first person plural: *We were Pharaoh's slaves.* The parent answers by including himself in the story of rescue. He does not stand outside the faith and explain it; he stands inside it and confesses it. That is harder than having the right answer, because it requires the parent to have been rescued from something and to remember it. A parent can say "God brought them out of Egypt" at no cost at all. "He brought *us* out" is a claim about the parent's own life, and a child hears the difference long before he can name it.

### From Catechism to Recitation

The church inherited this dialogue and formalized it. For most of Christian history the catechism was the church's great tool for handing the faith to children, and its form was always the same: a question, then an answer. The Reformation made that form nearly universal among Protestants, and it did so in response to a problem that sounds very modern. When Luther took part in the visitations of the Saxon parishes in 1527 and 1528, he was dismayed by what he found. Lifelong churchgoers, and many of their pastors, knew almost nothing of what they professed. Christendom had produced a population that performed the faith without understanding it. His answer was the Small Catechism of 1529, arranged so that the head of a household could teach it at home, the Deuteronomy 6 table restored. After each commandment, each article of the Creed, each petition of the Lord's Prayer, Luther asks the same thing: *What does this mean?* It is the child's question from Exodus, put back into the child's mouth.

The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563, drafted largely by Zacharias Ursinus for Frederick III of the Palatinate, opens with the most pastoral question in Protestant catechesis: "What is your only comfort in life and in death?" The answer begins "that I am not my own," and goes on in the first person to confess belonging to a faithful Savior. The child is not asked only what Christians believe; he is asked what *he* is trusting, in life and in death. The Westminster Shorter Catechism of 1647 asks what the chief end of man is and answers that it is to glorify God and to enjoy him forever. Generations of children learned those words before they could understand them, in the hope that the words would wait in them, like seed, until the day they could.

That is the catechism's gift: words for questions before a child knows how to ask them. Every child is catechized by something, and the only question is whether the catechist is the church's long memory or the algorithm's short one. But the form carries a danger the Reformers would have recognized, because they had just watched performed faith swallow a continent. A question-and-answer form can be learned in two different ways. It can be learned as a map, answers to real questions the child will one day ask for himself, so that when the question comes it finds a place already prepared. Or it can be learned as a script, correct outputs that earn the approving face across the table. The words are identical. What is happening in the child is not. When the answer is severed from any question the child actually has, it becomes a performance, and a performance is by definition done for an audience. The child who performs answers is not lying. He simply has not yet been asked whether any of it is his.

John Westerhoff, in *Will Our Children Have Faith?* (1976), described faith as growing the way a tree adds rings: an experienced faith, absorbed from the people who love you; an affiliative faith, the faith of belonging to a community and learning its story; a searching faith, marked by doubt, critical judgment, and experiment; and only then an owned faith. A child who is never allowed to search has not been protected from doubt; he has been kept from ownership. James Fowler's *Stages of Faith* (1981) made a related observation: the faith of adolescence is typically held in conformity to the people who matter most, largely unexamined, and moving beyond it requires a season of taking responsibility for one's own commitments. Many adults never make that move, and a borrowed faith holds only as long as the lenders stay in the room.

### What We Actually Handed Them

In 2005 the sociologist Christian Smith, with Melinda Lundquist Denton, published *Soul Searching*, drawn from the National Study of Youth and Religion. What they found was less rebellion than inarticulacy. Most teenagers were not hostile to their parents' religion; they simply could not say much about it, and the fragments they produced added up to something Smith and Denton named Moralistic Therapeutic Deism: a God exists who made the world and watches over it; God wants people to be good, nice, and fair; the central goal of life is to be happy and feel good about oneself; God need not be much involved except when there is a problem to solve; and good people go to heaven when they die.

The most uncomfortable finding was not about the teenagers. Smith and Denton concluded that parents were the most important influence on their children's religious lives, and that teenagers largely reflected the faith they had been given. Moralistic Therapeutic Deism was not invented at school. It was absorbed at home and in the churches parents chose. We handed our children a faith that had never been built to meet hard questions, because we ourselves had quietly stopped asking them. The children performed what we performed.

Six years later David Kinnaman, working from Barna Group research, published *You Lost Me* (2011) about young adults who had drifted from the churches of their childhood. Among the themes he identified was one he called *doubtless*: young Christians reporting that the church was not a place where they could voice serious questions. Many had not been defeated by an argument. They had questions, found no room to ask them, and went looking for rooms elsewhere.

Those rooms have changed again. Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) argues that between roughly 2010 and 2015 childhood shifted from a play-based childhood to a phone-based one, and that the shift coincided with a sharp rise in adolescent anxiety and depression. One need not accept every causal claim to recognize what he describes. A child raised with an audience in his pocket learns to experience the self as something constantly evaluated and managed for whoever is watching. Haidt distinguishes a child in *discover mode*, open and willing to take small risks, from a child in *defend mode*, scanning for threat and protecting his standing. A child in defend mode does not ask real questions. He produces whatever the room will reward.

We built an economy of performance, the grades and teams and metrics on the screen, and then introduced God into it and too often let him look like one more evaluator. Recite the right answer and the room warms; ask the wrong question and the room goes quiet. So the child learns to manage God the way he manages everyone else, performing the expected output and keeping his interior out of sight, and we, watching the confident performance, conclude that the formation is working.

Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) explains why this fails now. Taylor describes a world in which belief in God is no longer the default but one option among many, held in full awareness that intelligent and decent people hold others, so that every faith, Christian or secular, is held under cross-pressure. Peter Berger's *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) had described the older condition, in which a culture's institutions made belief plausible by leaning the same direction at once, and a child could absorb faith almost by osmosis. That canopy is gone. Whatever our children hold at thirty, they will hold because they chose it against real alternatives. A faith that has only ever been recited cannot survive that choice, because it was never offered as one.

### The Parent Who Is Afraid of the Question

The parent frightened by all this deserves to be heard in his strongest form, because his fear is not imaginary. Children are not small philosophers standing on neutral ground, and there is no neutral ground to stand on. Scripture calls believers out of spiritual childhood so that "we may no longer be children, tossed to and fro by the waves and carried about by every wind of doctrine, by human cunning, by craftiness in deceitful schemes" (Ephesians 4:14). Proverbs tells parents to "train up a child in the way he should go" (Proverbs 22:6), not to hold a seminar on the options. This father has watched friends' children "start asking questions" at nineteen and stop coming home at Christmas. He has noticed that questioning is often less a method than an identity, performed online and rewarded by peers, and he suspects, correctly, that "think for yourself" often means "think like your new friends." He is not afraid of thought. He is afraid of a script dressed up as thought.

Nearly all of this is true, and I concede it without hedging. Questions are not neutral. A child can perform doubt as fluently as he once performed belief, trading the approving face at the dinner table for the approving face in the dorm or the comment thread, and a faith lost that way was never examined any more than it was once held. Paul's counsel to Timothy is to "continue in what you have learned and have firmly believed, knowing from whom you learned it" (2 Timothy 3:14). Continuity matters.

But look closely at that verse, because it holds the answer to the fear. Timothy's confidence rests on *whom* he learned it from, and Paul names them earlier: "I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well" (2 Timothy 1:5). The word translated *sincere* is built from the Greek word for a stage actor, and means a faith without play-acting. What Lois and Eunice handed Timothy was an unperformed faith, trustworthy because it was real enough to survive examination.

So the fearful parent is right about the danger and wrong about the cure. Silence does not prevent the question. It relocates it, from the kitchen table where someone who loves the child can take it seriously to a bedroom at one in the morning, where it is answered by whoever reaches the child first with the most practice at persuasion. And the child most vulnerable to the culture's performed doubt is the child who only ever performed belief, because he has learned that faith means producing what the audience wants. Change the audience and you change the output. The child who owns his questions is harder to capture in either direction. He has learned to ask whether a thing is true, not only whether it is approved, and that habit guards him against the skeptic's slogans as much as the church's.

Anselm of Canterbury gave his *Proslogion* (around 1078) the working title *fides quaerens intellectum*, faith seeking understanding. The formula neither puts questions before faith, as the modern skeptic does, nor forbids them, as the anxious believer is tempted to. Faith is what does the asking, because it trusts there is something true at the bottom of the question and that God is not threatened by being sought. The frightened parent guards the answer. The free parent walks the child toward it.

### The Boy in the Temple and the Disciple Who Asked to Touch

The Gospels give us one story from the boyhood of Jesus, and it is a story about questions. At twelve he stays behind in Jerusalem after the Passover, the festival built around a child's question, and his parents search for three days. "After three days they found him in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions. And all who heard him were amazed at his understanding and his answers" (Luke 2:46–47). Listening and asking come before answering. Luke adds that he "increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52). The incarnation includes a childhood of learning, and the learning included asking.

The scene carries a word for parents that is easy to miss. When Jesus answers his distressed parents, "they did not understand the saying that he spoke to them" (Luke 2:50). Even the holiest home in history had parents who could not fully follow their child's inner life. Luke tells us that Mary "treasured up all these things in her heart" (Luke 2:51). She did not resolve what she did not understand. She kept it, and waited.

The disciple whose questions are most remembered has been treated less kindly by the church than by the Gospel that tells his story. We call him Doubting Thomas, and the nickname is a small injustice. When Jesus resolves to return to Judea where his enemies wait, it is Thomas who says, "Let us also go, that we may die with him" (John 11:16), the voice of a man with more courage than hope. In the upper room, when Jesus says they know the way, Thomas admits what the others likely thought: "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" (John 14:5). His honest confusion is the occasion for "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6).

Then comes the scene that gave him his name. Absent when the risen Jesus first appeared, he refuses to take the others' word: "Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into his side, I will never believe" (John 20:25). Eight days later Jesus comes again, and he does not rebuke the demand. He meets it point by point: "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe" (John 20:27). The risen Lord bends to the terms of the doubter's question, and the doubter, met in his doubt, makes the fullest confession in John's Gospel: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).

Jesus's final word, "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29), is often read as a scolding. But John turns at once to his readers: "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (John 20:31). The blessing reaches past Thomas to everyone after him, including our children, who will never touch the wounds but are handed the testimony of people who did. In neither the temple nor the upper room is the question treated as betrayal, and in the upper room what answers it is a person, still bearing the marks of what he suffered.

### What Owning a Question Looks Like at Home

None of this reduces to a technique, but a household does ordinary things differently once it stops rewarding recitation and starts welcoming questions. It begins with the parent's face. Children run a quiet test before they ask the real question: they float a small doubt, a trial balloon, and watch what happens to you. If your face tightens, the door closes, and the real questions go where you cannot follow. A child reads fear in the body before a word is spoken, and if the parent is secretly afraid that the question, fully entertained, might unravel the parent's own faith, the child will take that fear as the real answer. So the work begins in us. We have to have done enough of our own asking that the child's hardest question lands in someone who is not afraid of it.

It continues with the refusal to over-answer. A seven-year-old asking what happens when we die does not need a doctrine of the intermediate state. She needs to know the question is welcome and that you will be there for the next one. Over-answering is often a way of managing our own discomfort by retreating into expertise, and children hear the retreat.

It requires the courage to say *I don't know* as an invitation rather than an escape. One failure is the airtight answer delivered so fast that the speed itself says the question was unwelcome. The other is the shrug: nobody really knows, you'll find your own way. That sounds humble and abandons the child. What a child needs is a parent who can say, in effect, *Here is what I don't know. Here, separately, is what I would stake my life on, and why I'm still standing inside this faith.* That models what we most want our children to learn: that faith and uncertainty can share a room without one strangling the other.

It means listening for the question underneath the question. The teenager who announces that suffering disproves God may be making an argument, and it deserves a real answer. He may also be telling you that something terrible happened to someone he loves and no one in the church would sit with it honestly. Job's friends had correct theology and used it to avoid sitting with a suffering man, and at the end God says to them, "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7). Answer the syllogism and miss the wound, and you win the argument while losing the child. When a child's questions about meaning come joined to talk of not wanting to be alive, the conversation has moved beyond philosophy. Stay close, take it seriously, and get help: in the US, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

It means teaching children to think critically in both directions, asking whether a claim is true of the viral video as readily as of the Sunday sermon. Give your children the strongest version of the objection, not the embarrassing one, then show them how the church has answered it and where the answer is still incomplete. A child cannot think well while living permanently in front of an audience; owning a question takes some silence and unobserved time. And it means using the catechism as a map. After a child recites an answer, the next question can be *what do you think that means?* and then, perhaps, *do you believe it?* A child who says *not yet* has just told you the truth, and a household that can receive that without panic is one where the faith has room to become real.

Above all, it means answering the way Moses told Israel to answer: with testimony. Children can argue with a proposition all day. It is much harder to argue with a parent who says honestly, *this is what God has done for me, and here is where I would be without it.* I came to faith from atheism, and I cannot pretend the questions a skeptical child asks are stupid. What finally answers them is the parent's life more than the parent's cleverness, watched at close range for years by a child who knows exactly when a performance is a performance.

### The Wounds Are Still There

We have tended to think our task is to produce children with the right answers and to judge ourselves by whether they can recite them. The children who recited best are too often the ones now furthest away. That is an accusation against the formation we gave them before it is an accusation against them, and it falls on parents first, the one writing this included. We asked for performance and got it. We were afraid of questions because we had not faced our own, and our children, who miss nothing, learned from our fear what we never meant to teach: that the faith cannot bear being looked at directly.

The gospel does not let us off that charge, and it does not leave us under it. It tells us that the risen Christ came back to the room where a doubter was waiting and held out his hands. The resurrection did not erase the wounds. Jesus kept them and offered them, and they became the place where a man who could not believe was brought to say *my Lord and my God*. Whatever else that means, it means Christ is not afraid of being examined. He invites the finger into the nail mark. A household that follows him cannot treat as a threat the very questions he welcomed.

We cannot make our children believe. Lois and Eunice could not make Timothy believe; the faith that dwelt first in them had to come to dwell in him, and that last step was never in their hands. What we can do is be people from whom a child can learn, whose faith is unperformed enough to be trusted and honest enough to be questioned. That will cost us. It will mean repenting of the times we answered to end the conversation, going back to the child we shut down and saying so, and facing the questions we have been avoiding in ourselves.

For nearly two thousand years, Jewish families at the Passover table have heard a child ask why this night is different from all other nights, and every year the question is welcomed, because the answer was never meant to retire it. The question comes back. It will come back to your table in a different voice, at a worse hour, about something harder than you are ready for. What remains undecided is whether you will be in the room when it does, and whether the child finds there a script, or a parent who, like the Lord he follows, is not afraid of being touched.
