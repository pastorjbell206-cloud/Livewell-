Most Christian parents, asked how children come to faith, will describe some kind of teaching: the bedtime prayer, the Bible storybook worn soft at the corners, the catechism question and its answer. None of that is misguided. Moses told Israel's parents to teach God's words "diligently" to their children, and a child who has never been told the story has nothing to hold when the storm comes. The instinct to teach is obedient. The trouble is what it leaves out.

A child learns to lie long before anyone teaches him lying. The phone rings, the parent doesn't want to talk, and the child hears "tell them I'm not home." Nobody meant to teach anything, and a lesson landed anyway: words can be aimed at outcomes rather than at the truth. The same child may sit through a devotion on honesty that Sunday, and he will measure it against what he heard by the phone. Children learn faith the way they learn everything that matters most to them. They watch, they copy, and much of what they copy we never meant to hand them.

That isn't a modern discovery. It is the way the New Testament assumes every Christian is formed, and it makes Christian parenting both heavier and more merciful than a curriculum. Heavier, because what gets passed down is a life rather than a set of answers, and a life can't be faked across eighteen years of breakfasts. More merciful, because the life the New Testament holds up for imitation was never a flawless one. What a child most needs to see is a parent who has been forgiven and knows it, and who turns back to God in plain view.

### "Be Imitators of Me": How the New Testament Forms a Person

Read Paul's letters asking how he expected faith to pass from one person to another, and one word keeps surfacing. "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ" (1 Corinthians 11:1). The Greek is *mimētēs*, the root of our word "mimic," and Paul doesn't use it as an occasional flourish. He tells the Philippians, "Brothers, join in imitating me, and keep your eyes on those who walk according to the example you have in us" (Philippians 3:17). Near the end of that letter he names how they took the faith in from him, "What you have learned and received and heard and seen in me," and tells them to "practice these things, and the God of peace will be with you" (Philippians 4:9). Learning and hearing belong to a classroom. Receiving and seeing belong to a household.

The Corinthian verse closes three chapters on food offered to idols, in which Paul has described surrendering rights he could claim rather than trip a weaker brother, "not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, that they may be saved" (1 Corinthians 10:33). What they are told to imitate is a pattern of self-giving they had watched him live. Earlier he fastens the same word to the language of family: "For though you have countless guides in Christ, you do not have many fathers. For I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel. I urge you, then, be imitators of me" (1 Corinthians 4:15–16). And he sends Timothy, "my beloved and faithful child in the Lord, to remind you of my ways in Christ" (1 Corinthians 4:17). He says ways, and he means more than words. A father in the faith is known by what his children remember of how he lived.

The pattern spreads. "And you became imitators of us and of the Lord," Paul writes to the Thessalonians, "for you received the word in much affliction, with the joy of the Holy Spirit, so that you became an example to all the believers in Macedonia and in Achaia" (1 Thessalonians 1:6–7). The copies became originals for someone else, and what they copied was more than a doctrine. It was a way of receiving hardship with joy, which nobody has ever learned from a lecture.

Timothy is the fullest case. Near the end of his life Paul writes to him, "You, however, have followed my teaching, my conduct, my aim in life, my faith, my patience, my love, my steadfastness, my persecutions and sufferings that happened to me at Antioch, at Iconium, and at Lystra" (2 Timothy 3:10–11). Teaching comes first in the list and is at once surrounded by everything else. Lystra was, it seems, Timothy's own town (Acts 16:1–2), where on Paul's first visit the crowd "stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing that he was dead" (Acts 14:19). What Timothy followed was a whole life with the teaching folded inside it. Jesus had put the principle in one sentence: "A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone when he is fully trained will be like his teacher" (Luke 6:40).

The root lies deeper than teaching method. Jesus says of himself, "the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing. For whatever the Father does, that the Son does likewise" (John 5:19). Before imitation is a way of teaching, it is something in the life of God. Paul runs the line from there into the family: "Therefore be imitators of God, as beloved children" (Ephesians 5:1). His picture of the whole Christian life is a child copying a parent.

In the New Testament, doctrine travels inside a life. The question for parents was never whether our children will imitate us. It is what they will find when they do.

### Deuteronomy 6 and the Whole-Life Pattern

The Old Testament passage most Christian parents know here is Deuteronomy 6, usually read as a command to teach. It is one. But read the paragraph whole:

> And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise. You shall bind them as a sign on your hand, and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes. You shall write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates. (Deuteronomy 6:6–9)

The order matters. Before a word is said to a child, the words are to be "on your heart," and the verse just before names what that heart is for: "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might" (Deuteronomy 6:5). Teaching comes second, from a parent who has first been claimed. Then the passage walks through a day. Sitting and walking, lying down and rising, are paired extremes that take in everything between them; there is no hour when the teaching is off duty. The hand and the eyes follow, what a person does and what a person looks at, and then the doorposts and gates, the private house and the public threshold. The picture is of a life so full of love for God that it shows at the seams, and of talk that rises out of the sitting and the walking.

Israel later kept this passage literally, in the tefillin bound on arm and forehead and the mezuzah on the doorframe, and there is something fitting about obeying it with the body. But the history also shows what happens when a whole-life pattern hardens into a sign. Jesus could say of some of the most careful teachers of his day, "They do all their deeds to be seen by others. For they make their phylacteries broad and their fringes long" (Matthew 23:5). The phylacteries were the frontlets of Deuteronomy 6:8. The sign meant to mark a life given over to God had become a costume for being seen, and every religious household is tempted to make the same slide.

Deuteronomy knows a second danger, comfort. When Israel eats and is full in houses it did not fill, it must "take care lest you forget the LORD" (Deuteronomy 6:12). Forgetting in plenty rarely comes as a decision; the parents simply stop needing God anywhere the children can see. And when the son asks what all these practices mean, the answer begins, "We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt" (Deuteronomy 6:21). Israel's catechism starts with the parent counting himself among the slaves who needed rescue.

### What the Psychologists Saw, and What They Couldn't

Modern psychology came to the power of imitation late. Through the middle of the twentieth century much of American psychology assumed that learning ran on reinforcement: a behavior is tried, rewarded or punished, and so strengthened or weakened. In 1961 Albert Bandura, with Dorothea Ross and Sheila Ross at Stanford, reported the experiment that made him famous. Nursery-school children watched an adult attack an inflatable Bobo doll with a mallet and particular aggressive words, while other children watched an adult play quietly. Left alone afterward, the children who had watched the aggressive adult reproduced the aggression, down to specific blows and phrases. No one had rewarded them or told them to do it, and the adult hadn't been trying to teach anything.

In a 1965 study Bandura showed something more unsettling. Children who watched a filmed model punished for aggression imitated less, but when they were offered a reward for showing what the model had done, the difference largely disappeared. They had learned it all along and simply hadn't performed it. A child may take in what he sees years before he shows it, and the day he shows it may be the first day anyone knows he learned it.

In *Social Learning Theory* (1977) Bandura set out the fuller account: most human behavior is learned by watching models, which spares us having to find everything out by our own trial and error, and we attend most closely to the people who matter most to us. Put at its strongest, the theory is hard to resist. The parent is the model a child cannot stop studying, and the child is learning when neither of them knows it.

René Girard pressed further. In *Deceit, Desire and the Novel* (published in French in 1961) and at full scale in *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (1978), he argued that human beings copy not only one another's behavior but one another's desire. We don't arrive knowing what is worth wanting; we learn to want what our models want. Girard's larger account of how borrowed desire turns into rivalry and finds a scapegoat is still argued over, but the core insight lands in every kitchen. A child learns what his parents want, from the way his father's face changes when the talk turns to money, from what his mother checks first in the morning, from which of the children's achievements makes the adults glow. A parent can say for years that Christ is the treasure. The child studies what the parent actually reaches for, and learns to want that.

So the secular account got the mechanism right, and the church should say so without embarrassment. What it cannot tell us is what ought to travel through the channel, or what happens when the model itself is bent. The church has argued this ground before. In the early fifth century Pelagius and those around him taught that Adam's sin passed to his descendants by imitation, by bad example, rather than by any wound in human nature. Augustine answered in *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins* (411–412) that the trouble goes deeper than example, and the Council of Carthage condemned the Pelagian teaching in 418. If sin were only imitation, a good enough model would save a child. The church rejected that hope as too small. It sounds humane and turns out to be crushing, because it makes the parent the child's savior.

Luther gave the point a form a household can use. In *A Brief Instruction on What to Look For and Expect in the Gospels* (1521) he insisted that Christ must first be received as a gift, God's own act for us, before he can be followed as an example. The order is everything. Our children will imitate us, and Scripture blesses it, but no parent can be the gift. The one thing a parent can model that points beyond himself is the act of receiving it. Paul's sentence holds this in its second clause: be imitators of me, "as I am of Christ." Remove that clause and imitation is only another name for being shaped by whoever is closest. Keep it, and the parent becomes a window rather than a wall.

### The Actor's Mask: Hypocrisy and What Children See

The word Jesus uses again and again in Matthew 23 is *hypocrites*, and it comes from the Greek stage, where a *hypokritēs* was an actor speaking from behind a mask. The chapter, his last public teaching in Matthew's Gospel, opens with a concession we tend to skip: "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat, so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice" (Matthew 23:2–3). Their teaching was largely sound; the distance between the teaching and the life did the damage, and Jesus names it: "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger" (Matthew 23:4). Then comes the verse that ought to frighten every religious parent: "you travel across sea and land to make a single proselyte, and when he becomes a proselyte, you make him twice as much a child of hell as yourselves" (Matthew 23:15). Hypocrisy reproduces. The convert learns the mask along with the creed and wears it with a newcomer's zeal.

Children raised where the words and the life come apart tend to go one of two ways. Some become copies, wearing faith as a costume for certain rooms until the room changes. Others become cynics, rejecting the words because of the life; they aren't wrong to see the gap, only wrong to think the gap is the whole truth about God. I know the second group from the inside. I came to faith from atheism, and for years I was sure Christians didn't really believe what they said, because I had watched them. The gap between Sunday's words and Thursday's reflexes was the most persuasive argument against the faith I ever met. No skeptic argued me out of belief; hypocrites nearly kept me from it. I say that as a confession and not a complaint, because I am now the father of five sons who watch my Thursdays, and I have handed them the same gap that nearly kept me out.

The sociologists, working with surveys rather than parables, keep arriving at the same place. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton's *Soul Searching* (2005), drawn from the National Study of Youth and Religion, named the working faith of many American teenagers moralistic therapeutic deism, a God who wants people good and happy and is needed mainly for problems. The teenagers were mostly not rebelling against their parents' religion but reproducing it, and Smith and Denton concluded that parents tend to get, religiously, roughly what they are. Vern Bengtson's *Families and Faith* (2013), built on the Longitudinal Study of Generations begun in 1970, found that faith passed down most readily where children experienced their parents as warm, with fathers' warmth carrying particular weight, and less readily where parents were cold or harsh or where profession and life did not match. Christian Smith and Amy Adamczyk's *Handing Down the Faith* (2021) concluded that parents are by far the strongest influence on their children's religious lives, well ahead of congregations and youth programs, and that lasting faith was most tied to parents for whom the faith was plainly important and talked about in the ordinary week, joined to parenting that was warm and still held real expectations.

Those findings sit awkwardly against a century of church habit, in which parents learned, because we taught them, to think of formation as something delivered by trained people in rooms built for the purpose. I'm a pastor, and I have been part of that delivery system. The programs weren't the failure. The failure was letting parents believe the programs were the main thing, when Scripture and the research both say the kitchen outweighs the classroom.

The mask comes in more than one shape. Religious parents are tempted to perform piety. Parents who have seen that performance and fled it are tempted to something quieter: keeping their own convictions out of view so the child can choose for himself. It sounds like respect. What the child watches is a parent for whom God isn't important enough to practice. The performer teaches that faith is a costume. The withholder teaches that it is optional. Neither lets a child see what it looks like to live as though God were real.

### Neither Performance Nor Perfection

Here a serious parent can feel crushed. If children copy everything, desires included, who can bear it? Two escapes present themselves, and both are traps.

The first is performance: if the children are watching, give them something to watch. Jesus diagnosed it: "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them, for then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 6:1). And he answered it: "But when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you" (Matthew 6:6). The distinction that matters is audience. Performance is aimed at the one watching. Modeling is a life aimed at God that happens to be watched. Children are overhearers, formed most by the prayers they weren't meant to hear and the generosity no one announced.

The second trap is perfectionism, the belief that the answer to being watched is never to be seen failing. It looks like the opposite of hypocrisy and is the same mask worn tighter. A child raised on a parent's apparent flawlessness learns that the gospel is for people who don't really need it and that weakness must be hidden, which is to say he learns to hide from the God who comes looking for the lost.

Paul never offered that kind of model. The verses just before "join in imitating me" read, "Not that I have already obtained this or am already perfect, but I press on to make it my own, because Christ Jesus has made me his own" (Philippians 3:12). The man who asks to be imitated has just said he hasn't arrived; what he offers to be copied is the pressing on. And to Timothy he names the example he most wants the church to see: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost. But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe in him for eternal life" (1 Timothy 1:15–16). Paul's example is not his record. It is the mercy he received, put on display.

Here the weight of modeling turns toward mercy. The parent who repents in front of a child models the gospel. When a father who has spoken in anger comes back an hour later and says, "I was wrong to speak to you that way. I'm sorry. Will you forgive me?" his son has seen that a strong person can be wrong, that a wrong can be named without being excused, and that the way back runs through confession rather than around it. "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:8–9). A small child can't yet read that in a book. He can watch it happen in his own kitchen.

A repentance that forms a child names the wrong without a "but" attached, since the half-apology that blames the child for the parent's temper teaches blame rather than grace. It doesn't ask the child to manage the parent's guilt, because a child is not a confessor. It makes repair where repair is possible. And it happens more than once, because a single apology is an event and a pattern of them is a home. Psalm 51, David's confession after Nathan confronted him, carries the heading "To the choirmaster." The king's repentance for his worst sin was handed to the congregation to sing, and Israel's children learned the words of confession from their king's failure.

I have to be careful here. Repentance before a child is not a way to settle what has to be settled elsewhere. Where the wrong is violence or abuse in the home, an apology is not enough and is not safe to rely on. The child's protection comes first, and the way forward runs through outside help and real accountability; in the United States the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. Truth, protection and repair cannot be skipped by saying sorry well.

### The Saints, and the Model Behind Every Model

No parent was meant to be a child's only model. Hebrews tells the church, "Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God. Consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith" (Hebrews 13:7). The church took that seriously from the start. Ignatius of Antioch, under guard on the road to his death in Rome early in the second century, asked the Roman Christians to let him be an imitator of the suffering of his God. The account of Polycarp's martyrdom around 155 says the church loved the martyrs as disciples and imitators of the Lord. Athanasius wrote his *Life of Antony* around 360 so that others would follow the monk's pattern.

In Book VIII of the *Confessions*, Augustine tells how a visitor named Ponticianus described two imperial officials at Trier who came across the *Life of Antony*, read it, and gave up their careers on the spot to follow Christ. Augustine heard it in Milan in 386, and within days, in a garden, he heard a child's voice chanting "take up and read," opened Paul's letter to the Romans, and was converted. A chain of imitation ran from an Egyptian monk to two officials at Trier to the man who would shape the Western church for a thousand years. A millennium later Thomas à Kempis's *The Imitation of Christ*, written in the 1420s, gave the whole tradition its name.

The saints relieve a parent of being the only model; a household that reads the lives of faithful people and keeps company with older believers gives its children more faithful faces than any mother or father can supply. And they are hope for children whose parents failed them. Josiah's father Amon "did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, as Manasseh his father had done" (2 Kings 21:20), while Josiah "did what was right in the eyes of the LORD and walked in all the way of David his father" (2 Kings 22:2). The writer calls David, dead some three centuries, Josiah's father. Josiah copied a model his own father had abandoned. Timothy's father was a Greek (Acts 16:1), and so far as we're told did not share the faith; it came to Timothy through his mother and grandmother and then through Paul, who called him "my beloved and faithful child." I was raised without a father, and I don't read those passages as mere history. God has always been able to give a father in the faith to a child whose home had none.

The same Scriptures refuse to let parents turn this into a formula. Samuel served God faithfully from boyhood to old age, "Yet his sons did not walk in his ways but turned aside after gain. They took bribes and perverted justice" (1 Samuel 8:3). And Ezekiel, answering a generation that blamed its fathers, declares, "The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (Ezekiel 18:20). Modeling is the deepest channel of formation, not a machine that guarantees an outcome, and parents whose grown children have walked away should not read an essay like this as a verdict on them. Children are persons, not copies, and the Spirit is not bound by our examples.

### What the Children Will Read Back

Every parent is writing on their children in a hand they can't see, and the script is written in reflexes: what we reach for when bad news comes, how we speak of the people who wronged us, what makes our faces light up, what we do with money when no one is counting, and what we do in the hour after we have failed. Our children are reading it now. In twenty years they will read it back to us in their own marriages and their own bad Tuesdays, and we will recognize the handwriting because it will be ours.

That would be unbearable if what we were meant to pass down were our own righteousness. The parent is never the gift. The parent is a forgiven person who keeps returning to the one who is, and the most important thing a child can copy is the returning. So the deepest work of Christian parenting happens where no child can see it, in the parent's own confession before God and the slow undoing of reflexes that contradict what the mouth has professed. The children won't see most of that turning. They will inherit what it produces.

It ends at the cross, because every model ends there. At the cross the Son did what he saw the Father doing, all the way down, and the one whose example no one could fully copy became the gift anyone could receive. Our children will copy our anger and our appetites and our fears, and not all of that can be undone by an apology. But they may also copy the one thing we can hand them that points past us: a parent who knows he needs mercy, goes to get it, and comes back to the kitchen table forgiven. No one can stage that. It can only be lived, in the hours the children aren't supposed to be watching, and they are watching those hours now.
