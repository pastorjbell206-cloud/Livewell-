Over the last generation the word *exile* has moved from the commentaries into the ordinary talk of American churches, and many Christians who have never read Jeremiah use it to name a feeling they can't otherwise describe. The feeling is real. For most of this country's history the faith was the assumed default, and now it isn't. Believers find themselves holding convictions their neighbors no longer share, in a language the wider culture has stopped learning. People grieve that, and they're right to. Something good has been lost along with a great deal that deserved to go.

But the word gets used carelessly, and in two opposite directions. Some reach for exile as grievance, a way of saying *we are the wronged party now*, and the word becomes a costume for resentment. Others reach for it as permission to pull back from a culture they have decided is past saving, and the word becomes a door out of the city. The biblical word is truer than either use and harder than both. In Jeremiah, exile begins in judgment, is lived in a foreign city whose peace God commands his people to seek, is grieved without shame, and ends in a promise so long that almost no one who first heard it would live to see it kept. In the New Testament it becomes the name of the church's ordinary condition. Taken whole, the word doesn't flatter us. It forms us.

I came to this faith from atheism, which means I first met the church from outside its walls, and I owe the reader who is still outside them some honesty at the start. When the largest religious community in the most powerful nation on earth calls itself a band of exiles, a skeptic is entitled to laugh. That objection will get a real hearing. First, the letter itself, read where it was written.

### A letter to the deportees of 597 BC

Jeremiah 29 is usually quoted as if it were written after Jerusalem burned. It wasn't. The chapter dates itself: the letter went to the elders, priests, prophets and people whom Nebuchadnezzar had carried off, after King Jeconiah, the queen mother, the officials and the craftsmen had been deported (Jeremiah 29:1–2). That is the deportation of 597 BC, recorded in 2 Kings 24:10–16, when Babylon took the young king and the skilled and influential classes of Judah east and put Zedekiah on the throne as a vassal. The prophet Ezekiel went with them (Ezekiel 1:1–3). The temple was still standing, and it would stand for roughly another decade, until the Babylonians returned in 587 and burned it.

These exiles had every reason to think their displacement would be short. Jerusalem was still there, the house of God was still there, and their king was alive in Babylon. The prophets were telling them what they wanted to hear. In the temple courts Hananiah announced that within two years God would break Babylon's yoke and bring back the king, the captives and the sacred vessels, and he broke the wooden yoke Jeremiah had been wearing as a sign (Jeremiah 28:2–4, 10). Hananiah died that same year (Jeremiah 28:17). In Babylon other prophets were saying the same thing (Jeremiah 29:8–9, 21–23).

Into that hope Jeremiah sent a letter by the hand of two envoys of the king (Jeremiah 29:3), and it said the opposite:

> Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, to all the exiles whom I have sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon: Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease. But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare. (Jeremiah 29:4–7, ESV)

Three things in that paragraph deserve attention. The first is the subject of the verb: "whom I have sent into exile." Nebuchadnezzar's soldiers marched them east, but the letter won't let the exiles describe what happened as a pagan victory or an accident of geopolitics. God did it. That's a comfort, because they aren't beyond his reach in Babylon. It's also an indictment. Years earlier Jeremiah had stood at the gate of the temple and told the worshipers, "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (Jeremiah 7:4, ESV). The people had turned the presence of God into a guarantee that would secure them however they lived and whomever they oppressed (Jeremiah 7:5–7). The exile was the end of that illusion.

The second thing is the shape of the commands. Deuteronomy 28:30 had warned that a disobedient Israel would build a house and not live in it, plant a vineyard and not eat its fruit, betroth a wife and lose her. In the place of that curse, God tells the exiles to build and live, plant and eat, marry and raise children. Jeremiah's own commission had been "to pluck up and to break down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (Jeremiah 1:10, ESV), and Walter Brueggemann took the last two verbs as the title of his commentary on these chapters, *To Build, To Plant* (1991). The plucking up had happened. The building was to begin in Babylon.

The third thing is the word *welfare*, which translates the Hebrew *shalom* and appears three times in a single verse. It's the right ordering of a place, its peace and health and justice and the soundness of its common life. The exiles are told to seek that for the city that had deported them, and to pray for it, because their welfare and Babylon's are now bound up together. A people of God that prospers while its city decays has misread its location.

How the letter landed is recorded in the same chapter. A man named Shemaiah wrote from Babylon to the priest in Jerusalem, demanding to know why Jeremiah hadn't been put in the stocks, and he summarized the letter as his evidence: "Your exile will be long; build houses and live in them, and plant gardens and eat their produce" (Jeremiah 29:28, ESV). He meant it as proof that Jeremiah was a madman and a traitor. It was an accurate summary.

### Seventy years and a promise in the plural

The letter doesn't end with the command to settle. It turns to a promise, and the promise comes with a number attached: "For thus says the LORD: When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you, and I will fulfill to you my promise and bring you back to this place" (Jeremiah 29:10, ESV).

Seventy years is the span of a life. "The years of our life are seventy," says Psalm 90:10 (ESV), and most of the adults who heard this letter read aloud would die in Babylon. The number has been reckoned more than one way, from 605 to the return after Cyrus took Babylon in 539, or from the temple's destruction in 587 to the rebuilt temple's completion around 516, and Ezra opens by saying that Cyrus's decree fulfilled the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah (Ezra 1:1). The promise was real, and it lay on the far side of their graves.

Then comes the verse most American Christians know better than any other line in Jeremiah: "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope" (Jeremiah 29:11, ESV). It appears on graduation cards and hospital walls, usually as a private assurance that God intends good things for the reader's plans. Some of that comfort isn't wrong. The God who kept faith with a people keeps faith with each member of it. But the verse has been lifted so far out of its setting that its setting now contradicts its use. The "you" is plural in Hebrew. The "welfare" is the same *shalom* the letter has just told them to seek for Babylon. And the plans include seventy years as a subject people in a foreign empire. A promise that begins "when seventy years are completed" can't mean that nothing hard will happen to you; it means the hard thing isn't the last thing.

The verses that follow say what the future actually is: "Then you will call upon me and come and pray to me, and I will hear you. You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:12–13, ESV). The hope of the exiles was not first a return to real estate. It was God himself, found by a people who had lost everything they had used as a substitute for him.

A scene in Daniel shows what a faithful exile did with this letter. In the first year of Darius (Daniel 9:1), Daniel "perceived in the books the number of years that, according to the word of the LORD to Jeremiah the prophet, must pass before the end of the desolations of Jerusalem, namely, seventy years" (Daniel 9:2, ESV). The number is nearly up, and what he does next is the part we skip. Instead of celebrating, or blaming Babylon, he fasts and confesses: "we have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled, turning aside from your commandments and rules" (Daniel 9:5, ESV). The most blameless exile in the Old Testament reads the promise of return and prays in the first person plural.

That is where the word *exile* has to begin for us, if it's going to be used at all. When American Christians borrow it, we tend to cast ourselves as Daniel in the lions' den. We rarely cast ourselves as the people at the temple gate, reciting the name of a holy place as if it guaranteed our standing. Yet much of what the church in this country has lost, it lost by its own hand, trading witness for standing. Some of us tied the gospel to a party and a flag and called the bargain faithfulness. Others quietly subtracted whatever in the faith embarrassed the educated class and called that faithfulness too. Both made a sacred institution into a guarantee. Babylon didn't commit the scandals of abuse and cover-up. We did. I am a pastor, and the indictment runs through my own vocation before it reaches anyone else's. If the word *exile* fits us, it fits first at Daniel's prayer, not at Daniel's den.

### Daniel at court, and the psalm by the rivers

If Jeremiah 29 is the instruction, the first six chapters of Daniel are the instruction lived. These court tales tell of Judeans serving in a pagan administration. Daniel and his friends are chosen for the king's service and taught "the literature and language of the Chaldeans" (Daniel 1:4, ESV), given Babylonian names and raised high in the government. Nothing in the text treats any of this as compromise. Daniel goes so far as to counsel Nebuchadnezzar for the good of his soul and his realm: "break off your sins by practicing righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed" (Daniel 4:27, ESV). That is seeking the city's welfare at the highest level, and it includes telling the king the truth.

And yet there's a line, and the tales know exactly where it runs. "Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food, or with the wine that he drank" (Daniel 1:8, ESV). When Nebuchadnezzar raises a golden image and commands the empire to bow, the three friends refuse without knowing whether God will rescue them from the furnace: "But if not, be it known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image that you have set up" (Daniel 3:18, ESV). Years later, under a new empire, when a decree forbids prayer to anyone but the king, Daniel opens his windows toward Jerusalem and prays three times a day "as he had done previously" (Daniel 6:10, ESV). He doesn't stage a protest. He keeps doing what he had always done, in plain view, and lets the consequences come.

The line falls at worship, not at participation. That distinction is the whole art of exile, and the church has usually failed it in one of two ways. The first is assimilation: learning Babylon's language so well that you forget you ever spoke another, seeking the welfare of the city by becoming the city. The second is hostility: keeping your identity by abandoning your mission, building the walls high and praying for Babylon's ruin. Jeremiah forbids both. Daniel walks the narrow road between. The faithful exile serves the city, prays for it and tells it the truth, and he will not bow to it.

But the canon won't let exile sound tidy. Beside Jeremiah's letter it keeps another exile text, written after a worse catastrophe:

> By the waters of Babylon, there we sat down and wept, when we remembered Zion. (Psalm 137:1, ESV)

Psalm 137 looks back on the fall of 587, when the temple was gone and Edom had cheered the destruction (Psalm 137:7). The captors demand entertainment, a song of Zion, and the exiles hang their lyres on the willows. "How shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?" (Psalm 137:4, ESV). It vows never to forget Jerusalem, and it ends in a curse many Christians have never heard read in church, a blessing on whoever dashes Babylon's little ones against the rock (Psalm 137:8–9).

That ending shouldn't be smoothed over. But notice what the psalm does with its rage. It says it to God rather than organizing a raid on Babylon, handing the desire for vengeance to the one who says vengeance is his (Deuteronomy 32:35; Romans 12:19), which is the only safe place for it. The Scriptures that command prayer for Babylon's shalom also give the exiles words for fury, and neither text is allowed to silence the other. Exile hurts. A faith that can't say so has nothing to offer an exile.

Brueggemann built much of his work on this point. In *The Prophetic Imagination* (1978) he argued that the prophet's first task is to grieve, to bring to public speech the pain the powerful would prefer to numb, because a people that can't grieve can't receive anything new. In *Cadences of Home* (1997) he turned that reading on the American church, arguing that its loss of a taken-for-granted cultural world is best understood through the biblical experience of exile, and that preaching among exiles must make room for honest grief while resisting both assimilation into the empire's story and despair. He was writing chiefly about the mainline Protestant establishment. The diagnosis has turned out to travel.

Grief needs one distinction to stay healthy. Grief mourns what was lost, and there was real good in what we have lost: a shared moral vocabulary, a common calendar, funerals where everyone knew the words. Grievance insists that it was owed to us. Psalm 137 is grief, and even its rage is prayed. A great deal of what passes for Christian lament in this country is grievance. The exile who grieves can still seek the welfare of the city. The exile nursing a grievance can't, because he has already decided the city is his enemy.

### Elect exiles: Peter, Diognetus and the pilgrim city

The New Testament names the church's place in the world with the same word. Peter opens his first letter, "To those who are elect exiles of the dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1 Peter 1:1, ESV). Later he writes, "Beloved, I urge you as sojourners and exiles to abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul" (1 Peter 2:11, ESV). The Greek words are *parepidēmos*, a temporary resident away from home, and *paroikos*, a resident alien who lives in a place without belonging to it. At the end of the letter Peter sends greetings from "she who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen" (1 Peter 5:13, ESV), almost certainly the church in Rome. He is reading the church's whole situation through Jeremiah's categories. The empire is Babylon, the believers are the exiles, and the question is how to live there.

Scholars disagree about how literal the language is. John H. Elliott, in *A Home for the Homeless* (1981), argued that the words describe the recipients' real social situation, resident aliens and displaced people in Asia Minor to whom the letter offered a home in the household of God. Others read the language as chiefly theological. The letter holds both together. Its readers have been "born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1 Peter 1:3, ESV), and that new birth made them strange to neighbors who were surprised and offended when they stopped living as they once had (1 Peter 4:3–4). Their exile follows from their election, which is a different source from Judah's. Judah went into exile because of its idolatry; the church lives as exiles because it has been chosen and given a home that isn't yet visible. The American church's present discomfort is, I think, a mixture of the two, and honesty means saying which is which.

Peter's instructions to these exiles read like Jeremiah's letter written for a new empire:

> Be subject for the Lord's sake to every human institution, whether it be to the emperor as supreme, or to governors as sent by him to punish those who do evil and to praise those who do good. For this is the will of God, that by doing good you should put to silence the ignorance of foolish people. Live as people who are free, not using your freedom as a cover-up for evil, but living as servants of God. Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor. (1 Peter 2:13–17, ESV)

The strategy is neither withdrawal nor revolt but good conduct in plain sight, "so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:12, ESV). And the last line quietly puts the emperor in his place. He is to be honored, with the same verb Peter has just used for everyone. Only God is to be feared.

Probably in the second century, an anonymous Christian wrote to an inquirer named Diognetus to explain what kind of people the Christians were. The *Epistle to Diognetus* is an apology, a portrait composed for an outsider that real congregations surely fell short of, but its central description has shaped Christian self-understanding ever since. Christians, the writer says, are not marked off from others by country or language or dress. They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners; every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland a foreign land. They marry and have children like everyone else, but they don't expose their infants. They obey the laws, and in their own lives go beyond the laws. What the soul is in the body, the writer concludes, Christians are in the world. That is Jeremiah 29 as a way of life: people who live in the city more faithfully than its own citizens and never mistake it for home.

Then the arrangement changed. In 313 the Edict of Milan made Christianity legal, and in 380 the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the religion of the empire. When Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in 410, pagans blamed the abandonment of the old gods, and Christians who had tied their confidence to the empire were shaken. Augustine answered with *The City of God*, begun around 413 and finished around 426. He argued that two cities run through all of history, formed by two loves: the earthly city by the love of self reaching to contempt of God, the heavenly city by the love of God reaching to contempt of self (*City of God* 14.28). The heavenly city is on pilgrimage in this age. It makes use of the earthly city's peace and obeys the laws that order mortal life, so long as nothing hinders the worship of the one true God (19.17). And when Augustine wants a text for that posture, he goes to Jeremiah, recalling that the prophet told the captives to pray for Babylon, because in its peace they would have peace, the temporal peace the good and the wicked share (19.26).

The greatest theologian of Christendom's first century built his answer to the fall of Rome on Jeremiah's letter. Our exile didn't begin in 1963, or 1973, or 2015. The church has always been a pilgrim city living inside someone else's. Christendom let us forget it for a long time, and some of the forgetting was a gift, because the culture carried the faith to people who might never otherwise have heard it. But the forgetting was also a lie about who we are. What is new in our moment is not our exile. It's our discovery of it.

### Are American Christians exiles at all?

The modern revival of the word came from more than one direction. Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon's *Resident Aliens* (1989) marked Christendom's end with a small scene from Willimon's youth, a Sunday evening in 1963 when the Fox Theater in Greenville, South Carolina, opened in defiance of the old blue laws. They called the church a colony, an island of one culture in the middle of another, whose first political task is to be the church. Lee Beach's *The Church in Exile* (2015) developed the exile reading into a practical theology for congregations after Christendom.

The objection deserves to be stated at full strength, because it's mostly right. According to Pew Research Center's Religious Landscape Study, published in 2025, 62 percent of American adults still call themselves Christian. Christians hold most of the seats in Congress, churches keep their tax exemption, and between 2021 and 2023 the Supreme Court sided with religious claimants in *Fulton*, *Kennedy v. Bremerton* and *303 Creative*. Exile in the Bible meant deportation, siege and the loss of a homeland, and there are Christians today in prisons and refugee camps for whom the word is not a metaphor. Black Christians in this country sang of Pharaoh and the Jordan from inside slavery and Jim Crow, while the white Protestant churches held the cultural center. And in a democracy, Christians are citizens with votes and responsibilities. James Davison Hunter, in *To Change the World* (2010), argued that the posture he called "purity from," which he found among the neo-Anabaptists, Hauerwas included, becomes its own kind of defensiveness, and he proposed instead a "faithful presence within" the institutions of common life. A skeptic who suspects self-pity in the word, or an alibi for leaving the public square, is usually hearing correctly.

Two distinctions keep the word honest. The first separates exile from persecution. Peter knew the difference: "If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed," he wrote, and then, "let none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or as a meddler" (1 Peter 4:14–15, ESV). Persecution is suffering for the name of Christ. Losing a vote, a place of honor or a cultural presumption isn't persecution, and calling it that dishonors the believers who are actually in chains. The second separates exile from retreat. The exiles were told to build in Babylon, not to wall themselves off from it, and a community that uses the word to justify withdrawal from its neighbors has disobeyed the text it is quoting.

With those distinctions drawn, the verdict is this. Exile is not a sociological measure. It's a theological location. The critics are right that most American Christians aren't exiles in any sense a refugee would recognize. Peter's readers were called exiles because they had been chosen and born again into a home that isn't yet visible, not because they had lost status. In that sense Christians have always been exiles, in Rome, in Christendom and in Michigan, and we are exiles now whether we are winning elections or losing them. Whoever uses the word to complain has misread it. Whoever uses it to leave has disobeyed it.

### Building houses in a city that isn't home

What the letter asks is almost embarrassingly ordinary: build a house, plant a garden, marry, raise children, seek the welfare of the city and pray for it. None of that is heroic, and all of it is resistance, because each act refuses both the nostalgia that wants the old arrangement back and the panic that wants to flee. Praying for the city means knowing it: the schools that are failing, the families on the edge of losing their homes, the addicted, the lonely. Prayer rooted in knowledge is intercession. Prayer divorced from knowledge is sentiment. And seeking the city's shalom in a democracy means voting, serving and holding office without ever mistaking the office for the kingdom, as Daniel served Babylon without worshiping its king.

It also means repentance, because Jeremiah's exile began at the temple gate. Before the church in this country asks what the culture owes it, we have to ask what we did with the standing we had, and the honest answer will take more than one generation to give.

The letter to the exiles has a New Testament echo that finishes its meaning. Jesus "suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood. Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured. For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Hebrews 13:12–14, ESV). The Son of God took the exile's place himself, outside the walls, and the empty tomb is the pledge that the city to come is real.

Shemaiah's summary still stands: your exile will be long. Most of the people who first heard Jeremiah's letter died in Babylon, having planted gardens for grandchildren they would never meet. We may be that generation. The question the letter leaves with us isn't whether we'll see the return. It's whose welfare we're seeking this week, in the city where God has actually placed us, and whether our grief has quietly hardened into grievance.
