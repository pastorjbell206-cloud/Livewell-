Most parents of boys can feel it before they can name it. A son of twelve or fourteen is being told what a man is by voices that reached him through a screen long before his parents got around to the conversation. Some of them tell him that a man takes what he wants, never shows a wound, and treats women and weaker men as proof of his rank. Others tell him, more politely and from more respectable places, that the very things stirring in him (the wish to be strong, to compete, to protect, to be counted on) are the raw material of harm. The first voice inflates him. The second shrinks him. A boy who hears both is left with a choice that isn't really a choice: become the kind of man people are right to fear, or the kind of man nobody needs.

This essay is for the father, and for the mother raising a son on her own, who senses both pulls and wants something truer to hand him. It is written by a man raised without a father and now raising five sons, from inside the problem at both ends. A fatherless boy assembles manhood out of whatever is lying around; a father learns that his sons are assembling theirs out of him. The argument here is that the church does not meet this moment by baptizing one of the two scripts, which it has done more than once in American history. It meets it by recovering something older than both: strength as a virtue, formed in the affections, governed by love, and seen whole in a man who knelt with a towel around his waist on the night he was betrayed.

### Two false manhoods, and what each gets right

Call them the domineering and the diminished. The domineering script arrives loudly, through a profitable online economy of men who teach boys to rank everyone around them, to treat women as prizes and conversation as combat, to read kindness as weakness. It promises significance and delivers contempt, first for others and eventually for the boy himself. But be fair about why it works. It is nearly the only voice in his world telling him that his drive is not a disease and summoning him to discipline. Many of the boys drawn to it are not budding tyrants. They are lonely, behind in school, and hungry to be told that becoming a man is an achievement worth the effort. The domineering script is a lie told to a real hunger.

The diminished script arrives more quietly, often in the clothes of moral seriousness. It lives in the reflexive suspicion that male strength is a problem to be contained, in the ease with which "toxic masculinity" slides from naming particular sins to describing boys as such. In 2018 the American Psychological Association issued its first guidelines for practice with boys and men, warning that what it called traditional masculinity ideology, with its stoicism, competitiveness and dominance, harms the men who hold it. This script, too, is right about a great deal. Men commit the great majority of violent crime. A man who cannot weep or ask for help is not strong; he is sealed, and sealed men do terrible damage to the people who love them and to themselves. In the United States men die by suicide at roughly four times the rate of women, a pattern the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has reported for years. If you are carrying that kind of weight, or you're worried about a son who is, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, today. The diminished script's failure is that it offers a boy a long list of things not to be and almost nothing to become.

Richard Reeves, then a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution and writing from the center left, made the most careful recent case that the struggle is real. In *Of Boys and Men* (2022) he documented that boys trail girls at nearly every stage of education, that women earn a clear majority of American bachelor's degrees, and that the earnings of men without a college degree have stagnated or fallen in real terms since the late 1970s. The left, he argues, has been slow to admit that boys face structural disadvantages at all, treating male trouble as a symptom of masculinity itself. The right sees the trouble, blames feminism, and answers with nostalgia for an order that is not coming back. Each sees what the other refuses to see.

Set the two scripts side by side and a common grammar appears. Both make the boy's strength the whole question, one maximizing it and the other minimizing it. Neither asks what the strength is for or whom it answers to. Both keep the self at the center, one by enlarging it and the other by policing it. That shared assumption is much older than the internet.

### An old anxiety with a new feed

Anxiety about manhood is not a modern invention. The anthropologist David Gilmore, comparing cultures in *Manhood in the Making* (1990), found that in most societies manhood is treated as a precarious status that must be won, tested and publicly proven, and can be lost, in a way womanhood usually is not. The world into which the gospel came had its own sharp version. The Latin *virtus* grows from *vir*, a man, and Roman manliness meant mastery over one's household, one's inferiors, one's enemies and one's passions. Against that backdrop a crucified Lord was the most complete picture of unmanning the empire knew, a body stripped and displayed as a warning. Paul knew how it sounded: "we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23).

The American chapter begins in earnest in the late nineteenth century. Protestant congregations had long been majority female, and as middle-class men moved from farms into offices, a worry spread that the men and their churches were going soft. The historian Clifford Putney, in *Muscular Christianity* (2001), traces the answer that took shape between about 1880 and 1920. The movement built YMCA gymnasiums; at the YMCA training school in Springfield, Massachusetts, James Naismith invented basketball in 1891. Theodore Roosevelt gave the era its creed in his 1899 speech "The Strenuous Life." By 1925 Bruce Barton's bestseller *The Man Nobody Knows* presented Jesus as a robust outdoorsman and the founder of modern business. Some of this did real good for real boys. But notice the mechanism. A church anxious about losing men answered by remaking Jesus in the image of the man the culture already admired.

The pattern returned a century later. In 1990 Bill McCartney, then head football coach at the University of Colorado, founded Promise Keepers, which was soon filling stadiums with men who wept, confessed their failures and made promises about fidelity to their wives, presence with their children and reconciliation across racial lines. In October 1997 it gathered a vast crowd of men on the National Mall to pray. It is too easy to sneer at this from a distance; something real happened to many men. And yet its language of men reclaiming leadership could be heard, in the wrong house, as permission to rule. In 2001 John Eldredge's *Wild at Heart* told Christian men that every man longs for a battle to fight, an adventure to live and a beauty to rescue, and that the church had bored these longings out of them. The book met a genuine famine. It also located the heart of manhood in desires that Scripture treats as needing redemption, not simply release.

Kristin Kobes Du Mez gathered this history into a sustained indictment in *Jesus and John Wayne* (2020). Her argument is that for white American evangelicals, from the Cold War forward, a militant and patriarchal ideal of manhood, the rugged hero who protects by dominating, became a working theology, one that shaped their politics and helped them excuse strong men who abused their power. Whatever one concludes about every link in her chain, the pattern is recognizable, and it is the one Putney found a century earlier. Each generation, the church tried to win men back by making Jesus look like the man the culture already respected: the gentleman athlete, the executive, the coach, the cowboy. We have done this in our own pulpits and youth rooms. A son raised on the result learns to admire the image and never quite meets the man.

### The case for recovery, and the case against it

Both serious Christian positions deserve a hearing in the form their own defenders would sign. The case for recovering a robust masculinity runs like this. God made humanity male and female: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). Sexual difference is part of the goodness of creation, and a church embarrassed by it will lose the men it is embarrassed by. Reeves's data show that the drift of boys is real, not a partisan talking point. The sociologist W. Bradford Wilcox, in *Soft Patriarchs, New Men* (2004), found that churchgoing evangelical fathers with traditional convictions about family roles were, on average, more involved with their children and more emotionally engaged with their wives than many other men, which suggests the traditional vision in practice has often been gentler than its critics imagine. And there is a pastoral point that is hard to answer: if the church will not give boys a picture of courage and responsibility that calls on their strength, someone else will, and the someone else is already waiting in their phones.

The case against it is just as serious. Its defenders point out that "robust masculinity" in American churches has repeatedly served as cover for control. Mark Driscoll's Mars Hill Church, which made combative manhood part of its brand, came apart in 2014 amid accounts of bullying and abusive leadership. In 2022 an independent investigation of the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee reported that leaders had for years mishandled accusations of sexual abuse and treated survivors as threats to the institution. A movement that prizes strong men tends to protect them. The critics' biblical case is strong too. The fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5 is not sorted into masculine and feminine columns, and when the New Testament describes the man a congregation may entrust with authority, it asks for one "not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome, not a lover of money" (1 Timothy 3:3). Much of what is sold as biblical manhood is the admired manhood of American culture with verses attached.

Where a man's strength has already turned against his own household, the name for that is abuse, and no teaching on manhood or headship asks a wife or a child to endure it. In the United States the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

Underneath these two cases sits a second-order question that divides faithful churches without dividing the faith: whether Scripture assigns husbands a distinct headship in marriage and reserves certain offices in the church for men. Complementarian Christians read Ephesians 5 and 1 Timothy 2 as establishing an ordered, servant-shaped male leadership. Egalitarian Christians read the same passages in light of Galatians 3:28 as addressing their first-century setting rather than binding every age. Both confess the Nicene faith and both claim Scripture. This essay does not settle that question. It insists on something both sides can sign: whatever authority a man is given, Scripture measures it by what he spends for others, not by what he takes.

That leads to the distinction the argument turns on. Strength and dominance are not the same thing. Strength is the capacity to bear weight, to endure, protect, build and stand firm. Dominance is strength that has made itself its own end, spent on securing the self's rank over others. The domineering script pursues dominance and calls it strength. The diminished script gives up strength in order to be rid of dominance. Each side is right about the other's danger: a church that disarms boys leaves them defenseless, and a church that arms them without forming them breeds predators. The question is not how much strength a boy should have. It is what kind of heart will govern it.

### Men without chests

The oldest serious answer to that question is not Christian at all, and the church was wise enough to receive it. Aristotle, in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, treats courage as a virtue, a settled disposition of character, and places it as a mean between cowardice, which fears too much, and rashness, which fears too little. The brave man is not the one who feels no fear. He fears the right things, for the right reason, in the right way, and stands anyway because what he stands for is noble. Virtues, Aristotle insists, are acquired by practice: we become brave by doing brave acts, until effort becomes second nature. A boy is made brave by being given small, real things to be brave about, again and again, by people who expect him to manage them.

Read against Aristotle, the two false manhoods look like the vices on either side of one virtue. The domineering script sells rashness as courage. The diminished script, at its worst, sells timidity as conscience, raising a boy so afraid of being the wrong kind of man that he declines to be any kind at all.

Thomas Aquinas, taking up Aristotle in the *Summa Theologiae*, added a refinement that matters enormously for sons. The chief act of fortitude, he argued, is not attacking but enduring: standing firm under threat when retreat would be easier. He treated martyrdom, the refusal to abandon the truth at the cost of one's life, as an act of fortitude and among the most perfect acts a human being can perform. On this account the most courageous man is the one who will not leave his post when staying costs him everything, which is why the courage most sons will need looks less like a battle than like fidelity: to a wife through a hard decade, to a struggling child, to a conviction the room despises.

C. S. Lewis brought this tradition to bear on modern education in *The Abolition of Man* (1943). He was answering an English schoolbook, which he called *The Green Book*, that taught children in passing that calling a waterfall sublime says something about the speaker's feelings and nothing about the waterfall. Drawing on Plato, Lewis described the human being as a head, the reasoning intellect, and a belly, the appetites, joined by the chest: the seat of trained emotion, where a person has learned to love what is lovely, to be ashamed of what is shameful and to admire what is noble. The chest is where reason gets the strength to govern appetite. An education that debunks every emotion produces what Lewis called "men without chests," whose intellects may be sharp and whose appetites are certainly strong, with nothing between to hold the appetites in check. And then, Lewis observed, we demand virtue and enterprise from the very people we have made incapable of either.

The diagnosis fits our sons with uncomfortable precision. A boy taught that all his strong feelings are suspect and a boy taught that only his appetites are real have both been left without a chest. Augustine, in *The City of God* (426), gave a definition of virtue that answers both: virtue is rightly ordered love. The goal is a son who loves the right things in the right order, who feels anger at cruelty, tenderness toward the weak and shame at his own cowardice, and who has been shown by people he trusts what to do with each. That is the work of a whole childhood, as a boy watches what the adults around him admire, laugh at and grow angry about.

### Strength that kneels

The virtue tradition can describe the well-ordered heart, but it cannot supply the one thing a boy most needs, which is a person to look at. Scripture does not hand our sons a list of manly traits. It hands them a man.

Mark places Jesus' plainest teaching on power at a moment almost comic in its timing. He has just told the disciples, for the third time, that he will be handed over, mocked, flogged and killed (Mark 10:32–34). Immediately James and John ask for the seats of honor at his right and left, and the other ten are indignant. Jesus answers them all:

> "You know that those who are considered rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. But it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all. For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:42–45)

Jesus does not tell ambitious men that greatness is an illusion. He redefines it and grounds the definition in himself. The Gentile rulers are Roman manhood in its purest form, mastery exercised downward, and Jesus rules it out for his people. Greatness among them is measured by service, and the proof is a ransom.

John shows the same truth enacted. "Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end" (John 13:1), he writes, and then gives the most careful account of a household chore in Scripture. "Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God, rose from supper. He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist" (John 13:3–4). The service flows from the security. Jesus kneels because he is entirely sure of his standing, while the domineering script dominates because it is never sure of its worth. Having washed their feet, including the feet of the man about to betray him, he draws the conclusion: "If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet" (John 13:14).

Paul turns the pattern into the church's shared mind. Writing to Philippi, a Roman colony proud of its status and a congregation troubled by rivalry and conceit (Philippians 2:3–4), he says:

> Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. (Philippians 2:5–8)

The force of the passage depends on the height from which Christ descends. Only someone who has something can refuse to grasp it. Gentleness is available only to the strong, because the weak have nothing to restrain. What Paul describes is power under control, aimed away from the self, and Jesus used that language of himself: "learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29). The same man overturned the money changers' tables and wept at Lazarus's grave, and both came from one undivided love, fierce toward what destroys people and tender toward the people being destroyed.

Gethsemane completes the picture, and there Aristotle and Aquinas find more than they knew to ask for. Luke says "his sweat became like great drops of blood falling down to the ground" (Luke 22:44). Jesus asks for the cup to pass and lets his friends see his dread. Then he prays "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42), rises and walks toward the soldiers. That is courage in its fullest sense, fear felt completely and overcome by love and obedience. A boy who has been told that real men don't feel fear needs to know that the bravest man who ever lived sweat with it.

Here is the setting for the verse most often quoted to boys about manhood. At the close of his first letter to Corinth, Paul writes: "Be watchful, stand firm in the faith, act like men, be strong. Let all that you do be done in love" (1 Corinthians 16:13–14). "Act like men" translates a single Greek verb meaning to conduct oneself courageously, manfully. The defenders of a robust masculinity are right that Paul reaches for a word rooted in manhood without embarrassment. The critics are right to notice the context. The imperatives are plural and addressed to the whole congregation, the "brothers" of verse 15, women as well as men. And Paul fences the military verbs with love, in a letter to a church fractured by pride, where he has already defined what love is: "Love is patient and kind; love does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant or rude. It does not insist on its own way" (1 Corinthians 13:4–5). Courage without love is the domineering script. Love without courage is the diminished one. Paul allows neither.

### What David told his son

Scripture records its own charge from a father to a son about manhood. As David lay dying, 1 Kings records, he called for Solomon:

> "I am about to go the way of all the earth. Be strong, and show yourself a man, and keep the charge of the LORD your God, walking in his ways and keeping his statutes, his commandments, his rules, and his testimonies, as it is written in the Law of Moses, that you may prosper in all that you do and wherever you turn, that the LORD may establish his word that he spoke concerning me, saying, 'If your sons pay close attention to their way, to walk before me in faithfulness with all their heart and with all their soul, you shall not lack a man on the throne of Israel.'" (1 Kings 2:2–4)

Notice how David defines the manhood he commands. "Show yourself a man" is not followed by a call to conquest, though David was Israel's most celebrated warrior. It is followed by obedience: keep the charge, walk in his ways, attend to the law. The charge echoes Deuteronomy's instruction that Israel's king copy out the law and read it all his days, "that his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers" (Deuteronomy 17:20). In Israel's own memory, the measure of a strong man was a heart kept low before God.

And yet the text does not let David off easily. Within a few verses the same deathbed speech turns to settling old scores with Joab and Shimei (1 Kings 2:5–9). The record of David's fatherhood is harder still. After Amnon raped his half sister Tamar, the text says, "When King David heard of all these things, he was very angry" (2 Samuel 13:21), and it records nothing he did about it. Of Adonijah the narrator observes, "His father had never at any time displeased him by asking, 'Why have you done thus and so?'" (1 Kings 1:6). David took Bathsheba and arranged Uriah's death with the full weight of royal power, and at his own table he could not bring himself to correct his sons. The domineering and the diminished were not two different men in David's house. They were the same man, all force in the palace and all silence at the table.

That is the most uncomfortable thing Scripture says to fathers about manhood, and it applies to the one writing this as much as to anyone reading it. The two false manhoods do not only belong to the culture. They live in us, often in the same man, domineering when we are threatened and absent when we are tired. Our sons are formed less by the influencers we worry about than by the man in the house, or by his absence, and by whatever men the family has invited close.

That is where the ordinary work lies. A son learns what strength is for by watching it spent: on his mother when she is exhausted, on a neighbor who can't repay it, on a promise kept when keeping it costs something. He learns that a strong man can be wrong when his father comes to him and says, specifically, I was wrong to speak to you that way, and I'm sorry. He learns to govern his emotions by having them received without contempt and patiently shaped, which is Lewis's training of the chest done at the kitchen table. He learns courage by being handed responsibilities a little heavier than he thinks he can carry. And he needs other men; a mother raising a son alone has nothing to apologize for in that need, only something to meet, by bringing faithful men into his life on purpose. Most of all, he needs to see that the strength of the adults he admires is borrowed, that they pray because they need to. A son who never sees his father need God will conclude that faith is for the weak.

None of this guarantees a son. Solomon received the best charge a father ever gave and did not keep it; "when Solomon was old his wives turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kings 11:4). The promise that Israel would not lack a man on the throne was conditioned on sons who walked faithfully, and they didn't, and in 586 BC Babylon ended the line of kings in Jerusalem. The promise was finally kept not by a son who performed well enough but by the Son of David. He could have called on his Father for more than twelve legions of angels (Matthew 26:53) and let himself be nailed to a Roman cross instead. He "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2), and on the third day God raised him, vindicating that pattern of strength against every empire that ever mistook domination for greatness.

That is what a parent carries into an ordinary evening with a son who is already listening to other voices. We cannot manufacture the man we want him to become. We can show him, partially and with frequent repentance, a strength that kneels, and we can tell him where it came from. Then we have to entrust him to the only Father whose charge does not depend on the son's performance, knowing that the boy is watching what we do with our own strength tonight, and that he will remember it longer than anything we said.
