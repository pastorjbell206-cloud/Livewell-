In the summer of 2014 the journal *Science* published a set of experiments from the University of Virginia under a plain title, "Just Think." Timothy Wilson and his colleagues asked people to sit alone in a bare room for somewhere between six and fifteen minutes, with no phone, no book and nothing to do but think. Most of them didn't enjoy it. In the study that made the headlines, participants first felt a mild electric shock, and those who said they would pay money not to feel it again were then left alone for fifteen minutes with a button that would deliver it. About two-thirds of the men and a quarter of the women pressed the button at least once. Offered a choice between their own thoughts and a small pain they had just said they would pay to avoid, a good many ordinary people chose the pain.

It's tempting to read that as a verdict on the smartphone, and part of it is. But the room in that experiment is much older than the phone. In the late 1650s Blaise Pascal, a mathematician who had turned his formidable mind to the question of why human beings behave as they do, wrote among the notes later published as the *Pensées* (1670) a sentence that is quoted far more often than it is read. In substance it says that all of humanity's unhappiness comes from a single thing: we don't know how to stay quietly at rest in a room. The Virginia researchers weren't setting out to confirm a seventeenth-century Christian apologist. They built his room anyway, and then they watched people try to get out of it.

Pascal's observation has a quality a skeptic can respect. It asks for no belief at the outset. It asks you to sit down and notice what you do next. Anyone who has tried to be quiet on purpose, even for ten minutes, knows what happens. The silence is not empty. The moment the outer noise stops, an inner noise rushes in to fill it: the unfinished task, the old grievance, the rehearsed argument with someone who isn't in the room, the low dread that has no name. We don't avoid silence because there is nothing in it. We avoid it because it is full, and full of what we would rather not face.

The church is not exempt from this, and in some ways it has been worse at silence than the culture around it. We fill our services so that no gap goes unfilled, measure faithfulness by activity, and treat a quiet Christian as a disengaged one. I carry the same device as everyone else, and I reach for it in the same dead moments, and the fight to keep one more unnecessary thing out of a quiet morning is one I lose more often than I'd like. So this is not an essay written from the far side of the problem.

The Christian tradition has a long memory on this question, longer than the attention economy by sixteen centuries. In the fourth century men and women walked into the deserts of Egypt to be alone and silent on purpose, and what they learned there was gathered into a body of sayings that is still in print. In the sixth, Benedict wrote silence into the ordinary day of a working community. Behind both stand Elijah hearing God in a low whisper after the wind, the earthquake and the fire, and Jesus rising before dawn to pray in a desolate place. What they knew is that silence and solitude are not techniques for calm. They are the place where a person stops performing for an audience, stands before God without the noise he has been using to hide, and comes back able to love. Seeing that depends on two distinctions most talk of "unplugging" misses. Solitude is not loneliness, and silence is not avoidance. In each case the difference lies not in how quiet the room is but in who is in it with you.

### Pascal's room and the business of noise

Pascal's name for our condition was *divertissement*, usually translated diversion, and he meant something sharper than entertainment. He noticed that a king surrounded by every pleasure becomes miserable the moment he is left without something to occupy him, because then he begins to think about himself, about what he is and where he is headed. He noticed that the man who spends a day hunting a hare would not want the hare if you handed it to him. He wants the chase, because the chase keeps him from thinking. Diversion, for Pascal, is the whole machinery by which human beings keep their attention off their own condition, and above all off their mortality. We are, in his famous image, reeds, the weakest things in nature, but thinking reeds, and the thinking is exactly what we arrange our lives to avoid.

It matters that Pascal was not a scold about pleasure. His point was about the human creature. Our restlessness is a symptom of a ruined greatness, of beings made for something we no longer have and cannot stand to notice we lack. He was standing in a line that runs back to Augustine, who opened his *Confessions* (c. 400) by telling God that he had made us for himself and that our hearts are restless until they rest in him. The restlessness has a cause, and the cause is theological. A creature made for communion with God, estranged from him, will fill every silence with sound rather than hear the absence.

What changed in the modern period is that someone learned to sell the diversion. Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants* (2016) traces the business back to 1833, when Benjamin Day began selling the *New York Sun* for a penny and making his money not from readers but from the advertisers who paid to reach them. The reader's attention had become the product. Radio and then television refined the model, and in 1971 the economist Herbert Simon put the consequence in a sentence that has only grown truer: a wealth of information consumes the attention of those who receive it, and so creates a poverty of attention. The smartphone, introduced in 2007, finished the arrangement by putting the whole apparatus in a pocket and keeping it there through every waking hour. The notification is designed to interrupt. The feed is designed never to end. The noise of our age was built, carefully and profitably, by people whose income depends on denying us the half second of quiet in which we might put the thing down.

Sherry Turkle, who has studied our life with machines at MIT for decades, named the social result in *Alone Together* (2011): people physically in the same room, each of them somewhere else. In *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015) she built her argument around the three chairs Thoreau kept in his cabin at Walden, one for solitude, two for friendship, three for society, and made the case that the first chair comes first for a reason. The capacity to be alone with one's own thoughts, she argues, is where the capacity for real conversation and for empathy begins. A person who cannot bear solitude does not become more connected. He becomes more dependent on other people as a way of not being alone with himself, and so he cannot actually attend to them.

The critics of the attention economy have the symptom right, and Christians should say so without hedging. But Pascal sees something they mostly cannot. Put the phone in a drawer and the restlessness is still there, reaching for the next thing. The industry didn't create our flight from ourselves and from God. It found the flight already underway and learned to charge rent on it. That is why the answer the tradition gives is not merely a better relationship with technology. It is a different relationship with silence, and the people who first worked that out went a long way from any city to do it.

### Why Christians walked into the desert

Around the year 270 a young Egyptian Christian named Antony, not yet twenty and recently orphaned, walked into church and heard the gospel reading: "If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me" (Matthew 19:21). He took it as addressed to him. He gave away his inheritance, apprenticed himself to an old ascetic at the edge of his village, and over the following years went farther and farther out, first to the tombs, then to an abandoned fort in the desert where he lived alone for about twenty years. He died in 356, by tradition aged about a hundred and five, and within a few years Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote *The Life of Antony* (c. 360). That short book may have done more to shape Christian imagination about solitude than any other outside Scripture. In the eighth book of his *Confessions*, Augustine describes a visitor telling him and his friend Alypius about Antony, of whom they had never heard, and about two imperial officials at Trier who had read the *Life* and left the emperor's service on the spot. Augustine was undone by the story. His conversion in a Milan garden in 386 followed within days.

The timing of the desert movement matters. It swelled in the fourth century, after the Edict of Milan in 313 ended the persecutions and the church began its long rise to respectability. When it no longer cost anything to be a Christian in the city, some Christians went where it still did. They were easy to caricature then and remain easy to caricature now, and some of them earned it. By the fifth century Simeon the Stylite was living on top of a pillar in Syria. But the core of the movement was not spectacle. The sayings of these men and women were collected in the fifth century into what is called the *Apophthegmata Patrum*, the Sayings of the Desert Fathers, and in English translations such as Benedicta Ward's (1975) they read less like the notes of eccentrics than like the case files of unusually honest physicians of the soul.

Arsenius is a good place to start, because his life refuses the idea that the desert was for people who had no other options. He was a Roman of high standing who served as tutor in the household of the emperor Theodosius, and around 394 he left the court for the desert of Scetis. According to the Sayings, while he was still at the palace he prayed to be shown the way of salvation and heard a voice telling him to flee from men. In the desert he prayed the same prayer and received a fuller answer, usually rendered "Flee, be silent, pray," with the promise that these are the roots of a life free from sin. A saying attributed to him elsewhere in the collection makes the same point from the other side: he had often regretted having spoken, and never regretted having kept silent.

Amma Syncletica, one of the desert mothers whose sayings survive, understood that the desert could be abused as easily as the city. She warned that many who live in the mountains behave as if they were in town and waste their time, and that it is possible to be a solitary in one's mind while living in a crowd, and possible for one who lives alone to live in the crowd of his own thoughts. Geography guarantees nothing. She also described the beginning of the life with God as a great deal of struggle and suffering, followed by a joy that can't be put into words, and compared it to lighting a fire: at first the smoke makes you weep, and only then does the flame come.

The fathers had a name for the particular misery that drives a person out of the silent room. Evagrius Ponticus, who lived in the Egyptian desert in the last decades of the fourth century, called it *acedia*, and in his *Praktikos* he described it with a precision any modern reader will recognize. It makes the day seem endless. It sends the monk to the window again and again to see how far the sun has moved. It fills him with dislike for his place and his work, and it whispers that he could serve God so much better somewhere else. The monks read the "destruction that wastes at noonday" of Psalm 91:6 in their Greek Bible as the noonday demon, and that is what Evagrius meant: the restlessness that arrives in the flat middle of the day and says anything would be better than this. It is Pascal's diversion seen from inside the cell, fifteen centuries before the notification.

The answer the fathers gave to acedia was almost always the same. A brother came to Abba Moses asking for a word, and the old man told him, in the saying as it is usually rendered, "Go, sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything." Moses himself, by the accounts that come down to us, had been a violent man and a robber before he came to the desert, which is part of why the saying carries weight. The cell did not make him cold. When he was once summoned to sit in judgment on a brother's fault, he arrived carrying a vessel that leaked, and when asked what it meant he said that his own sins were running out behind him where he could not see them, and yet he had come to judge someone else. That is what staying in the silence produced in a man who had every reason to run from it, a humility deep enough to disarm the room.

Henri Nouwen, who organized *The Way of the Heart* (1981) around the three words spoken to Arsenius, argued that we have the desert almost exactly backward. We picture solitude as a private place to recover, a kind of restorative retreat. The fathers experienced it as what Nouwen called the furnace of transformation, the place where, stripped of the roles and the audience and the busyness by which we ordinarily hold ourselves together, we finally meet ourselves as we are and meet God without anything in between. Solitude is not where you go to get away from your problems. It is where you finally have them, without the noise you have been using to keep them at arm's length. The fathers who walked into that silence were rarely serene when they arrived, and they knew the only road to a quiet heart ran through the racket of a confronted one.
