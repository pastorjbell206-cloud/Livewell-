In the summer of 2014 the journal *Science* published a set of experiments from the University of Virginia under a plain title, "Just Think." Timothy Wilson and his colleagues asked people to sit alone in a bare room for somewhere between six and fifteen minutes, with no phone, no book and nothing to do but think. On the whole they didn't enjoy it. In the study that made the headlines, participants first felt a mild electric shock, and those who said they would pay money not to feel it again were then left alone for fifteen minutes with a button that would deliver it. About two-thirds of the men and a quarter of the women pressed the button at least once. Offered a choice between their own thoughts and a small pain they had just said they would pay to avoid, a good many ordinary people chose the pain.

It's tempting to read that as a verdict on the smartphone, and part of it is. But the room in that experiment is much older than the phone. Blaise Pascal left among his notes, unfinished at his death in 1662 and published as the *Pensées* (1670), a line quoted far more often than read. In substance it says that all of humanity's unhappiness comes from a single thing: we don't know how to stay quietly at rest in a room. The Virginia researchers weren't setting out to confirm a seventeenth-century Christian apologist. They built his room anyway, and then they watched people try to get out of it.

Pascal's observation has a quality a skeptic can respect. It asks for no belief at the outset. It asks you to sit down and notice what you do next. Anyone who has tried to be quiet on purpose for ten minutes knows the silence is not empty. The moment the outer noise stops, an inner noise rushes in to fill it: the unfinished task, the old grievance, the rehearsed argument with someone who isn't in the room, the low dread that has no name. We don't avoid silence because there is nothing in it. We avoid it because it is full, and full of what we would rather not face.

The church is not exempt. We fill our services so that no gap goes unfilled, measure faithfulness by activity, and treat a quiet Christian as a disengaged one. I carry the same device as everyone else, I feel the same pull and reach for it in the same dead moments, and I have to fight the same fight every quiet morning to keep from filling it with one more thing I don't need. So this is not an essay written from the far side of the problem.

The Christian tradition has a long memory on this question, longer than the attention economy by fifteen centuries: the desert fathers and mothers of fourth-century Egypt, Benedict's Rule, and behind them Elijah hearing God in a low whisper after the wind, the earthquake and the fire, and Jesus rising before dawn to pray in a desolate place. What they knew is that silence and solitude are not techniques for calm. They are the place where a person stops performing for an audience, stands before God without the noise he has been using to hide, and comes back able to love. Seeing that depends on two distinctions most talk of "unplugging" misses. Solitude is not loneliness, and silence is not avoidance. In each case the difference lies not in how quiet the room is but in who is in it with you.

### Pascal's room and the business of noise

Pascal's name for our condition was *divertissement*, usually translated diversion, and he meant something sharper than entertainment. He noticed that a king surrounded by every pleasure becomes miserable the moment he is left without something to occupy him, because then he begins to think about himself, about what he is and where he is headed. The man who spends a day hunting a hare would not want the hare if you handed it to him; he wants the chase, because the chase keeps him from thinking. Diversion, for Pascal, is the whole machinery by which human beings keep their attention off their own condition, and above all off their mortality. We are, in his famous image, reeds, the weakest things in nature, but thinking reeds, and the thinking is exactly what we arrange our lives to avoid.

Pascal was not scolding pleasure. Our restlessness, he argued, is a symptom of a ruined greatness, of beings made for something we no longer have and cannot stand to notice we lack. He was standing in a line that runs back to Augustine, who opened his *Confessions* (c. 400) by telling God that he had made us for himself and that our hearts are restless until they rest in him. The restlessness has a cause, and the cause is theological. A creature made for communion with God, estranged from him, will fill every silence with sound rather than hear the absence.

What changed in the modern period is that someone learned to sell the diversion. Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants* (2016) traces the business back to 1833, when Benjamin Day began selling the *New York Sun* for a penny and making his money not from readers but from the advertisers who paid to reach them. The reader's attention had become the product. Radio and then television refined the model, and in 1971 the economist Herbert Simon put the consequence in a sentence that has only grown truer: a wealth of information consumes the attention of those who receive it, and so creates a poverty of attention. The iPhone, introduced in 2007, finished the arrangement by putting the whole apparatus in a pocket and keeping it there through every waking hour. The notification is designed to interrupt. The feed is designed never to end. The noise of our age was built, carefully and profitably, by people whose income depends on denying us the half second of quiet in which we might put the thing down.

Sherry Turkle of MIT named the social result in *Alone Together* (2011): people in the same room, each of them somewhere else. In *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015) she built her case around the three chairs Thoreau kept at Walden, one for solitude, two for friendship, three for society, and argued that the first chair comes first for a reason. The capacity to be alone with one's thoughts is where the capacity for conversation and empathy begins. A person who cannot bear solitude does not become more connected. He uses other people to avoid himself, and so he cannot really attend to them.

The critics of the attention economy have the symptom right, and Christians should say so without hedging. But Pascal sees something they mostly cannot. Put the phone in a drawer and the restlessness is still there, reaching for the next thing. The industry didn't create our flight from ourselves and from God. It found the flight already underway and learned to charge rent on it. The tradition's answer is a different relationship with silence, and the people who first worked that out went a long way from any city to do it.

### Why Christians walked into the desert

Around the year 270 a young Egyptian Christian named Antony, born around 251 and orphaned at about eighteen or twenty, walked into church and heard the gospel reading: "If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me" (Matthew 19:21). He took it as addressed to him. He gave away his inheritance and went farther and farther out, first to the tombs, then to an abandoned fort in the desert where he lived alone for about twenty years. He died in 356, and within a few years Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote *The Life of Antony* (c. 360). In the eighth book of his *Confessions*, Augustine describes hearing of Antony for the first time, and of two imperial officials at Trier who read the *Life* and left the emperor's service on the spot. His own conversion in a Milan garden in 386 follows directly in the narrative.

The timing of the desert movement matters. It swelled in the fourth century, after the Edict of Milan in 313 ended the persecutions and the church began its long rise to respectability. When it no longer cost anything to be a Christian in the city, some Christians went where it still did. Some of them earned the caricature of eccentricity, but the core of the movement was not spectacle. The sayings of these men and women were collected in the fifth century into what is called the *Apophthegmata Patrum*, the Sayings of the Desert Fathers, and in English translations such as Benedicta Ward's (1975) they read less like the notes of eccentrics than like the case files of unusually honest physicians of the soul.

Arsenius's life refuses the idea that the desert was for people with no other options. By tradition he was a Roman of high standing who served as tutor in the household of the emperor Theodosius, and around 394 he left the court for the desert of Scetis. According to the Sayings, while he was still at the palace he prayed to be shown the way of salvation and heard a voice telling him to flee from men. In the desert he prayed the same prayer and received a fuller answer, which Ward renders "flee, be silent, pray always," with the promise that these are the sources of a life without sin.

Amma Syncletica, one of the desert mothers whose sayings survive, understood that the desert could be abused as easily as the city. She warned that many who live in the mountains behave as if they were in town and waste their time, and that it is possible to be a solitary in one's mind while living in a crowd, and possible for one who lives alone to live in the crowd of his own thoughts. Geography guarantees nothing.

The fathers had a name for what drives a person out of the silent room. Evagrius Ponticus, who lived in the Egyptian desert in the last decades of the fourth century, called it *acedia*, and in his *Praktikos* he described it with a precision any modern reader will recognize. It makes the day seem endless. It sends the monk to the window again and again to see how far the sun has moved. It fills him with dislike for his place and his work, and it whispers that he could serve God so much better somewhere else. The monks, praying the psalm in its Greek version, read the "destruction that wastes at noonday" of Psalm 91:6 as a noonday demon, the restlessness that arrives in the flat middle of the day and says anything would be better than this. It is Pascal's diversion seen from inside the cell, sixteen centuries before the notification.

The answer the fathers gave to acedia was almost always the same. A brother came to Abba Moses asking for a word, and the old man told him, in Ward's rendering, "Go, sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything." Moses himself, by the accounts that come down to us, had been a violent man and a robber before he came to the desert, and the cell did not make him cold. When he was once summoned to sit in judgment on a brother's fault, he arrived carrying a vessel that leaked, and when asked what it meant he said that his own sins were running out behind him where he could not see them, and yet he had come to judge someone else. That is what staying in the silence produced in a man who had every reason to run from it, a humility deep enough to disarm the room.

Henri Nouwen, who organized *The Way of the Heart* (1981) around the three words spoken to Arsenius, argued that we have the desert almost exactly backward. We picture solitude as a private place to recover, a kind of restorative retreat. The fathers experienced it as what Nouwen called the furnace of transformation, the place where, stripped of the roles and the audience and the busyness by which we ordinarily hold ourselves together, we finally meet ourselves as we are and meet God without anything in between. Solitude is not where you go to get away from your problems. It is where you finally have them, without the noise you have been using to keep them at arm's length. The fathers who walked into that silence were rarely serene when they arrived, and they knew the only road to a quiet heart ran through the racket of a confronted one.

### Elijah's whisper and the withdrawals of Jesus

The desert tradition was reading its Bible, and the Bible keeps staging the same scene. The clearest version comes right after the loudest victory in the Old Testament. On Mount Carmel, "the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the burnt offering" (1 Kings 18:38) in front of the prophets of Baal and the whole watching nation. A chapter later Elijah is running for his life from Jezebel, and he sits down under a broom tree and asks to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers" (1 Kings 19:4). God answers first with bread. An angel touches him and says, "Arise and eat" (19:5), and he eats and sleeps and eats again. Then he walks forty days to Horeb, the mountain of Moses, and there the old signs of Sinai pass in front of him, the signs of the day when "the LORD had descended on it in fire" and "the whole mountain trembled greatly" (Exodus 19:18):

> And behold, the LORD passed by, and a great and strong wind tore the mountains and broke in pieces the rocks before the LORD, but the LORD was not in the wind. And after the wind an earthquake, but the LORD was not in the earthquake. And after the earthquake a fire, but the LORD was not in the fire. And after the fire the sound of a low whisper. (1 Kings 19:11–12)

The Hebrew phrase behind "the sound of a low whisper," *qol demamah daqqah*, is strange enough that it is often glossed literally as "a sound of thin silence." The God who answered by fire on Carmel is not in the fire now. Elijah has had his spectacle, and it did not hold him. What meets him on the mountain is quiet, and out of the quiet comes a question, "What are you doing here, Elijah?" (19:13), and then a commission: go back, anoint kings and a successor, and know that seven thousand in Israel have not bowed the knee to Baal (19:15–18). The silence sent him back, corrected and accompanied.

Psalm 46:10 has suffered a similar softening. "Be still, and know that I am God" hangs on kitchen walls as an invitation to calm. In the psalm it comes in the middle of a war. "The nations rage, the kingdoms totter" (46:6), and the God of Jacob "makes wars cease to the end of the earth; he breaks the bow and shatters the spear; he burns the chariots with fire" (46:9). The Hebrew verb behind "be still," *harpu*, carries the sense of letting go, of ceasing, of hands falling slack. Interpreters differ over whether it is addressed to the raging nations or to God's frightened people, but either way it is spoken to hands gripping weapons. The stillness the psalm commands is not relaxation but surrender, the dropping of the means by which we try to secure ourselves, and only after that comes the knowing.

Then there is Jesus, who needed silence less than anyone who ever lived and sought it constantly. At his baptism the Father's voice says, "You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11), and "the Spirit immediately drove him out into the wilderness" (1:12). After a day of healing in Capernaum, "rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed" (1:35), and when the disciples tracked him down with the news that "Everyone is looking for you," he answered, "Let us go on to the next towns, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out" (1:37–38). Luke notes that as the crowds around him grew, "he would withdraw to desolate places and pray" (Luke 5:16). When he heard that John the Baptist had been killed, "he withdrew from there in a boat to a desolate place by himself" (Matthew 14:13), and after feeding the five thousand "he went up on the mountain by himself to pray. When evening came, he was there alone" (14:23). John adds that the crowd was about to take him by force and make him king, and so he "withdrew again to the mountain by himself" (John 6:15).

Grief, popularity, demand and the offer of power: those are the moments he goes off alone. In solitude he received from the Father the identity the crowd kept trying to hand him on its own terms, and he came back knowing what he was for. It is of a piece with what he told his disciples about prayer: "go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret" (Matthew 6:6). The room he commends is the one place where there is no audience to perform for.

### Solitude is not loneliness, and silence is not avoidance

Paul Tillich, in a sermon collected in *The Eternal Now* (1963), observed that our language has two words for being alone. Loneliness names its pain, and solitude names its glory. Scripture takes loneliness seriously. The first thing God calls not good in the created order is "that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18). Loneliness is a real wound, and in 2023 the United States Surgeon General issued a formal advisory calling it an epidemic. Loneliness is aloneness imposed and experienced as absence. Solitude is aloneness chosen, in the presence of God, for the sake of returning to others. One is a deprivation and the other is a discipline, and the tragedy of our moment is that we have more of the first and almost none of the second. We are rarely alone and rarely anything but lonely, which is exactly what Turkle's phrase "alone together" describes.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer drew the line from the other side. *Life Together* (1939) was written after the Gestapo closed his underground seminary at Finkenwalde in 1937, and in the chapter called "The Day Alone" he warns in both directions. In John Doberstein's translation: "Let him who cannot be alone beware of community." And the converse: "Let him who is not in community beware of being alone." Each guards the other. Community without solitude becomes a crowd we use to escape ourselves. Solitude without community becomes a private religion answerable to no one.

The second distinction is harder, because silence can be a hiding place too. Psalm 39 opens with a man who has put a muzzle on his mouth, meaning it as a guard against sinning with his tongue while the wicked are present (39:1), and he reports the result honestly: "I was mute and silent; I held my peace to no avail, and my distress grew worse" (Psalm 39:2). His silence is clenched against his own heart rather than offered to God, and it breaks only when he turns and speaks to the Lord (39:3–4). We know this silence: the husband who stonewalls instead of confessing, the family that never names what happened, and the church that stays quiet about abuse in its own house because speaking would cost something. It is avoidance wearing the clothes of restraint. Scripture says there is "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7), and the silence of the desert and the silence of cowardice can sound identical from the outside. The difference is direction. Avoidance is silence away from something. Holy silence is silence toward God, and so, eventually, back toward the truth and the neighbor.

Benedict of Nursia built this into the ordinary life of a community. His Rule, written around 530, opens with the word "Listen," and its sixth chapter, on restraint of speech, begins from Psalm 39's resolve to guard the tongue. Benedict reasons that if we should sometimes refrain even from good words for the sake of silence, how much more from evil ones, and he gives the reason: speaking and teaching belong to the master, and silence and listening belong to the disciple. His forty-second chapter asks that no one speak after Compline, the last office of the day. This is silence kept among brothers who share tables and fields, for the sake of attention: we keep quiet so that someone else, finally God, can be heard.

Thomas Merton, who entered the Trappist monastery at Gethsemani in Kentucky in 1941, found that the silence eventually sent him back to the world he thought he had left. In *Conjectures of a Guilty Bystander* (1966) he recounts standing on a street corner in downtown Louisville on March 18, 1958, and being overwhelmed by the sense that he loved all the strangers around him and could not be alien to them, as though he had woken from an illusion of separateness. Nouwen made the same claim more plainly: solitude is where compassion is born, because the person who has faced his own poverty before God can no longer stand above anyone else's.

One pastoral word belongs here. Elijah under the broom tree was not helped by being left alone. He was fed, he slept, and God came near. If the quiet brings up grief or despair that feels too heavy to carry, the faithful response is to reach for people: a pastor, a friend, a doctor. In the United States, you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at any hour.

### The objections worth taking seriously

The strongest objection comes from inside the church. In 1521, hidden at the Wartburg, Martin Luther wrote his judgment on monastic vows, and his case deserves a hearing on its own terms. The cloister, he argued, had turned a way of life into a higher grade of Christianity and a claim on God's favor, as if a vow could accomplish what only faith receives, and as if the farmer, the mother and the magistrate were serving God at a lower rank. Withdrawal from the neighbor, on those terms, is not holiness. It is a refusal of the callings God actually gives. Luther was largely right about what much of late medieval monasticism had become, and Protestants should not pretend otherwise. But the Reformation kept the critique and, over time, lost the practice. Many of us inherited an activism with no cell in it, a faith that reads sitting still before God as a failure to be useful.

A second objection is practical, and it is fair. Solitude sounds like a luxury for people with quiet houses and flexible mornings. The mother of three toddlers and the nurse working nights may hear this essay as one more demand they can't meet. Some of that is true. But Dallas Willard's *The Spirit of the Disciplines* (1988) helps, because Willard never treated the disciplines as achievements. He placed solitude and silence among the disciplines of abstinence and described all of them as training: activities within our power, done in small and ordinary ways, that make us able to do what we cannot do by direct effort. Training is scaled to the athlete, and ten minutes in a parked car can be a cell. And if Syncletica was right that one can be solitary in a crowd, then the weary are not shut out. The church, for its part, can guard one another's quiet, a spouse taking the children for an hour so the other can be alone with God.

The third objection is really a warning. Silence has been captured before. The market now sells quiet back to us as a productivity aid and a wellness routine, and the app that promises calm is often built by the same economy that stole it. The therapeutic version treats silence as self-care, and the activist church, whether its causes lean right or left, treats it as wasted time. They look like opposites, but they share a grammar. Both make silence a means to our own ends. Christian silence is time given to God with no promised return, which is precisely why it frees us.

### What the silence gives back

None of this reduces to a method, but it does become concrete. It usually starts with the device, because willpower is no match for an industry. Put it in another room. Keep the first minutes of the day for God before the world gets in, with a psalm read slowly and nothing required of you afterward. Expect acedia, because it will come. The minutes will stretch, and some part of you will insist that you could serve God better by doing something. That is the moment the fathers told their disciples to stay. And don't measure it by how it feels. Much of it will be dry, the way roots grow in a season when nothing shows above the ground. You are not in the silence to get something. You are there to become someone who can be still.

Underneath the practice is a deeper comfort, and it runs through the cross. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus "withdrew from them about a stone's throw, and knelt down and prayed" (Luke 22:41), and his friends slept. The next afternoon he entered a solitude no desert father ever knew, crying out, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). Then came the silence of a sealed tomb on a Saturday when nothing seemed to happen, and then Sunday. Because the Son went into the loneliest place there is and came out of it alive, the Christian's solitude is never abandonment. The promise God first made to Joshua, Hebrews gives to the church: "I will never leave you nor forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5). We can enter the quiet room because he has already been in the worst one, and he is there when we arrive.

So the room is still there, Pascal's room, the cell of Abba Moses, the mouth of Elijah's cave. It will not entertain you or flatter you. It will take away the noise you have been hiding behind and hand you back to yourself and to God, both of whom you may have been avoiding for years. And somewhere in the quiet, once the wind and the earthquake and the fire have passed, the same question is waiting that met a tired prophet on a mountain, the one we have built an entire economy to avoid hearing: What are you doing here?
