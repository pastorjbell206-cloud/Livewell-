### The story most people believe about the Bible

Most people who have decided the Bible can't be trusted didn't reach that conclusion by reading the Bible. They reached it by absorbing a story about the Bible, the way you absorb the rules of a game nobody ever explained to you. The story runs roughly like this. The texts were copied by hand for centuries, so they must have drifted like a message in the telephone game until nobody can know what was first written. A council of bishops in the fourth century voted on which books would make Jesus divine and burned the rest. And the books that survived contradict each other so often that only the willfully credulous could take them as history. Put those three claims together and trusting the Bible looks less like faith than like a failure to check.

I lived inside that story for years before I came to faith, and it carried real weight in my unbelief. When it gave way, it didn't make me a Christian. It took away an excuse. What I had taken for the clear-eyed position turned out to be the credulous one, because I had believed the folklore about the Bible on far thinner evidence than the Bible itself offered, and I never noticed I was doing it.

But the skeptic deserves better than the answers the church usually gives to that story, and it has to be said before anything else. We have answered the telephone game with inflated manuscript counts and tidy percentages copied from one apologetics handbook to the next. We have answered the charge of contradiction with harmonizations so strained they insult the reader's intelligence. And to our own children we have too often handed a Bible built like a house of cards, in which a single discrepancy between Kings and Chronicles is supposed to bring the whole structure down. Many of the people who now tell their deconversion stories weren't outargued by a skeptic. They left because the version of the Bible we gave them couldn't survive its first honest footnote. That failure belongs to us, and pastors carry more of it than anyone.

So the question has to be taken apart, because "Can you trust the Bible?" is really three questions wearing one coat. The first is a question of transmission: do we have, substantially, what the authors wrote? The second is a question of history: when these writers describe events, are they reliable witnesses to what happened? The third is a question of authority: does this book have a rightful claim on what I believe about God and how I live? The first two are open to any historian, believing or not, and they are answered, as far as they can be answered, by manuscripts, archaeology and the comparison of ancient sources. The third isn't settled by evidence alone, though it isn't settled without evidence either. Most confusion about the Bible comes from blurring the three. The anxious defender often treats the third as if it depended on winning every skirmish in the first two. The skeptic often assumes that a problem in the first two dissolves the third. They are making the same mistake from opposite ends.

### Copies of copies: what the manuscripts actually show

The telephone game works as an image because the game has one chain. Each player hears the message once and passes it to one other person, and nobody can check. The New Testament wasn't transmitted that way. From the second century on, its books were copied in many places at once, in Egypt and Syria and Asia Minor and Italy, by communities that didn't know one another and often disagreed with one another, and they were translated early into Latin, Syriac and Coptic. The result is not a chain but a branching tree, and a branching tree can be checked. A slip made by a tired scribe in one region shows up as an oddity against copies descended from other regions. Textual criticism is the discipline of reading those branches against each other to recover the earliest form of the text that the evidence allows.

The standard account of that discipline is Bruce Metzger's *The Text of the New Testament*, first published in 1964 and revised for its fourth edition in 2005 with his former student Bart Ehrman. It's worth knowing what kind of book it is. It isn't apologetics. It is a technical history of how the text was copied, corrupted and restored, and its subtitle names all three. It describes several thousand Greek manuscripts, now catalogued by the Institute for New Testament Textual Research in Münster. Most of them are medieval, but they stand alongside early papyri such as the collection of Paul's letters known as P46, usually dated around 200, and the great fourth-century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. The earliest fragment of all, a scrap of John 18 held in Manchester and known as P52, is often dated to the first half of the second century, though Brent Nongbri showed in 2005 that handwriting alone can't pin it down that tightly. For comparison, the first six books of Tacitus's *Annals* survive in a single manuscript copied in the ninth century, and no historian treats Tacitus as unknowable.

Honesty requires the strongest form of the objection, and Ehrman supplied it. He studied at Moody Bible Institute and Wheaton College before going to Princeton Theological Seminary to work under Metzger, and in *Misquoting Jesus* (2005) he told how the manuscripts undid the view of the Bible he had been raised with. His argument, put fairly, runs like this. We have none of the originals. The earliest copies were often made by scribes who weren't professionals, in the very period when changes were most likely to enter. There are more differences among our manuscripts than there are words in the New Testament. Some of those differences were deliberate, made by scribes who wanted a passage to say more plainly what they already believed, a pattern Ehrman had documented in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993). And some of them matter. The story of the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11) is missing from our earliest manuscripts. So are the last twelve verses of Mark. Luke's description of Jesus in Gethsemane, his sweat falling like drops of blood, is textually uncertain. At Mark 1:41 a few witnesses say Jesus was moved with anger rather than compassion when he healed the leper, and Ehrman argues the harder reading is the original. If God inspired the words, he asks, why didn't God preserve them?

That's a serious argument, and it should be answered as one. Daniel Wallace of Dallas Theological Seminary, who founded the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts in 2002 to photograph manuscripts around the world, has spent years answering it, in debate and in the volume he edited, *Revisiting the Corruption of the New Testament* (2011). His reply begins not with denial but with arithmetic. The number of variants is large because the number of manuscripts is large. One scribe's way of spelling a single name, repeated across hundreds of copies, produces hundreds of variants. Wallace sorts them into kinds. There are differences of spelling and plain slips of the pen, which make up the great majority. There are differences that don't change the meaning or the translation, such as shifts in word order that Greek permits freely. There are differences that are meaningful but have no real claim to be original, because they turn up in a single late manuscript. And there are differences that are both meaningful and plausibly original. That last category is real, and it is a small fraction of the whole. Every passage Ehrman names belongs to it, and every one is flagged in the notes or brackets of the ESV and nearly every other modern translation. The uncertainty isn't hidden from the reader. It's printed on the page.

The most telling witness here is Ehrman himself. His textbook with Metzger is far more measured than his popular book, and in an interview printed with the paperback of *Misquoting Jesus* he said his position did not actually stand at odds with Metzger's judgment that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by the textual variants. He has also written, in *God's Problem* (2008), that what finally ended his faith was not the manuscripts but the problem of suffering. Consider the most famous theological variant of all, the words in the King James Version of 1 John 5:7 naming the Father, the Word and the Holy Ghost as three who are one. They entered the printed Greek text in the sixteenth century from late sources, and modern translations rightly leave them out. But the church confessed the Trinity at Nicaea in 325 and at Constantinople in 381, and the Greek fathers who fought for that confession never cited the verse, because they never had it. The doctrine didn't rest on the variant. The same holds for the resurrection, the deity of Christ and the atonement. None of them hangs on a disputed reading.

Notice what has been granted and what hasn't. Textual criticism can tell us, with high confidence in most places and honest uncertainty in a few, what the authors wrote. It can't tell us whether what they wrote is true. A perfectly transmitted falsehood is still false. The telephone game is the wrong picture of the New Testament, and the right picture answers only the first question.

### What the Dead Sea Scrolls changed

The Hebrew Scriptures raise the same question across a longer span of time. Until the middle of the twentieth century, the oldest complete manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible were medieval: the Aleppo Codex from the tenth century and the Leningrad Codex from 1008, both products of the Masoretes, the scribal families who standardized the text and its vowels. A skeptic could fairly ask how anyone could know what Isaiah had said more than fifteen centuries before those codices were copied.

In 1947 Bedouin shepherds in the Judean desert near Qumran found jars in a cave holding scrolls, and over the following decade eleven caves yielded the remains of hundreds of manuscripts. Among the first finds was the Great Isaiah Scroll, a complete copy of all sixty-six chapters made in the second century before Christ, roughly a thousand years earlier than the Aleppo Codex. When scholars laid it beside the Masoretic text, the agreement was striking. The differences were overwhelmingly matters of spelling and small grammatical shifts, with a modest number of meaningful readings. A text that had passed through a thousand years of hand copying had come through substantially intact.

That's the half of the story apologists tell, and it's true. The other half matters just as much, and the skeptic should hear it from us before he hears it from someone else. The scrolls also showed that before the text was standardized, more than one form of some books was in circulation. Emanuel Tov, who later led the official publication of the scrolls, laid out the evidence in *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (1992). The clearest case is Jeremiah. The Greek translation known as the Septuagint had long preserved a noticeably shorter Jeremiah, arranged in a different order, and scholars had often assumed the translators abridged it. Then fragments from Qumran turned up a Hebrew Jeremiah that matched the shorter Greek form. Two editions of the book had existed side by side.

A flat view of the Bible finds that threatening, as though Scripture should have dropped from heaven as a single fixed copy. But the Bible never claims to have arrived that way, and Jeremiah least of all. The book describes its own making in human terms: the prophet dictating to Baruch the scribe, King Jehoiakim cutting the scroll and burning it column by column in his brazier, and Jeremiah dictating it all again on a fresh scroll. The chapter ends, "And many similar words were added to them" (Jeremiah 36:32). The book tells us it was written twice. What the scrolls show is a text guarded with great care by communities that held it holy, and a history of composition more human than a flat theory expects. Both are true. The skeptic who hears only the first is being sold something. The believer who hears only the second hasn't read Jeremiah 36.

### Who decided which books are in the Bible?

The second piece of the folklore is more dramatic. In Dan Brown's *The Da Vinci Code* (2003), a character explains that the emperor Constantine commissioned a new Bible, that Jesus was promoted to godhood by a vote at the Council of Nicaea in 325, and that the earlier gospels portraying him as a mortal man were suppressed. Millions of readers took it as history. None of it is. Nicaea was called to settle the Arian controversy over whether the Son is fully God, and its creed and canons say nothing about which books belong in the New Testament.

The real history is slower and messier, and it doesn't supply a villain. By around 180, Irenaeus of Lyons could argue in *Against Heresies* that there are four Gospels and could be neither more nor fewer, as if the point were already plain to the churches he wrote for. A Latin list found in Milan and published by Ludovico Muratori in 1740, known as the Muratorian fragment and usually dated to the late second century (a minority of scholars place it in the fourth), names the four Gospels, Acts and thirteen letters of Paul, along with other books, including the Wisdom of Solomon and an Apocalypse of Peter that some, it says, would not allow to be read in church. It rejects letters forged in Paul's name to advance Marcion's teaching. And it says the *Shepherd of Hermas* may be read privately but not in the church's public reading, because it was written recently, in the author's own day, and can't be counted among the prophets or the apostles. The principle is visible even in that fragment. A book had to come from the apostolic generation. Recent piety, however edifying, didn't qualify.

The edges took longer to settle. Writing around 325, Eusebius of Caesarea sorted books in his *Ecclesiastical History* into those acknowledged everywhere, those disputed (among them James, 2 Peter, Jude and 2 and 3 John), and those rejected. The first list that matches our twenty-seven books exactly appears in the thirty-ninth Festal Letter of Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, in 367, and North African councils at Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397 confirmed the same list. So the skeptic is right that there was a process, that it took centuries at the margins, and that councils eventually ruled. What the skeptic usually misses is that the core, the four Gospels and Paul's letters, was already functioning as Scripture across a scattered and frequently persecuted church long before any council could have imposed it. The councils mostly ratified what the churches had been reading aloud for generations.

The strongest scholarly challenge comes from another direction. Walter Bauer argued in *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* (1934) that in several regions the forms of Christianity later called heresy were as early as what became orthodoxy, or earlier, and Ehrman's *Lost Christianities* (2003) carried the argument to a wide audience: the winners chose the books, and we might have had a very different Bible. Something here is true. Early Christianity was not uniform, and the discovery of the Nag Hammadi codices in Egypt in 1945 put texts like the Gospel of Thomas into modern hands. But when you read those texts, most of them bear the marks of the second century or later, and many carry a theology the apostolic churches had already judged foreign, often one that despised the body and the created world that Genesis calls good. They didn't lose a vote. They arrived late and spoke of a different faith.

Michael Kruger's *Canon Revisited* (2012) presses the question underneath the history: what makes a book canonical at all? If the church's decision makes a book Scripture, then the church stands above Scripture. If modern historians must certify each book before anyone may trust it, then the historians stand above it. Kruger argues instead for a self-authenticating canon, drawing on a Reformed line that runs back to Calvin's *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1559), in which the books carry their authority in themselves. Their divine qualities, their apostolic origin and their reception by the church as a whole are, on his account, three ways that one authority shows itself. The church didn't create the canon so much as recognize it. A skeptic won't grant Kruger's theological premise, and Kruger doesn't pretend he must. But the model explains the historical pattern better than the conspiracy does. The books that won were the books the churches couldn't stop reading.

### Are the Gospels history or legend?

Transmission and canon tell us we have the books the early church read, substantially as they were written. They don't tell us whether those books are reliable accounts of what happened. For much of the twentieth century the dominant scholarly model, shaped above all by Rudolf Bultmann's *The History of the Synoptic Tradition* (1921), treated the Gospels as products of anonymous communities that shaped and reshaped oral traditions for their own needs until the historical core was hard to find beneath the preaching. That model still sits beneath many educated dismissals, often without the dismisser knowing its name.

Two books changed the terms of the argument. The first was Richard Burridge's *What Are the Gospels?* (1992). Burridge compared the Gospels with Greco-Roman lives, the genre called *bios*, including Plutarch's *Lives*, Tacitus's *Agricola* and Philo's *Life of Moses*, and concluded that the Gospels belong to that genre. That cuts in two directions, and both matter. Ancient biography was written about real people, to display their character through their words and deeds, and its readers expected a real subject. But ancient biographers also selected, arranged, compressed and paraphrased in ways a modern biographer would footnote, and they gave extraordinary space to a subject's death when the death revealed who he was, as the Gospels do. Genre sets the terms of trust. You don't read a *bios* as a court transcript, and you don't read it as a fable.

The second was Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006). Bauckham argued that the decades between Jesus and the written Gospels were not an anonymous gap but a period in which named eyewitnesses were alive, known in the churches and able to vouch for the tradition. He took seriously the testimony of Papias, a bishop in Asia Minor early in the second century, whose words Eusebius preserved. Papias said he valued the living voice of those who had known the apostles' followers more than anything in books, and he reported that Mark wrote down Peter's teaching accurately, though not in order. Bauckham noticed small details that do no work in the story, like Mark's remark that Simon of Cyrene, who was forced to carry the cross, was "the father of Alexander and Rufus" (Mark 15:21), a line that makes sense only if Mark's readers knew the sons. And working from Tal Ilan's lexicon of Jewish names in antiquity, he showed that the relative frequency of personal names in the Gospels closely matches the pattern of names in first-century Jewish Palestine, which is hard to reproduce from a distance or a generation later.

Bauckham has critics, and a skeptic should know it. Many scholars aren't persuaded that Papias can bear the weight he places on him, and Ehrman's *Jesus Before the Gospels* (2016) draws on memory research to argue that even eyewitness memory is reconstructive, shaped by the present and prone to error. That is a real point, and it cuts against anyone who treats eyewitness testimony as a camera. But notice how much ground it concedes to the older skepticism. The question is no longer whether the Gospels preserve anything from people who were there. It's how memory works in people who were there, which is a different and far more interesting argument.

The writers themselves tell us what kind of claim they meant to make. Luke opens his Gospel this way: "Inasmuch as many have undertaken to compile a narrative of the things that have been accomplished among us, just as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word have delivered them to us, it seemed good to me also, having followed all things closely for some time past, to write an orderly account for you, most excellent Theophilus, that you may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught" (Luke 1:1–4). By ancient standards that's the preface of a historian, naming sources, method and purpose. It doesn't prove Luke accurate. It tells us he wasn't writing myth, and that he expected to be checked. Paul, writing to Corinth in the mid-50s, goes further. He passes on a tradition he had himself received, that Christ "died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:3–6). *Most of whom are still alive* is not the language of legend. It's an invitation to go and ask.

### What about the contradictions in the Bible?

This is the objection that deserves the most respect, because it has real texture and the glib answers to it are bad. The Gospels don't agree on every detail. Mark has one young man at the empty tomb, Matthew one angel, Luke two men, John two angels. Matthew has Judas hang himself (Matthew 27:5), and Acts has him fall headlong (Acts 1:18). Matthew and Luke give different genealogies of Jesus. The first three Gospels place the cleansing of the temple in the last week of Jesus' life, and John places it near the beginning. Mark says, "And it was the third hour when they crucified him" (Mark 15:25), while John says that when Pilate brought Jesus out, "it was the day of Preparation of the Passover. It was about the sixth hour" (John 19:14). Pretending these tensions aren't there is a kind of lying, and it does the Bible no honor.

The moment Ehrman's view of the Bible first cracked is worth hearing, because it wasn't really about manuscripts. As a student at Princeton he wrote a paper trying to reconcile Mark 2:26, where Jesus says David ate the bread of the Presence "in the time of Abiathar the high priest," with 1 Samuel 21, where the priest is Ahimelech, Abiathar's father. His ingenious harmonization came back with a professor's comment suggesting that perhaps Mark had simply made a mistake, and Ehrman tells how, once he allowed that possibility, the rest of his view began to give way. Look at what kind of Bible made that moment a catastrophe. It was a Bible in which the whole weight of God's truthfulness rested on the precision of every incidental detail, so that one unresolved discrepancy meant the whole structure was unsound. Ehrman didn't invent that view. He was taught it. So were many of us, and some of us have taught it.

The church's older readers weren't so brittle, and they weren't ignorant of the problems. Around 170, Tatian, a student of Justin Martyr, wove the four Gospels into a single harmonized narrative called the *Diatessaron*, and some Syriac-speaking churches read it for generations. The church as a whole kept four. It preferred four witnesses with visible seams to one smooth account with the seams sanded off. Around 400 Augustine wrote *The Harmony of the Gospels* to address the discrepancies directly, and he argued that the evangelists could differ in order and in wording while agreeing in what they meant, each setting down what he remembered as it served his purpose. Some of Augustine's solutions are strained. His instinct was sound.

Historians who work with ancient sources recognize the pattern. Independent witnesses to the same event tend to diverge at the edges and converge at the center, and testimony that agrees in every detail is the kind that suggests collusion. The resurrection accounts disagree about the number of angels. They agree about the crucifixion, the burial, the empty tomb and the appearances, and they agree that the first witnesses were women, whose testimony counted for little in that world and whom no one inventing a story for that audience would have chosen. Michael Licona's *Why Are There Differences in the Gospels?* (2017) studies Plutarch, who sometimes tells the same event differently in different *Lives*, compressing time, transferring a line from one speaker to another, or moving an episode out of chronological order, and argues that the Gospel writers used the same compositional devices educated readers of their day accepted without complaint. Matthew's telling of the Jairus story is a small example. In Mark the father says, "My little daughter is at the point of death" (Mark 5:23). In Matthew's compressed version he says, "My daughter has just died" (Matthew 9:18), because Matthew has folded the later news of her death into the opening request.

None of this makes every difficulty vanish. Some remain hard, and a thoughtful Christian ought to be able to say "I don't know how these fit" without panic. What the evidence doesn't support is the leap from "the Gospels differ in detail" to "the Gospels are unreliable." By that standard no event in the ancient world could be known at all.
