### The story most people believe about the Bible

Most people who have decided the Bible can't be trusted didn't reach that conclusion by reading the Bible. They reached it by absorbing a story about the Bible, the way you absorb the rules of a game nobody ever explained to you. The story runs roughly like this. The texts were copied by hand for centuries, so they must have drifted like a message in the telephone game until nobody can know what was first written. A council of bishops in the fourth century voted on which books would make Jesus divine and burned the rest. And the books that survived contradict each other so often that only the willfully credulous could take them as history. Put those claims together and trusting the Bible looks less like faith than like a failure to check.

I lived inside that story for years before I came to faith, and it carried real weight in my unbelief. When it gave way, it didn't make me a Christian. It took away an excuse. What I had taken for the clear-eyed position was the credulous one: I had believed the folklore on far thinner evidence than the Bible itself offered.

But the skeptic deserves better than the answers the church usually gives. We have answered the telephone game with inflated manuscript counts and tidy percentages copied from one apologetics handbook to the next. We have answered the charge of contradiction with harmonizations so strained they insult the reader. And to our own children we have too often handed a Bible built like a house of cards, in which a single discrepancy between Kings and Chronicles is supposed to bring the whole structure down. Many of the people who now tell their deconversion stories weren't outargued by a skeptic. They left because the version of the Bible we gave them couldn't survive its first honest footnote. That failure belongs to us, and pastors carry more of it than anyone.

So the question has to be taken apart, because "Can you trust the Bible?" is really three questions wearing one coat. The first is a question of transmission: do we have, substantially, what the authors wrote? The second is a question of history: when these writers describe events, are they reliable witnesses to what happened? The third is a question of authority: does this book have a rightful claim on what I believe about God and how I live? The first two are open to any historian, believing or not. The third isn't settled by evidence alone, though it isn't settled without evidence either. Most confusion about the Bible comes from blurring the three. The anxious defender treats the third as if it depended on winning every skirmish in the first two. The skeptic assumes that a problem in the first two dissolves the third. They are making the same mistake from opposite ends.

### Copies of copies: what the manuscripts actually show

The telephone game works as an image because the game has one chain. Each player hears the message once, passes it to one other person, and nobody can check. The New Testament wasn't transmitted that way. From the second century on, its books were copied in many places at once, in Egypt and Syria and Asia Minor and Italy, by communities that didn't know one another and often disagreed, and they were translated early into Latin, Syriac and Coptic. The result is not a chain but a branching tree, and a branching tree can be checked. A slip made by a tired scribe in one region shows up as an oddity against copies descended from other regions. Textual criticism is the discipline of reading those branches against each other to recover the earliest text the evidence allows.

The standard account of that discipline is Bruce Metzger's *The Text of the New Testament*, first published in 1964 and revised for its fourth edition in 2005 with his former student Bart Ehrman. It isn't apologetics; its subtitle promises the transmission, corruption and restoration of the text, and it delivers all three. It describes several thousand Greek manuscripts, now catalogued by the Institute for New Testament Textual Research in Münster. Most are medieval, but they stand alongside early papyri such as the collection of Paul's letters known as P46, usually dated around 200, and the great fourth-century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. The earliest fragment, a scrap of John 18 known as P52, is often dated to the first half of the second century, though Brent Nongbri showed in 2005 that handwriting can't pin it down that tightly. For comparison, the first six books of Tacitus's *Annals* survive in a single manuscript copied in the ninth century, and no historian treats Tacitus as unknowable.

Honesty requires the strongest form of the objection, and Ehrman supplied it. He studied at Moody Bible Institute and Wheaton College before going to Princeton Theological Seminary to work under Metzger, and in *Misquoting Jesus* (2005) he told how the manuscripts undid the view of the Bible he had been raised with. His argument, put fairly, runs like this. We have none of the originals. The earliest copies were often made by scribes who weren't professionals, in the very period when changes were most likely to enter. There are more differences among our manuscripts than there are words in the New Testament. Some were deliberate, made by scribes who wanted a passage to say more plainly what they already believed, a pattern Ehrman had documented in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993). And some of them matter. The story of the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11) is missing from our earliest manuscripts. So are the last twelve verses of Mark. At Mark 1:41 a few witnesses say Jesus was moved with anger rather than compassion when he healed the leper, and Ehrman argues the harder reading is original. If God inspired the words, he asks, why didn't God preserve them?

That's a serious argument, and it should be answered as one. Daniel Wallace of Dallas Theological Seminary, who founded the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts in 2002, has answered it in debate and in the volume he edited, *Revisiting the Corruption of the New Testament* (2011). His reply begins with arithmetic: the number of variants is large because the number of manuscripts is large, and one spelling of a single name, repeated across hundreds of copies, produces hundreds of variants. Wallace sorts them into kinds: spelling and plain slips of the pen, the great majority; differences that don't change meaning or translation; meaningful readings with no real claim to be original; and readings both meaningful and plausibly original. That last kind is real, and it is a small fraction of the whole. Every passage Ehrman names belongs to it, and every one is flagged in the notes or brackets of the ESV and nearly every modern translation. The uncertainty isn't hidden from the reader. It's printed on the page.

The most telling witness is Ehrman himself. In an interview printed with the paperback of *Misquoting Jesus* he said his position did not stand at odds with Metzger's judgment that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by the variants. He has also written, in *God's Problem* (2008), that what finally ended his faith was not the manuscripts but the problem of suffering. Consider the most famous theological variant of all, the words in the King James Version of 1 John 5:7 naming the Father, the Word and the Holy Ghost as three who are one. They entered the printed Greek text in the sixteenth century from late sources, and modern translations rightly leave them out. But the church confessed the Trinity at Nicaea in 325 and at Constantinople in 381, and the Greek fathers who fought for that confession never cited the verse, because they never had it. The doctrine didn't rest on the variant, and neither does the resurrection, the deity of Christ or the atonement.

Notice what has been granted and what hasn't. Textual criticism can tell us, with high confidence in most places and honest uncertainty in a few, what the authors wrote. It can't tell us whether what they wrote is true. A perfectly transmitted falsehood is still false. The right picture of the text answers only the first question.

### What the Dead Sea Scrolls changed

The Hebrew Scriptures raise the same question across a longer span. Until the middle of the twentieth century the oldest complete manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible were medieval: the Aleppo Codex from the tenth century and the Leningrad Codex from 1008, both products of the Masoretes, the scribal families who standardized the text and its vowels. A skeptic could fairly ask how anyone could know what Isaiah had said more than fifteen centuries before those codices were copied.

In 1947 Bedouin shepherds near Qumran, above the Dead Sea, found jars in a cave holding scrolls, and over the following decade eleven caves yielded the remains of hundreds of manuscripts. Among the first finds was the Great Isaiah Scroll, a complete copy of all sixty-six chapters made in the second century before Christ, roughly a thousand years earlier than the Aleppo Codex. Laid beside the Masoretic text, it agreed strikingly. The differences were overwhelmingly matters of spelling and small grammatical shifts, with a modest number of meaningful readings. A thousand years of hand copying had left the text substantially intact.

That's the half of the story apologists tell, and it's true. The other half matters as much. The scrolls also showed that before the text was standardized, more than one form of some books circulated. Emanuel Tov, who later led the official publication of the scrolls, laid out the evidence in *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (1992). The clearest case is Jeremiah. The Greek Septuagint had long preserved a noticeably shorter Jeremiah in a different order, often blamed on its translators. Then fragments from Qumran turned up a Hebrew Jeremiah matching the shorter form. Two editions of the book had existed side by side.

A flat view of the Bible finds that threatening, as though Scripture should have dropped from heaven as a single fixed copy. Jeremiah never claims that. It describes its own making: the prophet dictating to Baruch the scribe, King Jehoiakim cutting the scroll and burning it column by column, and Jeremiah dictating it all again on a fresh scroll. The chapter ends, "And many similar words were added to them" (Jeremiah 36:32). The book tells us it was written twice. What the scrolls show is a text guarded with great care by communities that held it holy, and a history of composition more human than a flat theory expects. The skeptic who hears only the first half is being sold something. The believer who hears only the second hasn't read Jeremiah 36.

### Who decided which books are in the Bible?

The second piece of the folklore is more dramatic. In Dan Brown's *The Da Vinci Code* (2003), a character explains that Constantine commissioned a new Bible, that Jesus was voted into godhood at the Council of Nicaea in 325, and that earlier gospels portraying him as a mortal man were suppressed. Millions of readers took it as history. Nicaea was called to settle the Arian controversy over whether the Son is fully God, and its creed and canons say nothing about which books belong in the New Testament.

The real history is slower, and it supplies no villain. Around 180, Irenaeus of Lyons could argue in *Against Heresies* that there are four Gospels and could be neither more nor fewer, as if the point were already plain to the churches he wrote for. A Latin list published by Ludovico Muratori in 1740, known as the Muratorian fragment and usually dated to the late second century (a minority of scholars place it in the fourth), names the four Gospels, Acts and thirteen letters of Paul, along with other books. It says the *Shepherd of Hermas* may be read privately but not in the church's public reading, because it was written recently, in the author's own day. The principle is visible even there. A book had to come from the apostolic generation. Recent piety, however edifying, didn't qualify.

The edges took longer. Around 325 Eusebius of Caesarea sorted books in his *Ecclesiastical History* into those acknowledged everywhere, those disputed (among them James, 2 Peter, Jude and 2 and 3 John), and those rejected. The first list matching our twenty-seven books exactly appears in the thirty-ninth Festal Letter of Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, in 367, and councils at Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397 confirmed it. So the skeptic is right that there was a process, that it took centuries at the margins, and that councils eventually ruled. What he usually misses is that the core, the four Gospels and Paul's letters, was already functioning as Scripture across a scattered and often persecuted church long before any council could have imposed it. The councils mostly ratified what the churches had been reading aloud for generations.

The strongest scholarly challenge comes from another direction. Walter Bauer argued in *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* (1934) that in several regions the forms of Christianity later called heresy were as early as what became orthodoxy, and Ehrman's *Lost Christianities* (2003) carried the argument to a wide audience: the winners chose the books. Something here is true. Early Christianity was not uniform, and the Nag Hammadi codices, found in Egypt in 1945, put texts like the Gospel of Thomas into modern hands. But most such texts bear the marks of the second century or later, and many carry a theology the apostolic churches had already judged foreign, often one that despised the body and the created world Genesis calls good. They didn't lose a vote. They arrived late and spoke of a different faith.

Michael Kruger's *Canon Revisited* (2012) presses the question underneath the history: what makes a book canonical at all? If the church's vote makes a book Scripture, the church stands above Scripture; if historians must certify each book, the historians do. Kruger argues instead for a self-authenticating canon, in a Reformed line running back to Calvin's *Institutes* (1559), in which the books carry their authority in themselves; their divine qualities, their apostolic origin and their reception by the church as a whole are three ways that one authority shows itself. The church didn't create the canon so much as recognize it. A skeptic won't grant Kruger's premise, and Kruger doesn't pretend he must. But the model explains the historical pattern better than the conspiracy does. The books that won were the books the churches couldn't stop reading.

### Are the Gospels history or legend?

Transmission and canon tell us we have the books the early church read, substantially as written. They don't tell us whether those books are reliable accounts of what happened. For much of the twentieth century the dominant scholarly model, shaped above all by Rudolf Bultmann's *The History of the Synoptic Tradition* (1921), treated the Gospels as products of anonymous communities that reshaped oral traditions for their own needs until the historical core was hard to find beneath the preaching. That model still sits beneath many educated dismissals.

Two books changed the terms of the argument. Richard Burridge's *What Are the Gospels?* (1992) compared the Gospels with Greco-Roman lives, the genre called *bios*, including Plutarch's *Lives*, Tacitus's *Agricola* and Philo's *Life of Moses*, and concluded that the Gospels belong to that genre. That cuts in two directions. Ancient biography was written about real people, to display their character through their words and deeds, and its readers expected a real subject. But ancient biographers also selected, arranged, compressed and paraphrased in ways a modern biographer would footnote, and they gave great space to a subject's death when the death revealed who he was, as the Gospels do. Genre sets the terms of trust. You don't read a *bios* as a court transcript, and you don't read it as a fable.

Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006) argued that the decades between Jesus and the written Gospels were not an anonymous gap but a period in which named eyewitnesses were alive, known in the churches and able to vouch for the tradition. He took seriously Papias, a bishop in Asia Minor early in the second century whose words Eusebius preserved, who reported that Mark wrote down Peter's teaching accurately, though not in order. Bauckham noticed details that do no work in the story, like Mark's remark that Simon of Cyrene, forced to carry the cross, was "the father of Alexander and Rufus" (Mark 15:21), a line that makes sense only if Mark's readers knew the sons. And working from Tal Ilan's lexicon of Jewish names in antiquity, he showed that the frequency of personal names in the Gospels closely matches the pattern in first-century Jewish Palestine, which is hard to reproduce from a distance.

Bauckham has critics. Many scholars doubt Papias can bear the weight he places on him, and Ehrman's *Jesus Before the Gospels* (2016) draws on memory research to argue that even eyewitness memory is reconstructive and prone to error. That's a real point against anyone who treats testimony as a camera. But notice how much it concedes. The question is no longer whether the Gospels preserve anything from people who were there. It's how memory works in people who were there, which is a far more interesting argument.

The writers tell us what kind of claim they meant to make. Luke names his sources as "those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word" (Luke 1:2), and says he wrote "having followed all things closely for some time past," so that his reader "may have certainty concerning the things you have been taught" (Luke 1:3–4). By ancient standards that is a historian's preface, naming sources, method and purpose. It doesn't prove Luke accurate, but it tells us he expected to be checked. Paul, writing to Corinth in the mid-50s, passes on a tradition he had himself received, that Christ "was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:4–6). *Most of whom are still alive* is not the language of legend. It's an invitation to go and ask.

### What about the contradictions in the Bible?

This objection deserves the most respect, because it has real texture and the glib answers to it are bad. Mark has one young man at the empty tomb, Matthew one angel, Luke two men, John two angels. Matthew has Judas hang himself (Matthew 27:5), and Acts has him fall headlong (Acts 1:18). The first three Gospels place the cleansing of the temple in Jesus' last week, and John places it near the beginning. Mark says, "And it was the third hour when they crucified him" (Mark 15:25), while John says that when Pilate brought Jesus out, "it was the day of Preparation of the Passover. It was about the sixth hour" (John 19:14). Pretending these tensions aren't there is a kind of lying, and it does the Bible no honor.

The moment Ehrman's view first cracked is worth hearing, because it wasn't about manuscripts. As a student at Princeton he wrote a paper reconciling Mark 2:26, where Jesus says David ate the bread of the Presence "in the time of Abiathar the high priest," with 1 Samuel 21, where the priest is Ahimelech, Abiathar's father. The paper came back with a professor's comment that Mark may simply have made a mistake, and Ehrman tells how, once he allowed that, the rest of his view gave way. Only one kind of Bible makes that moment a catastrophe: one in which God's truthfulness rests on every incidental detail. Ehrman didn't invent that view. He was taught it. So were many of us, and some of us have taught it.

The church's older readers weren't so brittle, and they weren't ignorant of the problems. Around 170, Tatian wove the four Gospels into a single harmonized narrative, the *Diatessaron*, and some Syriac-speaking churches read it for generations. The church as a whole kept four. It preferred four witnesses with visible seams to one smooth account with the seams sanded off. Around 400 Augustine wrote *The Harmony of the Gospels* to face the discrepancies directly, arguing that the evangelists could differ in order and wording while agreeing in what they meant. Some of his solutions strain. His instinct was sound.

Historians of the ancient world recognize the pattern. Independent witnesses tend to diverge at the edges and converge at the center, and testimony that agrees in every detail suggests collusion. The resurrection accounts disagree about the angels. They agree about the crucifixion, the burial, the empty tomb and the appearances, and that the first witnesses were women, whose testimony counted for little in that world. Michael Licona's *Why Are There Differences in the Gospels?* (2017) studies Plutarch, who sometimes tells the same event differently in different *Lives*, compressing time, transferring a line from one speaker to another, or moving an episode out of sequence, and argues that the Gospel writers used devices educated readers of their day accepted. When Mark's Jairus says his daughter "is at the point of death" (Mark 5:23) and Matthew's says she "has just died" (Matthew 9:18), Matthew has simply folded the later news into the opening request.

None of this makes every difficulty vanish. Some remain hard, and a thoughtful Christian ought to be able to say "I don't know how these fit" without panic. What the evidence doesn't support is the leap from "the Gospels differ in detail" to "the Gospels are unreliable." By that standard no ancient event could be known.

### What it means to trust the Bible

Grant all of that, and the third question still stands. A historian can judge the Gospels early, sincere testimony and still conclude the witnesses were mistaken. Authority is a different kind of claim, and Christians haven't always stated it the same way.

The best-known modern statement is the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, issued in 1978 by the International Council on Biblical Inerrancy. Its strongest reason is theological rather than defensive: if God speaks in Scripture, and God does not lie, then what Scripture affirms is true, in history as well as in faith. Jesus argued from the wording of the Old Testament and said that "Scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35). The statement is also more careful than its caricature. It locates inerrancy in the original manuscripts. Its thirteenth article denies that inerrancy is undone by a lack of modern technical precision, round numbers, hyperbole, topical arrangement or differing selections of material in parallel accounts, and its nineteenth denies that confessing inerrancy is necessary for salvation. Its defenders trace the conviction through A. A. Hodge and B. B. Warfield's 1881 essay "Inspiration" back to Augustine, who wrote to Jerome that when something in Scripture seemed contrary to truth, he assumed the manuscript was faulty, the translator had missed the sense, or he himself had failed to understand.

Other formulations stand beside it. The Lausanne Covenant (1974) confesses Scripture as "without error in all that it affirms." The Second Vatican Council's *Dei Verbum* (1965) teaches that Scripture sets forth without error the truth God wished recorded for the sake of our salvation. N. T. Wright argues in *Scripture and the Authority of God* (2005) that "the authority of Scripture" is shorthand for the authority of God exercised through Scripture. These differences divide faithful churches, not the faith; the question is second-order. The strongest objection to Chicago is Ehrman's: it places inerrancy in originals no one possesses. The strongest objection to the alternatives is that someone must decide which affirmations serve salvation, and that someone is usually the reader. Neither objection is fully answered, and a reader who lands differently remains a brother or sister.

Beneath these lies the first-order confession, the Nicene Creed's "according to the Scriptures": Scripture as the authoritative witness through which God speaks. What did harm was the popular version that circulated in many of our churches, where every detail bears the full weight and one crack brings down the house. It shares a premise with the skeptic who walks away at the first discrepancy. Both assume the Bible must be a flat, modern reference work to be trusted at all; one insists it is, the other finds it isn't and leaves. The progressive who keeps the Bible as a record of religious experience, to be corrected wherever our moral sense objects, makes the same move in another key. Each time, the reader has quietly taken the judge's chair.

Jesus described Scripture differently. "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me, yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (John 5:39–40). A witness is trusted in a particular way, not because every sentence reads like an encyclopedia entry, but because of who testifies and whom the testimony is about. Luke says of the risen Christ on the road to Emmaus, "And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27). The Christian reason for trusting the Bible is finally not that the manuscripts are good, though they are. It's that Jesus trusted these Scriptures, and the church confesses that he rose. Paul hung everything on that hinge: "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14).

That leaves the skeptic with less than the folklore promised and more than he came for. The text is sound, the canon wasn't a coup, and the Gospels are early testimony with their seams showing. None of it compels faith. It moves the question from the book to the one the book is about, where its writers always meant it to go. And it leaves the church with something to repent of: the numbers we didn't check, the brittle Bible that broke our own children, and the habit of using a witness to the crucified and risen Lord as a club for winning arguments. The Great Isaiah Scroll lay hidden in a cave above the Dead Sea until 1947, and it contains the line, "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever" (Isaiah 40:8). The promise was never that the leather would last. It was that God would keep speaking. Whether he is speaking to you is not a question the manuscripts can settle. They only bring you to the place where it has to be asked.
