The question almost never arrives as a question. It arrives as a face.

Someone is sitting across from you, and they have just buried a grandmother who prayed five times a day toward Mecca for sixty years, or lit lamps to a god whose name you cannot pronounce, and prayed with a devotion that shames your own. And the person across from you says the thing that is really being asked whenever anyone asks whether Jesus is the only way. They say: are you telling me she is in hell. They are not asking for a doctrine. They are asking whether the God you believe in is the kind of God who would do that, and whether you have the nerve to say it to their face.

I want to be honest about where I am standing when I write this. I did not come to this question as a believer defending home turf. I came to it as the objection itself. For most of my life I was an atheist, and of all the things the church claimed, this was the one I found not merely improbable but obscene. The idea that the accident of where a person was born, the family that raised them, the book that happened to be on the nightstand, would decide their eternity, struck me as the clearest evidence that the whole system was invented by men who wanted to believe they alone had the map. I did not think the exclusive claim was hard to believe. I thought it was easy to see through.

So when I say the objection is serious, I am not being generous. I am remembering.

**Nobody at the table avoids an exclusive claim, the pluralist included, so the honest question is not whether to make one but which one is true. The Christian claim began as the defiance of the powerless rather than the boast of the strong, it locates salvation in a person received as a gift, and it settles who the Savior is without handing anyone the roster of the saved.**

### The Case for Many Ways, at Full Strength

Here is the objection in its strongest form, and I will not soften it, because a version you can knock over is not worth answering. The philosopher John Hick spent a career pressing it, and he pressed it well. He had come to faith as a young man in an evangelical form, and what moved him away from it was, by his own account, a city. From 1967 he taught at the University of Birmingham, and in the mosques, gurdwaras, temples and synagogues of his neighbors he found people doing what he believed Christians did in church, opening themselves to a reality greater than themselves and being changed by it.

In *God and the Universe of Faiths* (1973), Hick called for what he named a Copernican revolution in the way Christians think about other religions. For centuries, he said, we placed our own faith at the center and made every other tradition orbit around it, counted as true only insofar as it reflected ours. But what if the center is not Christianity at all. What if the center is God, or what Hick carefully called the Real, and Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and the rest are all planets in orbit around that one sun, each catching the light at a different angle, each a genuine human response to the same infinite reality no one of us can see whole. Theologians who tried to make room for other faiths while keeping Christ at the center, he compared to Ptolemaic astronomers adding epicycles rather than admit that the earth goes round the sun.

In *An Interpretation of Religion* (1989), which began as his Gifford Lectures, Hick gave the picture its philosophical engine. He borrowed Immanuel Kant's distinction between a thing as it is in itself and the thing as it appears to a mind that receives it through its own categories. The Real in itself, Hick argued, lies beyond every human concept, beyond even personal and impersonal. What the traditions meet is the Real as humanly experienced, filtered through a culture: as the personal God of the Bible and the Qur'an, as Vishnu or Shiva, or as the impersonal absolute of Brahman or the Buddhist emptiness. He then offered a practical test. Every great tradition aims at the same transformation, from self-centeredness to Reality-centeredness, and so far as anyone can observe, its fruit in compassion and holiness turns up no more often in one tradition than another. Judge the trees by the fruit, and the orchard looks level. Beneath both books sat a moral argument Hick pressed with real feeling: a God who loves every human being would not arrange salvation so that only the minority who happened to hear of one Jewish teacher could find it. On this account the sincerity of that grandmother is not a problem to be explained away. It is evidence. A God worth the name would not hide himself so that only the people handed the right pamphlet could find him.

And underneath the philosophy sits an intuition even harder to shake, because it does not require a philosophy degree to feel. If you had been born in Riyadh instead of a small town in Michigan, you would almost certainly be a Muslim, and you would hold it with exactly the confidence you now hold this. Your deepest convictions track your zip code. That should unsettle anyone. It unsettled me for years.

Grant all of it. Grant that the church has often held this doctrine like a weapon, has used the word only to bless conquest and baptize contempt, has said to the grieving what should never be said to the grieving. Grant that a great deal of what passes for defending the exclusivity of Christ has been the ugliest thing in the room. If you do not feel the force of that, you are not ready to say the next sentence.

### Three Answers the Church Has Given

Before the next sentence, it helps to know that Christians have not given one answer to this question but three. In *Christians and Religious Pluralism* (1983), the English theologian Alan Race sorted the church's responses into a scheme that nearly every discussion since has used, whether to adopt it or to argue with it: exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism.

Exclusivism holds that salvation comes through Christ alone and is received through explicit faith in him, so that the other religions, whatever truth and goodness they contain, are not roads to God. It has the longest pedigree. Cyprian of Carthage said in the third century that there is no salvation outside the church; the Council of Florence in 1442 declared that pagans, Jews, heretics and schismatics outside the Catholic Church would have no part in eternal life; the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) taught that those who do not profess the Christian religion cannot be saved, however diligently they live by the light of nature. Its most serious modern voice was the Dutch missiologist Hendrik Kraemer, whose *The Christian Message in a Non-Christian World* (1938), written for the missionary conference at Tambaram in India, argued that the gospel is not the summit of humanity's religious climbing but God's own act, standing in judgment over all religion, the Christian religion included. In the form its best defenders would sign, exclusivism is not contempt for other people. It is the conviction that sin is real, that we cannot find our own way back to God, and that the rescue has to come from his side or it does not come.

Inclusivism holds that Christ alone saves, but that his grace may reach people who have never known his name. Its architect was the Jesuit Karl Rahner, who argued in a 1961 lecture, "Christianity and the Non-Christian Religions," that because God truly wills all people to be saved, his grace is at work wherever human beings exist, and that a person who answers it in the depths of conscience may be saved by Christ without knowing him. Rahner called such a person an "anonymous Christian." The Second Vatican Council took up a version of the idea: *Lumen Gentium* (1964) taught that those who through no fault of their own do not know the gospel, but seek God with a sincere heart, may attain salvation, and *Nostra Aetate* (1965) said the church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions. At its strongest, inclusivism tries to hold together two sentences Paul put side by side, that God "desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth," and that "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:4–5). Traditional Christians feared it made preaching optional, and many Buddhists and Muslims did not care to be called Christians without knowing it. To Hick, it was one more epicycle.

Pluralism is Hick's own answer: no tradition is the center, and every tradition is a valid path to the one Real. In fairness to him, he knew its price and paid it openly. If Christianity is one planet among many, the church cannot go on saying that the eternal Son of God became a man in Jesus of Nazareth, because a God who has actually become one particular human being has made one tradition the center after all. So in 1977 Hick edited *The Myth of God Incarnate*, arguing with his colleagues that the incarnation should be read as a metaphor rather than as a literal claim about who Jesus was. That was consistent of him, and it shows what pluralism actually does. It does not add Jesus to the list of ways. It revises what the church has always said about Jesus until he fits on the list, and at that point the question is no longer whether Christianity can make room for other faiths but whether what remains is still Christianity.

### Who Is Telling the Story of the Elephant?

Now watch what the argument does when you turn it over.

Pluralism has an emblem, the old parable of the blind men and the elephant, told in Buddhist, Jain and Hindu versions long before it reached the modern West. One grasps the trunk and says the elephant is a snake, one the leg and says a tree, one the ear and says a fan. Each has part of the truth, none has the whole, and how foolish for any of them to insist his piece is the animal. It is a devastating little story. It has been used against Christian confidence for a century. But notice the one thing the parable cannot survive being asked. Who is telling it. In the Buddhist telling, a king has the blind men brought to the elephant and watches them quarrel. The man narrating the story is not one of the blind men. He is standing above the whole scene with his eyes open, describing the entire elephant that all the others are too limited to see. To tell the parable at all, he has to claim the exact thing he is denying to everyone else: a view of the whole.

Lesslie Newbigin, who spent most of the years between 1936 and 1974 as a missionary and then a bishop in India, and thought harder about this than almost anyone, put his finger on it. In the 1970s he and Hick lived in the same city, Birmingham, among the same mosques and temples, and came to opposite conclusions. In *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989), Newbigin pointed out that the elephant story only works from the king's side. If the king were blind too, there would be no story. The person who says all religions are partial glimpses of a reality none can grasp is not standing nowhere. He is standing somewhere, and it is a very particular somewhere, and from it he is making the largest and most exclusive claim in the room. He is claiming to know the real shape of God well enough to correct every prophet and every scripture that ever said otherwise.

Newbigin went further. Modern Western culture, he observed, sorts claims into two bins: facts, which are public and binding on everyone, and values, which are private and binding on no one but the chooser. Religion has been filed under values, and once a claim is filed there, nobody asks whether it is true, only whether it is sincere. Borrowing the sociologist Peter Berger's term, Newbigin called this our plausibility structure, the web of assumptions that makes some beliefs feel reasonable and others absurd before any argument begins. Pluralism, he argued, is not a view from above the religions. It is what religion looks like from inside one culture's assumptions, those of the educated West after the Enlightenment.

The religions themselves bear this out, as soon as we read what they teach instead of guessing. They disagree about whether ultimate reality is a someone who knows your name or a something into which the self dissolves, and about whether the human problem is sin to be forgiven, ignorance to be enlightened, or desire to be extinguished. They disagree about the most public fact in the Christian story: the church has always said Jesus died on a Roman cross, and the Qur'an (4:157) says he was neither killed nor crucified, only that it was made to appear so. These are not different routes up one mountain. They are descriptions of different mountains. Hick answered that the Real lies beyond all such categories. But that answer does not honor the traditions. It overrules all of them at once, telling the Muslim, the Buddhist and the Christian that what each holds most central is not finally true, while assuring all three that they are right. Timothy Keller put it plainly in *The Reason for God* (2008): the belief that all religions are equally valid is itself a religious belief, and one most religious people on earth reject.

This is the thing I did not see when I was on the other side. I thought pluralism was the humble position and exclusivity the arrogant one. It is the reverse. Pluralism does not refuse the total claim. It makes a total claim and calls the refusal humility. Everyone at the table is making a truth claim about the whole. The Buddhist who says the self is an illusion and the Christian who says the self is loved by name are not describing the same elephant from two angles. They are saying incompatible things about what is finally real, and the pluralist who says they are both partial is not standing outside that disagreement. He has joined it, on a third side, and pretended he floated above it.

### The Charge of Arrogance and the Accident of Birth

Still, the charge of arrogance deserves its own answer, because it is the one most people actually feel. The philosopher Alvin Plantinga took it up in "A Defense of Religious Exclusivism" (1995). The complaint is that the Christian believes what millions of thoughtful, devout people reject, and that holding on in the face of that disagreement is arrogant. Plantinga's reply is patient rather than clever. He asks what she is supposed to do instead. Suppose she has weighed the other faiths as carefully as she can, has found their followers admirable, and still finds that the gospel seems true. If she goes on believing it, she is doing what the pluralist does, holding a view about religion that others reject. If she suspends judgment, she has taken a third position that both of them think mistaken. There is no neutral ground. If believing what others deny is arrogance, everyone is arrogant, including the person bringing the charge. Plantinga conceded something, and the concession matters: knowing that wise and holy people see the world otherwise can reasonably lower a person's confidence and ought to send her back to examine what she holds. That is not a defeat. It may be the beginning of a faith held with open eyes rather than inherited ones.

He turned the geography argument the same way. Someone born elsewhere would likely hold another faith, but the pluralist is in the same position, since a person raised elsewhere would very likely not be a pluralist. A child raised by secular parents among secular friends will likely grow up believing there is no God. If where a belief came from makes it unreliable, the blade swings back and cuts the skeptic too. The objection runs together two questions: how a person came to hold a belief, and whether the belief is true. Your arithmetic tracks where you went to school, and two plus two is still four. The geography point is a needed warning against contempt. It is a poor argument about what is real.

Scripture does not treat geography as an accident at all. When Paul stood before the Areopagus in Athens, a city full of altars, he told the philosophers: God "made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place, that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him. Yet he is actually not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:26–27). The birthplace that the skeptic calls an accident, Paul calls an assignment, and he calls it that in a speech that goes on to name one man whom God raised from the dead (Acts 17:31). The God of the Bible is not a prisoner of the map. He drew it.

So the question was never whether we can avoid an exclusive claim. No one avoids one. The question is which exclusive claim is true. And that is a different kind of question, a harder and more honest one, and it deserves better than the charge of arrogance, because the charge applies to the one making it just as much.

### No Other Name, Spoken From the Dock

Then there is the history, which almost everyone gets backwards. When people hear the church say there is no other name, they hear Christendom talking. They hear cathedrals and crusades, the strong telling the weak that the weak must convert or be damned. But go back to where the sentence was first spoken. Peter says it in the fourth chapter of Acts, and the scene is not a throne room. Peter is a fisherman standing trial. He and John have been arrested, hauled before the very council that arranged the execution of Jesus a few weeks earlier, and asked by what power they had healed a lame man. It is into that, powerless before the people who could kill him as easily as they killed his teacher, that Peter says it: "And there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). The council's response is recorded in the next verse. They saw "that they were uneducated, common men," and "they were astonished" (Acts 4:13). That is not the language of empire. That is a condemned minority refusing to add the emperor's name, or the council's, to the name of the crucified man they will not stop preaching. The exclusivity did not begin as a boast of the powerful. It began as the defiance of the powerless, and it cost most of them their lives.

The scandal of the claim was never that it grabbed too much power. The scandal was where it located salvation. Not in a philosophy the clever could master, not in a law the disciplined could keep, not in a birthplace or a bloodline, but in an executed provincial nobody from an occupied backwater. That is the offense. Christianity does not say the way is narrow because God is stingy with the map. It says the way is a person, and a person is the one thing you cannot earn your way to or reason your way to or be born into. You can only receive him. Which means the door the doctrine seems to close on merit and pedigree and geography is the same door it throws open to the grandmother who never had the right book, because if salvation is a gift and not a grade, then the one thing that cannot disqualify her is that she was born in the wrong place.

This is also why the image of many paths up one mountain misses the Christian claim. Most religious systems are instructions for a climb. The gospel says the climb cannot be made, and that God came down into the valley where we are. The one way is not a wall built to keep others out but a rescue mounted for people who could not save themselves, and that company includes every Christian who has been smug about it. We have been smug about it. We have held the name of Jesus like a membership card rather than a rope thrown to the drowning, and a person pulled half dead out of the water has no standing to sneer at anyone still in it. The Jesus who said he was the way is the same Jesus who marveled at the faith of a Roman officer, "Truly, I tell you, with no one in Israel have I found such faith," and then warned that "many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, while the sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness" (Matthew 8:10–12). His hardest words were kept for the religious insiders who were sure of their place.

### The Savior and the Roster

I need to be careful here, because two questions get fused and they are not the same. One is whether Jesus is the only Savior, whether the cross is the sole ground on which any human being is ever reconciled to God. The historic church answers yes, and it is bound to, because the whole faith stands on it. The other question is whether every person must consciously hear and name him in this life to be reached by what he did. That question is genuinely harder, the church has held more than one faithful answer to it, and it is not the one I am settling today. Hold it open. What the exclusivity of Christ settles is the mechanism, that all grace runs through one cross. It does not by itself hand you the roster of who is saved, and anyone who tells you it does has said more than the text does.

Name the order of the question before arguing it. That Christ alone saves is first-order, confessed in the creeds, and to deny it is to leave the faith rather than to hold a view within it. How his saving work reaches those who never heard the gospel is a second-order question, one on which faithful churches have disagreed without ceasing to be churches, and each answer deserves its strongest voice.

The first answer holds that explicit faith is necessary, and it rests on Paul's own logic: "How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching?" (Romans 10:14). Its defenders note that this conviction sent William Carey to India in 1793 and thousands after him, and that a church which quietly assumes the unevangelized are safe usually stops going to them. The second answer, Rahner's and in a gentler key C. S. Lewis's, holds that Christ may save some who never knew his name; Lewis wrote in *Mere Christianity* (1952) that we know no one is saved except through Christ, but do not know that only those who know him can be saved through him. The third is a reverent refusal to say more than Scripture says, and Newbigin took it. The Christian claim, he held, is exclusive in affirming God's unique and decisive act in Jesus Christ, inclusive in refusing to limit God's saving grace to the members of the church, and pluralist in acknowledging God's gracious work in all people, while denying that the other religions are themselves roads to salvation. He noticed that the New Testament's scenes of judgment are full of surprises, and the people most often surprised are the ones who were certain they were in.

This essay lands where its first version landed, with the question held open before the one Abraham asked on behalf of strangers: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). The strongest objection to that openness is the one Romans 10 raises. If God may reach people without a messenger, why send one? The answer is partial and has to be said so. The church does not go because it has calculated the roster. It goes because the name has been given, because hidden grace is not the joy of knowing whom you have trusted, and because God "commands all people everywhere to repent" (Acts 17:30). The tension remains, and it should.

### The Way, Spoken to a Frightened Friend

Which brings the whole thing back to the face across the table, and to the verse people quote at that face without ever reading where it sits. "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6). We hear it as a bouncer at a door. Read the sentence before it. Thomas, frightened, says, "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" (John 14:5). The line about the only way is not spoken to an outsider to keep him out. It is spoken to a terrified friend who asked how to find God, and it is the tenderest possible answer: you do not have to know the way, because I am the way, and I am not leaving you to find it alone. The exclusivity and the comfort are the same sentence. They were never meant to be pried apart.

The same Thomas would soon become the patron of every doubter. After the crucifixion he refused the other disciples' report: "Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into his side, I will never believe" (John 20:25). A week later the risen Jesus came back for him, held out the wounds, and Thomas, who had asked how anyone could know the way, answered, "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28). The way turned out to be a man with scars, who came looking for the one who could not come to him.

I do not know the eternal standing of your grandmother, and I will not pretend to, and anyone who says it easily has not understood either the doctrine or the grief. What I know is that the claim I once thought was Christianity's cruelty is closer to its whole hope. A God you reach by being born right, or thinking right, or behaving right, is a God who belongs to the lucky and the strong. The God who comes down to one cross, and offers himself as the way rather than posting the way on a wall, is the only kind of God who could ever be good news for the person who got everything wrong.

That is not a smaller God than the pluralist's. It is a more dangerous one. He will not stay a symbol you assemble from all traditions at your convenience. He has a name, and he was killed under it, and he asks to be received rather than admired. You can refuse him. People did, to his face, and he let them walk. But do not refuse him for being arrogant. Refuse him, if you must, for being too particular, too specific, too unwilling to be one glimpse among many. And then sit with the possibility that the particularity you resent is the only shape love could take if it meant to reach the ones who could never have found it themselves.
