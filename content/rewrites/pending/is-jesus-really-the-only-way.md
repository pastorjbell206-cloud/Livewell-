The question almost never arrives as a question. It arrives as a face.

Someone is sitting across from you, and they have just buried a grandmother who prayed five times a day toward Mecca for sixty years, or lit lamps to a god whose name you cannot pronounce, and prayed with a devotion that shames your own. And the person across from you says the thing that is really being asked whenever anyone asks whether Jesus is the only way. They say: are you telling me she is in hell. They are not asking for a doctrine. They are asking whether the God you believe in is the kind of God who would do that, and whether you have the nerve to say it to their face.

I want to be honest about where I am standing when I write this. I did not come to this question as a believer defending home turf. I came to it as the objection itself. For most of my life I was an atheist, and of all the things the church claimed, this was the one I found not merely improbable but obscene. The idea that the accident of where a person was born, the family that raised them, the book that happened to be on the nightstand, would decide their eternity, struck me as the clearest evidence that the whole system was invented by men who wanted to believe they alone had the map. I did not think the exclusive claim was hard to believe. I thought it was easy to see through.

So when I say the objection is serious, I am not being generous. I am remembering.

The objection usually gets poor treatment from Christians, who either fire a verse across the table or change the subject with visible embarrassment. It also gets poor treatment from its own friends, who rarely notice what it takes for granted. Both failures have the same root. Neither side has looked closely at where the person asking the question is standing, or at where the first Christians were standing when they said the words that give offense.

**The claim of this essay is that nobody at the table avoids an exclusive claim, the pluralist included, so the honest question is not whether to make one but which one is true. The Christian claim began as the defiance of the powerless, not the boast of the strong, and it locates salvation in a person received as a gift rather than a road earned by the right birth or the right effort. And it settles who the Savior is without handing anyone the roster of the saved.**

### The Case for Many Ways, at Full Strength

Here is the objection in its strongest form, and I will not soften it, because a version you can knock over is not worth answering. The philosopher John Hick spent a career pressing it, and he pressed it well. Hick did not arrive at pluralism from outside the church. He had come to faith as a young man in a warm evangelical form, and what moved him away from it was, by his own account, a city. From 1967 he taught at the University of Birmingham, among Muslim, Sikh, Hindu and Jewish neighbors, and when he sat in their mosques, gurdwaras, temples and synagogues he found people doing what he believed Christians did in church: opening themselves in prayer to a reality greater than themselves, and being changed by it. He could not make his inherited theology account for what he saw.

*God and the Universe of Faiths* (1973) is the book that came out of those years. In it Hick called for what he named a Copernican revolution in the way Christians think about other religions. For centuries, he said, we placed our own faith at the center and made every other tradition orbit around it, counted as true only insofar as it reflected ours. But what if the center is not Christianity at all. What if the center is God, or what Hick carefully called the Real, and Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and the rest are all planets in orbit around that one sun, each catching the light at a different angle, each a genuine human response to the same infinite reality no one of us can see whole. The theologians who tried to make room for the good in other faiths while keeping Christ at the center, Hick compared to the Ptolemaic astronomers who kept adding epicycles to their charts rather than admit that the earth goes round the sun. Every adjustment, he thought, was a sign the old picture had failed.

Sixteen years later, in *An Interpretation of Religion* (1989), which began as his Gifford Lectures, Hick gave the picture its philosophical engine. He borrowed Immanuel Kant's distinction between a thing as it is in itself and the thing as it appears to a mind that must receive it through its own categories. The Real in itself, Hick argued, lies beyond every human concept, beyond even personal and impersonal. What the traditions meet is the Real as humanly experienced, filtered through a culture's language and practice: as the personal God of the Bible and the Qur'an, as Vishnu or Shiva, or as the impersonal absolute of Brahman or the Buddhist emptiness. None of these is false, and none is the Real as it is. Hick then offered a test that was meant to be practical rather than speculative. Every great tradition, he said, aims at the same transformation of human life, from self-centeredness to Reality-centeredness, and so far as anyone can observe, its fruit in compassion, holiness and self-giving turns up no more often in one tradition than another. Judge the trees by the fruit, and the orchard looks level.

Underneath both books sat a moral argument that Hick pressed with real feeling. If God is love, and loves every human being, it is hard to believe he would arrange salvation so that only the minority who happened to hear of one Jewish teacher could find it. On this account the sincerity of that grandmother is not a problem to be explained away. It is evidence. A God worth the name would not hide himself so that only the people handed the right pamphlet could find him.

And underneath the philosophy sits an intuition even harder to shake, because it does not require a philosophy degree to feel. If you had been born in Riyadh instead of a small town in Michigan, you would almost certainly be a Muslim, and you would hold it with exactly the confidence you now hold this. Your deepest convictions track your zip code. That should unsettle anyone. It unsettled me for years.

Grant all of it. Grant that the church has often held this doctrine like a weapon, has used the word only to bless conquest and baptize contempt, has said to the grieving what should never be said to the grieving. Grant that a great deal of what passes for defending the exclusivity of Christ has been the ugliest thing in the room. If you do not feel the force of that, you are not ready to say the next sentence.

### Three Answers the Church Has Given

Before the next sentence, it helps to know that Christians have not given one answer to this question but three. In *Christians and Religious Pluralism* (1983), the English theologian Alan Race sorted the church's responses into a scheme that nearly every discussion since has used, whether to adopt it or to argue with it: exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism.

Exclusivism holds that salvation comes through Christ alone and is received through explicit faith in him, so that the other religions, whatever truth and goodness they contain, are not roads to God. It has the longest pedigree. Cyprian of Carthage said in the third century that there is no salvation outside the church. The Council of Florence in 1442 declared that pagans, Jews, heretics and schismatics outside the Catholic Church would have no part in eternal life. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) taught that those who do not profess the Christian religion cannot be saved, however diligently they frame their lives by the light of nature. In the twentieth century its most serious Protestant voice was the Dutch missiologist Hendrik Kraemer, whose *The Christian Message in a Non-Christian World* (1938), written for the missionary conference at Tambaram in India, argued that the gospel is not the summit of humanity's religious climbing but God's own act, which stands in judgment over all religion, the Christian religion included. Stated in the form its best defenders would sign, exclusivism is not contempt for other people. It is the conviction that sin is real, that we cannot find our own way back to God, and that the rescue has to come from his side or it does not come.

Inclusivism holds that Christ alone saves, but that his grace may reach people who have never known his name. Its great architect was the Jesuit theologian Karl Rahner, who argued in a 1961 lecture, "Christianity and the Non-Christian Religions," that because God truly wills all people to be saved, his grace must be at work wherever human beings exist, and that a person who responds to that grace in the honest depths of conscience may be saved by Christ without knowing him. Rahner called such a person an "anonymous Christian." The Second Vatican Council took up a version of the idea: *Lumen Gentium* (1964) taught that those who through no fault of their own do not know the gospel, but seek God with a sincere heart and try to do his will as conscience shows it, may attain eternal salvation, and *Nostra Aetate* (1965) said the church rejects nothing that is true and holy in the other religions. At its strongest, inclusivism is an attempt to hold together two sentences Paul put side by side, that God "desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth," and that "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:4–5). The objection came quickly from both sides. Traditional Christians feared it made the preaching of the gospel optional, and many Buddhists and Muslims did not care to be told they were Christians without knowing it. To Hick, it was one more epicycle.

Pluralism is Hick's own answer, and in 1987 he and the Catholic theologian Paul Knitter gathered its advocates in a volume called *The Myth of Christian Uniqueness*. Its logic is the Copernican revolution carried all the way through: no tradition is the center, and every tradition is a valid path to the one Real.

What must be said about pluralism, in fairness to Hick, is that he knew its price and paid it openly. If Christianity is one planet among many, the church cannot go on saying that the eternal Son of God became a man in Jesus of Nazareth, because a God who has actually become one particular human being has made one tradition the center after all. So in 1977 Hick edited *The Myth of God Incarnate*, in which he and his colleagues argued that the incarnation should be read as a metaphor, a way of saying that Jesus was open to God, rather than as a literal claim about who Jesus was. That was consistent of him. It also shows what pluralism actually does. It does not add Jesus to the list of ways. It revises what the church has always said about Jesus until he fits on the list. The Nicene Creed's "for us and for our salvation" has to be rewritten before the planets will line up, and at that point the question is no longer whether Christianity can make room for other faiths. It is whether what remains is still Christianity.
