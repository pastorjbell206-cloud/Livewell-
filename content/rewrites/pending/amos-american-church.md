Most American Christians know one sentence from the book of Amos, and a good many know it secondhand. "But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24, ESV). Martin Luther King Jr. gave it to the country twice in 1963, once in April in a letter written from a Birmingham jail and once in August from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, and it has been quoted from pulpits of every kind ever since. It gets preached on the right and on the left, and it is almost always preached about somebody else.

What rarely gets quoted is the sentence it finishes. The verse everyone loves is the end of an oracle nobody wants to hear: "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21). Amos wasn't addressing a pagan empire or a hostile culture. He was addressing the closest thing ancient Israel had to a church: a prosperous, worshiping, theologically confident people, gathered at a sanctuary their king maintained, singing songs God said he would not listen to. That is why the book is uncomfortable to read slowly in an American congregation. We recognize the room.

The claim of this essay is that Amos speaks more directly to a church like ours than almost any book in the Old Testament, and that he cuts in both directions at once. He exposes a worship that has made its peace with injustice, and with the same blade he exposes a religion that has agreed to serve as chaplain to a nation or a party. The distinction the whole book turns on is written into the name of the town where he preached. Bethel means "house of God." The priest who ran it called it "the king's sanctuary." A church can carry the first name and serve as the second without noticing, and that is the danger Amos was sent to name. I write as a pastor who stands inside that danger every week, not as a commentator looking down on it.

### A shepherd from Tekoa in a boom economy

The book opens with a date. "The words of Amos, who was among the shepherds of Tekoa, which he saw concerning Israel in the days of Uzziah king of Judah and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel, two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1). The earthquake was remembered for centuries; Zechariah could still write, "as you fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" (Zechariah 14:5). These words were spoken to real people, in a real economy, under a king whose name they knew.

That king was Jeroboam II, who reigned in Samaria for forty-one years (2 Kings 14:23), and the middle decades of the eighth century BC were, by every outward measure, good years for the northern kingdom. Aram-Damascus, Israel's old enemy to the north, had been battered by Assyria at the close of the ninth century, and Assyria itself then slipped into a long season of weakness. Into that vacuum Jeroboam expanded. The historian of Kings says he "restored the border of Israel from Lebo-hamath as far as the Sea of the Arabah" (2 Kings 14:25), which is to say that Israel held more territory than it had since Solomon. The wealthy built what Amos calls "the winter house along with the summer house" and "the houses of ivory" (Amos 3:15), and when excavators worked at Samaria in the 1930s they recovered a large hoard of carved ivory fragments, the inlay of expensive furniture.

Notice how Kings explains that prosperity. It says Jeroboam "did what was evil in the sight of the LORD" (2 Kings 14:24), and then that God saved Israel by Jeroboam's hand anyway, because the LORD saw how bitter Israel's affliction had become and that there was no one left to help (2 Kings 14:26–27). The boom was a mercy given to a suffering people. It was not a certificate of approval for the people who got rich from it. The elites of Samaria read it the other way, and understandably. Deuteronomy promised blessing for obedience, and here the blessing was, piled up in ivory and wine. If God was prospering them, God was pleased with them. That reasoning was not stupid. It was only wrong, and it is the most natural wrong a prosperous religious people can make.

Into that confidence walked a man from the south. Tekoa was a village in the hill country of Judah, a few miles below Bethlehem at the edge of the wilderness, so Amos crossed a border to preach. He was not a member of the northern establishment, not even a citizen of the kingdom he addressed. When the priest at Bethel told him to go home and earn his living as a prophet there, he answered: "I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son, but I was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs. But the LORD took me from following the flock, and the LORD said to me, 'Go, prophesy to my people Israel'" (Amos 7:14–15).

Popular preaching often makes Amos a poor farmhand, and he may have been. But the word for him in the first verse is the one 2 Kings 3:4 uses for Mesha king of Moab, a sheep breeder on a royal scale, so some scholars think Amos had means. The text doesn't settle it, and the point doesn't depend on it. What Amos is claiming is that his authority came from neither the sanctuary nor the court. He had no guild behind him and no salary from the institution he criticized. He had a summons. "The lion has roared; who will not fear? The Lord GOD has spoken; who can but prophesy?" (Amos 3:8).

Hold the whole arc in view, because Amos preached in the calm before the end. In 745 BC Tiglath-pileser III took the Assyrian throne and turned the empire west again. In 722 BC Samaria fell, and the northern kingdom was carried off and never came back (2 Kings 17). Within about four decades of Amos's preaching, its houses of ivory were rubble. Nobody at the festivals of Jeroboam's golden years could see that coming.

### The sermon that turned on its audience

Amos's first recorded sermon is among the most skillful rhetoric in Scripture, and it works by letting the audience enjoy it. He begins with Damascus, the enemy to the north: "For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not revoke the punishment, because they have threshed Gilead with threshing sledges of iron" (Amos 1:3). Then Gaza, for selling whole communities into slavery. Then Tyre. Then Edom, Ammon and Moab, who were not strangers at all but kin, the descendants of Esau and of Lot. The circle is tightening. Then Judah, the brother kingdom to the south, "because they have rejected the law of the LORD, and have not kept his statutes" (Amos 2:4). Israel would have liked that one best of all.

And then the eighth oracle, the one the other seven were built to deliver. "For three transgressions of Israel, and for four, I will not revoke the punishment, because they sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6). The foreign nations were judged for atrocities of war. Israel is judged for what happened in its markets and courts: the poor sold into debt slavery over trivial sums, those "who trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth and turn aside the way of the afflicted" (Amos 2:7). And then the detail that fuses worship and exploitation into a single image: "they lay themselves down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge, and in the house of their God they drink the wine of those who have been fined" (Amos 2:8).

The Law of Moses had anticipated exactly this. If you took a poor man's cloak as security on a loan, you gave it back before sunset, because it was his only blanket: "in what else shall he sleep?" (Exodus 22:27). The worshipers at Bethel kept the cloaks overnight and lay on them at the altar. Nothing in their liturgy told them anything was wrong. The sacrifice was offered correctly, the wine was poured, and the blanket of a man shivering somewhere in Samaria was folded under their knees.

Then Amos gives the principle that explains why Israel's indictment is the longest: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2). Election was not an exemption from judgment. It was the reason judgment would be more exacting.

This is the structure of the whole book, and it's the structure of Nathan's parable to David: let the hearer condemn the sin at a safe distance, then turn and say, you are the man. And it is precisely the structure the American church is most practiced at avoiding. We love the oracles against the nations. The right-leaning congregation has its list: a secular culture, a hostile media, the moral collapse of the other side. The progressive congregation has its own list, and it is often the first congregation's membership. Each can hear a sermon that thunders against Damascus and go home stirred and unchanged. Whatever list we bring into the room, the sermon is not over until it reaches us.

Abraham Joshua Heschel, the Jewish philosopher whose *The Prophets* (1962) remains the most searching modern study of these books, noticed that what horrified the prophets looks, to most of us, like the ordinary friction of economic life: a little cheating in business, a court that leans toward the side with money. We call these slight. The prophets called them catastrophe. Heschel's explanation was that the prophet does not see injustice from the human side alone. He feels something of what God feels about it, what Heschel called the divine pathos, God's own involvement in the fate of the wronged. Christians who hold, with the classical tradition, that God is not tossed about by passions as we are will want to state that more carefully than Heschel did. The prophets weren't exaggerating. We are underreacting, because our scale of moral weight has been set by what we are used to.

### Songs God will not hear

Amos's attack is so severe that it has often been misread as an attack on worship itself. It isn't. It is an attack on worship that has become an alibi.

Listen to the shape of the charge. "Come to Bethel, and transgress; to Gilgal, and multiply transgression; bring your sacrifices every morning, your tithes every three days" (Amos 4:4). Amos is mimicking a call to worship, and the punch is that the worship is itself the transgression, not because the rituals were wrong but because of what they had been enlisted to do. A few verses earlier he addresses the wives of Samaria's elite as "you cows of Bashan, who are on the mountain of Samaria, who oppress the poor, who crush the needy" (Amos 4:1). Bashan's cattle were famous for being well fed. The image is brutal, and the brutality is aimed at comfort paid for by someone else's hunger.

"For thus says the LORD to the house of Israel: 'Seek me and live; but do not seek Bethel, and do not enter into Gilgal or cross over to Beersheba'" (Amos 5:4–5). Seek me, not the sanctuary. The people assumed that going to Bethel was the same thing as seeking God, and Amos pries the two apart. A little later he adds a phrase that should stop any comfortable congregation: "Seek good, and not evil, that you may live; and so the LORD, the God of hosts, will be with you, as you have said" (Amos 5:14). As you have said. They already said God was with them. Amos does not dispute that God can be with them. He disputes that saying so makes it true.

Only then comes the oracle that ends in the famous verse. "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies. Even though you offer me your burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them; and the peace offerings of your fattened animals, I will not look upon them. Take away from me the noise of your songs; to the melody of your harps I will not listen. But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:21–24).

It is worth steelmanning the people in that crowd, because they were not hypocrites in the cheap sense. Nothing in the text suggests they were insincere. Their sacrifices were real animals, their songs were real songs, their tithes were generous by any standard. They believed God had blessed them, and they had reasons from Scripture to believe it. That is exactly what makes the passage dangerous to us. The sin Amos names is not the absence of religious feeling. It is religious feeling that has been sealed off from the parts of life where the covenant would cost something, so that the worship certifies the worshiper instead of reforming him.

The sharpest picture of it comes in chapter 8. "Hear this, you who trample on the needy and bring the poor of the land to an end, saying, 'When will the new moon be over, that we may sell grain? And the Sabbath, that we may offer wheat for sale, that we may make the ephah small and the shekel great and deal deceitfully with false balances, that we may buy the poor for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals and sell the chaff of the wheat?'" (Amos 8:4–6). These merchants kept the Sabbath. They did not break it. They simply couldn't wait for it to end, because the rigged scales were waiting on the other side of it. Worship had become the interruption they endured before the real business of the week resumed.

The American version is the Sunday that asks nothing of Monday: a service that moves us deeply and never once touches what we pay the people who work for us, what terms we lend on, what we charge, what we are willing not to notice about how our comfort is supplied. The Hebrew pair behind "justice" and "righteousness" (*mishpat* and *tsedaqah*) is not a private feeling of goodwill. Amos locates it "in the gate" (Amos 5:15), the place where the elders sat as a court, where contracts were witnessed and disputes heard. Justice in Amos is a matter of scales, courts and debts, structural because it concerns how a people arranges its common life, and personal because every false balance had a hand on it.

Walter Brueggemann, in *The Prophetic Imagination* (1978), gave this condition a name that fits Amos exactly, though his own book lingers more on Moses and Jeremiah. He described a "royal consciousness," the settled mind of a society organized around affluence and managed security, whose religion has become a fixture of the establishment, where God is always available and never disruptive. Its characteristic disease, he argued, is numbness: the inability to feel the pain the arrangement is producing. The prophet's first work is to break it by grieving out loud. Amos proves the diagnosis in chapter 6, which opens, "Woe to those who are at ease in Zion" (Amos 6:1), and ends by sending the loungers first into exile (Amos 6:7). Between the two, the elite "drink wine in bowls and anoint themselves with the finest oils, but are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph" (Amos 6:6). Not grieved. Their sin was not only what they did. It was what they no longer felt.

The canon agrees with Amos. Isaiah opens his book with the same charge against Jerusalem's festivals. Micah asks what the LORD requires. Jesus, standing in the temple courts eight centuries later, tells the most scrupulous worshipers of his day that they "tithe mint and dill and cumin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matthew 23:23). The teaching is not that worship is optional. It's that worship which leaves justice untouched has become something other than worship, and God declines to be its audience.

### Bethel, the king's sanctuary

Bethel had an old and holy name. Jacob gave it after his dream of the ladder (Genesis 28:19). But the sanctuary Amos walked into was founded as policy. When the kingdom split around 930 BC, the first Jeroboam feared that if his people kept going up to the temple in Jerusalem, their hearts would turn back to the house of David and they would kill him (1 Kings 12:26–27). So he made two calves of gold, set one at Bethel and one at Dan, and said, "Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (1 Kings 12:28). Many scholars think the calves were meant as pedestals for the invisible LORD rather than rival gods. If so, the royal sanctuary didn't preach a foreign deity. It told the true story of the exodus, in the true name, for the king's purpose.

Nearly two centuries later its priest, Amaziah, heard Amos out and sent word to the king: "Amos has conspired against you in the midst of the house of Israel. The land is not able to bear all his words" (Amos 7:10). Notice that he shaded the report. Amos had said God would "rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword" (Amos 7:9), a word about a dynasty under judgment. Amaziah told the king that Amos said Jeroboam himself would die by the sword (Amos 7:11). The chaplain translated a theological warning into a security threat. Then he told the prophet to go home: "never again prophesy at Bethel, for it is the king's sanctuary, and it is a temple of the kingdom" (Amos 7:13).

Amaziah deserves a fair hearing. He didn't arrest Amos. He advised him to leave, and a man charged with a nation's central shrine in anxious times might sincerely believe that unity was a sacred trust and that a foreigner's words could tear the country apart. That is what makes him frightening. He is not a villain in his own story. He is a responsible man, and his theology fits in one clause: the sanctuary belongs to the king, so the word must fit the kingdom.

Robert Bellah argued in "Civil Religion in America" (1967) that the United States has a civil religion of its own, distinct from Christianity, which at its best holds the nation under a judgment higher than itself and at its worst becomes the nation's worship of itself. The Christian question is sharper than Bellah's: whose sanctuary is the church?

On the right the answer has too often been the nation or the movement that promises to defend it: the pulpit as chaplain to a political project, the preacher who troubles "our side" treated as a conspirator, and prosperity read as proof that God is with us, as we have said. On the left the king wears other colors. A pulpit can echo a party platform as faithfully as any flag-draped stage, and "prophetic" can become a badge a congregation pins on itself for agreeing with its own class. H. Richard Niebuhr described what the liberal Protestantism of his day had made of the faith in a sentence that still stings: "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross" (*The Kingdom of God in America*, 1937). That is Amos 5:24 cut loose from the God who says "I hate" three verses earlier. The weight isn't always equal, and every tradition owes an honest account of which pull it has felt most. For many white evangelical churches in recent decades that pull has been toward the right; mainline churches were charged with the mirror image a generation earlier. But the instrument is the same, and so is the sin.

Martin Luther King Jr. met his Amaziahs in April 1963, and they weren't segregationist firebrands. They were eight white Alabama clergymen who had called his campaign "unwise and untimely." His letter from the Birmingham jail answered that the moderate who prefers order to justice is a greater obstacle than the open enemy, and he asked whether Amos had not been an extremist for justice. That August, at the Lincoln Memorial, he said, "we will not be satisfied until justice rolls down like waters, and righteousness like a mighty stream." Now everyone quotes him. Jesus named that pattern: "you build the tombs of the prophets and decorate the monuments of the righteous" (Matthew 23:29). Every generation honors the dead prophet and manages the living one.

I am not exempt from this. I have silenced voices that were inconvenient. I have managed prophetic challenge rather than received it. I have felt the impulse to tell someone to go prophesy somewhere else. Amaziah is the character a pastor is most likely to become, with a building to keep open and a congregation to keep together. And the reverse error is real too: not every loud critic is Amos. Before Amos ever confronted Amaziah, he prayed for the people he would indict: "O Lord GOD, please forgive! How can Jacob stand? He is so small!" (Amos 7:2). A self-appointed prophet who enjoys the condemnation and never prays that prayer is not standing where Amos stood.

### Darkness, and not light

Israel had a hope, and Amos turned it inside out. The people longed for the day of the LORD, when God would rise up, crush their enemies and vindicate his people. "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18). The day would come, but not as they pictured it, because they had assumed they were the ones to be vindicated.

American Christians on both sides keep a version of that hope. One pictures the day the nation is restored and the other side routed. The other has secularized it into "the right side of history," the confidence that progress will vindicate us and shame our opponents. The shape is identical: the day belongs to us. Amos's question falls on both. Why would you want the day of the LORD, if the LORD is the one whose scales it brings? Heschel drew a distinction that belongs here. Guilt may belong to the few who rig the scales, but responsibility belongs to the whole people who permit it, who profit from it, and who stop grieving. By that measure few of us are the merchants in Amos 8, and none of us is outside the gate.

Then Amos says something stranger. "And on that day," declares the Lord GOD, "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight" (Amos 8:9). He adds that God will make it "like the mourning for an only son" (Amos 8:10). The Gospel writers don't cite Amos when they tell of the crucifixion, but no reader who knows this book can miss what they describe: "And when the sixth hour had come, there was darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour" (Mark 15:33). The day of darkness came at noon, and the mourning was for an only Son. The judgment Amos announced fell, and it fell on the one person who had never kept a cloak overnight or rigged a scale. The cross does not let us around Amos. It is the only way through him. And the way through begins where it began at Bethel, with the worshipers.

### The fallen booth of David

Amos ends with hope. "In that day I will raise up the booth of David that is fallen and repair its breaches, and raise up its ruins and rebuild it as in the days of old" (Amos 9:11). Many critical scholars since the nineteenth century have judged this ending a later addition to a book of judgment; others answer that a prophet of judgment can see past it. The canon ends the book here, and the New Testament reads it as Scripture. It does not soften a line of the eight chapters before it. The house of David is a booth now, a flimsy harvest shelter, not a palace of ivory. Restoration begins in ruins.

Read closely, the promise reverses the curse word for word. In chapter 5 God said, "you have built houses of hewn stone, but you shall not dwell in them; you have planted pleasant vineyards, but you shall not drink their wine" (Amos 5:11). In chapter 9 he says, "they shall rebuild the ruined cities and inhabit them; they shall plant vineyards and drink their wine" (Amos 9:14). What injustice forfeited, grace gives back, on the far side of judgment and never in place of it.

Around AD 49 the apostles met in Jerusalem to decide whether Gentile believers had to be circumcised, and James settled the question with this passage: "After this I will return, and I will rebuild the tent of David that has fallen; I will rebuild its ruins, and I will restore it, that the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the Gentiles who are called by my name, says the Lord, who makes these things known from of old" (Acts 15:16–18). He follows the Greek translation, which reads "mankind" where the Hebrew has "Edom," two words very close in Hebrew spelling. James read the booth as already rising: the Son of David raised from the dead, and a people gathered to him from every nation. That is the answer to Bethel. The house God rebuilds belongs to no king but Christ and to no nation at all, which means it can never again be anybody's sanctuary but his.

So the church inherits both halves of Amos, and it doesn't get to keep the hope without the indictment. It inherits the question "as you have said": whether God is with us because we say so or because we seek him. It inherits the gate, where our scales, our wages and our debts are weighed. It inherits the stranger from Tekoa, who still arrives without credentials and says what our sanctuary would rather not hear. The booth of David has been raised, and it was raised from ruins. What remains for us, on an ordinary Sunday in an American church, is to learn Amos's prayer before we borrow his thunder, and to find out whether we can still grieve over the ruin of Joseph.
