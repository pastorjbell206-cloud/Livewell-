A child learns what the word father means years before anyone teaches her theology. She learns it from the man who answers to the name in her house, or from the empty chair where that man should have been. By the time the church says "Our Father," the word is already full: full of a particular face and a particular tone of voice, of a car that pulled into the driveway at six every evening or never pulled in at all. The doctrine arrives inside a word the child has already defined, and the definition was written at home, in the years before she could read a single verse.

I was raised without a father. When I came to faith out of atheism, decades later, and first heard God called Father by people who meant it, I wasn't hearing a clean word. I was hearing a word my childhood had filled with absence, and the doctrine had to do its work against the grain of the definition I'd inherited. I also have five sons, which means I've spent the years since on the other side of the same word, writing its first draft for someone else. Both facts sit underneath this essay.

Most men asking what the Bible says about being a father stand somewhere between those two places. Many are trying to be the father they didn't have, and they describe a recognizable exhaustion. They have done everything their own fathers didn't, they never miss a game, and something still isn't working that they can't name. The culture spent a generation correcting a real failure by telling men to be there, and the correction was right, but it quietly hardened into a ceiling. Presence is the floor of fatherhood, and a great many good men have mistaken it for the house.

**This essay turns on two distinctions and one claim beneath them. Authority is not the same thing as presence, and provision is not the same thing as attention; American fathers have usually been handed one half of each pair and told it was the whole. Underneath both lies the claim the church made in the fourth century against men who read God's fatherhood off their own: our fatherhood is not the pattern for God's. His is the pattern for ours. That answers the skeptic who says God is a father projected onto the sky, and it's the only good news I know for a man whose father left the word empty.**

### The Strongest Case That God Is a Father Projected Onto the Sky

The skeptic's objection deserves to go first, in its strongest form. In 1841 Ludwig Feuerbach published *The Essence of Christianity*, a book Marian Evans (later George Eliot) translated into English in 1854 and one that taught the nineteenth century to read religion from below. Feuerbach's claim was that theology is anthropology in disguise. What believers worship as God is humanity's own nature, its reason and will and capacity to love, lifted out of human beings, perfected, and set over against them as a separate being. Feuerbach didn't sneer at Christianity; he took its doctrines seriously and claimed to have found the human truth hidden inside each one. His charge isn't that believers are fools. It's that they are talking about themselves without knowing it.

Sigmund Freud sharpened that knife to a point aimed straight at this essay's subject. In *The Future of an Illusion* (1927) he argued that religious ideas grow out of the helplessness of childhood. A small child is surrounded by forces it can't control, and the father is both its protector against them and a figure it fears. When the grown child finds he is still helpless before nature and death, he reaches for a father again, this time an enormous one whose power can make the world safe. Religion, Freud concluded, is an illusion in his technical sense, a belief whose driving force is a wish. He was careful with the word: an illusion, as he defined it, isn't necessarily false, only believed because we want it to be true.

Anyone who has spent years in churches knows Freud saw something real. There are people whose God is plainly the shape of their father: a God who must be perpetually appeased, or who is faintly disappointed, or who is never there when you need him, because that is what the man at the head of the table was. The catechism says God is love. The body says fathers leave, or rage, or go quiet, and the body usually wins. Freud is right that the picture of God most people carry is drawn at home. The question is whether he's right that nothing stands behind the picture.

The psychologist Paul Vitz turned Freud's instrument around in *Faith of the Fatherless* (1999). If a wish for a father can produce belief, Vitz reasoned, a wound from a father can produce unbelief just as easily. He found that a striking number of the modern West's most influential unbelievers had lost their fathers early or had fathers they couldn't respect. Friedrich Nietzsche's father died when the boy was four. Bertrand Russell's died when he was three, and Jean-Paul Sartre's before he was two. Vitz offered this as a hypothesis, modestly. He didn't claim it proved God exists, only that the psychological argument cuts both ways.

No theory of this kind should be trusted to explain any one man too neatly, and the hypothesis's real force lies elsewhere: if psychology can tell a plausible story for belief and an equally plausible one for unbelief, it can't decide between them. Freud describes how people picture God. He can't tell us whether God is there. Where a belief came from is a different question from whether it's true, and the honest skeptic and believer both have to hand that question back to the evidence: to history, to the claims of a particular man from Nazareth, to whether a tomb outside Jerusalem was empty.

But the Christian has a stranger answer than a draw, and it's the one that matters most for fathers. The church saw the projection problem coming. It fought it fifteen centuries before Feuerbach, on the ground of fatherhood itself.

### Named From Above: What Athanasius Saw

Around the year 318 a presbyter in Alexandria named Arius began teaching that the Son of God, however exalted, was himself a creature: there was a time, his followers said, when the Son was not. Much of the force of that position came from what everyone knows about fathers and sons. A father exists before his son; if God begot the Son, then God existed first, and the Son had a beginning. It was the common sense of human fatherhood applied directly to God. The Council of Nicaea rejected it in 325, confessing the Son "begotten, not made, of one substance with the Father," but the argument ran on for most of the century, and its most stubborn opponent was Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria from 328, exiled five times for refusing to give ground.

Athanasius saw that the Arians had made precisely the mistake Feuerbach would later accuse the whole church of making. They had taken human fatherhood as the pattern and read God off it. His reply, pressed again and again in his *Orations Against the Arians*, reversed the direction. God isn't a father the way we are fathers, a man who once wasn't a father and then became one. He is eternally Father because he eternally has a Son, and so there was never a moment when God was not Father and never a moment when the Son was not. To make the point, Athanasius reached for a sentence from Paul: "For this reason I bow my knees before the Father, from whom every family in heaven and on earth is named" (Ephesians 3:14–15). The Greek word rendered family, *patria*, is built on the word for father, *patēr*, and some translations render it "fatherhood." Athanasius read it that way and drew the conclusion that God does not take his fatherhood from us. We take ours from him. He is the original; every human father is a derived and partial copy of a fatherhood older than the world.

This is the deepest answer to Feuerbach and Freud, and the creeds were written, in part, to forbid projection. When Christians confess "I believe in God, the Father almighty," they aren't saying God resembles the best father they ever knew. They are confessing a Fatherhood that the best father they ever knew was faintly reflecting and the worst father they ever knew was betraying. The doctrine doesn't depend on the human analogy being intact. It is the standard the analogy is measured by.

For the child whose father left the word empty, that changes the whole question. If God's fatherhood were a projection of ours, a boy with no father would have nothing to project, and the doctrine would be closed to him. If ours is named from his, then the definition was never located in the man who left. It sits upstream of him, untouched by his leaving.

Israel's Scriptures call God Father sparingly, usually of the nation as a whole. Jesus made it the ordinary way of speaking to God, and taught his disciples to begin, "Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name" (Matthew 6:9). Joachim Jeremias argued in *The Prayers of Jesus* (1967) that Jesus's habit of calling God *Abba*, the Aramaic word a child used for its father, had no real parallel in the Jewish prayer of his day. Popular preaching turned that into "Abba means Daddy," which Jeremias himself resisted and James Barr took apart in a 1988 article bluntly titled "Abba Isn't Daddy." Grown children used the word too; it was closeness that carried respect.

The correction matters, because *Abba* holds together what our culture keeps pulling apart. You can see both halves in the one place the Gospels keep that Aramaic word on Jesus's lips. In Gethsemane, the night before he died, "he said, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will'" (Mark 14:36). That is a son telling his father exactly what he fears and trusting his father's will over his terror, in one breath. Paul then insists that the same word belongs to everyone joined to that Son: "For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15; see Galatians 4:6). The prayer of Gethsemane is put into the mouths of adopted children, brought into a sonship they weren't born into.

J. I. Packer saw where this leads. In *Knowing God* (1973) he wrote that "adoption is the highest privilege that the gospel offers: higher even than justification." Justification is the courtroom, the verdict of acquittal. Adoption is the acquitted man taken home and given the Father's name. For a man raised without a father, that chapter reads less like theology than like a deed of transfer. The office no man handed me was handed to me by God.

### How American Fathers Went Missing

The absent father has a history, and it doesn't begin with divorce. Israel's law assumed a father who would teach the commandments "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way" (Deuteronomy 6:7), a father whose instruction was woven through shared days. On the farm and in the workshop a son learned a trade, and a way of being a man, from a man he watched all day. The industrial revolution of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries moved paid work out of the house. The father became a breadwinner who left at dawn and came home at dark, and the home was reimagined, in the century's sentimental vocabulary, as the mother's sphere. Provision survived the change. Daily presence did not.

The second break came after 1960, and the document most associated with it has been more argued over than read. In March 1965 Daniel Patrick Moynihan, then an assistant secretary of labor in the Johnson administration, wrote an internal report titled *The Negro Family: The Case for National Action*. It argued that the civil rights victories would not reach many Black Americans unless the nation confronted the strain on Black families in the cities, and it traced that strain to causes it named plainly: slavery, which denied enslaved people lawful marriage and sold fathers away from their children; the long humiliations of Jim Crow; the migration into cities; and above all the persistent unemployment of Black men. It called for national action, though it left the program itself unspecified. When the report became public that summer, it was read against the backdrop of the Watts uprising, and it was heard as an indictment.

The critics had a real point. The report used the phrase "tangle of pathology" and described a "matriarchal structure," language that put the weight on the family rather than on the forces pressing down on it, and that read to many Black women as blame for the very endurance that had held their households together. The psychologist William Ryan coined the phrase "blaming the victim" in his 1971 book of that title, and Moynihan was one of his targets: a society that had inflicted the injury was examining the wound and calling it a defect in the wounded. His defenders had a point too: he had located the causes in history and economics, not character, and asked the country to take responsibility for the repair. The decades since have made plain that the unraveling he described was never a racial story. Father absence rose across every group in American life, working-class white communities included. Moynihan named early a wound that was becoming the whole nation's. And the church, whose Southern denominations had defended the slavery that sold fathers away from their children, was never in a position to hear his report as someone else's problem.

The careful research came later. Sara McLanahan and Gary Sandefur, in *Growing Up with a Single Parent* (1994), found that children raised apart from one of their biological parents, usually the father, fared worse on average: more likely to leave high school unfinished, to become teenage parents, and to be out of both work and school as young adults. Lower income explained a large share of the gap, but not all of it. Something besides the money was missing, and the regressions could see its shadow without being able to name it. I grew up inside those columns, and I can say what the numbers see and what they miss. They see the absence. They can't see the emptiness of a father who is in the house, in the photographs, at the game, and forming no one. His children graduate and are counted among the successes, and the damage surfaces twenty years later, in rooms no survey reaches.

Scripture had already named where the fatherless stand. "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation. God settles the solitary in a home" (Psalm 68:5–6). The verse isn't sentimental. It's a statement of jurisdiction. The fatherless are under God's own protection, and a people that neglects them answers to him. We in the American church have been loud about the family as a political cause and quieter about the fathers in our own pews, the ones who have handed the formation of their children to a youth program and the ones who have simply gone. We've grieved the breakdown of the family as though it happened to us, rather than among us and sometimes through us.

### The Provider's Case, and What It Leaves Out

There is a man who hears all of this and bristles, and he deserves his hearing in the form he'd sign. He works fifty or sixty hours a week, sometimes nights, sometimes two jobs, and misses the recital because the shift can't be traded. He has never thought of that as absence. It is love in its most costly and least sentimental form. Perhaps he grew up in a house where the lights were shut off, and swore his children would never know that fear. He points out, correctly, that poverty does real harm, that the research itself found income explained much of the gap, and that the men who lecture him about presence usually hold jobs that allow it. He can cite Scripture: "But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever" (1 Timothy 5:8). And he can point out that when Jesus wanted to show the Father's goodness, he reached for provision: "Or which one of you, if his son asks him for bread, will give him a stone? . . . If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!" (Matthew 7:9, 11). To this man, talk of presence sounds like the ethic of the salaried class, and he suspects it of looking down on men like his own father, who never said much but never missed a rent payment.

He's right about more than his critics admit. Provision is a real part of fatherhood, and Scripture honors it without embarrassment. The class critique has teeth. A church has no business asking a man who works two jobs to feel guilty for feeding his children.

But look again at the text he cites. The bread in Jesus's picture is given in answer to a request: *if his son asks him*. The father provides because he has heard. The picture assumes a father near enough to know what was asked for, and that's the first distinction the provider's case leaves out. **Provision meets a child's needs. Attention knows the child.** A father can meet every need a child has without ever learning who the child is, and a child raised that way receives everything except the knowledge that he's known. Jesus's parable of the two sons shows what that can produce, though there the fault lies with the son, not the father. The elder brother has lived his whole life inside his father's provision, and when he finally speaks his heart it sounds like a payroll dispute: "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command, yet you never gave me a young goat, that I might celebrate with my friends." His father answers, "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours" (Luke 15:29, 31). Everything had been provided, and the son had never grasped that he was loved. A household that runs on provision alone tends to raise that son, dutiful and resentful, clocking hours in what was supposed to be his home.

The provider often holds a second thing along with the paycheck: authority, the right to set the terms of the house. Here the strongest objection comes from the other direction and deserves the same fairness. It holds that paternal authority has so often been a license for domination that it is better retired, and it can point to every household where the father's word was law because his fist enforced it. It's right about all of that.

Where it goes wrong is in concluding that because the thing was abused, the thing itself was the abuse. **Authority is not the same thing as presence, and a father needs both.** Authority without presence is a verdict handed down from a distance. Presence without authority is company without direction, a pleasant man who hands his children no account of what a life is for. The tyrant and the ghost look like opposites, but both have declined the actual office, which is neither to rule the child nor to befriend him but to form him. The sociologist W. Bradford Wilcox reported in *Soft Patriarchs, New Men* (2004) a finding neither side quite expected: churchgoing evangelical fathers, who held traditional views of male headship, were on average more engaged with their children and more affectionate toward them than other fathers. He called them soft patriarchs. Whatever one makes of headship, a second-order question faithful churches answer differently, the finding cuts against the assumption that authority and tenderness must trade off.

Paul holds both in a single sentence. "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). Both clauses are commands aimed at the same man. The text won't let the father abdicate, and it won't let him crush. The ghost has dropped the second clause's content. The tyrant has dropped its restraint.

### The Father Who Ran, and the Hearts of the Fathers

The parable of the two sons also gives the clearest picture in Scripture of what a father's authority is for. The younger son demands his inheritance while his father still lives, spends it, and comes home rehearsing a speech about being taken on as a hired hand. "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). Kenneth Bailey, who taught the New Testament for decades in the Middle East, argued in *Finding the Lost* (1992) that in the village world Jesus described, a man of standing did not run; to run he had to gather up his robes and make himself a spectacle, and the father takes that shame on himself to reach his son before the village does. The text itself is plain about where the father's authority goes. It's still his house, his robe, his ring and his feast, and he gives the orders. He spends all of it closing the distance. Then he leaves his own party to go out to the elder son as well: "his father came out and entreated him" (Luke 15:28). That's authority spent as presence, and it is what the Father whose name we carry is like.

Paul reached for the same office with the Thessalonians: "like a father with his children, we exhorted each one of you and encouraged you and charged you to walk in a manner worthy of God" (1 Thessalonians 2:11–12). The office is directional. It points a child somewhere, toward this God and away from that idol. But a father can't hand down a weight he doesn't carry, which is why the office begins with the father being fathered. A man who has never received the Father's name has nothing upstream to pass along but his own willpower, and willpower runs out.

It also means the father who forms his children best isn't the one who never fails. A father who performs flawlessness teaches a child that the way to be loved is never to be caught needing anything, which is the opposite of the gospel he's trying to hand down. What writes the right definition of father is the recovery: the man who gets it wrong, names it to his child, asks forgiveness, and turns around. My sons have been formed less by my instruction than by watching me get it wrong, name it, and come back, and that is the actual shape of the Christian life. A child who has seen his father come home like that has been given the parable before he can read it.

Some readers' fathers weren't absent but dangerous, and nothing here asks you to pretend otherwise. Honoring a father never requires returning to harm, and the doctrine of God the Father isn't a demand that you call cruelty love. If the danger is in your house now, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233.

The Old Testament, in the order Christians read it, ends on fathers. "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes. And he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers, lest I come and strike the land with a decree of utter destruction" (Malachi 4:5–6). Four centuries later the angel who announces John the Baptist takes up those words (Luke 1:17). The prophet read the estrangement of fathers and children as the mark of a land under a curse, and its healing as the first sign of God's coming. The curse isn't fate. Exodus warns of iniquity visited "to the third and the fourth generation," but the same verse opens with the Lord "keeping steadfast love for thousands" (Exodus 34:7), and the asymmetry is the point. A father wound is usually handed down when nothing intervenes. It stops by being grieved, not hidden, named before God the way the Psalms name everything. What is denied gets transmitted. What is grieved can be redeemed.

The intervention has a name. The Son who said *Abba* in the garden prayed from the cross the psalm of a man who feels abandoned: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22:1). He didn't stop being the Son, and the Trinity didn't come apart. But he went into the place where the fatherless live and cried their cry, so that everyone who has cried it could be brought home. "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?" (Romans 8:32). On the third day the Father answered, and the Son who had been handed over was handed back alive.

None of this lets a father off. The requirement is heavier than being there. It asks a man to become someone worth imitating, to know his children and not merely supply them, to spend his authority closing the distance, and to let them watch him fail and return. Most of us aren't that man, and many were never shown him. The word father will be full by the time the church gets to your children, and you are filling it now: at the dinner table, in the car, in the evenings you're too tired to come out of your room. The only way I know to give them a word worth carrying is to receive one first, from the Father every family is named after, who was running toward the fatherless long before any of us knew his name. Presence is where fatherhood starts. It was never where it was allowed to stop, and the rest of the work is still in front of you.
