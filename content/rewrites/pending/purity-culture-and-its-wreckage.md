There are two kinds of adults who came through the evangelical youth groups of the 1990s and still flinch at the word *purity*. The first kept the promise. They signed the card, wore the ring and waited, and many found that a body trained for a decade to treat desire as danger didn't stop when the vows were said. The second didn't keep it, or had it taken from them by someone else, and they absorbed the other half of the lesson: that something in them was spent and couldn't be recovered. Both were told they were receiving the Bible's teaching. Many now wonder whether any of it was true, and whether the faith that came wrapped in it can be trusted.

Purity culture is the name that has settled on that world. It describes a movement across American evangelicalism, strongest from the early 1990s through the late 2000s, that made abstinence before marriage the chief public marker of a young Christian's faithfulness and built around it an apparatus of pledges, rings, rallies, bestsellers, curricula and merchandise. Its critics mostly came from inside it, and their testimony is too consistent to wave away. Its defenders insist, with some reason, that it was trying to teach something true. Both are right about something.

The ethic purity culture claimed to teach is older and truer than the movement, and the movement failed because it taught that ethic with a different gospel. The failure turns on one confusion. Virginity is a fact about a person's history. Chastity is a virtue, the right ordering of desire, and the church has always asked it of everyone: the married and the single, the widow, the teenager, the man who failed last year. A history can be lost and never regained. A virtue can be practiced, lost, and by grace practiced again. Purity culture collapsed the second into the first, and most of its damage follows from that collapse.

I came to faith out of atheism, so no one handed me a pledge card as a teenager. I'm raising five sons inside the church, so whatever the church now teaches in its place will be handed to them. The churches that built purity culture were churches like mine, and the silence about what it cost has been ours.

### How purity culture was built

Most accounts begin in 1993, where the pledge cards begin, but the movement's instincts are older. In 1873 the Comstock Act made it a federal crime to send "obscene" material through the mail, contraceptive information included. In the 1880s and 1890s the social purity movement, carried largely by women in groups like the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, fought prostitution, pressed for a single moral standard for men and women, and campaigned to raise the age of consent. Its main target was the behavior of men and its main concern the protection of girls. A century later the weight would shift.

Sara Moslener, in *Virgin Nation* (2015), traces evangelical purity rhetoric from those campaigns through the Cold War to the 1990s and argues that it has repeatedly carried a second message: that the sexual conduct of the young is a gauge of the nation's health. That helps explain why a campaign about teenagers' private lives kept staging itself as a national event.

The proximate cause was the sexual revolution. The Food and Drug Administration approved the first oral contraceptive in 1960, and within a generation the link between sex, marriage and children, assumed by Western law and custom for centuries, had largely come apart in practice. Evangelicals answered by building a parallel culture of Christian publishing, music, schools and youth ministries. Then, in June 1981, federal health officials published the first reports of what would be called AIDS, and the same year Congress passed the Adolescent Family Life Act, which funded programs promoting abstinence for teenagers. For the next two decades the youth group and the federal government were saying much the same thing for different reasons.

True Love Waits began among Southern Baptists in 1993. Teenagers signed cards committing themselves, before God, their families, and their future spouses and children, to sexual abstinence until marriage. The cards were gathered and displayed by the tens of thousands, including on the National Mall in Washington. Purity rings followed, and in some circles fathers took their daughters to formal "purity balls" where the father pledged to guard his daughter's purity until he gave her away. In 1996 the federal welfare reform law created a dedicated stream of federal funding for abstinence-only-until-marriage education. The movement's premise had become policy.

The next year Joshua Harris, homeschooled and in his early twenties, published *I Kissed Dating Goodbye* (1997). Harris carried the logic of the pledge past sex into romance itself. Dating as teenagers practiced it, he argued, trained young people in attachment and breakup, more rehearsal for divorce than preparation for marriage, and the wiser course was courtship entered only with marriage in view, under the eye of parents. The book became a bestseller and something close to a manual for evangelical teenagers.

Christine Gardner's *Making Chastity Sexy* (2011) studied how these campaigns persuaded, and found that they borrowed the rhetoric of the culture they opposed. They sold waiting the way advertisers sell anything, as the route to the best outcome, and the outcome promised was a spectacular marriage and spectacular sex. Wait now and you'll be rewarded later. That promise was a different gospel in miniature.

### What the pledges did, and what they left behind

The movement's defenders and critics both tend to overstate the evidence, so it's worth being precise about it. In 2001 the sociologists Peter Bearman and Hannah Brückner published a study in the *American Journal of Sociology* using the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health, a large federal survey of American teenagers. They found that pledging did delay first intercourse, and the delay was real. But it held only under a particular condition. The pledge worked when pledgers were a minority in their school, when taking it marked a young person as part of a distinct community. Where pledging became common, the effect faded. Their interpretation was that the pledge functioned less as a private vow than as a badge of identity, and identities work partly by being different.

Linda Kay Klein, who grew up in the evangelical youth culture of the 1990s, spent years interviewing women raised in it and published *Pure* in 2018. The women she describes speak of anxiety and shame that did not lift at marriage, of fear attached to their own bodies, and of worth measured by one metric they could fail at any moment. What unsettled many readers was that the pattern showed up among women who had kept every rule. Compliance didn't protect them.

In 2021 Sheila Wray Gregoire, Rebecca Gregoire Lindenbach and Joanna Sawatsky published *The Great Sex Rescue*, built on a survey of some twenty thousand Christian women. The authors are evangelicals who hold the historic sexual ethic, so their critique can't be dismissed as hostility from outside. They reported that women who had believed certain common teachings when they married, among them that every man struggles with lust and that a wife owes her husband sex on demand, reported lower sexual and marital satisfaction and more sexual pain. The teachings handed to teenagers didn't stay in youth group. They followed people into marriage and did measurable harm there.

Joshua Harris's own story became a public parable he never asked to be. After years of hearing from readers who said the book had hurt them, he announced in 2018 that he no longer agreed with its central ideas and that the book would be discontinued. In 2019 he announced that he and his wife were separating and, later that month, that by the measures he knew he no longer considered himself a Christian. It isn't mine to judge a man's soul from his public statements, and he deserves better than to be used as an illustration by either side. But many who grew up with his book read his arc as confirmation that the whole framework was built on sand, and the church has to reckon with why that reading felt so plausible.

### The strongest case for the movement

Before any verdict, the movement deserves to be stated in the form its best defenders would sign, and the case is stronger than its critics admit. The parents and pastors who built purity culture were responding to real wreckage. They watched the sexual revolution promise freedom and deliver, for many, broken homes, absent fathers and disease. The AIDS epidemic was killing people, and the internet was about to put pornography in every bedroom. They had every reason to believe that sex outside the covenant of marriage does harm, and that belief is no longer confined to Christians. Louise Perry, a secular feminist, argued in *The Case Against the Sexual Revolution* (2022) that the revolution's freedoms were shaped largely around male desire and that women have paid most of its costs. The purity movement said something like that thirty years earlier, and was mocked for it.

The defenders go on. The ethic itself is not an evangelical invention. That sex belongs within the lifelong covenant of a man and a woman has been the teaching of the Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches for two thousand years. The pledge gave teenagers a public way to say no, and the best research found that under some conditions it did delay sex. Many who pledged are grateful and married well. Klein's interviews, however moving, are testimony rather than a random sample. And the phrase "purity culture" has become elastic enough that some now use it for any teaching that sex belongs in marriage at all.

The last point is the sharpest, and it deserves a name. Some critics didn't stop at the distortion. Nadia Bolz-Weber's *Shameless* (2019) argued for replacing the church's marriage-bound ethic with one grounded in consent and mutuality, and she invited women to send in their purity rings to be melted into a sculpture. The gesture shows the overcorrection the defenders fear: a move from "your sexual history determines your worth" to "your sexual choices are your own," as if those were the only two options. Both put the self and its sexuality at the center. The second simply says so aloud.

Every piece of that case is true, and it still misses the center. The intentions were mostly good and the ethic was true. What failed was not the conviction that sex belongs in marriage but the gospel the conviction was wrapped in, and a true ethic taught inside a false gospel will form people in the false gospel. The defensive answer protects a method, and the progressive answer throws out the truth the method betrayed. The work in front of the church is harder than either: to keep the ethic and repent of the gospel it was taught with.

### Holiness sold as a transaction

Gardner showed what the campaigns promised. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton showed where the promise came from. Their National Study of Youth and Religion interviewed American teenagers in the early 2000s, the pledge generation itself, and in *Soul Searching* (2005) they named the working faith they heard: moralistic therapeutic deism. God exists, wants people to be good and nice, wants them to feel good about themselves, stays mostly out of the way unless needed, and takes good people to heaven when they die. Smith and Denton weren't describing purity culture, but purity culture spoke that folk faith's dialect. God keeps the books, obedience is a deposit, and the return is paid out on the wedding night. It is the same logic the church rightly condemns in the prosperity preachers, moved from the bank account to the bedroom.

The trouble with a transaction is what happens when the payment doesn't come. The person who waited and married into a hard marriage, the person who waited and never married, the wife whose husband left anyway: each had been told, in effect, that God owed them something, and when he didn't pay, a faith sold as an investment looked like a fraud. Some of the deepest disillusionment comes from people who kept the rules and found the contract had never been binding.

Scripture teaches holiness on entirely different grounds. One of Paul's earliest letters, written around AD 50 to a young church of Gentile converts in Thessalonica, puts the ethic without softening it: "For this is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from sexual immorality; that each one of you know how to control his own body in holiness and honor, not in the passion of lust like the Gentiles who do not know God; that no one transgress and wrong his brother in this matter, because the Lord is an avenger in all these things, as we told you beforehand and solemnly warned you. For God has not called us for impurity, but in holiness. Therefore whoever disregards this, disregards not man but God, who gives his Holy Spirit to you" (1 Thessalonians 4:3–8).

These were converts from a Greek city where a free man's sexual access to slaves and prostitutes was widely taken for granted, and Paul asks for something costly. But the demand rests on the will of God, on knowing God as the pagans around them did not, on justice to the neighbor who is wronged when sex is taken where it doesn't belong, and on the Spirit whom God gives. There is no reward promised in pleasure. Sanctification here is a calling and a gift, something God does in a person over a lifetime rather than an asset the person holds and might lose. The ethic is the same one purity culture preached. The gospel underneath it is not.

This is where the distinction between virginity and chastity earns its keep. C. S. Lewis, in *Mere Christianity* (1952), called chastity the most unpopular of the Christian virtues and stated the old rule without apology: either marriage with complete faithfulness to one's spouse, or total abstinence. That rule has no expiration date. It binds the married man in how he looks at other women as surely as it binds the teenager. The pledge card, by contrast, was worded to run until the wedding, so the movement concentrated its whole moral energy on adolescence and had almost nothing to say to the married, the divorced, or the long single. A status belongs to one season. A virtue belongs to all of them, and it can be taken up again by someone whose history is already written.

### Shame where grace belonged

Purity culture quoted this passage often, and needed to finish reading it. "Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a person commits is outside the body, but the sexually immoral person sins against his own body. Or do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God? You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:18–20).

Paul does treat sexual sin as distinctive, and honesty requires saying so. He's writing to Corinthian men who had decided that what they did with their bodies, including visiting prostitutes, couldn't touch their spiritual lives, and he answers that it touches them at the root, because the body is joined to Christ. A church that talks as if sex were morally weightless has left Paul behind. But look at why he says it's serious. The body is a temple, inhabited by the Spirit. It will be raised. It has been bought. Its dignity comes from union and redemption, not from being unused. And a few verses earlier, after a list that includes sexual sins, Paul says to the same church: "And such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11). The church he warns is a church of the washed.

"You are not your own" cuts against the revolution's claim that my body is mine to use as I please. It also cuts against purity culture's favorite image, the body as a gift wrapped and saved for a future spouse, which quietly made the spouse the owner and the wedding the moment of delivery. The body belongs to the Lord who bought it, and the price he paid doesn't fall when its history changes. The youth-group object lessons, the chewed gum no one would want, the rose passed around the room until its petals were gone, taught the opposite: that a body is a depreciating asset. Guilt can be confessed and forgiven. A ruined status can only be hidden.

The church has refused that logic before. After the Goths sacked Rome in 410, Christians faced the question of the women, consecrated virgins among them, who had been raped by the invading soldiers. Had they lost their chastity? Should they, like Lucretia in the old Roman story, have preferred death? Augustine answered in the first book of *The City of God* (413–426) that chastity is a virtue of the mind, held in the will, and that violence done to the body against the will cannot take it away. The sin belonged to the men who committed it. Sixteen centuries before the pledge cards, Augustine had rejected the idea that a violated person becomes damaged goods. A movement that treated purity as a physical possession sometimes taught abused girls exactly what Augustine denied.

The movement also got the order of sins backward. Lewis, in the same book, called sins of the flesh the least bad of all sins, ranked pride far worse, and warned that a cold, self-righteous churchgoer may be nearer hell than a prostitute. He was following Jesus, who told the chief priests and elders, "Truly, I say to you, the tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the kingdom of God before you" (Matthew 21:31). Purity culture made sexual sin the master sin and let pride and the pleasure of judgment pass as seriousness.

And because sexual sin became the unforgivable sin in practice, it became the unconfessable one. Dietrich Bonhoeffer observed in *Life Together* (1939) that sin wants to get a person alone, and that the more isolated he becomes, the more destructive its power; confession to a brother breaks the isolation. A youth group where the worst thing you could be was impure was a youth group where no one would say so aloud. The secrecy made no one purer. It made the sin stronger and the sinner lonelier.

### Whose eyes, whose hands

Jesus's own teaching on lust is addressed to men, and it is severe. "You have heard that it was said, 'You shall not commit adultery.' But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart. If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away. For it is better that you lose one of your members than that your whole body be thrown into hell. And if your right hand causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away. For it is better that you lose one of your members than that your whole body go into hell" (Matthew 5:27–30).

The language is hyperbole, and the church has generally read it that way, but its direction couldn't be plainer. The one who looks is responsible for the look. The remedy is applied to his own eye and his own hand. Jesus doesn't say that if her clothing causes you to sin, she should cover it. Purity culture's modesty teaching reversed the passage. Girls were told their bodies could make their brothers stumble and that the thoughts of the boys around them were in some measure theirs to answer for. Boys were told that lust was their permanent condition, which sounded like honesty and functioned as an excuse. Jesus assumes the opposite. He treats men as moral agents capable of radical self-government, and he never makes a woman the custodian of a man's heart.

Scripture does commend modesty. Paul asks that "women should adorn themselves in respectable apparel, with modesty and self-control, not with braided hair and gold or pearls or costly attire" (1 Timothy 2:9). But the contrast there is with the display of wealth in worship. Modesty in the New Testament is a form of humility, never a system for managing male desire.

The scene in John 8 makes the point in narrative. The passage (John 7:53–8:11) is missing from the earliest manuscripts, and the ESV brackets it, but many scholars who doubt it belongs to John's original text still regard it as an early memory of Jesus. The scribes and Pharisees set a woman caught in adultery in front of everyone. The law they cite named both the man and the woman (Leviticus 20:10; Deuteronomy 22:22), but only the woman is there. John says plainly why she was brought: "This they said to test him" (John 8:6). She was a prop in an argument among men. Jesus bends down and writes on the ground, and when they press him he says, "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her" (John 8:7). They leave one by one, the older ones first, until he is alone with her. Then: "Woman, where are they? Has no one condemned you?" She said, "No one, Lord." And Jesus said, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:10–11).

He doesn't drop the ethic; "sin no more" is a real call. But he removes the condemnation first and then gives the call, and only after the men who had used her have gone. Purity culture too often reversed that order, issuing the call first and holding the condemnation in reserve for anyone who failed, and it rarely asked where the man was.

Where the teaching met abuse, it became dangerous. A movement that tells a girl her body causes male sin hands an abuser his script and his victim her silence. In 2022 the Southern Baptist Convention, whose youth ministry had launched True Love Waits, released an independent investigation by Guidepost Solutions finding that for years leaders of its Executive Committee had mishandled reports of sexual abuse and resisted reform, treating survivors as a legal liability rather than as people to be protected. Baptists like me preached purity to teenagers for a generation while that was happening at the top. The indictment belongs to us before it belongs to anyone else.

If someone is hurting you now, or you are still carrying what someone did to you, the shame of it was never yours. In the United States the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, and the National Sexual Assault Hotline is 1-800-656-4673. If you are thinking of ending your life, call or text 988. If a child is in danger, call 911 and report it to the police. A church that asks you to handle abuse quietly inside its walls is protecting itself, not you.

### A high priest who was tempted

The letter to the Hebrews says something about Jesus that purity culture seldom let itself say. "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin. Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:15–16).

Jesus had a body and was tempted in every respect, so temptation itself is not sin, a distinction the youth room blurred when it taught teenagers that the arrival of desire was already failure. Because he knows the weight of temptation from inside, the person who has failed is invited toward him with confidence rather than away from him in dread. The throne of grace is there for the time of need, which is to say for the person who didn't keep the pledge, and for the one who kept it and still can't make peace with her own body.

Reconstruction starts there. Lauren Winner argued in *Real Sex* (2005) that chastity is a communal discipline rather than a private achievement, sustained by a church's shared life rather than a promise signed alone at fourteen. Bearman and Brückner's data point the same way: whatever power the pledge had came from belonging to a people who held the conviction in common. A church that wants its young people chaste has to be a community in which chastity makes sense: one that teaches the virtue to every age, the married included, confesses sexual sin alongside pride and greed and hears that confession without horror, teaches boys that Matthew 5 is about their own eyes, never teaches a girl that her body is a problem, and reports abuse to the authorities every time. And it has to ground the whole ethic where Paul grounded it: "The body is not meant for sexual immorality, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body. And God raised the Lord and will also raise us up by his power" (1 Corinthians 6:13–14). The body matters because it will be raised, not because it must be delivered unopened.

The gospel has a body at its center. At the cross the soldiers divided his garments (John 19:23–24), and the Son of God was exposed, mocked and shamed in public, in his flesh, for people whose flesh had failed them and whose flesh had been used by others. And when he rose, he showed his disciples the wounds. The risen body kept its scars and was not diminished by them. That is the body Christian hope looks toward, and it is the answer to the rose with no petals.

That leaves the church with two debts, and it can't pay one by defaulting on the other. It owes the people purity culture harmed a confession that doesn't hurry to add that the intentions were good. And it owes their children the ethic itself, taught this time as a calling rather than a contract, held in a community that hears confession, and anchored in a body that was broken and raised. The women Klein listened to, and the men who were told they couldn't help themselves, are sitting in our churches now, and some of them teach the youth group. What they were handed was a card to sign. What they were owed was the gospel, and we still owe it.
