Almost everyone in the American church wants to be prophetic now. The word has become a compliment Christians pay themselves on the way to saying something the other side will hate. On the right it names the preacher who defies the culture over sexuality and the unborn; on the left it names the activist who defies the church over race, money and power. Both uses tend to mean the same thing: I am saying something hard, to people I am not, at no cost I can see. I don't exempt myself. Anyone who stands in a pulpit knows the small pleasure of being called brave for telling a room what it already believes about someone who isn't in it.

Walter Brueggemann, an Old Testament scholar in the United Church of Christ who taught for decades at Eden Theological Seminary in St. Louis and later at Columbia Theological Seminary in Georgia, wrote a short book in 1978 that should have made that use of the word impossible. *The Prophetic Imagination*, reissued in a second edition in 2001, argues that the prophet's work is not chiefly to predict the future, and not chiefly to denounce the present. It is to nurture a way of perceiving the world that the dominant culture has made unthinkable, and to do it through two acts that sound soft and are anything but: grief and hope. The prophet criticizes by grieving what the powerful refuse to feel, and energizes by hoping for what the powerful cannot imagine. The book has shaped a generation of preachers, most of them in the mainline churches, and it has also served as a license for people who read its title and skipped its argument.

The argument deserves better on both counts. This essay sets out what Brueggemann actually claimed, from Moses through Solomon, Jeremiah and the prophet of the exile to Jesus, then tests it where his critics have pressed hardest, and then turns it on the American church, right and left alike. One distinction runs through all of it. In Brueggemann's usage, imagination is not invention. It is perception, the capacity to see what is really there and what the reigning order has trained people not to see. A prophet isn't a person with an unusually creative mind. He is a person who has stopped being numb.

### The empire Moses left and the people he formed

Brueggemann begins not with the writing prophets but with Moses, because in his reading the exodus is the event that makes prophecy possible at all. Pharaoh's Egypt is his picture of a settled world: a vast economy of surplus, secured by forced labor and blessed by gods whose chief work was to guarantee that the arrangement would go on forever. He described its three parts in terms that have stuck to the book ever since. There was an economics of affluence, in which the few were well supplied and the many made the bricks. There was a politics of oppression, in which order was kept by the taskmaster. And there was a religion of static triumphalism, in which the gods were fixtures of the regime, never surprised, never moved, never on the side of the brickmakers. Each part protected the others. A god who can't be moved by the cry of a slave is exactly the god a slave economy requires.

Against that world, Brueggemann argues, Moses did something more radical than lead a revolt. He dismantled the empire's religion by announcing a God who is free, and he formed a community whose economics and politics answered to that God. The story's hinge is a sound. "During those many days the king of Egypt died, and the people of Israel groaned because of their slavery and cried out for help. Their cry for rescue from slavery came up to God. And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exodus 2:23-24, ESV). Brueggemann makes much of that groan. Pain spoken aloud is already a break with the empire, whose stability depends on pain staying private, unvoiced and therefore unreal. When the slaves cry out, they are saying for the first time that the way things are is not the way things must be. And the God of Israel answers in words no Egyptian god could use: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters. I know their sufferings" (Exodus 3:7, ESV).

The community that came out of Egypt was meant to be Egypt's opposite in each of its parts. Its economics began with manna, bread that could not be hoarded: "whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack" (Exodus 16:18, ESV), and whatever was stored overnight against the command bred worms and stank. It was an economics of enough, and the canon never forgot it; Paul quotes that verse to the Corinthians when he asks them to share with the poor believers in Jerusalem (2 Corinthians 8:15). Its politics were bound to covenant rather than to a throne, and when Deuteronomy finally made room for a king, it hedged him with prohibitions that read like a portrait of Pharaoh in reverse. The king "must not acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses," and "he shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away, nor shall he acquire for himself excessive silver and gold" (Deuteronomy 17:16-17, ESV). Its religion began in a song Miriam sang on the far side of the sea: "Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea" (Exodus 15:21, ESV). Brueggemann hears that doxology as the first act of a new social imagination. The horses were the empire's power, and the song says they are gone.

It's worth saying plainly what the story does not flatter. The alternative community was fragile from its first weeks. In the wilderness the people looked back on slavery with longing: "Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full" (Exodus 16:3, ESV). That may be the most honest sentence in the Pentateuch about how the royal consciousness holds people. It does not hold them only by force. It holds them by the memory of full stomachs. The empire's best argument has never been the whip; it is the meat pots, and a free people will always be tempted to trade its freedom back for them.

### How Solomon rebuilt Egypt in Jerusalem

The center of Brueggemann's book is his claim that Israel, within a few generations of Moses, rebuilt Egypt with its own hands, and that Solomon was the builder. He calls the result the royal consciousness, and he means more by it than a set of policies. It is a way of seeing the world from the palace, a settled imagination in which the present arrangement is assumed to be permanent, prosperity is taken as proof of God's favor, and whatever the arrangement costs is kept out of sight. Brueggemann reads Solomon's reign, in the tenth century BC, as a counterrevolution against Moses, with each part of Egypt restored.

The text supplies his evidence, and the narrator of Kings sets it down with a dry eye. The economics of affluence appear in the palace's daily supply list: "Solomon's provision for one day was thirty cors of fine flour and sixty cors of meal, ten fat oxen, and twenty pasture-fed cattle, a hundred sheep, besides deer, gazelles, roebucks, and fattened fowl" (1 Kings 4:22-23, ESV). The politics of oppression appear in the labor draft: "King Solomon drafted forced labor out of all Israel, and the draft numbered 30,000 men" (1 Kings 5:13, ESV). The book notes, without comment, that Solomon made a marriage alliance with Pharaoh (1 Kings 3:1), that he imported his horses from Egypt (1 Kings 10:28), and that his wives and his gold reached the numbers Deuteronomy had forbidden. A reader who knows the law of the king is meant to hear each line as a charge. The people who came out of Egypt now had a Pharaoh of their own.

The religion changed with the economy. The tabernacle of the wilderness had moved with the people; the temple Solomon built stood beside his palace, and he dedicated it with words Brueggemann reads as the royal consciousness in a single sentence: "I have indeed built you an exalted house, a place for you to dwell in forever" (1 Kings 8:13, ESV). The God who had heard slaves and gone with them into the desert now had an address, and the address belonged to the king. In Brueggemann's account the temple did not so much honor God's presence as domesticate it. God became available on the regime's terms, guaranteeing its permanence and never disturbing its accounts. That is what static triumphalism means: a God who has already won, whose victory is identical with the present order, and who therefore has nothing left to say to it.

Brueggemann's most penetrating word for what this does to people is numbness. A society organized around its own permanence can't afford to feel its own costs, so it trains its members not to. Pain is managed, grief is hurried, death is kept out of the conversation, and public speech turns relentlessly cheerful. He meant this as a description of Solomon's Jerusalem, but he plainly meant it of the affluent West in the late 1970s as well, and it has lost none of its force since. The numbness is not cruelty. Most people inside a royal consciousness are decent. They simply can't feel what their comfort costs someone else, because the whole arrangement has been designed so that they won't have to.

The bill came due quickly. When Solomon died, the northern tribes came to his son Rehoboam at Shechem with a single request: "Your father made our yoke heavy. Now therefore lighten the hard service of your father and his heavy yoke on us, and we will serve you" (1 Kings 12:4, ESV). It is Exodus 2 again, the groan of people under forced labor, and Rehoboam answered it the way Pharaoh had: "My father disciplined you with whips, but I will discipline you with scorpions" (1 Kings 12:11, ESV). The kingdom split around 930 BC and was never whole again. The royal consciousness only feels stable from inside the palace.

### Criticism begins in grief

If the royal consciousness runs on numbness, Brueggemann argues, then the first prophetic act is not an argument. It is grief spoken in public. The prophet brings to speech the pain the arrangement has made unspeakable, and by doing so he ends the pretense that nothing is wrong. His model is Jeremiah, and the choice is exact.

Jeremiah was called in the thirteenth year of King Josiah (Jeremiah 1:2), about 627 BC, and he did not want the work: "Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth" (Jeremiah 1:6, ESV). He spent some forty years telling Jerusalem that its royal and religious establishment would fall, and nearly everyone in authority told the city the opposite. The dominant voices of his day were not unbelievers. They were priests and prophets who spoke in God's name, and their message was reassurance. Jeremiah quotes it back to them twice: "They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (Jeremiah 6:14, ESV; compare 8:11). In the temple sermon, which Jeremiah 26 places early in the reign of Jehoiakim, he named the religion of static triumphalism in its own vocabulary: "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (Jeremiah 7:4, ESV). The people believed God's house guaranteed their safety. Jeremiah told them God was not bound to the building, and the priests and prophets demanded his death for saying so (Jeremiah 26:11).

What makes Jeremiah the prophet of grief, rather than merely the prophet of doom, is that he weeps over the people he indicts. "My joy is gone; grief is upon me; my heart is sick within me" (Jeremiah 8:18, ESV). A few lines later: "Oh that my head were waters, and my eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people!" (Jeremiah 9:1, ESV). He doesn't stand outside Jerusalem pronouncing sentence. He stands inside it, sharing its fate, having been lowered into the mud of a cistern for his trouble (Jeremiah 38:6), and when the sentence falls in 587 BC he is still there. Brueggemann's point is that the grief is itself the criticism. The royal consciousness can absorb opposition; it has offices for that. What it can't absorb is someone who feels what it has forbidden people to feel and says so where everyone can hear.

Here Brueggemann leaned on Abraham Joshua Heschel, whose *The Prophets* (1962), grown from his doctoral work in Berlin in the 1930s, remains the deepest modern study of what it was like to be one. Heschel's central idea is divine pathos. The God of the prophets, he argued, is not the unmoved mover of Greek philosophy, serenely indifferent to what happens below. He is affected by what people do to one another, wounded by injustice and grieved by his people's faithlessness, and the prophet is the person drawn into sympathy with that pathos. Jeremiah's tears are not only his own. They are the grief of God carried in a human voice. Heschel also noticed how disproportionate the prophets sound to comfortable ears: what strikes the rest of us as a minor lapse strikes them as a catastrophe, because they measure it by God's concern and not by social convention. And he pressed a point the American church badly needs. In a society where wrong is committed by some and tolerated by the rest, he argued, few may be guilty, but all are responsible.

Christian theology has long argued over how to speak of God's pathos, and the classical doctrine of divine impassibility exists to guard something true: God is not at the mercy of moods, not changed by the creatures he made, never less than perfectly himself. One doesn't have to settle that argument to receive Heschel's point, which the prophets make on nearly every page. Whatever else God is, he is not indifferent. The prophet who grieves is not projecting his own feelings onto heaven. He is answering a God who has already taken the side of the slain daughter of his people.

This yields the distinction on which the rest of the argument turns. Grief and outrage can use the same words, but they are opposite acts. Outrage stands outside the people it condemns and enjoys the distance; it needs an enemy, and it is quietly disappointed if the enemy repents. Grief stands inside, shares the loss, and wants nothing so much as the repentance that would end it. The prophets were often angry, and the anger of God is real in their books. But prophetic anger is anger on behalf of the wounded, spoken by someone who is himself inside the wound. Anger without grief is only the royal consciousness with a microphone.

### Hope that comes after grief

Grief is the first prophetic task, but in Brueggemann's account it is never the last. The second is what he calls energizing: the offer of a hope so concrete and so unlike the present that people become able to live toward it. He found his model in the chapters of Isaiah addressed to the exiles in Babylon, chapters 40 through 55, which he, with most critical scholars, attributes to an anonymous prophet of the exile usually called Second Isaiah. Many conservative scholars hold that the whole book comes from Isaiah of Jerusalem in the eighth century, speaking ahead of the events. That question doesn't need to be settled here, because on either view these chapters address a people who had lost their city in 587, whose temple was ash, and who had concluded that their God had been defeated or had forgotten them. The text names Cyrus of Persia (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1), whose decree of 538 BC would let them go home.

The prophet begins where Jeremiah's grief had left them, and his first word is tenderness: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (Isaiah 40:1, ESV). He quotes their despair back to them, which is the only honest way to answer it: "Why do you say, O Jacob, and speak, O Israel, 'My way is hidden from the LORD, and my right is disregarded by my God'?" (Isaiah 40:27, ESV). Then he does something Babylon couldn't stop. He mocks the empire's gods, pictured as baggage loaded onto tired animals and carried off into captivity themselves ("Bel bows down; Nebo stoops; their idols are on beasts and livestock," Isaiah 46:1, ESV), and he announces a future the exiles had stopped letting themselves picture: "Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it? I will make a way in the wilderness and rivers in the desert" (Isaiah 43:18-19, ESV). The new thing is described in the vocabulary of the old one. It is another exodus. The God who heard the groan in Egypt hears it in Babylon.

Brueggemann insisted that this hope is not a mood. It is a way of speaking that breaks the empire's monopoly on the future. Babylon's world was meant to feel total: its gods, its calendar, its account of what was possible. The prophet's doxology punctures it, and a people who can "sing to the LORD a new song" (Isaiah 42:10) by the rivers of Babylon are no longer entirely Babylonian. A second distinction matters here, one the royal consciousness works hard to blur. Optimism and hope are different things. Optimism is confidence that the present arrangement will keep improving on its own terms. It is the characteristic temper of the palace, and it needs no grief because it never admits a loss. Hope is trust in a God who acts from beyond the arrangement, and it is available only to people who have admitted that the arrangement has failed. That is why the order matters. Isaiah 40 comes after Lamentations, and the comfort is spoken to people who have wept.

Brueggemann's closing chapters argue that Jesus of Nazareth brings both prophetic tasks to their fullness, and the Gospels support him more than his critics sometimes allow. The criticism begins before Jesus is born, in his mother's song: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate; he has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich he has sent away empty" (Luke 1:52-53, ESV). Brueggemann's most arresting claim is that Jesus's compassion was itself a form of criticism. When Jesus "saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34, ESV), he was feeling what the crowd's rulers had stopped feeling and taking seriously the pain their order treated as normal. The blessings and woes in Luke run along the same seam: "Blessed are you who weep now, for you shall laugh," and "Woe to you who laugh now, for you shall mourn and weep" (Luke 6:21, 25, ESV). As he came down toward Jerusalem for the last time, Jesus did what Jeremiah had done: "And when he drew near and saw the city, he wept over it, saying, 'Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes'" (Luke 19:41-42, ESV). Then he entered the temple and turned Jeremiah's own temple sermon against it: "It is written, 'My house shall be a house of prayer,' but you have made it a den of robbers" (Luke 19:46, ESV; compare Jeremiah 7:11).

For Brueggemann the cross is the deepest criticism of all. The royal consciousness, Roman and religious together, executed the one man who would not be numb, and God entered the very death it inflicted. The resurrection is the deepest energizing, the new thing no empire can administer. On that much the church can stand with him gladly. It is also the place where his argument most needs pressing.

### Where Brueggemann needs pressing

A fair reading of *The Prophetic Imagination* owes its critics a hearing, and they come from more than one direction.

The first objection concerns Solomon, and it is that Brueggemann's villain is sharper than the canon's. The same book of Kings that records the labor draft also says that God gave Solomon wisdom as a gift (1 Kings 3:12), that the glory of the LORD filled the temple at its dedication so that the priests could not stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10-11), and that Solomon's prayer that day contains one of the great confessions of God's freedom in Scripture: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27, ESV). The promise to David had said his son would build a house for God's name (2 Samuel 7:13). Isaiah received his call inside that temple (Isaiah 6:1), and Jesus called its successor his Father's house (John 2:16). A reader who takes the whole canon will not set Moses against the temple as though one were liberation and the other captivity. The fairer reading is that the institution was given by God and then captured by the royal consciousness, and that the prophets attacked the capture, not the gift. Kings itself holds the tension openly; one verse even says that Solomon made no slaves of the people of Israel (1 Kings 9:22), a line that sits awkwardly beside the draft in chapter 5 and that interpreters have long worked to reconcile. Brueggemann's scheme is illuminating, but it is a scheme, and it runs smoother than the text.

The second objection concerns history, and it arrives from two opposite sides. Brueggemann's picture of early Israel as an egalitarian alternative to the city-states of Canaan sat close to the sociological reconstructions of George Mendenhall, whose 1962 article described Israel's origins as a kind of peasant revolt, and Norman Gottwald, whose *The Tribes of Yahweh* (1979) developed that thesis at length. Later scholarship has criticized those reconstructions heavily, and critics have asked how much of Brueggemann's Moses is the Moses of Exodus and how much is a social theory of the 1970s read back into him. Meanwhile the archaeologists Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman argued in *The Bible Unearthed* (2001) that tenth-century Jerusalem was a modest highland town and that Solomon's glittering kingdom is largely the literary creation of later writers, a conclusion others, among them Amihai Mazar and Yosef Garfinkel, have contested from their own excavations. If the minimalists are right, the royal consciousness Brueggemann described may belong less to Solomon's actual reign than to the historians who wrote about it.

That objection does less damage than it first appears to. Even on the minimal view, the portrait of Solomon in Kings exists, and it exists as Israel's indictment of itself. The people who told this story put their most celebrated king in the dock and read him against the law of Deuteronomy. The critique of the royal consciousness is therefore not something Brueggemann imported into Scripture. It is Scripture's own, and it would stand as a word of judgment even if the palace menu had been inflated.

The objection from the other side goes deeper, and it is the one this essay can't set aside. In his *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997), Brueggemann organized Israel's faith around testimony and counter-testimony, and he deliberately declined to rule on what lay behind the testimony, treating the text's speech about God as the place where theology happens. Critics pressed him on whether that bracket could hold, and the same question applies to the earlier book. If the exodus is chiefly a story that energizes, and the resurrection chiefly a symbol that interrupts, then the alternative the prophets offer is only as strong as the imagination of whoever is telling it. A community sustained by a moving story is still, finally, sustained by itself.

This is where the distinction from the opening has to be held firmly. The prophetic imagination is perception, not invention. Jeremiah did not imagine Babylon; he saw it coming when the court could not. The prophet of the exile did not invent Cyrus. And the energizing hope of the New Testament rests on a claim about what happened on a particular morning outside Jerusalem. Paul put it without a hedge: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, ESV). The church confesses the bodily resurrection as an event, not as a metaphor for renewal, and that confession is exactly what gives prophetic hope its teeth. The royal consciousness can live comfortably with a beautiful story about liberation; it sells such stories every day. What it can't live with is a God who actually acts, who raises the dead and brings down the mighty, whose new thing doesn't wait on our capacity to picture it. Brueggemann's book is at its strongest where it trusts that God, and at its weakest where it seems content with the imagining.

The third objection is political, and it deserves its strongest form. For many conservative Christians, "prophetic" has become the word the left uses to baptize its platform. The prophets are invoked to endorse a set of policies, the policies turn out to be the ones the educated progressive class already held, and the result is not a free God but a partisan one, a static triumphalism of the other side. That critique has real force. The book was taken up most eagerly in the mainline Protestant world, and some of what has been called prophetic there has been the royal consciousness of a different palace. But the remedy is not to put the prophets down. It is to read them whole. Amos condemned the comfortable women of Samaria who crushed the needy (Amos 4:1) and the courts that took bribes and turned the poor aside in the gate (Amos 5:12). Isaiah opposed Jerusalem's reliance on Egypt's horses and chariots (Isaiah 31:1) as fiercely as he opposed its worship without justice (Isaiah 1:13-17). The prophetic instrument cuts in every direction, and any use of it that only ever cuts one way has already stopped being prophetic.

### The royal consciousness in the American church

Brueggemann wrote about Solomon, but he was writing to Americans, and nearly half a century later his categories fit us better than they did then. The honest way to use them is to turn them on every part of the church with the same instrument and the same grief, beginning with ourselves.

Start with the religion of static triumphalism, because it is the part we are least able to see. Robert Bellah argued in "Civil Religion in America" (1967) that the United States has long carried a religious dimension of its own, a faith in the nation's calling under God that runs alongside the churches without being identical to them. At its best that civil religion held the nation under judgment, as Lincoln's second inaugural did. At its worst it produces what Jeremiah heard at the temple gate. On the Christian right the phrase has often been "this is a Christian nation," spoken with the same confidence and doing the same work as "the temple of the LORD": a guarantee that God's favor rests on the arrangement, so that whatever threatens the arrangement threatens God. Where that confidence has fused the cross with the flag and made God the guarantor of national greatness, it has crossed from patriotism into Christian nationalism, and the prophets had a plainer word for it, which was idolatry. The conservative case deserves its strongest statement first. Many who love this country believe, with reason, that its liberties grew from Christian soil, and they fear, also with reason, a culture grown hostile to the faith that shaped it. That fear isn't foolish. But Jeremiah's hearers had good reasons to love Jerusalem too, and their love was precisely what made them deaf.

The left has its own temple, and its worshipers find it harder to see because it doesn't fly a flag. In much of progressive Christianity the God who presides is the guarantor of history's direction, a God who has already won because the educated consensus is already right, whose will turns out to match the views of the reasonable people in the room. Confidence that one stands on the right side of history is static triumphalism in a newer suit. It needs no grief for its own sins, because its sins are always somebody else's, and it can call itself prophetic without once saying anything its own congregation would find costly. Where that faith has subtracted the offense of the gospel (the judgment, the resurrection, Christ's claim on the body and the bed and the bank account) until nothing costs anything, it has become the chaplaincy of a different class. The progressive case deserves its strongest statement too. The church in America has often stood on the wrong side of the poor and the stranger, and Christians who have seen that are right to refuse silence. But a prophet who only ever indicts the other tribe has become a court prophet, and it hardly matters which court.

Beneath both temples runs a common religion that sociologists have named. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, in *Soul Searching* (2005), found that the working faith of many American teenagers, across denominations, was what they called moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be good and nice, wants them to feel good about themselves, and otherwise stays out of the way, a kind of divine butler and cosmic therapist. The teenagers learned it from us. That God is static triumphalism for private life. He never grieves, never judges and never surprises, and he sits as comfortably in a conservative megachurch as in a progressive mainline sanctuary.

And beneath all of it runs the economics of affluence, with its companion numbness. American Christians, right and left, are among the wealthiest believers in the history of the church, and our worship shows it. Our services move briskly from the week's pain toward uplift. Lament, the most frequent form of prayer in the Psalms, has all but disappeared from many of our liturgies. The phone in every pocket supplies a steady feed of outrage, and outrage, as Jeremiah's tears show by contrast, is not grief. It is numbness that has learned to shout. We are angry about nearly everything and sorrowful about almost nothing, and that is the royal consciousness fully furnished.

I can't write this from outside it. I have pastored a church in a small Michigan city for twelve years, and every pastor knows the pull to send people home lighter than they came, to hurry past grief because grief is hard on a Sunday morning, and to say the hard thing about people who aren't in the room. Pastors are the priests at Jeremiah's temple gate far more often than we are Jeremiah. Few of us are guilty of building the palace, to borrow Heschel's distinction, but all of us are responsible for living comfortably inside it.

What the prophetic imagination asks of a church like ours is less a program than a recovery. It would ask for grief first: lament prayed aloud in worship, confession that names our own side's sins before the other side's, and a refusal to let a death, a shuttered plant, a deported neighbor or an aborted child become a statistic. It would ask for concreteness, because the royal consciousness is at ease with generalities about justice and life and cannot bear specifics about the particular people in our own towns whose pain pays for our ease. It would ask for an economics of enough in households and congregations that have forgotten what manna was. And it would ask us to hope in the only way hope is available, after grief and not instead of it, trusting a God who acts rather than a history that is bound to turn out our way.

The last image belongs to Jeremiah. In the tenth year of Zedekiah (Jeremiah 32:1), with the Babylonian army around Jerusalem and the prophet confined in the court of the guard, God told him to buy a field. It was his cousin's land at Anathoth, already behind enemy lines and about to be worthless. Jeremiah weighed out seventeen shekels of silver, signed the deed before witnesses, and had it sealed in a clay jar so that it would last a long time, because "thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: Houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (Jeremiah 32:15, ESV). The man who had wept for the city more than anyone bought property in its ruin. His grief had not been despair, and his hope was not optimism. He had seen the end of the royal consciousness clearly enough to mourn it, and he had seen the faithfulness of God clearly enough to invest in what would come after.

That field is where the church is asked to stand. The cross is God's grief over a world that crucifies whoever will not be numb, and the empty tomb is God's refusal to let the palace have the last word. Between them lies the work of a people who can weep and still buy the field. Whether we can do either is an open question. Most of us, on the right and the left, have learned to feel strongly about the other side's sins and to feel very little about our own. Until that changes, we are not the prophets. We are Solomon's court, with better music, waiting for a groan we have trained ourselves not to hear.
