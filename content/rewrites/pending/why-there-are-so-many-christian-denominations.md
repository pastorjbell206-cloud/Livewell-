### The number, and the prayer it answers

Someone always brings up the number. There are thirty thousand denominations, or thirty-three thousand, or more, depending on who's telling it, and the conclusion follows as though it were arithmetic: if Christians can't agree on what their own book means, why should anyone else take it seriously? The doubting Christian hears it with embarrassment, because she drives past four churches on the way to her own and couldn't say what separates them. And the newcomer searching for a church finds that many of the results call themselves non-denominational, which sounds like a way out of the whole quarrel, and wonders whether that's the safe choice.

These questions deserve better than the two answers they usually get. One says the divisions don't matter, that we're all basically the same, and that anyone who cares about baptism or bishops is a crank. The other says the divisions prove everything, that the churches are human institutions splitting over human opinions. The history is more painful than the first answer admits and more interesting than the second allows. I pastor First Baptist Church of Fenton, Michigan, a church with a denomination in its name, and the tradition I serve is one of the reasons the count runs as high as it does.

Start with the number, since it carries more of the argument than it can bear. The figure usually quoted traces to the *World Christian Encyclopedia*, the survey begun by the missions researcher David Barrett, whose second edition (2001, prepared with George Kurian and Todd Johnson) listed something over thirty-three thousand denominations. The encyclopedia counted organized Christian bodies country by country, so that one communion present in many nations is entered once for each nation, and it treated independent networks and congregations registered under their own names as bodies of their own. That's a sensible count for missionaries who need to know who is working where. It isn't a count of rival theologies. Sort the world's churches by what they actually confess and practice, and the real families come to a few dozen: the Eastern Orthodox and the older Oriental churches, the Catholic Church, the Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican and Anabaptist traditions of the Reformation, the Baptists and Methodists, the Pentecostals, and their near relations.

But taking the number apart doesn't dissolve the problem, and the Christian who stops there has won a point and lost the argument. A few dozen divided families is still a scandal for a body whose founder prayed that it would be one. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus prayed not only for the eleven but for everyone who would come after them:

> I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me. The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you loved me. (John 17:20–23, ESV)

The prayer grounds the church's unity in the communion of the Father and the Son, and it ties that unity to the credibility of the message. Twice it names the world as the watching party. The skeptic who points at the divided church is only reading the prayer back to us.

And the first generation was already failing. Within a few years of the church's founding in Corinth, believers were sorting themselves into parties under the names of their favorite teachers, and Paul wrote back: "What I mean is that each one of you says, 'I follow Paul,' or 'I follow Apollos,' or 'I follow Cephas,' or 'I follow Christ.' Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?" (1 Corinthians 1:12–13, ESV). The question isn't rhetorical decoration. Faction puts a human name where Christ's name belongs, when it was the cross, not the preacher, that made them one people. Every denomination ever founded has had to answer Paul's question, and some have answered it better than others.

### Four breaks, each with a date

The first lasting division came earlier than most Western Christians realize. After the Council of Chalcedon in 451 defined Christ as one person in two natures, much of the church in Egypt, Syria and Armenia refused the council's wording, and the Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian) have lived apart ever since. In the last half century their patriarchs and the bishops of Rome have signed common declarations acknowledging that they confess the same Christ in different vocabulary.

The break everyone calls the Great Schism came in 1054. The Latin West and the Greek East had been drifting for centuries over language, liturgy, the reach of the bishop of Rome's authority, and a single word. The Nicene Creed, as the councils of 325 and 381 left it, confesses that the Spirit proceeds from the Father. Western churches, beginning at the Third Council of Toledo in 589, added *filioque*, "and the Son," and Rome eventually adopted the addition itself early in the eleventh century. The East objected to the theology and, just as sharply, to changing the common creed without a common council. In July 1054 the papal legate Cardinal Humbert laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, and the patriarch Michael Cerularius answered in kind. What sealed it was less an argument than a crime: in 1204 the armies of the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople and looted its churches, and a quarrel between bishops became a grievance between peoples. Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the mutual excommunications in December 1965. Communion has not been restored.

The third break began on October 31, 1517, when Martin Luther posted ninety-five theses against the sale of indulgences. Luther meant to reform a church, not to found one, and he was excommunicated for it in 1521. The Protestants were dividing from one another almost at once. At Marburg in 1529, Luther and the Zurich reformer Ulrich Zwingli agreed on nearly every article put before them, then parted over what Christ meant by "This is my body." From that parting came the Lutheran churches, gathered around the Augsburg Confession of 1530, and the Reformed churches, shaped by Zwingli and decisively by John Calvin in Geneva. England came by a different road. Henry VIII's Act of Supremacy in 1534 broke with Rome for reasons that had more to do with a king's marriage than a doctrine of grace, but under Thomas Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer (1549) the Church of England became something of its own, Reformed in much of its theology and catholic in its bishops and liturgy, the root of the Anglican communion.

The fourth break ran through the Reformation itself. In Zurich in January 1525, a circle of young radicals around Conrad Grebel concluded that Zwingli had stopped halfway, that baptism belonged only to those who could confess faith for themselves, and they baptized one another. Their enemies called them Anabaptists, "rebaptizers." In January 1527 the Protestant authorities of Zurich drowned Felix Manz in the river Limmat for the crime of rebaptizing. Sit with that date. Within a decade of the Reformation's birth, Protestants were executing other Protestants over the meaning of baptism. No tradition gets to tell this history with clean hands, mine least of all, since Baptists stand partly downstream of the drowned man and partly downstream of the magistrates who drowned him.

Europe tried to settle the wreckage by drawing borders. The Peace of Augsburg in 1555 let each German prince choose Lutheran or Catholic for his territory; the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 added the Reformed; the Anabaptists were never included. Europe now had divided churches, but not yet denominations in the modern sense, because each body still claimed to be the church of its land. What turned those divisions into the denominational world we know was America.

### How America multiplied the churches

Ernst Troeltsch drew a distinction in *The Social Teaching of the Christian Churches* (1912) that makes the American story legible. A church, in his sense, is the body a person is born into, embracing a whole society and making peace with its institutions. A sect is a body a person chooses, gathered out of society and set against its compromises. America produced a third thing, and the word for it carries a theory inside it. A denomination is a named part of the church that doesn't claim to be the whole, one "name" among others, each assuming the true church is larger than itself.

My own tradition helped make that possible. The first Baptist congregation was gathered among English exiles in Amsterdam by John Smyth and Thomas Helwys in 1609. Helwys brought a congregation home to England in 1612 and published *A Short Declaration of the Mystery of Iniquity*, pleading that the king had no power over conscience, whether his subjects were heretics, Jews or Muslims. He died in prison a few years later. Roger Williams, banished from Massachusetts, gathered a Baptist church in Providence in 1638. What those dissenters paid for was vindicated when the First Amendment (1791) forbade a national establishment of religion, and the last state establishment, in Massachusetts, ended in 1833. From then on no American church had the state's help, and every church had to persuade. A voluntary religion under a free government will multiply.

Revival supplied the fuel. John Wesley's renewal movement became a church of its own in America at the Christmas Conference in Baltimore in 1784, and Methodist circuit riders and Baptist farmer-preachers outran every established clergy into the backcountry. The camp meeting at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801 became the emblem of the Second Great Awakening. Nathan Hatch's *The Democratization of American Christianity* (1989) is the essential account of what followed. Hatch argues that the democratic energy of the Revolution flowed into religion, that ordinary people in the early republic stopped deferring to learned clergy and formal confessions, and that populist preachers built movements by trusting common people to read the Bible and judge for themselves. The Methodists, the Baptists, the Stone-Campbell "Christians," the Mormons and the Black churches all grew in that ground. Hatch is admiring and uneasy at once. The same upheaval that gave ordinary people their Bibles made every gifted preacher a potential founder.

The Stone-Campbell movement is the parable of the era. Thomas Campbell's *Declaration and Address* (1809) insisted that the church of Christ on earth is one by its very nature, and the movement he and his son Alexander led with Barton Stone set out to end denominations by calling Christians back to the Bible alone, with "no creed but the Bible" as its watchword. It was a sincere and moving hope. Within a century the unity movement had divided, over instruments in worship and missionary societies, into the Churches of Christ, the Disciples of Christ and the independent Christian Churches. A movement founded to end denominations became three, which is the clearest demonstration in American history that refusing a tradition's name doesn't free anyone from having one.

Then comes the harder truth, the one H. Richard Niebuhr wouldn't let the church avoid. In *The Social Sources of Denominationalism* (1929), Niebuhr charged that denominationalism was a hypocrisy the church had never admitted, and he argued that most American divisions weren't theological at root. They followed the lines of class, nation, region and race. The churches of the poor grew respectable within a generation or two, and the poor left to found new sects; immigrant churches divided along the languages of the old country; and the church, he said, had accommodated itself to the caste system of human society. The American record gives him more evidence than he needed. The Methodists divided North and South in 1844, the Baptists in 1845 (when the Southern Baptist Convention was organized in Augusta, Georgia), the Presbyterians in 1861, and the question each time was slavery. Richard Allen and the Black Methodists of Philadelphia, humiliated at prayer in a white congregation, organized the African Methodist Episcopal Church as a denomination in 1816. They did not divide over a doctrine. They were driven out over the color of their skin, and the doctrine they carried out with them was the one their persecutors professed.

Pentecostalism tested Niebuhr's thesis from the other side. In April 1906 William J. Seymour, a Black holiness preacher and the son of formerly enslaved parents, began meetings in a former church building on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, and for a few years the revival crossed every line America drew, Black and white and Latino, men and women, praying together when that was scandalous. It didn't hold. Within a decade the movement had largely sorted itself by race: the Church of God in Christ, under Charles Harrison Mason, who had received the Pentecostal experience at Azusa, became the great Black Pentecostal church, and the Assemblies of God, organized at Hot Springs, Arkansas, in 1914, became largely white. Even a revival of the Spirit could be recaptured by the social sources Niebuhr named.

The fundamentalist and modernist controversies of the 1920s and 1930s then divided Northern Presbyterians and Northern Baptists, and those fights, unlike many before them, were partly over first things: whether Christ rose bodily, whether the Bible could be trusted.

### The Catholic and Orthodox case at full strength

The most serious critique of this history doesn't come from skeptics. It comes from Catholic and Orthodox Christians, and it should be stated as they would state it. Christ founded one visible church, built on the apostles and kept by their successors, and that church received the Scriptures, fixed the canon and read the Bible within a living tradition. The Reformers cut Scripture loose from the community that carried it and handed every believer the final word on its meaning. The predictable result was division without end, because once no one can settle a dispute, every dispute becomes a new church. John Henry Newman, received into the Catholic Church in 1845, put it in one line in the introduction to his *Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine* (1845): "To be deep in history is to cease to be a Protestant." The sociologist Christian Smith, writing as an evangelical in *The Bible Made Impossible* (2011), named the problem pervasive interpretive pluralism: sincere, competent readers who share a high view of the Bible keep reaching different conclusions on things that matter, and a biblicism that can't account for that has undercut itself. Smith soon became a Catholic. The Second Vatican Council's decree on ecumenism, *Unitatis Redintegratio* (1964), said with real grief that division contradicts the will of Christ, scandalizes the world and harms the preaching of the gospel, and it said so while calling Protestants brothers whose baptism truly joins them to Christ. The Orthodox press further back. The nineteenth-century Russian theologian Alexei Khomiakov argued that Rome and the Protestants share one Western habit, treating authority as something outside the church's common life, a juridical office or an individual's reasoning, so that Protestantism is Rome's child rather than its cure.

A Protestant who doesn't feel the weight of this isn't paying attention. The American story told above isn't a distortion of the Protestant principle so much as one of its possible fruits.

The Protestant answer turns on a distinction the critique usually passes over. Keith Mathison, in *The Shape of Sola Scriptura* (2001), distinguishes *sola scriptura*, the Reformers' conviction that Scripture is the only infallible rule, read within the church, under the ancient creeds and alongside its teachers, from what he calls *solo scriptura*, the individual alone with a Bible and answerable to no one. The magisterial Reformers held the first; the Augsburg Confession's first article begins by affirming the decree of Nicaea. What Hatch describes in the early republic, and what "no creed but the Bible" enshrined, is much closer to the second, and much of the fragmentation Newman foresaw belongs to it. Kevin Vanhoozer argues in *Biblical Authority after Babel* (2016) that the Reformation's principles, rightly held, yield a catholic Protestantism, in which disagreement inside the boundaries of the creed is the church reading in many voices rather than the collapse of reading. And honesty runs both ways. The schism of 1054 divided two churches that both had bishops in succession from the apostles, and the Orthodox communion has been broken in our own time, as when Moscow cut communion with Constantinople in 2018. A visible hierarchy is a real good. It hasn't prevented schism either.

This essay lands where its writer stands, inside the Protestant and Baptist tradition, and it has to name the objection it hasn't fully answered. Protestantism has no final court on earth for a second-order dispute. Its answer, that the Spirit rules the church through Scripture read together over time, is true, but it's slow, and for five centuries it has let pride split congregations with a clear conscience. I've been part of that. I have worn my tradition's distinctives like a team jersey, defined more by what I'm against than by the Lord I'm for, which is the Corinthian disease exactly. I have made jokes from the pulpit about traditions whose members have suffered more for Christ than I ever will. I have felt the small tribal warmth of being among the ones who got it right, a warmth Felix Manz and the men who drowned him both knew. "I follow Paul" is alive and well. It has only changed names.

### What the divided churches still confess

Now the fact the number hides. Put a Pentecostal from Lagos, an Orthodox priest from Thessaloniki, a Catholic from Manila and a Baptist from Michigan in one room, and they will find that they confess the same God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, the same Christ, crucified under Pontius Pilate and raised bodily on the third day, and the same life of the world to come. Most of their churches recite the Nicene or the Apostles' Creed, and most that don't still teach what the creeds say. The remarkable thing isn't that Christians have disagreed about bishops and baptism. It's that across two thousand years, every culture on earth and all the blood they've shed, they have kept the same center.

Paul's letter to the Ephesians shows how that center holds. The letter's first half tells how the deepest division in the ancient world, between Jew and Gentile, was ended at the cross. Only then does Paul turn to conduct, and he begins with character before structure: walk "with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (Ephesians 4:2–3, ESV). The verb is *maintain*. The church doesn't manufacture unity by agreement; the Spirit has already made it, and the church can guard it or squander it. Then comes the ground: "There is one body and one Spirit," and "one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all" (Ephesians 4:4–6, ESV). The honest reader notices that "one baptism" names the very thing Christians have divided over most bitterly. Paul's claim stands anyway. There is one baptism because there is one Lord into whose name it is given, and our disagreements about its subjects and mode haven't changed who that Lord is.

That's why the distinction between orders of doctrine matters so much here. First-order doctrines are the ones the creeds bind: the Trinity, the deity and humanity of Christ, his bodily resurrection, salvation by grace through the cross. On those the historic churches aren't divided, and a body that denies them has left the faith rather than chosen a denomination. Second-order doctrines, such as baptism, the Supper, church government and the gifts of the Spirit, are real enough to divide congregations but not deep enough to divide the faith, which is why a Presbyterian and a Baptist can each think the other wrong about baptism and both be right that the other is a brother. Third-order questions, such as the shape of the millennium or the style of worship, are genuinely open. Gavin Ortlund's *Finding the Right Hills to Die On* (2020) gives the method its modern name, theological triage, but the instinct is old; a Lutheran writer in the 1620s, during the Thirty Years' War, coined the maxim often misattributed to Augustine: in necessary things unity, in doubtful things liberty, in all things charity. Even the apostles needed it. Paul and Barnabas had "a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other" over whether to take Mark with them (Acts 15:39, ESV), and years later Paul wrote, "Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11, ESV).

The tragedy of the last five centuries is that the orders were confused in both directions. Zurich treated a second-order question as a first-order one and drowned a man for it. The modernists of the 1920s treated first-order questions as though they were third-order and called it tolerance. Lesslie Newbigin, a bishop of the Church of South India, which in 1947 united Anglicans, Methodists, Presbyterians and Congregationalists, argued in *The Household of God* (1953) that each great tradition had kept hold of something the others let slip: Protestants the church as the congregation of the faithful gathered by the Word, Catholics the church as the visible body of Christ continuous through time, Pentecostals the church as the community where the Holy Spirit is present in power. Each needs what the others have kept.

### What "non-denominational" actually means

Walk into a typical non-denominational church in America and you can predict the room: a band, songs on screens, a topical sermon series, a building meant to feel like a venue, and a statement of faith that's broadly evangelical and quiet on the questions that divide denominations. The word on the sign promises no institutional baggage and no tribal loyalties.

Some of that promise is honest, and these churches have real gifts. They reach people who would never walk into a building with a denomination on the sign. At their best they teach the Bible plainly, move quickly where older churches are paralyzed by committees, and put believers in one another's homes. Many are theologically serious.

But the room is not the absence of a tradition. It's one, and its sources can be named. The movement grew out of the Jesus Movement of the late 1960s, when Chuck Smith's Calvary Chapel in Costa Mesa, California, took in young converts who had no use for inherited structures. John Wimber's Vineyard churches shaped its worship in the 1980s, and Willow Creek (1975) and Saddleback (1980) shaped its seeker-oriented preaching. The church growth movement gave it a strategy. Donald McGavran's *Understanding Church Growth* (1970) taught that people prefer to become Christians without crossing lines of race, language or class, and churches built on that "homogeneous unit principle" turned Niebuhr's social sources from a confession of sin into a method. The practice is recognizably baptistic: local autonomy, believer's baptism by immersion, a memorial Supper. The invitation at the end of the sermon descends from Charles Finney's anxious bench in the 1830s. "No creed but the Bible" is the Stone-Campbell watchword with the history filed off.

C. S. Lewis, in the preface to *Mere Christianity* (1952), compared the faith all Christians share to a hall with doors opening into rooms, and he warned that the hall is a place to wait in, not to live in; the fires and chairs and meals, he said, are in the rooms. The non-denominational church believes it is standing in the hall. It's a room, and a tradition that doesn't know it's one can't examine itself. Much of that room is my own tradition with the sign taken down, and the sign on our building is no guarantee that we know our tradition any better.

The missing name has costs. The movement now has de facto denominations in networks such as Acts 29 and the Association of Related Churches, often held together by shared method and brand rather than a shared confession. The gravest cost is accountability. In a denominational system, a pastor who teaches falsely or abuses his authority can be investigated by someone outside his own church. In many independent churches, the board that oversees the pastor was chosen by the pastor. The collapses at Willow Creek, Mars Hill and Harvest Bible Chapel had different causes, but each exposed governance that couldn't check a gifted founder. Denominations fail too; the Southern Baptist Convention's reckoning with sexual abuse, laid out in an independent report in 2022, proves that a structure is only as honest as the people inside it. The structural point still stands. Accountability needs someone with the independence to say no.

So anyone weighing a church, with a name on the sign or without one, should ask plainly what it teaches about baptism and the Supper, which historic confession it stands under, who holds the pastor accountable, who owns the building, and what happens when the founder leaves. A healthy church will answer without defensiveness.

### The wall already broken

The number was always a distraction. The real scandal isn't that Christians hold different convictions about baptism or bishops. It's how many of our divisions Niebuhr traced correctly to class, region, nation and race, and how often we have called those divisions faithfulness. That calls for repentance, and a better statistic won't substitute for it.

Ephesians gives the ground for that repentance. "For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility" (Ephesians 2:14, ESV), reconciling Jew and Gentile "to God in one body through the cross" (Ephesians 2:16, ESV). The unity Jesus prayed for in John 17 isn't ours to engineer. It was purchased, and every wall we rebuild around a color line or an income bracket or a preacher's name is a refusal to live inside what was bought. Second-order convictions can be held with a clear conscience and an open hand. The walls in Niebuhr's book can't be held at all.

And the stubborn fact the skeptic still has to explain lies underneath all of it. After the anathemas, the crusade, the river in Zurich and the separate churches of Philadelphia, Christians are still confessing the same crucified and risen Lord. Every Sunday the sign over our door names a division, and the prayer in John 17 names its end, and none of us gets to close that distance by pretending it isn't there.
