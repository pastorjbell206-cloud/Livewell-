### What the question is not asking

Begin with the concession, and make it without reservation, because the argument that follows collapses the moment it becomes grudging. You don't need to believe in God to be good. Unbelievers are not, as a class, worse neighbors, parents or citizens than the people in the pews, and many are more honest and more generous than the Christians who argue with them. When I was an atheist, the decent unbelievers in my life weren't a category in somebody's apologetics lecture. They were the people I actually knew, and if you had told any of them that their kindness required a theological license, they would have laughed, and they would have been right to laugh. If the claim on the table were that people without faith can't behave well, it would be refuted by every ordinary Tuesday.

That was never the claim. Christians have often argued as though it were, and we have earned the contempt that followed. Philosophers keep apart two questions that the popular debate runs together. The first belongs to moral knowledge and moral behavior: how we come to know that cruelty is wrong, and whether we act on what we know. The second belongs to what philosophers call moral ontology: what right and wrong actually are, and what in the structure of reality makes cruelty wrong rather than merely unpopular. The moral argument for God, in the form serious people make it, says nothing against the atheist on the first question. It grants that he knows the good and often does it better than the believer. Its question is whether his picture of the world has room for the thing he knows.

The modern world was built on this distinction, somewhat nervously. While the Universal Declaration of Human Rights was being drafted, UNESCO canvassed thinkers from rival traditions about the philosophical basis of the proposed rights. Jacques Maritain, the Catholic philosopher who wrote the introduction to the volume that collected their replies, told a story there with wry candor. At a UNESCO discussion of human rights, someone expressed astonishment that champions of fiercely opposed ideologies had agreed on a list of rights, and the answer came back that yes, they agreed on the rights, on condition that no one asked them why. The Declaration, adopted in December 1948, opens by naming "recognition of the inherent dignity and of the equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family" as "the foundation of freedom, justice and peace in the world." Recognition is the right word. The document recognizes a dignity it declines to ground. That was wise diplomacy in a world just out of the camps, but it is a truce between moral knowledge and moral grounding, and a truce is not a house anyone can live in forever.

So press the question gently. When you say that gratuitous cruelty is wrong, you don't mean you dislike it the way you dislike a certain food. You mean it would be wrong if a whole society approved it, and the way we honor reformers proves that you mean it. When the abolitionists called slavery evil while the law of their country protected it, they weren't proposing a better contract. They were saying the contract itself stood condemned by something higher, and we remember them as right and the law as wrong.

I had three answers to this when I was an unbeliever, and I used them in rotation, depending on who was asking. Cooperation outbred selfishness, so the bands that guarded their children left more children. The revulsion I felt at cruelty was equipment, installed by that same pressure and no more chosen than my fear of heights. And underneath both, if I'm honest, it simply felt wrong, and I thought that feeling it strongly enough was the same as its being so. All three are true as far as they go, and they are good explanations of why I flinch. What I could never get any of them to do was tell me the cruelty was still wrong when I wasn't in the room to flinch at it.

### Plato's dilemma and the door it hides

At this point the believer usually reaches for God, and the skeptic has a reply older than the church. Plato set it down in the *Euthyphro*, a short dialogue set in 399 BC outside the Athenian court where Socrates was about to answer the charge of impiety that would end in his death. He meets Euthyphro, a man on his way to prosecute his own father for the death of a laborer, and very sure that he knows what holiness requires. Socrates asks him a question that has never gone away: is the holy loved by the gods because it is holy, or is it holy because the gods love it?

Put into the terms of biblical monotheism, the question becomes this. Is a thing good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Both doors seem to open onto a cliff. If a thing is good only because God commands it, then goodness is arbitrary. Had God commanded cruelty, cruelty would have been virtue, and the confession that God is good would mean nothing more than that God does what God says. But if God commands a thing because it is already good, then goodness stands above God as a standard he consults rather than authors, and you have found your objective morality without needing him at all. Either God is arbitrary or God is redundant. I sprang that trap on believers with real satisfaction, and I thought it ended the conversation.

The first horn has had brave defenders. Divine command theory, in its strict form, says that moral obligation simply is what God commands, and that asking for a standard behind the command is asking God to submit to something other than himself. Its defenders want to protect the sovereignty of God, which is not an unworthy thing to want. But the strict form pays for that protection with the meaning of the word *good*. If God's goodness is only the fact that he does whatever he wills, praising him for it is like praising a line for being as long as itself.

The older Christian answer never took either horn. Thomas Aquinas, writing the *Summa Theologiae* between 1265 and 1274, placed the moral law inside a larger order. The eternal law is God's own wisdom governing all things. The natural law is the rational creature's share in that eternal law, which is why its first precept, that good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided, can be known by anyone who reasons, baptized or not. On this account goodness isn't a decree that might have gone the other way, and it isn't a code God peers up at. It is written into the natures of things because those natures come from a wisdom that is itself good. Natural law explains something the strict command theory can't: why pagans in Athens and Rome reasoned their way to so much moral truth without Sinai.

Robert Merrihew Adams gave the modern form of the answer in *Finite and Infinite Goods* (1999), and his central move is a distinction. He separates the good from the obligatory. The good, Adams argues, is God himself, the standard of excellence in person, and finite things are good insofar as they resemble or image that goodness. Obligation is a different thing. It arises, as it does among human beings, within a relationship, as the demand of one person on another, and our obligations are constituted by the commands of a God who is loving and just. That distinction cuts the dilemma at the joint. God's commands are not arbitrary, because they flow from a character that was never chosen and could not be otherwise. And goodness does not stand above God, because goodness is what God is. Ask why God couldn't command gratuitous cruelty, and the answer is not that a higher law forbids him. The command would contradict his own nature.

Scripture had spoken this way long before the philosophers. "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2, ESV). The command has no free-standing rulebook behind it. It points to a person. When a man knelt before Jesus and called him "Good Teacher," Jesus answered, "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18, ESV). He located goodness neither above God nor beneath him. He identified it with God, "the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (James 1:17, ESV).

The skeptic can fairly ask whether the believer has only moved the question back a step. Why should God's nature be the standard? But every account of morality stops somewhere, and the question is whether it stops at the kind of thing that could serve as a standard. For years I assumed this third door was a dodge. It isn't. It doesn't prove that God exists, and no honest defender says it does. What it shows is that the *Euthyphro* refutes a caricature of Christian ethics, not the thing itself.

### The secular answers at full strength

The skeptic doesn't need the *Euthyphro* to make his case, and the serious secular accounts of morality are not the cartoons Christians sometimes debate. Four deserve to be heard in the form their own defenders would sign.

The first and most dignified is Immanuel Kant's. In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Kant argued that the moral law is given by reason itself. Act only on a principle you could will to be a universal law. Treat humanity, in your own person and in every other, always as an end and never merely as a means. On this view obligation needs no divine command, and Kant thought that doing right from fear of divine punishment would spoil the moral worth of the act. The law has authority because it is the law of our own rational nature, and much of what the modern West believes about the dignity of persons runs through this idea.

Yet Kant himself did not think morality could finally stand without God. In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) he argued that the moral law commands us to pursue the highest good, virtue crowned with the happiness it deserves, and that nothing in the course of nature guarantees that virtue and happiness will ever meet. Reason, he concluded, must therefore postulate God and the immortality of the soul, not as objects of knowledge but as conditions without which the moral life would be commanding the impossible. Kant didn't ground the moral law in God. He did think that a world without God would make the moral law absurd in the end.

The second account is utilitarianism, which Jeremy Bentham set out in 1789 and John Stuart Mill defended in *Utilitarianism* (1863). The right act is the one that produces the greatest happiness and the least suffering, counting everyone's equally. It is a morality of impartial compassion, and it did real work in the world: prison reform, the protection of animals, Mill's own case for the equality of women. Its difficulty was named most honestly by its most careful defender. Henry Sidgwick, in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), could find no argument showing why a rational person must prefer the general happiness over his own when the two collide. He called the problem the dualism of practical reason, and he admitted that without something like a moral government of the world, reason was left divided against itself. The utilitarian can tell you how to pursue the good once you already care about everyone. He cannot, from within the theory, tell you why you must.

Sam Harris's 2010 book on science and values offers a confident recent version of the same instinct. Morality, Harris argues, concerns the well-being of conscious creatures, and well-being is something science can in principle study, much as medicine studies health, a concept loose at the edges and yet objective enough to build a science on. He is right that relativism is absurd and that suffering is not a matter of taste. But the old gap that David Hume noticed in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–1740), the quiet slide from what *is* the case to what *ought* to be done, is still there. That an act increases well-being is a fact. That I am bound to increase it, including someone else's at a cost to my own, is the very thing in question, and Harris takes it as a starting point rather than showing it.

The third account is evolutionary ethics, and it is the one I lived inside. Charles Darwin argued in *The Descent of Man* (1871) that the moral sense grew from social instincts sharpened by intelligence, and the account explains a great deal: why every culture prizes loyalty and punishes betrayal, why we care for kin, why we keep score of favors. Darwin was also honest about what it implied. He suggested that if human beings had been raised under conditions like those of hive-bees, the unmarried females would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and no one would think to interfere. Different conditions of survival, in other words, would have produced a different conscience with the same feeling of sacred duty. That explains why we believe what we do about right and wrong. It doesn't yet tell us whether we are correct.

The fourth account is the strongest. The robust moral realist doesn't say morality is a useful fiction or a warm feeling. He says objective moral facts exist and need no God to hold them up, in the way the truths of mathematics need no God. Two and two make four in every possible world, and gratuitous cruelty is wrong in every possible world. Derek Parfit gave the last years of his life to this in *On What Matters* (2011), arguing that there are normative truths, discovered rather than invented, binding on every rational mind. Erik Wielenberg makes the case squarely as an atheist in *Robust Ethics* (2014): moral facts are brute and necessary, and they need no foundation outside themselves because they are the foundation. This is a serious metaphysics, and it grants the reality of right and wrong while denying that God is needed to secure it. If you are going to reject the moral argument, reject it here, where it is strong.

### What the strongest answer costs

So press it where it's strong, and press honestly. It carries two costs, and in each case the bill was drawn up by a philosopher with no interest in defending God.

J. L. Mackie, in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), called objective values queer. They would be entities unlike anything else in the universe, facts that merely by existing require something of you, and knowing them would take a faculty unlike any of our ordinary ways of knowing. Mackie concluded they don't exist: our moral talk aims at objective truth and misses, because there is none to hit. That is a coherent place to stand. What is harder for the secular realist is the admission Mackie made in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982), that objective moral values, if there were any, would make the existence of God more probable than it would otherwise be. The theist and the error theorist agree on the conditional and part ways at the conclusion. The robust realist keeps the strange facts and refuses the frame that would make them less strange. He is entitled to. But he is carrying a stranger cargo than he usually admits.

The second cost is sharper. Grant that the moral facts are out there, timeless and binding. How did an animal come to know them? Sharon Street pressed the point in a 2006 essay, "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value." Natural selection shaped our moral dispositions by rewarding whatever helped our ancestors survive and reproduce. The realist must say either that selection tracked the independent moral truths, which is poor biology, since fitness explains the dispositions without them, or that it didn't, in which case our moral beliefs match the moral facts only by an extraordinary stroke of luck. Street's conclusion was not theism. She concluded that values are not independent of us at all, and defended a constructivist view on which they are made by the standpoint of valuing creatures. Darwin's hive-bees stand behind her argument.

Wielenberg's reply is ingenious. He offers what philosophers call a third-factor explanation: the cognitive capacities that lead us to believe persons have rights are the same capacities that make us beings who have rights, so belief and fact share a common cause and their agreement isn't luck. Notice the shape of the answer. It explains the fit between mind and moral fact by pointing to something beneath both. The Christian account has exactly that shape with a different third factor. The one whose character is the good also made the minds that perceive it, made them "in his own image" (Genesis 1:27, ESV). The real question is which account better explains a world in which the moral fact binds and the moral mind reads it. That is a comparison of explanations, not a proof.

One more pressure point remains. A fact that obligates is a strange sort of fact. The truth that a triangle's angles sum to two right angles lays no claim on my will. The truth that I must not betray a friend does, and when I ignore it I don't experience a factual error. I experience guilt, which has the texture of a debt owed to someone. Adams takes that texture at face value: obligation is social by nature, a demand made by someone on someone. The robust realist has a law without a lawgiver and a debt without a creditor. Wielenberg would answer that obligation is itself a brute feature of reality, and the answer is consistent. But the Christian account doesn't have to call the addressed quality of conscience an illusion while asking you to keep obeying it. The summons feels addressed because it is.

### Nietzsche's unpaid bill

This is where I have to stop describing the skeptic and describe myself, because I wasn't consistent, and my inconsistency is the point. I said morality was evolved and useful and felt. I said it with conviction. And then I lived every waking hour as though some things were simply wrong, wrong the way stone is hard, wrong before I felt anything about them at all. When I read of a man who abused a child, I didn't think that my neural equipment was firing. I thought that the act was evil, and I meant it as a report about the man, not about my nerves. I couldn't get my behavior to sit down inside my theory. The theory said preference, and I lived conviction.

The most honest atheist of the nineteenth century would have had no patience with me. In *The Gay Science* (1882), Friedrich Nietzsche imagined a madman running into the marketplace to announce that God is dead, and the people who laugh at him are not believers. They are unbelievers who haven't noticed what their unbelief costs. Seven years later, in *Twilight of the Idols* (1889), he made the charge plainly, aiming it at George Eliot and the English moralists who had given up the Christian God and imagined they could hold all the more firmly to Christian morality. Nietzsche found this incoherent. Christianity, he argued, is a system, a whole view of things thought out together, and if you break out its central concept, faith in God, you break the whole. The equal worth of persons and the duty to the weak were, for him, Christian valuations that would not survive the death of God by default, and he did not want them to survive. Nietzsche was not being cruel. He was being consistent, more consistent than I was, and more consistent than most of the kind unbelievers I have loved. I didn't want him to be right, and wanting had nothing to do with it.

Tom Holland, a historian of Greece and Rome, arrives at Nietzsche's history from the other side in *Dominion* (2019). His argument is that the assumptions most secular Westerners regard as simply human, that every person has dignity and that the weak and the victim have a claim on the strong, are not the common inheritance of humanity. They are the long afterlife of a scandalous claim, that God had been crucified. Crucifixion was the death Rome reserved for slaves and rebels, designed to humiliate, and the church made a crucified man its Lord. Paul knew exactly how that sounded: "we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23, ESV). He also knew what it overturned: "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27, ESV). Holland's contention is that the West has spent two thousand years absorbing that reversal until it no longer recognizes it as Christian at all. Even the secular critic who condemns the church for failing the weak is judging it by a standard the church taught him.

This cuts at the church before it cuts at anyone else. Having a foundation is not the same as standing on it. In 1845, Baptists in the South left their national mission body over whether a slaveholder could be appointed a missionary, and for decades preachers of my own tradition defended slavery from the pulpit while confessing every doctrine that grounds human dignity in the image of God. The same instrument has to measure every side. A Christianity that uses the moral law as a weapon for its own tribe has forgotten that the law judges its keepers first. A secularism that condemns the faith in the faith's own vocabulary has forgotten where the vocabulary came from. Both are living on an inheritance they don't honor.

### The law written on the heart

The Christian claim, rightly understood, is not that unbelievers can't see the moral law. It is the reverse, and the apostle who made it was the first to concede what this essay began by conceding. In Romans, Paul builds a long indictment from 1:18 to 3:20, and its most surprising turn comes at the start of chapter 2, where he stops accusing pagans and turns on the religious man who has been nodding along: "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things" (Romans 2:1, ESV). Only then does he speak of the Gentiles who never had the law of Moses:

> For when Gentiles, who do not have the law, by nature do what the law requires, they are a law to themselves, even though they do not have the law. They show that the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness, and their conflicting thoughts accuse or even excuse them. (Romans 2:14–15, ESV)

Read in its setting, the passage does two things at once. It grants that people who never had Israel's law *by nature do what the law requires*. Paul concedes pagan virtue without embarrassment, which is why an unbeliever can put a churchgoer to shame in kindness without a single doctrine to his name. And it explains why: the law is written on the heart, and the writing has an author. Moral knowledge is universal because moral grounding is personal. But Paul's purpose isn't to flatter anyone. The conscience that bears witness also accuses, and the argument ends where it was always heading, with Jew and Gentile, moralist and pagan, standing on level ground: "for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23, ESV). The law written on the heart is the reason no one can plead ignorance, beginning with the people who thought they had the law in their pockets.

Read in Paul's frame, then, the moral argument becomes a mirror before it can ever be a weapon. If the good is real enough to bind the atheist, it is real enough to judge the Christian. The man who knelt before Jesus and called him "Good Teacher" had kept the commandments since his youth, and Mark records that "Jesus, looking at him, loved him" (Mark 10:21, ESV). Then Jesus asked him for the one thing he could not give, and the man went away sorrowful. The question "Can you be good?" turns out to have two edges. On the first, the answer is plainly yes: people without faith are good every day, often better than we are. On the second, measured against the one of whom Jesus said "No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18, ESV), the answer is no, and it is no for the believer as much as for the skeptic.

Here the *Euthyphro* comes back one last time, and the answer to it turns out to matter for more than philosophy. An arbitrary God could waive the law by decree, and forgiveness would cost nothing because the law never meant anything. A God who answered to a law above him could not forgive at all; he could only enforce. The God whose own character is the good does neither. He will not pretend the law is nothing, because it is the expression of who he is, and he will not leave us under it, because he is love. Paul says the cross was "to show his righteousness at the present time, so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26, ESV). The standard was not lowered. It was borne, by the one whose nature it expresses.

I'm not going to tell you that you can't be good without God, because you can, and pretending otherwise would be the lie that discredits every true thing standing near it. The question was always quieter and harder than that. The trouble is saying what good *is*, why it holds when no one is feeling it, and how a clever animal on a cooling planet came to be bound by something it didn't invent. I never once resolved that in all the years I argued it. I just went on treating cruelty as though it were genuinely, bindingly wrong, the way you go on treating the ground as solid without auditing it, and the people I knew who believed nothing did exactly the same. We were all standing on something none of us had put there. The Christian claim is that the ground has a name, and a face, and wounds in its hands. You don't have to believe it tonight. But the next time you know, with a certainty no argument can shake, that something is wrong, it's worth asking whose voice you are hearing, and what it would mean if it were also the voice that forgives.
