### What the question is not asking

Begin with the concession, and make it without reservation, because the argument that follows collapses the moment it becomes grudging. You don't need to believe in God to be good. Unbelievers are not, as a class, worse neighbors, worse parents or worse citizens than the people in the pews, and many of them are more honest, more patient and more generous than the Christians who argue with them. When I was an atheist, the decent unbelievers in my life weren't a category in somebody's apologetics lecture. They were the people I actually knew, and if you had told any of them that their kindness required a theological license, they would have laughed, and they would have been right to laugh. If the claim on the table were that people without faith can't behave well, it would be refuted by every ordinary Tuesday.

That was never the claim. Christians have often argued as though it were, and we have earned the contempt that followed. A believer who tells a kind agnostic that she can't really be moral has insulted her, misread his own Bible, and handed her an excellent reason to stop listening. Philosophers keep apart two questions that the popular debate runs together. The first belongs to moral knowledge and moral behavior: how we come to know that cruelty is wrong, and whether we act on what we know. The second belongs to what philosophers call moral ontology, the question of what right and wrong actually are, and what in the structure of reality makes it true that cruelty is wrong rather than merely unpopular. The moral argument for God, in the form serious people make it, says nothing against the atheist on the first question. It grants that he knows the good and often does it better than the believer. Its question is whether his picture of the world has room for the thing he knows.

The distinction isn't a seminar trick. The modern world was built on it, somewhat nervously. While the United Nations was drafting the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted in December 1948, a UNESCO committee canvassed thinkers from rival traditions about the philosophical basis of the rights being proposed. Jacques Maritain, the Catholic philosopher, who took part, later reported the outcome with a wry candor: the participants found they could agree on the list of rights, on condition that no one asked them why. The Declaration's preamble speaks of "recognition of the inherent dignity and of the equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family." Recognition is the right word. The document recognizes a dignity it declines to ground. That was wise diplomacy in a world just out of the camps. But it is a truce between moral knowledge and moral grounding, not a resolution of the question, and a truce is not a house anyone can live in forever.

So press the question gently. When you say that gratuitous cruelty is wrong, you don't mean that you dislike it the way you dislike a certain food. You mean it would be wrong if a whole society approved it. The clearest proof that you mean this is the way we honor reformers. When the abolitionists called slavery evil while the law of their country protected it, they weren't proposing a better contract. They were saying the contract itself stood condemned by something higher than any contract, and we remember them as right and the law as wrong. That judgment only makes sense if moral truth sits somewhere above every society's agreement.

I had three answers to this when I was an unbeliever, and I used them in rotation, depending on who was asking. Cooperation outbred selfishness, so the bands that guarded their children left more children. The revulsion I felt at cruelty was equipment, installed by that same pressure and no more chosen than my fear of heights. And underneath both, if I'm honest, it simply felt wrong, and I thought that feeling it strongly enough was the same as its being so. All three are true as far as they go, and they are good explanations of why I flinch. What I could never get any of them to do was tell me the cruelty was still wrong when I wasn't in the room to flinch at it.

### Plato's dilemma and the door it hides

At this point the believer usually reaches for God, and the skeptic has a reply older than the church. Plato set it down in the *Euthyphro*, a short dialogue set in 399 BC outside the Athenian court where Socrates was about to answer the charge of impiety that would end in his death. He meets Euthyphro, a man on his way to prosecute his own father for the death of a laborer, and very sure that he knows what holiness requires. Socrates asks him a question that has never gone away: is the holy loved by the gods because it is holy, or is it holy because the gods love it?

Put into the terms of biblical monotheism, the question becomes this. Is a thing good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Both doors seem to open onto a cliff. If a thing is good only because God commands it, then goodness is arbitrary. Had God commanded cruelty, cruelty would have been virtue, and the confession that God is good would mean nothing more than that God does what God says. But if God commands a thing because it is already good, then goodness stands above God as a standard he consults rather than authors, and you have found your objective morality without needing him at all. Either God is arbitrary or God is redundant. I sprang that trap on believers with real satisfaction, and I thought it ended the conversation.

The first horn has had brave defenders, and they deserve a fair hearing. Divine command theory, in its strict form, says that moral obligation simply is what God commands, and that asking for a standard behind the command is asking God to submit to something other than himself. Its defenders have wanted above all to protect the freedom and sovereignty of God, which is not an unworthy thing to want. But the strict form pays for that protection with the meaning of the word *good*. If the goodness of God is only the fact that he does whatever he wills, then praising him for it is like praising a line for being as long as itself.

The older Christian answer never took either horn. Thomas Aquinas, writing the *Summa Theologiae* between 1265 and 1274, placed the moral law inside a larger order. The eternal law is God's own wisdom governing all things. The natural law is the rational creature's share in that eternal law, which is why its first precept, that good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided, can be known by anyone who reasons, baptized or not. On this account goodness isn't a decree that might have gone the other way, and it isn't a code God peers up at. It is written into the natures of things because those natures come from a wisdom that is itself good. Natural law explains something the strict command theory can't: why pagans in Athens and Rome reasoned their way to so much moral truth without Sinai.

Robert Merrihew Adams gave the modern form of the answer in *Finite and Infinite Goods* (1999), and his central move is a distinction. He separates the good from the obligatory. The good, Adams argues, is God himself, the standard of excellence in person, and finite things are good insofar as they resemble or image that goodness. Obligation is a different thing. It arises, as it does among human beings, within a relationship, as the demand of one person on another, and our obligations are constituted by the commands of a God who is loving and just. That distinction cuts the dilemma at the joint. God's commands are not arbitrary, because they flow from a character that was never chosen and could not be otherwise. And goodness does not stand above God, because goodness is what God is. Ask why God couldn't command gratuitous cruelty and the answer is not that a higher law forbids him. The command would contradict his own nature, and his nature is the last court of appeal.

Scripture had spoken this way long before the philosophers. "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2, ESV). The command there has no free-standing rulebook behind it. It points to a person. When a man knelt before Jesus and addressed him as "Good Teacher," Jesus answered, "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18, ESV). He didn't locate goodness in a standard above God or in a decree beneath him. He identified it with God. James speaks of "the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (James 1:17, ESV), which is exactly the claim the answer to Euthyphro needs: a goodness that is personal and yet does not waver.

The skeptic has a fair reply here. Hasn't the believer just moved the question back a step? Why should God's nature be the standard? But every account of morality stops somewhere. The question is whether it stops at the kind of thing that could serve as a standard. For years I assumed this third door was a dodge. It isn't one. It doesn't prove that God exists, and no honest defender says it does. What it shows is that the *Euthyphro*, whatever else it does, isn't a refutation of Christian ethics. It refutes a caricature of it.

### The secular answers at full strength

The skeptic doesn't need the *Euthyphro* to make his case, and the serious secular accounts of morality are not the cartoons Christians sometimes debate. Four deserve to be heard in the form their own defenders would sign.

The first and most dignified is Immanuel Kant's. In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Kant argued that the moral law is given by reason itself. Act only on a principle you could will to be a universal law. Treat humanity, in your own person and in every other, always as an end and never merely as a means. On this view obligation needs no divine command, and Kant thought that doing right out of fear of divine punishment would actually spoil the moral worth of the act. The law has authority because it is the law of our own rational nature, and our dignity lies in being creatures who can give it to ourselves. It's hard to overstate how much good this idea has done. Much of what the modern West believes about the dignity of persons runs through Kant.

Yet Kant himself did not think morality could finally stand without God. In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) he argued that the moral law commands us to pursue the highest good, virtue crowned with the happiness it deserves, and that nothing in the course of nature guarantees that virtue and happiness will ever meet. Reason, he concluded, must therefore postulate God and the immortality of the soul, not as objects of knowledge but as conditions without which the moral life would be commanding the impossible. Kant didn't ground the moral law in God. He did think that a world without God would make the moral law absurd in the end.

The second account is utilitarianism, which Jeremy Bentham set out in 1789 and John Stuart Mill defended in *Utilitarianism* (1863). The right act is the one that produces the greatest happiness and the least suffering, counting everyone's equally. It is a morality of impartial compassion, and it did real work in the world: prison reform, the protection of animals, Mill's own case for the equality of women. Its difficulty was named most honestly by its most careful defender. Henry Sidgwick, in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), could find no argument showing why a rational person must prefer the general happiness over his own when the two collide. He called the problem the dualism of practical reason, and he admitted that without something like a moral government of the world, reason was left divided against itself. The utilitarian can tell you how to pursue the good once you already care about everyone. He cannot, from within the theory, tell you why you must.

Sam Harris's 2010 book on science and values offers a recent and confident version of the same instinct. Morality, Harris argues, concerns the well-being of conscious creatures, and facts about well-being are facts that science can in principle study, which means moral questions have right and wrong answers the way medical questions do. He compares well-being to health, a concept loose at the edges and yet objective enough to build a science on. He is right that relativism is absurd, and right that suffering is not a matter of taste. But the old gap that David Hume noticed in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–1740), the quiet slide from what *is* the case to what *ought* to be done, is still there. That an act increases well-being is a fact. That I am bound to increase it, including someone else's at a cost to my own, is the very thing in question, and Harris takes it as a starting point rather than showing it.

The third account is evolutionary ethics, and it is the one I lived inside. Charles Darwin argued in *The Descent of Man* (1871) that the moral sense grew from social instincts sharpened by intelligence, and the account explains a great deal: why every human culture prizes loyalty and punishes betrayal, why we care for kin, why we keep score of favors. Darwin was also honest about what his account implied. He suggested that if human beings had been raised under conditions like those of hive-bees, the unmarried females would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and no one would think to interfere. His point was that the content of conscience is shaped by the conditions of survival, and that different conditions would have produced a different conscience with the same feeling of sacred duty. That is an explanation of why we believe what we do about right and wrong. It is not yet an account of whether we are correct.

The fourth account is the strongest, and it is the one a Christian should take most seriously. The robust moral realist doesn't say morality is a useful fiction or a warm feeling. He says objective moral facts exist and need no God to hold them up, in the way the truths of mathematics need no God. Two and two make four in every possible world, and gratuitous cruelty is wrong in every possible world, and both are necessary truths built into reality. Derek Parfit gave the last years of his life to this in *On What Matters* (2011), arguing that there are normative truths as real as any, discovered rather than invented, and binding on every rational mind. Erik Wielenberg makes the case squarely as an atheist in *Robust Ethics* (2014). Moral facts, he argues, are brute and necessary, and they require no foundation outside themselves because they are themselves the foundation. This is not a man ducking the question. It is a serious metaphysics held by serious people, and it grants everything I have said about the reality of right and wrong while denying that God is needed to secure it. If you are going to reject the moral argument, reject it here, where it is strong.
