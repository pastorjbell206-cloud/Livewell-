Sooner or later a Christian child asks it, usually at the table or in the car, and usually without any hostility at all. Why do we believe this when nobody else does? The question is less a rebellion than an observation, and most of the time an accurate one. The friend down the street is kind and funny and doesn't pray. The coach is a good man who has never been inside a church. The favorite teacher treats religion, when it comes up, as a thing some families do. The child has noticed that the faith of the household is not the faith of the world, and the child wants to know what that means.

I have five sons, and that question has come to my own table. I didn't have a good answer the first time, and this essay is partly the answer I wish I had given. Underneath the words is a serious question: how can something be true when almost none of the people he respects seem to need it?

The first honest thing to say to that child is that he's right. Most of the people around us don't believe what we believe. The second honest thing is that this has been true for most of the Christians who have ever lived, and that the Bible was written almost entirely for people in exactly his position. The strange thing isn't that our children are growing up as a minority. The strange thing is that for a few centuries their grandparents didn't have to.

### What the Culture Used to Do for Us

The sociologist Peter Berger gave us the language for what has changed. In *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) he argued that every picture of the world, religious or otherwise, stays believable only as long as it is held up by what he called a plausibility structure: the web of people, institutions and ordinary conversations that treat it as obviously real. Nobody believes alone. For most of Western history the Christian story was held up by a canopy of that kind. The parish, the calendar, the school, the court and the family table all assumed the same story, and a child raised under that canopy absorbed the faith the way he absorbed his language.

Put dates on it. The Edict of Thessalonica in 380 made Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire, and for more than a thousand years afterward to be born in the West was to be baptized into a Christian people. The Reformation after 1517 broke the one canopy into several, but the Peace of Augsburg in 1555, with its principle later summed up as *cuius regio, eius religio*, still assumed that faith was something a whole territory held together. The American founding refused a national church, and the canopy survived informally for nearly two more centuries. Public schools opened with prayer and read the King James Bible. When the Supreme Court ended school-sponsored prayer in *Engel v. Vitale* (1962) and devotional Bible reading in *Abington School District v. Schempp* (1963), it wasn't creating a secular society so much as acknowledging one that had already arrived.

Aaron Renn, in a 2022 essay for *First Things*, offered a rough periodization: a positive world, lasting until about 1994, in which being known as a churchgoer was a modest social asset; a neutral world, from about 1994 to 2014, in which faith was treated as a private preference that cost little either way; and a negative world since, in which Christian moral commitments, especially on sexuality, are more often treated as a liability. The scheme is a sketch, and the positive world was never positive for everyone. Black Christians in the Jim Crow South lived under a canopy that called itself Christian and was used to crush them. But for most white American churchgoers and their children, Renn names something real.

Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) explains why the change goes deeper than law or public opinion. Taylor is interested less in how many people believe than in the conditions of belief, the shift from a society in which it was nearly impossible not to believe in God to one in which belief is one live option among many, and often the harder one to hold. In that setting every conviction becomes what Taylor calls fragilized. Belief is weakened less by the arguments of hostile unbelievers than by the daily presence of decent people who live well without it. The atheist professor of Christian legend is a much smaller threat to a twelve-year-old than the kind friend whose family seems happy and has no use for God.

Lesslie Newbigin, home in Britain in 1974 after nearly four decades as a missionary in India, saw a mechanism that runs straight through the school day. In *Foolishness to the Greeks* (1986) he argued that modern Western culture divides the world into a public realm of facts, which everyone is expected to accept, and a private realm of values and beliefs, where everyone is entitled to his own. A child learns that division long before he could name it. Photosynthesis is taught as a thing we know; the resurrection, if at all, as a thing some people believe. The child doesn't need anyone to attack his faith. He simply learns where to file it, and he files it with the things that are true for you.

None of this means the old canopy was a golden age. It produced genuine saints, and it also produced whole generations for whom Christianity was a citizenship rather than a conversion. In *Soul Searching* (2005), drawing on the National Study of Youth and Religion, Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton named the faith most American teenagers actually held, including many raised in church: moralistic therapeutic deism, the belief that a distant God wants people to be nice and to feel good about themselves and mostly stays out of the way. Smith and Denton found that these teenagers had, for the most part, faithfully inherited what their parents and churches were actually practicing. The canopy had been doing our catechesis for us, and we had mistaken its work for our own. When it came down, it exposed how little the household had been carrying.

The indictment starts with us. My generation of Christian parents, and the pastors who served them, which includes me, grew used to a culture that reinforced what we said on Sunday. We outsourced the formation of our children to the ambient Christianity of the neighborhood and then to programs designed to replace it, and we measured success by attendance. The culture stopped reinforcing us, and we discovered how thin the thing we had been passing on really was.

### The Minority Household Is the Normal One

The panic in many Christian homes assumes that the minority position is a new and unnatural condition. The New Testament assumes the opposite. Every letter in it was written to small communities inside a culture that did not share their faith, and much of what they say about the household assumes homes where only some members believed.

The Roman household had its gods, and its head chose them. Plutarch, writing his *Advice to the Bride and Groom* around the end of the first century, told a new wife that she should worship and know only the gods her husband honored, and keep the door shut against strange rites and foreign superstitions. Christian wives were violating exactly that. Peter writes to women married to unbelieving husbands and tells them that their husbands "may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives" (1 Peter 3:1). Paul writes to Christians married to unbelievers and tells them not to leave on that account, for "the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife, and the unbelieving wife is made holy because of her husband. Otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is, they are holy" (1 Corinthians 7:14). The verse assumes a household divided by faith and refuses to treat its children as lost to God. The early church was full of people practicing a faith their household had not chosen for them.

Timothy was raised in one of those homes. Luke tells us he was "the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer, but his father was a Greek" (Acts 16:1), and Paul, writing near the end of his life, traces Timothy's faith through the women who raised him: "I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well" (2 Timothy 1:5). He adds that "from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (2 Timothy 3:15). The father did not share the faith, and neither did the town. A grandmother and a mother, holding a minority conviction in a mixed home, handed on the Scriptures, and the boy became the man Paul trusted most.

We can see the same pattern from the outside. Around the year 112, Pliny the Younger, the Roman governor of Bithynia and Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan to ask what to do with the Christians being brought before him. Bithynia is one of the provinces Peter addresses. Pliny reported that people of every age and rank, and of both sexes, were being accused, that the movement had spread beyond the cities into villages and farms. He asked whether the very young should be treated differently from the grown, which suggests that children were among those brought before him. The faith was being held, and transmitted, in homes where holding it could cost a life.

A generation or two later the anonymous *Epistle to Diognetus*, written to a pagan inquirer, described Christians as people who live in their own countries as resident foreigners, sharing everything with their neighbors and enduring everything as strangers. Among its first examples is the household: they marry and have children like everyone else, but they do not cast off their newborns, in an empire where exposing unwanted infants was legal and common. The Christians had no influence to point to, so the writer points to the shape of their homes. The sociologist Rodney Stark, in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996), argued that the church grew through the first three centuries largely along the lines of family and friendship, and that its refusal of infanticide and its treatment of women and marriage gave it a weight the surrounding culture couldn't match. Historians debate his numbers more than that basic picture.

So when a child says nobody else believes this, the Christian parent can tell him the truth with some confidence. You are in the company of Timothy, and of the children in Pliny's courtroom, and of every Christian child in the second century. The minority household isn't a sign that something has gone wrong with the faith. It's the condition the faith was born in.

### Exile Is Not a Siege

A family that realizes it has become a minority tends to reach for one of two pictures of itself: siege or exile. They look alike from a distance, but they produce very different children.

A city under siege lives behind walls, organized around holding out until relief arrives. The children of a besieged city learn that every stranger is a possible spy and that the adults are afraid. Sometimes that fear is warranted. But a siege is a temporary condition by definition. Its logic depends on its ending, in rescue or surrender, and a people who try to live in siege for generations become brittle, suspicious and exhausted.

Exile is something else. The exile lives inside the foreign city, under its laws, among its people. And in the case that defines the word for the Bible, he is there because God sent him. When Nebuchadnezzar carried the leading families of Jerusalem to Babylon in the deportations of 597 and 587 BC, the exiles understandably wanted to hear that it would be over soon. A prophet named Hananiah obliged them. He announced in the temple that within two years God would break Babylon's yoke and bring back the temple vessels (Jeremiah 28:2–4). Jeremiah wrote a letter to the exiles that said the opposite:

> Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, to all the exiles whom I have sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon: Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease. But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare. (Jeremiah 29:4–7)

Much of that letter is about raising children. The first instruction to a people who have lost temple, king and land is to build homes, marry and see their children married. The household is not a retreat from the exile's calling. It is the first instrument of it. And the time frame is deliberately long. "When seventy years are completed for Babylon," the letter continues, "I will visit you" (Jeremiah 29:10). Most of the adults reading the letter would die in Babylon, and their grandchildren would go home. The word translated "welfare" is *shalom*, the full flourishing of a place, and God tells the exiles to pray for the shalom of the empire that had carried them off. They were not to wall themselves off from Babylon. They weren't to become Babylonians either.

Daniel shows what that looks like in a single life. He was taken to Babylon young, and the empire set out to remake him. He was given a new name and three years of training in "the literature and language of the Chaldeans" (Daniel 1:4), and he learned it so well that he served in the court of successive kings. Daniel did not refuse Babylon's learning, or its government, or its work. But at one point he drew a line: "Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food, or with the wine that he drank" (Daniel 1:8). Interpreters still argue about why the food was the line. What matters is that there was one, and that Daniel knew where it was before anyone forced the question. Scripture tells us nothing about the household in Jerusalem that raised him. Whoever they were, they had formed a boy who could be carried off, renamed and educated by the empire, and still be recognizably theirs and God's.

The church's two most common responses to the end of Christendom are both siege mentalities. One is the fortress: seal the home and wait for the culture to collapse or the Lord to return. The other is reconquest: treat the loss of the canopy as a defeat to be reversed by political power. That second impulse is where Christian nationalism comes from, and it has its Hananiahs, prophets who promise that the right election or the right court will restore what was lost within two years. It turns the cross into a flag. But the church has a third failure, of a different kind: the exile who forgets he's in exile, who decides that Babylon's gods are close enough to Israel's, and who quietly subtracts from the faith whatever the city finds embarrassing until nothing is left that could cost anything. Much of progressive Christianity has made that trade, and much of the respectable middle has made it without noticing. Berger argued in *The Sacred Canopy* that religious communities under pluralism face a choice between accommodating themselves to the culture's demands and entrenching behind their own walls to preserve a subculture. Accommodation and entrenchment are the two ordinary exits from exile. Jeremiah's letter refuses both.

I'm not writing from outside any of these. Pastors are as vulnerable as anyone to the siege, because fear fills rooms, and to the slow accommodation, because it keeps them full, and many of us have taken a turn at both.

### The Benedict Option and Its Critics

No recent book has pressed the question of the Christian household harder than Rod Dreher's *The Benedict Option* (2017), and it deserves to be stated in the form its admirers would sign. Dreher took his title from the last page of Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981), which compared our moment to the late Roman Empire, when people of good will turned from propping up the imperium to building local communities in which the moral and intellectual life could survive the dark ages, and closed by saying that we too were waiting for another, very different St. Benedict. Dreher argued that the culture war was lost, that the sexual revolution and the Supreme Court's decision in *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) marked the end of any Christian claim on American public life, and that Christians who wanted Christian grandchildren had to build thick communities of practice on the pattern of the Rule of St. Benedict, with its ordered prayer, stability, work and hospitality. He commended classical Christian schools and homeschooling, tight congregations, limits on children's technology, and what the Czech dissident Václav Benda called a "parallel polis," the underground institutions that let dissidents live truthfully under communism.

The strongest form of Dreher's case is sociological, and it's hard to answer. If Berger is right that belief depends on a plausibility structure, then a household cannot hold a faith against the culture by itself. A child spends seven hours a day in a school, several more in front of screens, and a few minutes a week in family prayer. Everything else argues, gently and constantly, with what we say at the table. On this account, withdrawal isn't cowardice. It's realism about how formation works, and it's what every minority community that has survived has done in some form. Orthodox Jewish families have kept a demanding faith for generations in secular cities by building dense communities with their own schools, calendars and food. Dreher's critics, he could fairly say, want the fruit of thick community without paying its price.

His critics have their own case, and it deserves the same care. Some argued that the book confused the loss of Christian political influence with the loss of Christian faithfulness, and that its sense of emergency tracked the fortunes of one political coalition closely enough to look like partisan grief. Others pointed out that the strategies it commends, from classical schools to rural intentional communities, are far more available to prosperous families than to a single mother working two jobs, and that a Christianity for those who can afford to withdraw is a strange Christianity. And the deepest objection is theological. The church has always been tempted to seek purity by distance, and the New Testament keeps sending it back into the city. Jesus prayed not that his disciples would be taken out of the world but that they would be kept from the evil one (John 17:15). A child raised entirely inside a Christian enclave may keep the faith and never learn to love a neighbor who doesn't share it, and a child who has only ever heard the objections to Christianity from Christians will meet them for the first time at nineteen, from someone kind, and be defenseless. Dreher insisted he wasn't counseling a flight to the hills, and it's fair to read him that way, though much of the book's reception heard exactly that.

James Davison Hunter's *To Change the World* (2010), written before Dreher's book, gives the argument a frame. Hunter described three postures American Christians have taken toward the culture, defensive against it, relevant to it and pure from it, and argued that each is more captive to the culture's own assumptions about power than its adherents realize. His alternative, faithful presence within, is close to Jeremiah's letter.

Where the essay lands is this. Whether a family homeschools, chooses a Christian school or sends its children to the public school down the street is a prudential question, and faithful families reach different answers for good reasons. What this essay rejects is the sealed home, the attempt to protect a child's faith by ensuring he never meets a serious alternative to it. That strategy mistakes a siege for a vocation, and its children tend either to shatter at first real contact with the world or to leave with a bitterness proportioned to the walls. Any schooling choice can be made as siege or as exile. The posture matters more than the choice.

But the strongest objection deserves to stand. Dreher would answer that faithful presence, without thick community to sustain it, is usually just assimilation with better manners, and the last fifty years of American church life give him a good deal of evidence. A family that sends its children into the city without a plausibility structure strong enough to hold them is practicing wishful thinking more than exile. That objection hasn't been fully answered here. The nearest thing to an answer is that Jeremiah wrote to a people, not to a family. The exile needs a congregation as well as a home, and no household can be that congregation by itself.

### Every Household Keeps a Liturgy

If the congregation is the exile's wider plausibility structure, the household is its daily one, and James K. A. Smith helps explain why. In *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) Smith argues that human beings are lovers before they are thinkers, liturgical creatures whose deepest loves are shaped less by the ideas we hold than by the practices we repeat. His best-known example is the shopping mall, which teaches a whole vision of the good life without making an argument. The implication for parents is uncomfortable. Every household already keeps a liturgy. The Sunday tournament that outranks worship, the phone at the table, the anxious years of building a résumé for the right college: these are catechisms, and they teach a child what his parents actually love more reliably than anything the parents say. I can't write that sentence from a safe distance. Pastors' homes keep these liturgies too.

Daniel's story turns on a small phrase. When a decree made prayer to anyone but the king a capital crime for thirty days, Daniel "went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously" (Daniel 6:10). As he had done previously. The lions' den revealed what ordinary days had already formed. Christian Smith and Amy Adamczyk, in *Handing Down the Faith* (2021), found that parents are the most important influence on whether children carry a religious faith into adulthood, and that it passes most reliably in homes where warmth and clear expectations go together and faith is part of ordinary conversation rather than a Sunday compartment.

So exile, in a household, looks mostly unremarkable. It looks like a Lord's Day kept even when it costs something the child wanted. It looks like parents who pray aloud in plain words, who confess their own sins to their children and ask forgiveness, and who tell the truth about the church's failures before a critic does. It looks like a table where the unbelieving neighbor is a welcome guest, and where the children never hear that neighbor spoken of as the enemy, because they are watching their parents seek the shalom of the city. And it looks like a home where the child's question, why do we believe this when nobody else does, is received as the beginning of a conversation rather than a threat.

### Elect Exiles

Peter opens his letter to the Christians of Bithynia and the neighboring provinces by calling them "elect exiles" (1 Peter 1:1), and he tells them what that name requires: "Beloved, I urge you as sojourners and exiles to abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul. Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:11–12). Both halves are there, the refusal and the presence, the king's food declined and the city's welfare sought. Peter assumes the neighbors are watching and will misunderstand, and he tells the exiles to live in a way that can't finally be explained without God.

Berger made a further observation in *The Heretical Imperative* (1979). Modernity, he argued, turns fate into choice. Where a medieval child inherited his religion like his native tongue, a modern child has to choose, and the old word for choosing, *hairesis*, is where we get "heresy." Our children will choose. No household can prevent it, and Christian parents should grieve that less than we do, because a faith that was only ever fate was never quite the faith of the New Testament. I came to faith from atheism, and that taught me that God isn't confined to the children of Christian homes. That doesn't lift the parent's duty. It lifts the parent's illusion that he can guarantee the outcome.

The letter to the Hebrews gives exile its center. "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood. Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured. For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Hebrews 13:12–14). The exile household lives inside the city and seeks its good, but its Lord was crucified outside that city's gate, and its hope is fixed on a city still to come. Christendom let us forget the gate. Many of us, if we're honest, loved the canopy more than we loved him and mistook its shelter for his. The end of Christendom is a summons to repent of that, and to go out to him again, bearing the reproach he bore.

The child will ask again, at the table or in the car. Why do we believe this when nobody else does? The honest answer is that we're exiles, that God sent us here, and that he has asked us to seek the good of a city that doesn't share our hope. That answer won't settle the question for him. It isn't supposed to. Whether it rings true will depend on what he has watched in our house on ordinary days, long before he thought to ask.
