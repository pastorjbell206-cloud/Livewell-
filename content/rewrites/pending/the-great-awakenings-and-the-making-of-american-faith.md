### The faith you were handed has a history

Ask an American evangelical when she became a Christian and there's a fair chance she can give you a date. A summer camp when she was twelve, a folding chair at the back of a gymnasium, the Sunday she finally walked the aisle while the congregation sang the last verse one more time. Ask how she chose her church and she may tell you how the worship moved her. None of this sounds strange. It feels like the shape of the faith itself: a personal decision, a felt encounter, a congregation chosen by the believer rather than a congregation that received a child at the font and raised her. But very little of it would have been recognizable to most Christians in most centuries, and none of it was inevitable. It has a history, and the largest part of that history runs through two waves of revival that historians call the First and Second Great Awakenings.

The textbook answer to "what was the Great Awakening" goes like this. The First Great Awakening was a series of Protestant revivals that swept the British colonies in the 1730s and 1740s, carried by Jonathan Edwards in New England and by the itinerant George Whitefield up and down the seaboard. The Second Great Awakening was a longer and looser season of revivals, running from roughly the 1790s into the 1840s, from the camp meetings of the Kentucky frontier to the town campaigns of Charles Finney, and it made the Methodists and Baptists the two largest families of Protestants in the country. All of that is correct, and thin. It tells you what happened without telling you what the revivals did to the meaning of being a Christian in America, and that second question matters because every congregation in the country, including the one I pastor, worships somewhere downstream of the answer.

One caveat belongs at the front, since honest history begins with what's contested. The name itself came later. Joseph Tracy gave the colonial revivals the title that stuck when he published *The Great Awakening* in 1842, a full century after the events. In 1982 the historian Jon Butler went further, arguing in the *Journal of American History* that "the Great Awakening," understood as a single, unified, colony-wide event, was an interpretative fiction: the revivals were scattered, regional and brief, and later writers stitched them into a national awakening that could be credited with preparing the ground for the Revolution. Butler turns out to be more useful to believers than he first appears, and we'll come back to him. For now it's enough to say that the revivals were real and that the story we tell about them was assembled afterward. Both are true.

### The First Great Awakening: Northampton, the open field and Enfield

The first fire caught in Northampton, Massachusetts, a river town in the Connecticut Valley, in the winter of 1734 and 1735. Its pastor, Jonathan Edwards, had succeeded his grandfather Solomon Stoddard in that pulpit and would become the most formidable theological mind colonial America produced. After a series of sermons on justification by faith, the town changed. Edwards counted converts in the hundreds, the young people gave up the frolics he had preached against, and by the following spring, he wrote, the town seemed to be full of the presence of God. He set it all down in a long letter that became a book, *A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God*, published in London in 1737 and read on both sides of the Atlantic. The word to notice is *surprising*. Edwards had preached as he always preached. He hadn't engineered the season and didn't claim to understand its timing. He reported it the way a farmer reports rain.

He also reported its ending, which admirers tend to skip. In 1735 his uncle, Joseph Hawley, sank into religious despair and took his own life, and Edwards records that after this the work in the town visibly declined. He didn't hide the death or explain it away. That honesty would matter a decade later, when he had to decide what a revival actually proved.

The wider fire came with George Whitefield. He was an Anglican priest in his mid-twenties, and when he arrived in the colonies in 1739 he did what the established churches considered close to indecent. He preached outdoors, to anyone, without regard to parish or denomination, and he preached like an actor. Harry Stout argues in *The Divine Dramatist* (1991) that Whitefield's genius was theatrical: he carried the techniques of the London stage into the pulpit, weeping, impersonating biblical characters, turning the sermon into a performance a crowd could not look away from. Frank Lambert shows in *Inventing the "Great Awakening"* (1999) how deliberately Whitefield and his allies used newspapers, advance notices and printed journals, so that a revival was announced as a great work of God before he arrived in a town and reported as one after he left. So the awakening was partly constructed, as Butler says, but by the revivalists themselves, in real time.

Benjamin Franklin, who was no believer, heard him in Philadelphia and left two telling details. He paced off the distance at which Whitefield could still be heard and reckoned the man could preach to more than thirty thousand people at once. And by his own amused account in the *Autobiography*, having resolved to give nothing to the collection for Whitefield's orphan house in Georgia, he gave his copper, then his silver, and finally emptied his pocket, gold and all. The skeptic's purse opened before his convictions did. It's a comic story, and a warning about what a gifted speaker can do to a crowd whether or not God is in the room.

Edwards's most famous sermon belongs to this second, wider season. On July 8, 1741, as a guest preacher at Enfield, Connecticut, he preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" from Deuteronomy 32:35. The popular memory of that sermon, a ranting preacher frightening rustics, is almost exactly backward. Edwards was not a showman, and the sermon is an argument rather than a tantrum: its burden is that nothing but the sheer mercy of God stands between the sinner and judgment at any given moment, and that this mercy is being offered now. A minister who was present, Stephen Williams, recorded in his diary that the crying out in the meetinghouse grew so loud that Edwards could not finish. The response did not come from technique. It came from people believing, suddenly and all at once, that what they had heard all their lives was true.

The awakening divided the churches almost immediately. In 1740 Gilbert Tennent preached *The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry* at Nottingham, Pennsylvania, warning that unconverted ministers were leading their people to ruin and that hearers were free to go and sit under someone else. The idea was explosive: the individual listener, not the parish or the presbytery, would judge whether a minister was fit to be heard. The Presbyterians split into Old Side and New Side in 1741; in New England the parties were Old Lights and New Lights. The Old Lights found their voice in Charles Chauncy of Boston, whose *Seasonable Thoughts on the State of Religion in New England* (1743) argued that the revival was largely enthusiasm, the old word for a delusion in which people mistake their own passions for the voice of God. They had evidence. In March 1743 the radical itinerant James Davenport led followers at New London, Connecticut, in burning books and fine clothing as idols. The New Lights answered that the Old Lights were defending a dead religion of respectable habit. They had evidence too.

Thomas Kidd's *The Great Awakening* (2007) refuses to let this quarrel collapse into two camps. Kidd distinguishes the moderate evangelicals, Edwards and Whitefield among them, who defended the revivals while policing their excesses, from radicals like Davenport, who carried the logic of immediate experience all the way to separatism and visions, and from the antirevivalists who opposed the whole business. Against Butler, Kidd argues that something real and connected did happen across the colonies and the Atlantic, and that its lasting product was the evangelical movement itself. I think Kidd has the better of the argument. Butler's warning survives it anyway. Every generation of revivalists has told the story of its revival in a way that vindicated its own methods, beginning with the first.

### Edwards's test: the difference between heat and fruit

Edwards spent the rest of the decade on the question the revival forced on him. His mature answer came in *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections*, published in 1746, after the fire had cooled and he had watched some of its converts drift back to where they began. No American has written a better book on telling true religion from its counterfeit.

It fights on two fronts at once. Against the Old Lights, Edwards insists that "true religion, in great part, consists in holy affections." By affections he means something larger than emotion in our thin modern sense: the strong inclinations of the whole self toward or away from something, what the heart loves and what it hates, and a faith that never moves them isn't faith. So he refuses to sneer at the weeping in the meetinghouse. But against the enthusiasts he builds a long catalogue of signs that prove nothing either way: that religious affections are very intense, that they affect the body, that people talk fluently and often about religion, that Scripture texts come suddenly to mind, that the person feels great confidence of his own salvation. Any of these can accompany real grace, and any can be counterfeited or self-produced. His list of true signs builds to the last and weightiest: gracious affections bear their fruit in Christian practice, in a changed and persevering life of obedience that is still there after the meeting has ended.

This is the distinction the whole history turns on. Heat is the intensity of an experience. Fruit is the settled change of a life over time. Revivals produce heat in great quantities, and heat is not nothing, but heat proves nothing about its source. Only fruit does, and fruit can only be seen slowly, in community, by people who know you long enough to watch. "You will recognize them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16, ESV), Jesus said, and he said it in a warning about prophets who look and sound exactly like the real thing. John's first letter says the same in another key: "Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God, for many false prophets have gone out into the world" (1 John 4:1, ESV). Testing takes time. It also takes a church.

Edwards then acted on his own test, and it cost him his pulpit. His grandfather Stoddard had opened the Lord's Supper to baptized people of outwardly moral life, on the theory that the Supper could itself be a converting ordinance. Edwards came to believe that the table should be kept for those who could make a credible profession of faith, which is to say the church should look for fruit before welcoming someone to the table. The town that had once seemed full of the presence of God voted him out in 1750. The irony is exact. The greatest theologian of the First Awakening tried to bind revival to the ordinary discipline of a congregation, and the revived congregation dismissed him for trying.

### The Second Great Awakening: Cane Ridge, Finney and the burned-over district

The Second Great Awakening was a different animal, and the difference was the new republic. After the Revolution the established churches were dismantled state by state (Massachusetts, the last to let go, ended its establishment in 1833), the population poured west beyond the reach of any parish system, and the old educated clergy were too few and too slow for the frontier. Into that open space came revival, no longer as an occasional surprise but as the ordinary means of gathering a church.

Its emblem is Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801. It began as a Presbyterian communion season, days of preaching and preparation around the Lord's Supper, with roots in the Scottish "holy fairs" that Leigh Eric Schmidt traces in *Holy Fairs* (1989). Contemporary estimates of the crowd ran from about ten thousand to more than twenty thousand. Presbyterian, Methodist and Baptist preachers spoke from stumps and wagons at the same time, day and night, and observers recorded the "exercises": people falling as if dead, jerking, crying out, laughing. The most famous camp meeting in American history started as a sacrament of the church and ended as something that no longer needed a church in order to happen.

The people who knew how to use this new world were the Methodists and the Baptists. The Methodists under Francis Asbury sent out circuit riders, mostly young and mostly unschooled, to preach wherever a settlement formed; they gathered converts into weekly classes and rode on. The Baptists raised preachers from their own congregations, farmers who preached on Sunday, with no seminary between the call and the pulpit. In *The Democratization of American Christianity* (1989), Nathan Hatch argues that between the Revolution and about 1830 American religion was remade by populist movements (Methodists, Baptists, the restorationist Christians, the Black churches and the early Mormons) that took religious authority from the educated clergy and handed it to ordinary people, their experience, their own preachers and their own reading of the Bible. By midcentury the Methodists, marginal in 1776, and the Baptists were the two largest Protestant bodies in the nation. Hatch doesn't tell this as a simple fall; the democratization gave dignity and voice to people the old establishment never bothered to reach. But it also taught Americans to trust the populist preacher over the learned one, the fresh reading over the tradition, and the individual's experience over the church's judgment.

Mark Noll's *America's God* (2002) describes the theology that grew in that soil. By the early nineteenth century, Noll argues, American Protestantism had fused with the republican ideology of the Revolution, with its suspicion of inherited authority, and with a commonsense moral philosophy that trusted every person's ordinary judgment to read the world, and the Bible, plainly. The fusion Christianized much of a young nation. It also left American Christians assuming that any sincere reader of Scripture would arrive at the same conclusions, and that assumption was about to break.

Then came Charles Finney, a lawyer converted in 1821, whose campaigns through the towns of western New York, culminating at Rochester in 1830 and 1831, made him the most influential revivalist of his century. He brought the "new measures": protracted meetings running night after night, the "anxious bench" at the front where those under conviction sat to be prayed over and pressed, public prayer for sinners by name, and above all the demand for an immediate decision. In *Lectures on Revivals of Religion* (1835) he gave the theory in a sentence: "A revival is not a miracle, nor dependent on a miracle, in any sense. It is a purely philosophical result of the right use of the constituted means." Set that beside the title of Edwards's book a century earlier. Edwards called the revival surprising. Finney called it a result.

His theology matched his method. Finney rejected the inherited doctrine of original sin as a corruption of nature, taught that sinners had full natural ability to repent at once, and treated the sinner's own refusal as the only real obstacle. If that's true, the preacher's task is to remove every excuse and press for the decision, and a revival that doesn't come is a failure of means.

The region Finney worked became known as the burned-over district, swept by revival so often that nothing was left to burn. Whitney Cross's *The Burned-over District* (1950) shows what else grew in that scorched ground: Joseph Smith's *Book of Mormon*, published at Palmyra in 1830; William Miller's movement predicting Christ's return, which ended in the Great Disappointment of October 1844; John Humphrey Noyes's perfectionist community at Oneida. None of it can simply be laid at Finney's feet, but it shares his premise. The individual, freed from the discipline of a tradition, reaches for a direct and immediate experience of God, and a movement forms around whoever seems to have it.

### Revival, reform and slavery

A fair account has to give the Second Awakening its due, and its due is large. The revivals that produced the anxious bench also produced Bible and tract societies, Sunday schools, missions and colleges. The American Temperance Society was founded in 1826. Theodore Dwight Weld, converted under Finney, became one of the most effective abolitionist organizers in the country. When the trustees of Lane Seminary in Cincinnati moved to silence student abolitionists after the debates of 1834, many of the students left for Oberlin, which admitted Black students and women and where Finney taught and later served as president. The revivalist conviction that a sinner could and must repent immediately became the abolitionist conviction that the nation could and must end slavery immediately. Turned against the nation's great sin, the logic of decision became a prophetic weapon.

Yet the same revival culture made its peace with slavery in the South. The Methodists had once officially opposed slaveholding among their members, then softened and dropped the demand as the price of preaching to planters. When the question finally broke the churches, it broke them along the lines of revival's own success. The Methodists divided in 1844, and in 1845 the Southern Baptist Convention was organized at Augusta, Georgia, after the national Baptist mission board refused to appoint slaveholders as missionaries. Noll's point came due. Both sides believed any sincere reader could see what the Bible said, both read it and saw opposite things, and the democratized Protestantism the awakenings had built had no court beyond the individual reader to settle the matter. The war settled it instead.

The awakenings also had a Black history of their own, and it isn't a footnote. Enslaved and free Black Americans came to faith in large numbers, overwhelmingly in the Baptist and Methodist forms, which offered a conversion that didn't depend on literacy or rank, preachers who could rise from among them, and worship in which their own voices counted. Albert Raboteau's *Slave Religion* (1978) describes the "invisible institution," the hidden gatherings in brush arbors and cabins where enslaved people heard the Exodus as their own story and found in the crucified Christ a Lord who had suffered what they suffered. The preaching that masters hoped would make the enslaved docile was heard by the enslaved as a word that judged the masters.

When white churches refused Black members as equals, Black Christians built their own. Richard Allen, born enslaved in 1760, was converted under Methodist preaching and bought his freedom. In Philadelphia he, Absalom Jones and others walked out of St. George's Methodist Church after Jones was pulled up from his knees during prayer for kneeling in the wrong part of the gallery. Allen founded Bethel Church in 1794, and in 1816 he gathered Black Methodist congregations from several cities into the African Methodist Episcopal Church and became its first bishop. It was the first fully independent Black denomination in the country. In one sense the AME Church is the purest fruit of the awakenings: a church of people converted by revival preaching who refused to let the preachers' own churches define them as less than members of Christ's body. In another it is the heaviest evidence against the white revival churches, because the fruit Edwards told us to look for, a new and lasting love for God and neighbor, was so often missing exactly where the neighbor was Black.

### What revivalism gave, and what it cost

The strongest case for the revivals should be made in the form their defenders would sign. Before the First Awakening, a great many colonists had been baptized and catechized without ever believing a word of it. Revival insisted that faith must be personally received, and that insistence is biblical. "You must be born again" (John 3:7, ESV) was said to Nicodemus, a teacher of Israel with every religious advantage. At Pentecost about three thousand were baptized in a single day (Acts 2:41). The revivals reached people no settled parish would have reached, the frontier family, the poor, the enslaved, and there are people alive in Christ today because a revival preacher pressed them for a decision no respectable church would have asked of them. Whoever sneers at the tent should count its fruit first.

But read the next verse. "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42, ESV). The three thousand didn't go home with a date to remember. They were folded into a people who taught them, fed them at a table and prayed with them, day after day. The commission binds the two just as tightly: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" (Matthew 28:19–20, ESV). Baptizing and teaching, the moment and the long formation. Revivalism at its weakest kept the first and let the second go.

John Williamson Nevin saw what was being lost. In *The Anxious Bench* (1843) the Mercersburg theologian set two systems side by side. The system of the bench treats conversion as a crisis produced in a meeting by pressure on the will. The system of the catechism treats it as the fruit of ordinary church life, baptism and instruction and preaching and sacrament and household prayer, a person formed into Christ over years by a community that received the faith and hands it on. Nevin didn't deny that God converts people suddenly. He denied that sudden conversion could become the normal pattern of a church's life without hollowing the church out. Jesus had already named the risk. The seed on rocky ground belongs to "the ones who, when they hear the word, immediately receive it with joy. And they have no root in themselves, but endure for a while" (Mark 4:16–17, ESV). Immediate joy is the very thing a revival meeting counts, and Jesus names it as the sign of shallow soil. He doesn't despise it; the seed is the true word and the joy is real. But the harvest is measured in roots.

Iain Murray's *Revival and Revivalism* (1994) gives the distinction its name. Revival, in Edwards's sense, is a work of God that the church receives and then must test. Revivalism is the belief that such a work can be reliably produced by the right methods. "The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit" (John 3:8, ESV). Revivalism assumes we do know, and can schedule it.

Two costs follow, and both are ours. The first is conversion without catechesis. If the decision is the thing, then whatever comes after is follow-up, and a church can grow by counting decisions while its people never learn the creed, the story of Scripture or the shape of a holy life. The second is the individual over the church. If the Spirit's work is known chiefly by what I feel, I become its final judge, and a congregation becomes a place I attend while it helps me. Tennent's hearer, free to leave for a converted minister, becomes the church shopper, and Finney's constituted means run forward into the altar call, the stadium crusade and the service designed by audience research to clear every obstacle before the decision. We didn't stumble into consumer Christianity. We built much of it in the revival tent, with the best intentions in the world. And the inheritance doesn't belong to one side. Heirs on the right kept Finney's decisionism and dropped his reform; heirs on the left kept his confidence that the right means could perfect society and dropped the conversion. Both kept the premise that faith is what the individual chooses and does. Neither kept the catechism.

### Heat, fruit and the church that tests them

I can't write any of this from outside. I preach for conversions, I want the room to feel something, and I know how to make it happen: the music under the appeal, the pause, the story, the lowered voice. I have at times mistaken the warmth I produced for the work only God does, and counted a Sunday's response the way a shopkeeper counts a till. I came to faith from atheism, and what reached me was no atmosphere anyone engineered. It was truth that would not move. I should know better than anyone. I still reach for the dials.

This is where Butler turns out to be a mercy. His reminder that the Great Awakening was partly a story told afterward unsettles anyone who wants revival to prove itself, but it is Edwards's point in a historian's voice: every revival is interpreted, the interpreter has interests, and the Spirit's real work can be counterfeited. So it has to be tested, over time, by a church that knows the people it is testing.

The way forward begins where Jesus's own preaching began: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ESV). For churches like mine that means repenting of counting people as results, and of handing new believers a date and a card instead of a people and a catechism. For churches that watched the revivals from a safe liturgical distance, it means repenting of a faith that never expected God to do anything at all. Christ promised, "And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself" (John 12:32, ESV). A church that believes him can stop manufacturing the drawing and give itself to the slower work that follows it.

We live in both halves of the awakenings' inheritance, the fire and the hearth, the date of a decision and the decades that test it. Edwards asked his own town for fruit before the table, and the town dismissed him for asking. The question now stands at the end of every service that closes with an invitation, mine included, and it can't be answered that night. The fire will be felt in the room. What it grew will only be known by the people who stay long enough to see.
