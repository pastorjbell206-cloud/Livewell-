There is a moment many Christian parents know and few admit to. It can come at a child's baptism or dedication, when the pastor asks whether you will raise this child in the faith, you say that you will, and some quiet part of you wonders what exactly you have promised to hand over. Or it comes on an ordinary weeknight, praying before dinner to a God who has felt, for months, like an empty chair at the table. You believe, mostly. You also doubt, some seasons more than mostly. And a child is taking from you, whether you intend it or not, some picture of what faith is and what it costs.

The fear underneath is specific. You assume the faith passes down through the parent's certainty, the way water moves through a pipe, and you can feel the cracks in the pipe. So you perform a confidence you don't have, and lie awake afterward feeling like a fraud. Or you conclude it would be more honest to hand on nothing and let the children find their own way, and you feel, less sharply but for much longer, that something has been withheld.

This essay is not first about your children's questions. It is about yours: what a parent with real uncertainty owes a child, and what exactly gets handed on. A parent can carry the faith in front of a child in three ways. Performed certainty teaches the child that faith is theater and that adults keep the real conversation offstage. Contempt, the knowing half-smile at the tradition, teaches that faith is something serious people outgrow. The third is honest uncertainty held inside the tradition, and it is the posture Scripture actually models. **What a Christian parent hands on was never their own certainty. It is what they received, delivered by hands that sometimes tremble, and the faith has been passed down that way from the beginning.**

### A Father Who Could Only Half Believe

The most honest prayer in the Gospels was prayed by a parent. Mark sets the scene with care (Mark 9:14–29). Jesus comes down from the mountain of the transfiguration and walks into an argument: a crowd, some scribes, his own disciples, and a father whose son has been seized since childhood by a spirit that throws him into fire and water. The father had brought the boy to the disciples, and they could not help him. By the time he stands in front of Jesus, his hope has worn down to a conditional clause: "But if you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us" (Mark 9:22).

Jesus takes hold of the phrase and hands it back to him. "'If you can'! All things are possible for one who believes" (Mark 9:23). And the father answers with a sentence that has kept more believers inside the church than any argument ever devised: "Immediately the father of the child cried out and said, 'I believe; help my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:24).

He does not produce the faith Jesus has just described, because he doesn't have it and is too desperate to pretend. Nor does he walk away because his faith falls short. He confesses belief and unbelief in the same breath and hands the whole divided thing to the only one who can do anything about either. Notice the plural in his first request: "have compassion on *us*." The father cannot separate his own faith from his child's need, and neither can any believing parent. Your faith, however thin, is never only your own business, because someone small is standing inside its weather.

Jesus does not send the man away to come back when his faith has been purified. He speaks to the spirit, and then "Jesus took him by the hand and lifted him up, and he arose" (Mark 9:27). Mark never tells us the father's faith became strong enough, only where it went: to Jesus, divided, asking for help with the division. Later, alone with the disciples, Jesus explains their failure without a word about the father's wavering: "This kind cannot be driven out by anything but prayer" (Mark 9:29). The ones who had tried with confidence had failed. The one who had prayed with half a faith had been heard.

The parent who whispers *I believe; help my unbelief* at a child's bedside is not standing at the edge of the faith. He is standing in one of its oldest places, in the company of a man whose son Jesus healed.

### Why Belief Feels So Fragile Now

Part of what you feel is personal: a grief that never resolved, a prayer that went unanswered, a church that failed you. But part of it is the air. You are doubting in a particular century, and it helps to know what that century has done to belief.

Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) asks how the West moved from a world around 1500 in which it was almost impossible not to believe in God to one in which belief is an option, and often not the easiest one to hold. Taylor's most useful word for parents is *cross-pressured*. Almost no one in a modern society, on his account, believes or disbelieves naively. The believer feels the pull of the closed, self-sufficient world the culture assumes, where everything can be explained without God, and the unbeliever feels the pull of transcendence, the sense that the flattened world leaves something out. Belief has been what Taylor calls *fragilized*, not because believers are weaker than their grandparents but because they live beside neighbors, colleagues and family members who see the world otherwise and are plainly neither stupid nor wicked. The novelist Julian Barnes opened his memoir *Nothing to Be Frightened Of* (2008) with a sentence that captures the other side of the same pressure: "I don't believe in God, but I miss Him."

I came to faith from atheism, and I can say that the pressure runs both ways; the unbeliever is not at rest either. For the believing parent, the point is a relief. The fact that you feel doubt does not, on its own, prove that your faith is failing. It may only prove that you live in this century and are paying attention. The pastor writing this feels the same pressure.

The century has done something subtler as well. It has handed us a false standard for what counts as believing. In 1637, in the *Discourse on Method*, René Descartes resolved to accept nothing as true that he could possibly doubt and to rebuild knowledge on a foundation beyond doubt. The project quietly changed what Western people meant by knowing. Knowledge came to mean certainty no reasonable doubt could touch, and anything less was opinion.

Lesslie Newbigin, the missionary bishop who spent decades in India before turning to the West, argued in *Proper Confidence* (1995) that this standard damaged Christians on both flanks. Some tried to meet it, defending every doctrine as if it could be demonstrated beyond doubt, and built a brittle faith that shatters at the first real crack. Others concluded that since the standard cannot be met, faith must be a private feeling with no claim to truth. Drawing on Michael Polanyi's *Personal Knowledge* (1958), Newbigin argued that the Cartesian ideal describes no real knowledge at all. All knowing, even in the laboratory, involves personal commitment, trust in a tradition of inquiry, and a willingness to act before every doubt is closed. What the Christian is given is a proper confidence, grounded not in the believer's grip on God but in God's faithfulness to the believer.

This is the first release a doubting parent needs. You were never asked to be certain in the way Descartes meant, and no generation of believers ever was. You were asked to trust someone, and trust can live with a great deal of unresolved question, the way a long marriage lives with a great deal of unresolved mystery about the person you married. When you tell a child *I'm not sure about that, but I trust him*, you are describing what faith has always been.

### What Asaph Would Not Say to the Children

Psalm 73 is the testimony of a man who nearly let go. It opens with the creed he was raised on, "Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart" (Psalm 73:1), and at once admits that he almost stopped believing it: "But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped. For I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (Psalm 73:2–3). What follows is a bitter portrait of people who ignore God and flourish anyway, and then the conclusion his experience had been pressing on him: "All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence" (Psalm 73:13). That is no mild wobble. It is a man concluding that his faithfulness has been a waste of his life.

Then comes a verse that could have been written for a parent: "If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children" (Psalm 73:15). Read carelessly, it sounds like an endorsement of performed certainty: keep your doubts away from the children. But Asaph did not hide his doubt forever. It is in the psalm, in detail, and the psalm was sung in Israel's worship for generations by the very children he refused to betray. What he would not hand them was his verdict, the conclusion that it had all been in vain, announced as though it were settled before he had taken it to God. "But when I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task, until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end" (Psalm 73:16–17).

The whole question turns on that distinction. There is doubt as a question you carry to God, and there is doubt as a verdict you pronounce over others. Asaph brought the first into the sanctuary and withheld the second until he had seen what the sanctuary showed him. What he finally handed down contains the entire passage through the dark: the stumbling, the envy, the confession that "I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you" (Psalm 73:22), and then the turn: "Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand" (Psalm 73:23). Israel's children were given the doubt inside the story of where it had been carried. That is a different inheritance from a mask, and a different inheritance from a sneer.

With the psalm in view, the three postures are easier to see. Performed certainty keeps every question out of sight and presents a faith with no seams. It comes from love, and it fails because children eventually meet their own verse 13. When the death of a friend arrives, a child raised on the unbroken front concludes that she is the first believer ever to feel this, and that a faith which never seemed to have room for it was never meant for someone like her. We cut our children off from half the Psalter and then wonder why they have no words for the dark.

Contempt is harder to recognize because it can pass for honesty. It is the parent who is less uncertain than embarrassed, who hands the faith on at arm's length: the eye roll at Genesis, the jokes about church people, attendance kept up while the distance is made visible. Doubt takes a question seriously enough to keep asking it. Contempt has stopped asking and kept the sneer, and children imitate a tone long before they can weigh an argument. They learn from it that the faith isn't worth the trouble of finding out. Contempt comes in two dialects, and both deserve the same grief. One is the sophisticated parent's condescension toward an embarrassing tradition. The other is the defensive parent's contempt for questions themselves, which is, whether he knows it or not, contempt for the Psalms. Both teach a child that the faith cannot survive being looked at directly.

Honest uncertainty sounds different from either. It sounds like Asaph after the sanctuary: *I've wondered about that too. Here's where I took it. Here's what I found there, and here's what I still don't understand.* It sounds like Habakkuk, whose book opens with an accusation: "O LORD, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear?" (Habakkuk 1:2). God's first answer only makes the problem worse, and the prophet does not leave. "I will take my stand at my watchpost and station myself on the tower, and look out to see what he will say to me" (Habakkuk 2:1). And the book ends with a song sung over failed fig trees, empty fields and empty stalls, before anything in the prophet's circumstances has changed: "yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation" (Habakkuk 3:18). A child who watches a parent keep the watchpost learns that waiting on God is something grown people actually do.

Jude's short letter holds both halves of a parent's task. He urges his readers "to contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (Jude 3), and near the close tells the same readers, "And have mercy on those who doubt" (Jude 22). He sees no contradiction between guarding what was delivered and showing mercy to the one who wavers, and a doubting parent may extend that mercy to himself without loosening his hold on the deposit. One caution belongs here. A long winter of doubt sometimes travels with depression, and when the darkness includes not wanting to be alive, it has become more than a theological question. Tell someone you trust, see a doctor, and in the US call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

### Handed Over

When Paul wrote to the Corinthians about the resurrection, he used the vocabulary of a courier. "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:3–5). *Received* and *delivered* were the ordinary words for passing on a tradition, and the noun that belongs to them, *paradosis*, is what the New Testament means by tradition: literally, a handing over. The Jewish teachers spoke the same way; the Mishnah's tractate Avot, compiled around the year 200, opens by saying that Moses received the Torah at Sinai and handed it on to Joshua, and Joshua to the elders, a chain Paul, trained as a Pharisee, would have known well.

What Paul hands over is not his religious feelings or his confidence on a given morning. It is a report: an event, with names and a sequence and witnesses, "most of whom are still alive" (1 Corinthians 15:6), which is to say people a skeptical Corinthian could go and question. And Paul stakes everything on the report rather than on himself: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). The content of the tradition does not rise and fall with the courier's inner weather. That is the second release a doubting parent needs. You are not asked to hand your child the certainty you lack. You are asked to hand on faithfully what you received, to say *this is what the church has confessed since the beginning, this is what I was given, and this is where I stand inside it today.* A trembling hand can still deliver a letter it did not write.

Tradition can also go wrong, and Jesus said so bluntly: "You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8). Much of what feels like doubt about God turns out, on examination, to be doubt about a tradition of men handed down alongside the gospel and mistaken for it: a subculture's rules, one way of reading the first chapters of Genesis, a political identity dressed in the faith, a church's habit of treating every question as rebellion. Some of that deserves to be doubted. Part of honest parenting is sorting the deposit from the packaging, letting go of the second and holding the first more firmly. Call it excavation rather than demolition: the work of digging past what a family or a movement added until you reach the older faith the creeds confess, and building there.

The word has a shadow worth remembering. In the Great Persecution under the emperor Diocletian, beginning in 303, Christians who surrendered their Scriptures to the authorities to be burned were called *traditores*, those who handed over. Handing on the faith and handing it away to its enemies were named by the same Latin word. Every generation of parents hands something to its children; the question is what, and to whom.

The sociologists, working with very different tools, arrive somewhere Paul would recognize. Vern Bengtson's *Families and Faith* (2013) drew on the Longitudinal Study of Generations, begun in 1970, which followed families across as many as four generations. One of its clearest findings was that warmth mattered: children who experienced their parents as warm and affirming were far more likely to take up their parents' faith, and the warmth of fathers carried particular weight. Where parents were cold, harsh, or visibly inconsistent between profession and life, the faith passed down less often. I was raised without a father, and I cannot read that finding without feeling it. Christian Smith and Amy Adamczyk's *Handing Down the Faith* (2021), built on national surveys and long interviews with American parents, concluded that parents are by far the strongest influence on their children's religious lives, well ahead of congregations and youth programs. The pattern most associated with a lasting faith was a parent for whom the faith was plainly important, who talked about it in the ordinary course of the week, joined to parenting that was warm and also held real expectations.

Neither study, so far as I know, measured whether parents told their children about their doubts, and it would be dishonest to claim it did. But neither found that faith passes down through an unbroken front. What they found turned on warmth, seriousness and conversation, and a parent who is honestly struggling can offer all three.

Nor should the parent try to carry this alone. Peter Berger argued in *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) that every view of the world depends on a social base, which he called a plausibility structure: the network of people, conversations and practices that makes a way of seeing seem obviously real. Remove the network and the view grows harder to hold, whatever the arguments. Newbigin took up the idea in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989), noting that the secular West has its own plausibility structure, which forms children just as surely. The church's answer is a congregation that believes the gospel and lives by it, which Newbigin called the only real interpretation of the gospel a watching world will ever see.

For a doubting parent, that is a mercy. You were never meant to be your child's entire plausibility structure, and one tired adult's certainty was never going to hold up the faith for a household. The worst thing a struggling parent can do is withdraw from the church to spare the children the sight of his doubt, because the church is where you borrow the faith of others when your own is thin. It is where your children hear old people sing words you cannot sing this month, and learn that the faith is held up by a people and not by one parent's face.

### The Parent Who Would Rather Not Choose for Them

Some parents have settled on the other path, and their argument deserves its strongest form. A child cannot consent to a religion. Whatever a parent tells a four-year-old about God, the child believes because a parent said it, so religious upbringing works through the one authority a child cannot question. If I am unsure the faith is true, teaching it to my child as true is a lie, however gentle. Many of these parents have also seen religious upbringing at its worst: children taught to fear hell before they could read, shamed for questions, controlled in God's name. Peter Berger gave the instinct a sociological footing in *The Heretical Imperative* (1979). The Greek *hairesis*, from which we get "heresy," first meant choice, and Berger argued that modernity makes heretics of us all in that literal sense: what was once received as fate must now be chosen. If the child will have to choose anyway, why not let her choose from a clean slate?

Much of this should be conceded. A faith installed by fear is not what the New Testament means by faith, and much that passed for Christian upbringing earned the resistance it produced. Where a child has been abused by someone with spiritual authority, that is abuse, not zeal; it should be reported, and nothing in the duty to hand on the faith excuses it. Teaching as certain what you do not believe is dishonest. And Berger is right that our children will choose.

The argument fails at the clean slate. Newbigin argued in *Foolishness to the Greeks* (1986) that the modern West sorts claims into public facts, taught to every child as true, and private values, left to preference. No parent tells a child that whether cruelty is wrong is hers to decide at eighteen. To leave the question of God for later is already to have filed Christianity among preferences, which answers the very question supposedly left open. The faith Paul delivered is a report of something that happened, and declining to pass on a report is not neutrality. A child raised in no faith still learns a view of the world: her parents' working assumption that the question of God is not important enough to be discussed at home.

Blaise Pascal saw this clearly. His *Pensées*, published in 1670 from the notes he left at his death, contain the famous wager, usually retold as a cynical bet for heaven. At its strongest it is an argument about our situation rather than our payoff: reason cannot settle the question by demonstration, yet no one can decline to live, so we are already embarked, and refusing to wager is itself a wager. Pascal also wrote that "the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know," meaning not feeling over thought but the knowing by which we grasp what we cannot prove. He wrote as a man who had met God; the record of his night of fire, 23 November 1654, was found sewn into his coat after his death.

Every household is embarked, and the real choice is between shaping a child openly and shaping her by default. This essay lands on raising children in the faith, honestly: telling them what you believe, what you doubt, and why you stay, and telling them plainly that one day the choice will be theirs. A child can only really weigh a faith she has been allowed to live inside. The strongest objection is not fully answered. A parent's words carry weight a child cannot measure, and even honest teaching uses that weight. The best answer I have is that every parent uses it for something, and it is better used in the open, with warmth, than hidden behind a neutrality that does not exist. Thoughtful people will weigh that differently. If your doubt has hardened into settled unbelief, do not perform a faith you have left. But be sure that what you left was the faith and not a tradition of men, and that you have not mistaken a winter for a death.

### On the Night He Was Handed Over

Paul uses the same pair of verbs once more in that letter, and the second use should stop every parent: "For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread" (1 Corinthians 11:23). In Greek, *delivered* and *betrayed* are the same verb. The tradition Paul hands on is the story of the night Jesus was handed over by a friend to his enemies, and beneath that, by the Father: "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Romans 8:32), where "gave him up" is that verb again. The gospel is a handing over before it is ever a tradition handed on.

Look, too, at who stands at the head of the chain: "he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:5). Cephas is Peter, who days before had sworn he did not know the man. The first named witness in the oldest tradition the church possesses failed at the exact moment it mattered, and all four Gospels tell of his denial. The church has handed down its founding witness's worst night alongside the empty tomb, the failure inside the story of the one who came back for him. That is Asaph's psalm again, on a larger scale.

That means the parent's first work is repentance: for the certainty we performed, the contempt we let show, the tradition of men we handed on as though it were the gospel. Going back to a child and saying so is itself a way of handing on the faith, because it shows the child what a Christian does with failure.

Your children will not inherit your certainty. It was never the inheritance. They will inherit what you received, if you deliver it, and the way you held it: whether you carried your doubts into the sanctuary or pronounced them as verdicts, whether the faith lived in your house as a performance, a joke, or a trust. You may not know for years which of these they took, and some parents will not know in this life. The father in Mark 9 did not receive certainty. He received his son, lifted by the hand of Jesus, which is not the same thing and is better. The next chance will come at a bedside or a kitchen table, and you may meet it with half a faith. Pray that father's prayer over yourself and over the child, and hand on what you received anyway. The one who was handed over for you both did not send that father away.
