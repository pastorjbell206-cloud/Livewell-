### The proof I carried

There is a feeling deep in the modern mind that the question of miracles was settled a long time ago. Nobody quite remembers the hearing, but everyone knows the verdict. The dead stay dead, water stays water, and a grown person who says otherwise has stepped out of the world the rest of us live in. The feeling rarely presents itself as an argument. It presents itself as maturity, which is exactly why it is so hard to examine, because nobody cross-examines their own adulthood.

The feeling has a respectable pedigree inside the church as well as outside it. Around 1820 Thomas Jefferson took a razor to his Gospels, kept the moral teaching, and left the miracles on the floor. In 1941 the German New Testament scholar Rudolf Bultmann argued, in the lecture published that year as "New Testament and Mythology," that a person who uses electric light and the radio and trusts modern medicine cannot honestly go on believing in the New Testament's world of spirits and wonders. Bultmann meant it as a service to faith, a way of keeping the gospel's summons to decision while releasing modern hearers from what he took to be an ancient picture of the world. Jefferson and Bultmann were very different men, but both thought they were rescuing Jesus from embarrassment, and both assumed the embarrassment was settled fact.

I carried that assumption for years, and I carried it in its strongest form. I came to faith from atheism, and while I was an atheist I thought I had a proof. It wasn't a hunch or a mood. It was a proof, borrowed from a Scottish philosopher who had been dead for two centuries, and I remember the particular pleasure of reading it, like hearing a heavy door swing shut. It took me a long time to notice that what I was calling a proof was a definition doing all the work.

This essay won't try to prove that miracles happen. It asks a narrower question, and the honest answer is less comfortable for the skeptic than he usually expects and less comfortable for a good many Christians too. Can a reasonable person believe in miracles, and if he does, what exactly is he believing? The philosopher I borrowed from ended his argument with a sentence meant to close the matter for good: "So that, upon the whole, we may conclude, that the Christian Religion not only was at first attended with miracles, but even at this day cannot be believed by any reasonable person without one." He meant it as irony. Faith, on his account, is so contrary to reason that believing it would itself require a miracle. Keep that sentence in mind. It says more than its author intended.

### Hume's argument at full strength

David Hume published "Of Miracles" in 1748 as the tenth section of the book he later retitled *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*. A version of him that can be knocked over is worth no one's time, so he should be heard as he made the case.

The first part of the section is an argument about evidence. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence." Some beliefs rest on experience that has never once failed, and Hume calls that kind of evidence a proof. Others rest on experience that usually holds but sometimes doesn't, and there we weigh probabilities. Testimony belongs to the second kind. We trust witnesses only because experience has taught us that what people report usually matches what happened, and experience has also taught us that people lie, misremember, exaggerate and see what they want to see. A law of nature belongs to the first kind. Hume puts it this way: "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined." So when someone reports that a dead man walked out of his tomb, the scale holds, on one side, the ordinary and fallible reliability of witnesses, and on the other the unbroken experience of the whole human race that the dead stay dead. Hume's rule follows: "That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish." Before you believe that a man rose, you must find it more astonishing that his witnesses were wrong than that he rose. Almost nothing is less astonishing than human error, so the scale never moves.

Hume saw the obvious objection coming and answered it. Wouldn't this rule have kept us from believing anything new? He tells of an Indian prince who refused to believe the first reports of frost, and he says the prince reasoned justly. Water turning solid was outside the prince's experience, but it wasn't contrary to it, since he had never observed water in a cold climate at all. A miracle, Hume insists, is a different kind of thing. It isn't merely unfamiliar. It runs against experience that is uniform.

The second part of the section adds four observations drawn from history, and they are the part of Hume most people half-remember. No miracle, he says, has ever been attested by enough witnesses of such good sense, learning and integrity, in so public a place, that deception would have been impossible to hide. Human beings love the marvelous, and religious zeal adds a further temptation, since a man may persuade himself that a holy cause excuses a loose report. Miracle stories, he observes, abound chiefly among "ignorant and barbarous nations," and they thin out as a people grows educated. And the miracles of rival religions cancel one another, since each is offered to establish its own system and so to discredit the others. He sharpens the point with a thought experiment. If every historian in every language reported that from the first of January 1600 the whole earth lay in darkness for eight days, he says, we should believe it and look for the cause. But if the histories reported that Queen Elizabeth died, was buried, and a month later returned to govern England for three more years, he wouldn't believe it for a moment, and if the report were attached to a new religion, that fact alone would make him suspect a fraud.

Much of this is simply true, and it should be granted without grudging. Most miracle claims dissolve under inspection. The love of wonder is real, and religious zeal has lied for God more often than the church likes to remember. Hume himself discusses the healings reported at the Paris tomb of the Jansenist deacon François de Pâris in the cemetery of Saint-Médard, a movement that turned to public convulsions until a royal order closed the cemetery in 1732. The story is still told that a wit then posted a couplet on the gate announcing that, by order of the king, God was forbidden to work miracles in that place. Whether or not anyone ever posted it, the joke has lasted because it catches something true about how such movements end. And Christians have given Hume more ammunition than he could have hoped for. We pass along the healing video we would laugh at if another faith had posted it. The preacher who promises a miracle in return for a seed gift is doing precisely what Hume said religious zeal does, letting the holiness of the cause excuse the looseness of the evidence. Anyone who preaches to people who want God to do something knows how much pressure that longing can put on the truth. If believing in miracles means believing every wonder that flatters our side, then Hume has already won, and he deserves to.

So the question isn't whether Hume's caution is wise. It is. The question is whether his argument settles the matter before any particular claim is heard, as I once thought it did. It doesn't, and the flaw isn't at the edges. It sits in the middle of the argument, in the word "law."

### What a law of nature is, and what a miracle is

Hume wasn't the first to object, and the older objection is in some ways the harder one for a Christian, because it is theological. In 1670 Baruch Spinoza devoted the sixth chapter of his *Theological-Political Treatise* to miracles. The laws of nature, he argued, are simply the decrees of God, flowing from the necessity and perfection of the divine nature, so a miracle against nature would be God contradicting himself. Worse, since a miracle is by definition something we can't explain, it can teach us nothing about God, while the fixed and unchanging order of nature teaches us a great deal. Gottfried Leibniz pressed a cousin of the objection against Isaac Newton's circle in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke in 1715 and 1716. A God who has to step in and adjust his world from time to time, Leibniz complained, is like a clockmaker who has to keep mending his clock, and a perfect craftsman wouldn't need to.

A Christian ought to feel the force of that. Scripture itself praises the dependability of the world as the fruit of God's promise. After the flood God says, "While the earth remains, seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease" (Genesis 8:22, ESV). The regularity of nature is covenant faithfulness, and a God who is constantly patching his creation would look less like the God of Genesis than like a harried mechanic.

But notice what Spinoza, Leibniz and Hume all assume. Each pictures a miracle as a breach, whether a contradiction in God's decrees, a repair to a defective mechanism, or a violation of an unalterable rule. That picture isn't the only one, and it isn't the oldest. Early in the fifth century Augustine argued in *The City of God* (book 21, chapter 8) that a portent happens not contrary to nature but contrary to what is known of nature. For Augustine the will of the Creator is the nature of every created thing, so nothing God wills can be against nature in the deepest sense. It can only be against our expectations.

C. S. Lewis gave the idea its modern form in *Miracles* (1947), where he defined a miracle as "an interference with Nature by supernatural power." His illustration has lasted because it is ordinary. Put sixpence in a drawer on Monday and another sixpence on Tuesday, and the laws of arithmetic say that, other things being equal, you'll find a shilling there on Wednesday. If a thief has been at the drawer, you may find only twopence, and the laws of arithmetic haven't been broken. They were always conditional: they tell you what happens if nothing else interferes. Lewis noticed something further. When a new event enters nature, nature immediately takes it up and carries on by its own rules. Miraculous wine will intoxicate. Miraculous bread will be digested. A miraculously conceived child grows in the womb for nine months like any other. The laws aren't suspended. They are handed something new to work on.

Alvin Plantinga made the same point in the vocabulary of physics in *Where the Conflict Really Lies* (2011). The laws of classical physics describe how matter behaves in a closed system, one in which nothing outside the system acts on it. They are silent on whether the universe is such a system. That further claim, that nothing beyond nature exists or acts, isn't a finding of physics. It is a philosophical position called naturalism, and it usually slips into the argument without being announced. Even Hume's own book undercuts the language he needed. In section seven of the same *Enquiry*, Hume argued that we never observe any necessary connection between causes and effects, only one thing regularly following another. A "firm and unalterable experience" is a strange guest in a book that denies we ever perceive anything firm or unalterable in the order of causes.

Those who want to keep the word "violation" can do so carefully. Richard Swinburne, in *The Concept of Miracle* (1970), argued that a violation of a law of nature is best understood as a non-repeatable counter-instance to it. A scientist rightly revises a law when an exception can be reproduced, because the formula then fails to predict what keeps happening. But an exception that can't be reproduced leaves the law standing as our best account of every repeatable case, and Swinburne argued that ordinary historical evidence, testimony and physical traces and the like, could in principle show that such an event occurred.

Put these witnesses together and the question changes shape. It was never whether nature is regular. Everyone in the argument agrees that it is, and a miracle only means anything against that background, as a signature only means something on a page that was written in an ordinary hand. The question is whether nature is all there is. If it is, Hume hardly needs an argument, since a closed system has nobody who could act on it. If it isn't, if the regularities we call laws are the settled habits of a God who "upholds the universe by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3, ESV), then a miracle isn't a crack in the machine. It is the one who keeps the machine running acting, for once, in a way he usually doesn't.

### Where the proof cheats

With that distinction in hand, the circle in Hume's first part becomes visible. His scale works only if experience is uniform against miracles. But how could anyone know experience is uniform against them? Only by knowing that every reported miracle in history is false, including the one now on the table. If even one of them happened, experience isn't uniform, and the whole weight on that side of the scale falls away. Lewis pressed exactly this point in *Miracles*: we can know that experience against miracles is uniform only if we already know that all the reports are false, and we can know that only if we already know that miracles never happen. The verdict has been written into the premise.

Hume's own handling of the Saint-Médard healings shows that the first part, not the second, is doing the work. He concedes that those reports were attested by many witnesses of credit, examined by judges of integrity, in a learned age, on the most public stage in Europe. That is very nearly the standard his own first historical observation said no miracle had ever met. And what does he set against it? Only the impossibility, the miraculous nature, of the events reported. The historical observations were never the real argument. The definition was.

The most searching modern critique comes from a writer with no religious case to make. The philosopher of science John Earman, in a book he titled bluntly *Hume's Abject Failure: The Argument Against Miracles* (2000), tested Hume's reasoning against the mathematics of probability. There is an irony in that choice of tools. The theorem Earman uses was worked out by Thomas Bayes, a Presbyterian minister, and communicated to the Royal Society in 1763 by Richard Price, a dissenting minister who went on to answer Hume in his *Four Dissertations* of 1767, a few years after George Campbell's *Dissertation on Miracles* of 1762. Earman argues that Hume's maxim, stripped to what it can defend, is either a truism about probability or simply false, and that taken as Hume applied it, it would forbid believing any genuinely novel event on testimony, however good. He also argues that Hume ignored how quickly independent witnesses multiply the force of evidence, since the chance that several independent witnesses are all mistaken in the same way falls off very fast. Earman doesn't conclude that any miracle has occurred. He concludes that Hume didn't settle the question, and that each claim returns to the ordinary work of weighing evidence.

An ordinary case shows why. The odds against any particular lottery number being drawn are enormous, and yet you believe the newspaper that reports it, because a low prior probability is never the whole calculation. What matters just as much is how likely the report would be if the event hadn't happened. That is the question Hume's scale never asks.

Hume's third observation, that miracle reports thrive among "ignorant and barbarous nations," has aged worst of all. Craig Keener's two-volume *Miracles* (2011) gathers a very large body of contemporary eyewitness accounts of healing from Africa, Asia, Latin America and the West, many of them from educated people and some with medical documentation. Keener doesn't claim every report is a miracle, and he grants that many may have natural explanations. His point is narrower and harder to dismiss. Eyewitnesses do make such claims, in great numbers, so the miracle reports in the Gospels can't be waved away as the kind of thing no eyewitness would say. And the assumption that such claims are always the product of ignorance turns out to be one culture's habit, the habit of the modern educated West, which dismisses most of the world's believers in advance and calls the dismissal neutral.

Here is where the argument I loved left me. I didn't lose it to a better argument. I lost it to the discovery that I had never had one. I had described the universe as a locked room, then congratulated myself on finding that nothing had ever come through the door.

### What the Gospels themselves do with miracles

Hume's fourth observation, that rival miracles cancel each other, assumes that a miracle is essentially a credential, a piece of evidence offered to win an argument between religions. It is worth asking whether the Gospels treat miracles that way, and the answer surprises most skeptics and a good many Christians. The Gospels are more restrained about miracles than Hume was, and far more restrained than much of the church.

Start with vocabulary. The Gospels don't have a word that means "violation of a law of nature." The Synoptic Gospels usually speak of *dynameis*, acts of power, and John of *sēmeia*, signs. A sign points past itself. When John records the wine at Cana, he doesn't linger on the chemistry. "This, the first of his signs, Jesus did at Cana in Galilee, and manifested his glory. And his disciples believed in him" (John 2:11, ESV). Lewis observed that many of Jesus's miracles do suddenly and locally what God does slowly and everywhere. Every autumn water becomes wine through soil, vine and sun, and at Cana the same Lord did it in a moment, at a wedding, for a family about to be shamed. The healings, the feedings and the cleansing of lepers aren't random displays of force. They are creation being put back the way it was meant to be, in small, particular places, as a preview.

Then notice what John says about his own selection. "Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (John 20:30–31, ESV). A writer building a case by volume would have done the opposite. John tells us he left most of them out, and that the ones he kept were chosen to point to a person, not to overwhelm a reader.

Mark goes further, and the setting matters. Jesus has just fed four thousand people in the wilderness when "the Pharisees came and began to argue with him, seeking from him a sign from heaven to test him. And he sighed deeply in his spirit and said, 'Why does this generation seek a sign? Truly, I say to you, no sign will be given to this generation'" (Mark 8:11–12, ESV). The demand for a sign comes immediately after a miracle, from people who want a proof on their terms, and Jesus refuses with a sigh. The pattern runs through the Gospels. In the wilderness he declines to throw himself from the temple to force God's hand, answering, "Again it is written, 'You shall not put the Lord your God to the test'" (Matthew 4:7, ESV). In his hometown, Mark says plainly, "he could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them" (Mark 6:5, ESV). At the cross the mockers make the most reasonable-sounding offer in the whole story: "Let the Christ, the King of Israel, come down now from the cross that we may see and believe" (Mark 15:32, ESV). He stays where he is. And in the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, Abraham gives the verdict on proof by spectacle: "If they do not hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead" (Luke 16:31, ESV).

That is not how a religion built on credentials behaves. In the Gospels a miracle is a sign that can be refused, and the refusal is left standing. The sign respects the person it is offered to; it doesn't coerce belief.

This cuts against the church in both of its familiar directions. The Christianity that cut the miracles out to stay respectable, from Jefferson's razor to Bultmann's demythologizing, treated miracles as credentials it was embarrassed to present. The Christianity that sells miracles, the healing crusade that never follows up and the prosperity preacher who promises a breakthrough for a gift, treats them as credentials it is proud to present. One is ashamed and the other is greedy, but they share the same mistake about what a miracle is for. Neither sounds like Mark's Jesus, who sighed at the demand for a sign and went on healing the few who came to him.

### The one miracle that carries the weight

Christianity doesn't ask anyone to believe in miracles in general, and it certainly doesn't ask anyone to believe every report. It stakes everything on one event, and it says so with a bluntness no skeptic could improve on. "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). A few lines later Paul adds that without the resurrection "your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, ESV). This isn't a symbol a person can keep after the history collapses. It is a claim about a tomb, and Paul invites the check. In the same chapter, writing in the mid-50s of the first century, he reports that the risen Jesus "appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:6, ESV). That is not the posture of a man hiding from Hume's scale. It is a man setting his one claim on it and telling you to weigh it.

What needs explaining is a cluster of facts most historians of the period grant, whatever they believe. Jesus was crucified under Pontius Pilate. Within a short time his followers, who had scattered, were proclaiming in Jerusalem, the city where he was executed, that God had raised him bodily from the dead. N. T. Wright argued at length in *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) that this belief was itself a historical anomaly. Many Jews of the period expected a resurrection of the righteous at the end of the age, all together. Nobody expected one man to be raised in the middle of history while the world went on as before. The early Christian claim was a mutation in Jewish hope, and mutations of that kind need a cause. The naturalistic explanations, theft, hallucination and slowly growing legend, have each been offered, and each has to set aside some part of the evidence to work.

History doesn't deliver proof the way a syllogism does, and no historical argument can compel faith. What the evidence can do is refuse to let the question be closed from the armchair. If there is no God, Hume wins without needing to argue, because the resurrection is ruled out before the evidence is heard, and any natural explanation, however strained, will always beat it. If there might be a God, the resurrection is no longer absurd. It becomes a claim about a particular man on a particular morning, to be weighed against its rivals like any other surprising report from witnesses who gained nothing by it and lost a great deal. Lewis saw that the question of miracles finally depends on the question of naturalism, and that is where the honest skeptic has to decide whether his certainty is a conclusion he reached or a definition he started with.

One more text belongs here, because it is usually fired as a slogan and reads very differently in its setting. "With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible" (Matthew 19:26, ESV). Jesus doesn't say it about physics. He says it after a rich young man has walked away sad, and after he has told the disciples that it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom. They are "greatly astonished, saying, 'Who then can be saved?'" (Matthew 19:25, ESV). The impossible thing in the passage isn't a body leaving a tomb. It is a human heart letting go of what it holds.

Which brings us back to Hume's parting shot, that the Christian religion "cannot be believed by any reasonable person without" a miracle. He meant it as mockery, and the church, in its own way, has always said something close to it. Faith isn't a conclusion argued into being. The New Testament speaks of it as a gift, a new birth, a heart raised from the dead by the same power that raised Jesus. I didn't argue my way out of atheism, and no one argued me into faith. The argument did something smaller and necessary: it showed me that the lock on the door was one I had installed myself.

I can't hand anyone the miracle Hume was mocking. Nobody can. What I can do is ask the question I refused to ask for years. Is your certainty that the dead stay dead a conclusion you reasoned to, or a definition you began with? If it is the second, you haven't weighed the resurrection at all. You've only refused, in advance, to put it on the scale, and the tomb outside Jerusalem is still there in the record, waiting for an explanation you haven't yet been given good reason to refuse.
