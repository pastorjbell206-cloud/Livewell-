Most of what we do wrong in this part of parenting doesn't feel wrong while we are doing it. It feels like love. The forgotten lunch is driven to school before noon. The science project gets quietly finished at the kitchen table after the child has gone to bed. The parent emails the teacher about the grade, calls the coach about playing time, and walks a step ahead of the child through every day, smoothing the ground before his foot lands on it. None of this feels like sabotage, and very little of it is sinister. But much of it, repeated across eighteen years, raises a person who has never once found out that he can survive a hard thing, because someone made sure he never had to.

The obvious correction is also wrong. There is a kind of parent, and a kind of Christian parenting literature, that hears all this and reaches for severity: toughen them up, let the world knock the softness out of them, call the bruises character. That parent is not raising a resilient child. He is raising a wounded one and naming the wound a virtue. The answer to overprotection was never its opposite, and a parent who swings from one to the other has usually changed tactics without changing what he was afraid of.

I have five sons, and I was raised without a father, so I come to this subject with two reasons to hold on too tightly. I know from the inside what an absent parent costs a boy, and that knowledge makes presence feel like the whole of the job. It is a large part of the job, but a presence that never steps back does its own damage. **The distinction this essay turns on is between protecting a child from danger and protecting a child from difficulty. The first is a parent's plain duty, and Scripture's judgment on adults who fail at it is as severe as anything Jesus said. The second, pursued as a way of life, disables the child it means to serve. It usually comes from a fear that has less to do with the child than with us, and from a belief we would never say aloud: that if God will not keep our children, we must.**

### How Childhood Became a Full-Time Project

The overprotective parent is not a personality type that appeared out of nowhere around 2005. He is the late product of a long change in what a child is. The sociologist Viviana Zelizer, in *Pricing the Priceless Child* (1985), traced how American children passed, roughly between 1870 and 1930, from being economically useful to being economically useless and emotionally priceless. A child on a farm or in a mill had been a worker. The fights over child labor and compulsory schooling moved the child out of the economy and into a sacred category of his own, valued for love alone. That was a real moral gain, and no Christian should want it undone. But a thing that is priceless is also a thing whose owner cannot bear to see scratched. As families grew smaller across the twentieth century, each child carried more of the household's hope, and more of its fear.

The vocabulary followed. Haim Ginott's *Between Parent and Teenager* (1969) recorded a teenager complaining that his mother hovered over him like a helicopter, and the image stuck. By 1996 the sociologist Sharon Hays could describe, in *The Cultural Contradictions of Motherhood*, an ideology she called intensive mothering: child-centered, guided by experts, emotionally absorbing, labor-intensive and expensive, and growing more demanding just as more mothers were working outside the home. Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003) showed the pattern splitting along class lines. Middle-class families practiced what she called concerted cultivation, filling a child's week with organized activities and training him to speak up to teachers and coaches. Working-class and poor families more often left children to the accomplishment of natural growth, with long unstructured hours among siblings and cousins. Lareau romanticized neither. But she made it plain that childhood had become, for those who could afford it, a project with a finish line, and the finish line was usually an admissions letter.

Fear sharpened the project. In May 1979, six-year-old Etan Patz disappeared in New York on the first morning his parents let him walk alone to the school bus. In July 1981, Adam Walsh was taken from a department store in Hollywood, Florida, and murdered. By the mid-1980s the faces of missing children were printed on milk cartons, and cable news, which arrived with CNN in 1980, could carry every such horror into every living room. Yet violent crime in the United States climbed into the early 1990s and then fell for two decades, while parental fear kept rising as if the numbers were going the other way. When the journalist Lenore Skenazy wrote in 2008 about letting her nine-year-old son ride the New York subway home by himself, she was christened America's worst mom, and her book *Free-Range Kids* (2009) became the manifesto of a small resistance.

By 2015 the results were arriving at college. Julie Lythcott-Haims, who had served as Stanford's dean of freshmen, wrote *How to Raise an Adult* (2015) out of years of watching students with extraordinary records struggle with the ordinary business of adult life, their parents still handling matters that once belonged to the student alone. The same years brought the turn Jonathan Haidt describes in *The Anxious Generation* (2024): between roughly 2010 and 2015, childhood moved onto the smartphone. His summary indicts the careful and the careless alike. We have overprotected our children in the real world and underprotected them in the virtual one.

### What Doesn't Kill You

Nassim Nicholas Taleb gave the problem its most useful vocabulary in *Antifragile* (2012). Some things are fragile: they break under stress, like a wine glass. Some are robust: they resist stress and stay the same, like a rock. And some are what Taleb called antifragile: they grow stronger because of stress, within limits, and weaken without it. Muscles are antifragile. So are bones, which thicken under load and thin in astronauts who spend months without gravity. The error Taleb warned against is treating an antifragile system as fragile, removing its stressors in the name of protection and starving it of what it needed to grow.

Greg Lukianoff and Jonathan Haidt applied this directly to children in *The Coddling of the American Mind* (2018). Children, they argued, are antifragile. They named the mistaken belief that what doesn't kill you makes you weaker, and they gave the wider culture of protection a name, safetyism, by which they meant a setting in which safety becomes a sacred value, so that people become unwilling to weigh it against any other good. Their illustration was the peanut. For years parents were advised to keep peanuts away from young children for fear of allergy, and allergies rose. When researchers in the LEAP study tested the opposite approach, feeding peanut products early to infants at high risk, those children were far less likely to develop the allergy at all. The protection had produced the weakness it was meant to prevent. Lukianoff and Haidt did not think every childhood problem was a peanut. They thought the logic was general, and that a generation had been raised on the wrong half of it.

Peter Gray, a developmental psychologist, made a related case in *Free to Learn* (2013). He argued that the long decline of free play, the kind children choose and run themselves, has run alongside a rise in anxiety and depression among the young, and that the connection is more than coincidence. In unsupervised play children make rules and argue about them, take small risks and misjudge them, get left out and find a way back in. A game of pickup baseball with no adult present is a school in self-government. A travel league with a paid coach and a row of parents in folding chairs is many good things, but not that.

The mechanism is simple once it is named. Resilience is built the way a callus is built, by repeated contact with a manageable amount of friction. Each time a child meets a difficulty slightly beyond him, struggles, and comes through, he lays down a piece of evidence that hard things happen and that he can survive them. No amount of praise can install that evidence. It has to be earned. When we rescue a child from a difficulty he could have survived, we don't merely solve the problem in front of him. We remove the evidence before it forms, and we teach a different lesson in its place: hard things happen, and someone bigger makes them stop. A child who has learned that second lesson a thousand times will meet his first adult problem that no one can fix with nothing inside him to say that he can bear it.

We should also be honest about whose pain the rescue usually relieves. A nine-year-old who forgets his lunch is hungry for an afternoon and checks his backpack the next morning. The one who cannot stand it is the parent, and we drive the lunch to school to spare ourselves and call it sparing him.

### The Case for the Protective Parent

The protective parent deserves to be heard first, in the form he would sign, and he would begin with Etan Patz. The first morning a child was trusted to walk to the bus alone was the morning he vanished, and no statistic about the rarity of stranger abduction has ever comforted a mother standing at that corner. Odds describe populations. A parent doesn't have a population. He has this child, and if the rare thing happens to this child, it has happened completely. The costs of overprotection are slow and spread thin: a little less confidence, a little more anxiety, a young adult who calls home too often. The costs of underprotection can be sudden, singular and permanent. A parent who weighs those two kinds of cost differently is not being irrational. He is being a parent.

He would add that the world has not stopped being dangerous because the crime rate fell. Water still drowns children, cars still kill them, and some neighborhoods are violent in ways their critics rarely live beside. A mother who keeps her son inside after dark on such a street is not suffering from safetyism. Many Black parents in America give their sons careful instructions about encounters with police, and a critic who has never needed that conversation should be slow to call it overprotection.

And the protective parent has one more argument, which the church should hear with its head down. The institutions that most confidently told children to trust the adults around them were too often the places where children were betrayed. The Catholic scandals are well known. In May 2022 an independent investigation commissioned by the Southern Baptist Convention reported that for years its leaders had mishandled allegations of sexual abuse and put the protection of the institution ahead of survivors. A parent who learned that and decided to trust no youth pastor, no coach and no camp counselor without checking had learned something true. We in the church taught some of that fear, and we taught it by failing.

So the distinction has to be drawn with care. Danger is harm a child cannot recover from, or harm that was never his to carry: the pool, the road, the loaded gun, the drug, the predator, the stranger who has found his way into a child's bedroom through a phone. From danger a parent protects, fiercely and without apology. Abuse is never a lesson a child needs to learn, and anyone who calls it character-building has abandoned both love and the gospel. Jesus reserved one of his hardest sayings for it: "whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea" (Matthew 18:6). If you suspect a child is being abused, or you are young and someone is hurting you, call the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline at 1-800-422-4453. If a child's struggle has turned into talk of not wanting to live, it is no longer a difficulty to let him carry; call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. And some children's anxiety is more than ordinary worry and needs a doctor or counselor. Nothing here is medical advice.

Difficulty is something else. It is friction proportioned to the child, which he can survive and from which he can grow: the forgotten homework and its zero, the lost game, the unfair teacher, the friend who drops him, the demanding first boss, the walk to the store alone, the tree climbed one branch higher than he's sure of. Our protective energy has often run in exactly the wrong direction. Most children who are abused are harmed by someone they know and trust, not by a stranger at the park. And Haidt's charge is that we kept children in from streets that were growing safer and then handed them a device that opened their rooms to anyone in the world. We guarded them from difficulty and left the door open to danger. The overprotective parent and the careless parent are very often the same parent on different days. The question was never whether to protect. It was what.

### Suffering That Makes and Suffering That Breaks

Scripture makes a claim about suffering that no secular account quite reaches. Paul writes, "Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us" (Romans 5:3–5). It is often quoted as a law of nature, as though suffering always produced character the way heat produces steam. Read in its place, it is more careful. The chapter opens, "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). The chain from suffering to hope runs inside a settled relationship, and it ends in love poured out. Paul does not say that suffering, as such, builds anyone. Plenty of suffering only breaks people. He says that suffering held inside the peace and love of God is turned to good.

That is the secular finding brought under Scripture, and corrected by it. Lukianoff and Haidt got the symptom right: children need friction to grow strong. What their account cannot supply is the condition. Friction builds a child who is held, and it wounds a child who is alone. The parent's task is not to manufacture hardship. It is to be the kind of love inside which ordinary hardship can do its work.

James writes to scattered and largely poor believers, "the twelve tribes in the Dispersion" (James 1:1), who had no power to spare themselves anything. "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness. And let steadfastness have its full effect, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing" (James 1:2–4). The phrase a parent should linger over is *let steadfastness have its full effect*. Endurance does its work over time, and it can be interrupted. Every rescue from a difficulty the child could have borne cuts the process short, and a child whose every trial is cut short arrives at adulthood, in James's terms, incomplete.

The Letter to the Hebrews speaks most directly in the language of fathers and sons. Its twelfth chapter, after the long roll of the faithful and the picture of Jesus enduring the cross, quotes Proverbs 3 and then says, "It is for discipline that you have to endure. God is treating you as sons. For what son is there whom his father does not discipline?" (Hebrews 12:7). The word for discipline, *paideia*, is the ordinary Greek word for the upbringing and education of a child. It includes correction, but it is wider than punishment. The writer then makes an admission every parent should find both humbling and freeing: "For they disciplined us for a short time as it seemed best to them, but he disciplines us for our good, that we may share his holiness" (Hebrews 12:10). Human fathers do it as it seems best to them, which is to say imperfectly, and Scripture does not pretend otherwise. And then: "For the moment all discipline seems painful rather than pleasant, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it" (Hebrews 12:11). *Trained* translates a word from which we get gymnasium. The image is athletic. No one grows strong in a gym where the weights have been removed out of kindness.

The same letter says of Jesus himself, "Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered" (Hebrews 5:8), and Luke says the boy "increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52). The eternal Son took a human childhood, with its growth, and Scripture does not blush to say his humanity was formed through difficulty. We should be slow to believe our children are too precious for what he was not spared.

### The Father Who Let Him Go

Jesus told his most famous story about a father to men grumbling that he "receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2). The parable of the two sons is about the Father's welcome of sinners, not a parenting manual, and it should not be pressed into one. But it does show what the Father's love looks like, and one thing it looks like is release. The younger son asks for his share of the estate while his father is still alive. Kenneth Bailey, who spent decades teaching in the Middle East, argued in *Poet and Peasant* (1976) that in that world the request amounted to wishing the father dead. The father's response is almost unbearable to read: "he divided his property between them" (Luke 15:12). He does not follow the boy to the far country. The son "squandered his property in reckless living" (15:13), and the famine came, and the father let it come.

It is in the far country, at the bottom, feeding pigs, that the son "came to himself" (15:17). No one could have handed him that recovery of sight; it had to happen to him where the consequences were. And the father's love, which had not prevented anything, is revealed at the road: "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (15:20). Bailey notes that a patriarch of that culture did not run. The father gave up his dignity at the same moment he had refused to give up his son's freedom. We should notice, too, that this was a grown son claiming what the law allowed him. The parable does not tell a parent to let a seven-year-old walk into a famine. It tells us that the love of God is measured by the welcome more than by the prevention.

Then there is the elder brother, the one who never left. "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command" (15:29). He had stayed close and done everything right, and he thought of himself as a hired hand. The father has to tell him what he is: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours" (15:31). A child can be kept home all his life and never know he is a son. Nearness produced by control is not the same as belonging.

Beneath the parable lies the cross, and here a parent must be careful. The Father "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). Some have heard in that a father inflicting suffering on an unwilling child. That picture is false. The Son says, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18). The cross is the one God, Father, Son and Spirit, entering our suffering, not a parent imposing it, and it licenses no parent to manufacture pain. What it ends is the idea that love always proves itself by stopping the suffering of the one it loves. At the center of our faith is a love that did not prevent Friday, and did not leave the tomb shut.

### Providence and the Roots of Our Fear

So why can't we let go? Part of the answer is love, and it should be honored. Part is less flattering. For many of us the child's performance has become the verdict on our own lives. Søren Kierkegaard called anxiety the dizziness of freedom in *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), and a parent watching a child's freedom open in front of him feels that vertigo exactly. But the deepest root is theological. A culture that no longer believes anyone is governing the world has to find someone to do the job, and in the modern household the job falls to the parent. If there is no providence, then every outcome in a child's life depends on us, and no amount of vigilance is enough. The overprotective parent is very often a practical atheist with a Christian vocabulary. I have to put myself in that sentence. Most of us who believe in providence on Sunday parent on Tuesday as though we had repealed it.

We even read our Bibles that way. "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6) is a proverb, a statement of how things generally go under God, and we turn it into a warranty whose fine print is our own effort. The rest of Proverbs corrects us: "The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps" (Proverbs 16:9). And the psalm ascribed to Solomon says it plainly: "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain. Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain. It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep" (Psalm 127:1–2). The wisest father in Israel's history could not secure his own son, and Rehoboam's folly split the kingdom (1 Kings 12).

The Reformers held the doctrine of providence as a release from exactly this burden, and they held it in a world far more dangerous for children than ours. John Calvin, who with his wife buried their only son in infancy, argued in the *Institutes* (1559) that ignorance of providence is the worst of miseries and the knowledge of it the highest blessedness, because it frees the believer from a fear that would otherwise never end. The Heidelberg Catechism (1563) taught that all things come to us not by chance but by God's fatherly hand, and it named the fruit of believing that: patience in adversity, thankfulness in prosperity, and confidence for the future. The Westminster Confession (1646) added the distinction that keeps this from becoming passivity. God ordinarily works through means. A parent is one of those means, and a real one: the fence around the pool and the conversation about the phone are how providence often keeps a child. But a means is not the author. The parent is responsible for faithfulness, not for the outcome, and that difference is the whole of the freedom. The Puritan John Flavel, in *The Mystery of Providence* (1678), taught his readers that some providences can only be read backward, long after the fact, which is how most parents will read the years they are living now.

### What Letting Go Looks Like on a Tuesday

In an ordinary week, this looks smaller than the theology. It looks like letting the forgotten homework earn its zero in fourth grade, the cheapest tuition the child will ever pay for a lesson that would cost him a job at twenty-four. It looks like sitting beside him while he works the problem, or fails to, and asking what he thinks he'll do rather than telling him. It looks like risk proportioned to age, a little more each year. And when he fails, it looks like telling him the truth, because he knows it mattered and a reassurance that it didn't only teaches him we can't be trusted. Then comes the thing that holds all of it up: his failure has changed nothing between us. Real consequences held inside unconditional love are the shape of how God fathers, and they are the only soil in which a child grows both strong and tender, strong because he failed and survived, tender because he was never alone in it.

For some of us it will also look like repentance, which is harder than any technique. It may mean going back to a grown child and admitting that our rescuing was often for our sake, and that our fear taught him more than our words did. That confession is the gospel practiced at the kitchen table.

Every child eventually leaves for some far country, a dorm or a job or a city or a doubt, and none of us will be allowed to follow. What goes with him is whatever was built while we were near: the evidence, laid down one survived difficulty at a time, that he can bear hard things, and the memory of a love never withdrawn when he fell. What stays behind is the parent at the window, who has done what could be done and now has to find out whether he believes that the Lord watches over the city. The father in Luke's story did not know whether his son would come home. He kept watching the road, and he ran when he saw him. That is not a technique. It is faith, and every parent will be asked for it, usually sooner than we'd like.
