In 1999, by Gallup's count, seven in ten Americans belonged to a church, a synagogue or a mosque. When Gallup reported the figure for 2020, it was 47 percent, the first time in the eight decades the firm had been asking that membership fell below half. That is one generation. A national default became a minority habit while most of us were arguing about the music.

I have sat in the meetings where churches explain this to themselves. Better marketing. A younger worship leader. A sermon series with a sharper title. I have voted for some of it. Nearly every one of those answers assumes the people who left misunderstood us, and that if we could only present ourselves more clearly they would come back. The other common answer runs the opposite direction and is just as comfortable: the culture has turned secular and hostile, Sunday belongs to travel soccer now, and the people who left were never very serious anyway. The first explanation blames our packaging. The second blames the world. Neither asks much of us.

Both contain something true. But the honest account is harder than either. The culture made leaving the church possible in a way it had never been before. The church, too often, made leaving reasonable. And the people who walked out are right about more than we would like to admit and mistaken about one thing that matters more than all the rest, which is the belief that Christ can be kept while his body is set down. That belief is sincere, and it is widely held, and it almost never survives a second generation. Seeing why requires going back further than the latest survey.

### Who the Nones Are and When They Arrived

The word came out of survey method before it named a movement. Since 1972 the General Social Survey, run by NORC at the University of Chicago, has asked a national sample of Americans, "What is your religious preference?" Someone who answers "none" is counted as one of the nones. For most of the next two decades that answer came from roughly one American in twenty, and the line on the chart barely moved. Then, in the 1990s, it began to climb, and it has not come back down. By 2021 the Pew Research Center put the religiously unaffiliated at 29 percent of American adults, larger than any single Christian tradition in the country.

The first serious attempt to explain the turn came from two sociologists at Berkeley, Michael Hout and Claude Fischer, in a 2002 article in the *American Sociological Review* titled "Why More Americans Have No Religious Preference." They found that the share of Americans claiming no religion had roughly doubled across the 1990s, and that most of the new nones were not atheists. Many still believed in God and still prayed. What they had stopped doing was naming a religion. Hout and Fischer's explanation had two parts. One was generational, since each cohort of young adults was somewhat less attached than the one before. The other was political. The new nones were concentrated among political moderates and liberals, and Hout and Fischer read their departure as a reaction against the public alliance of organized religion with conservative politics. These people were not renouncing God. They were declining to be counted with the Religious Right.

Robert Putnam and David Campbell widened the frame in *American Grace* (2010), describing modern American religion as one shock followed by two aftershocks. The shock was the 1960s, the sudden loosening of sexual and social norms. The first aftershock was the conservative religious response of the 1970s and 1980s, the growth of evangelical churches and their mobilization into politics. The second aftershock began around 1990, when young Americans who had come of age during the culture wars turned away from organized religion itself, many because they had come to see it as bound up with a conservative politics, especially on sexuality, that they did not share. In Putnam and Campbell's telling the rise of the nones is, in large part, a reaction to a reaction.

Ryan Burge, a political scientist who is also a Baptist pastor, has done more than anyone to put a face on the category. His book *The Nones* (2021) insists that the unaffiliated are not a single thing. The atheists and agnostics, the people the church pictures when it hears the word, are a minority of the nones. The largest group, by a wide margin, describes itself as "nothing in particular," and Burge's portrait of them is less of hostility than of detachment. They are not so much arguing with God as disconnected, from civic life in general and not only from church. Pew's 2024 profile of the nones found the same shape: most describe themselves as nothing in particular rather than as atheist or agnostic.

I checked that box once. In my case it meant atheist, which makes me the exception the church keeps mistaking for the rule.

One distinction clarifies nearly everything that follows. Sociologists measure three different things that headlines tend to blur: belonging, which is membership or the name you give when asked your religion; behaving, which is whether you actually attend; and believing, which is what you hold to be true about God. Gallup and the General Social Survey measure the first. Attendance and belief are separate lines. A person can drop the name and keep praying, or keep the name and never walk through a door. The most important question in this essay lives in the gap between belonging and believing, and whether that gap can stay open for long.

### What Secularization Theory Got Wrong and What It Got Right

For much of the twentieth century, sociologists thought they already knew the ending of this story. The classic statement came from Peter Berger in *The Sacred Canopy* (1967). Religion, Berger argued, is held up by what he called plausibility structures: the web of conversation, institutions and shared practice that makes a belief feel obviously true to the people inside it. Modernity tears that web. Cities mix strangers with different gods. Science and bureaucracy explain large parts of life without reference to heaven. As the canopy frays, religion retreats into the private self, and eventually, many of Berger's contemporaries assumed, it fades. The more modern a society, the less religious it would become.

Then the world refused to cooperate. Religion surged across the global South and reshaped politics from Tehran to São Paulo. By the late 1990s Berger had publicly reversed himself. In the essay that opens *The Desecularization of the World* (1999), a volume he edited, he argued that the assumption that we live in a secularized world was false, and that most of the world remained as intensely religious as it had ever been. The two great exceptions, he said, were Western Europe and a thin, internationally connected class of academics and cultural elites, the very people who had been writing the theory. The same year Rodney Stark published an article with the blunt title "Secularization, R.I.P." The United States was the favorite exhibit of the critics: the richest and most modern nation on earth, and still full of churches.

Almost exactly as the theorists buried secularization, the American numbers began to move. David Voas and Mark Chaves returned to the question in 2016 in the *American Journal of Sociology* and asked whether the United States had ever really been a counterexample. Their answer was no. American religiosity, they argued, had been declining generation by generation for decades, the same pattern seen across the West, only starting from a higher level and arriving later. The exception was a matter of timing.

Berger's own last word on the subject is the one worth keeping. In *The Many Altars of Modernity* (2014) he argued that what modernity reliably produces is not secularism but pluralism. It does not necessarily make faith impossible. It makes faith a choice. When everyone around you shares a belief, you hold it the way you hold your native language, without deciding to. When your neighbors, your coworkers and your children's friends hold a dozen different beliefs or none at all, you have to choose yours, and what can be chosen can be unchosen. Charles Taylor made a parallel argument in *A Secular Age* (2007): the decisive change in the West was the move from a society in which belief in God was unchallenged to one in which it is one live option among several, and often not the easiest one to hold.

The privatization Berger foresaw had already reshaped American faith long before the nones arrived. In *Habits of the Heart* (1985), Robert Bellah and his colleagues described a young nurse they called Sheila Larson who named her religion after herself. She called it "Sheilaism," a faith of one, carried by her own inner voice, with no need for a congregation. Bellah's team treated her as a portrait of what they called expressive individualism, the conviction that the truest self is the one found inside and expressed outward, with every institution judged by whether it serves that self. They wrote when church membership was still high. The exit came later, but the idea that made it feel natural was already sitting in the pews.

And the church is not the only institution losing its members. Putnam's *Bowling Alone* (2000) documented the slow collapse of American joining in the last third of the twentieth century: fraternal lodges, unions, parent-teacher associations, civic clubs and bowling leagues all thinned out together. Some of the nones are nones from everything. They did not leave the church for another community. They left community.

Everything in that account is true, and a church that ignores it will blame itself for weather it did not make. But it does not explain as much as it is often asked to. Pluralism removed the social cost of leaving. It did not supply a reason to leave, and it cannot tell us why one person with every freedom to walk away stays while her neighbor, with the same freedom, goes. The culture made leaving possible. The next question is what made it worth doing.

### The Case the Leavers Make

The people who left with good reasons deserve to have their case stated as they would state it. It goes something like this. We believed what you taught us. You told us the truth mattered, that the weak were precious to God, that sexual integrity was holy, that humility was the mark of a disciple, and that Jesus is Lord and Caesar is not. Then we watched what you did with power, with money, with children, and with the people who asked questions you didn't want asked. We did not leave because we stopped believing your sermons. We left because we believed them, and you didn't seem to.

That case has a history behind it, and the history has dates. Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority in 1979. Pat Robertson's Christian Coalition followed in 1989. Within a generation, the word "evangelical" had come to name a voting bloc in the minds of many Americans before it named anything about the gospel. The research on the 1990s points mainly in this direction, and honesty requires saying so plainly: Hout and Fischer, and after them Putnam and Campbell, found that the political alliance of conservative religion with one party drove a meaningful share of Americans to drop their religious label.

The same instrument has to be turned the other way, because the mechanism is not a partisan one. The mainline Protestant denominations have been losing members since the mid-1960s, and Dean Kelley was already diagnosing the decline in 1972 in *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing*. His argument was that churches that ask little of their members and offer little that can't be found elsewhere lose them. Where mainline congregations aligned their public voice closely with progressive causes, people who shared the politics often found they could have it without the Creed and without getting up on Sunday morning. The mechanism is identical in both directions. When a church's politics reliably predict its preaching, it becomes a chaplaincy to a party. People outside the party leave in protest. People inside it eventually leave too, because the party offers the same thing at a lower price.

Then there is the wound that needs no theory. In January 2002 the *Boston Globe*'s Spotlight team documented how the Archdiocese of Boston had moved priests accused of abusing children from parish to parish rather than removing them. Protestants who treated this as a Catholic problem had their own reckoning coming. In 2019 the *Houston Chronicle* and the *San Antonio Express-News* published an investigation of sexual abuse in Southern Baptist churches, and in May 2022 an independent report by Guidepost Solutions found that staff of the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee had kept a private list of accused ministers for years while resisting reforms, largely out of concern for legal liability. A Baptist pastor has no standing to read that report as somebody else's scandal. The logic it describes, protect the institution and manage the victim, is available to every church of every size.

If you were abused by someone in a church, you are owed safety and the truth, not a reconciliation meeting. Abuse of a child should be reported to the police. In the United States the National Sexual Assault Hotline is 1-800-656-4673, and if you are in danger at home, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

The sociologists Josh Packard and Ashleigh Hope spent time with people who had left, and their book *Church Refugees* (2015) named a group the church had not expected. They called them the "dones." They were not the marginal attenders. They were among the most committed people a congregation had, the teachers, the leaders, the volunteers who had given years to the institution, and they had not stopped believing. In Packard and Hope's account, they had come looking for real community and found judgment, wanted to take part in the church's life and found bureaucracy, wanted honest conversation about hard questions and found answers handed down in advance, and wanted to serve their neighbors and found themselves maintaining a building. Many of them left because they cared a great deal and could not find a place inside the institution to put it.

Scripture does not let the church hear any of this defensively. When the Lord indicts Israel's leaders through Ezekiel, he doesn't blame the sheep for scattering. "Thus says the Lord GOD: Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" (Ezekiel 34:2). The charge that follows is specific: "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them. So they were scattered, because there was no shepherd" (Ezekiel 34:4–5). Paul makes the same move against his own people when he quotes the prophets: "For, as it is written, 'The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you'" (Romans 2:24). The watching world's contempt for God is traced, in both passages, back to the people who claimed to represent him.

This is where a distinction becomes necessary. Some people leave because of the offense of the gospel itself. Paul was plain that "we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23), and no amount of repentance or reform will make a crucified and risen Lord easy to accept. That offense the church can't remove and mustn't try to. But a great many people leave because of the offense of the church: the cover-up, the partisanship, the contempt for questions, the budget that tells a different story than the sermon. That offense the church must remove. American congregations have a habit of claiming the first when they are guilty of the second, describing every departure as persecution and every critic as someone who couldn't bear the truth. It is a comfortable story. It lets us keep everything.

### Drift, and the Faith We Handed Down

Not everyone who left was wounded, and treating every departure as a wound misreads most of them. In *The Great Dechurching* (2023), Jim Davis and Michael Graham, working with Ryan Burge's survey research, estimated that roughly forty million American adults who once attended church regularly no longer do, and they argued that this is the largest and fastest religious shift in the country's history. Their most important finding was about how people left. The largest share did not walk out in anger. They drifted. They moved, changed jobs, had a baby, or found that a season of disruption had turned into a way of life. A smaller group had been harmed, by abuse or by leaders who misused their authority, and the authors take that group seriously. But most dechurching, in their account, was ordinary. Sunday came, and nobody went, and then it kept happening.

That distinction matters because the reason a person left and the reason a person has stayed gone are rarely the same. The first might be a real wound, and a real wound deserves to be honored and never rushed. The second is usually something quieter: a mix of inertia and relief. Sunday morning is the last unclaimed stretch of time in an overcommitted life. Nobody asks anything of you. It isn't a sin to shame anyone for, but it is a different thing from a wound, and it calls for a different answer. A person who was abused needs protection and justice. A person who simply stopped going needs someone to ask whether the faith they still claim is being fed by anything at all.

Why did drifting away cost so little? Part of the answer is the pluralism Berger described. But part of it lies closer to home. In *Soul Searching* (2005), Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton reported on a large national study of American teenagers and their religious lives, and they gave a name to the faith that, in their judgment, dominated among them. They called it moralistic therapeutic deism. In their summary, it teaches that a God exists who made the world and wants people to be good and nice, that the central goal of life is to be happy and feel good about oneself, that God doesn't need to be much involved except when there's a problem to solve, and that good people go to heaven. Smith and Denton's sharpest point was that the teenagers had not invented this faith. They learned it, mostly from their parents and their churches.

Look at what that faith does not need. It does not need a congregation, because its God is a helper on call rather than a Lord who gathers a people. It does not need the sacraments, because nothing in it is received from outside the self. It does not need the long discipline of other people's company, because its goal is feeling good, and other people are often the chief obstacle to that. If this is what a generation of American churches taught, then many of the nones are less rebels against the faith they were given than its honest heirs. We preached a faith that did not require a body, and people believed us.

The contrast with the early church could hardly be sharper. The letter to the Hebrews was written to believers who had once paid a real price for gathering. The writer reminds them that they "endured a hard struggle with sufferings, sometimes being publicly exposed to reproach and affliction," and it adds, "you joyfully accepted the plundering of your property" (Hebrews 10:32–34). For them, gathering cost something and leaving offered relief. For most Americans until recently, gathering cost almost nothing and leaving cost a little social standing. Now leaving costs nothing at all, and the question is whether the church has given anyone a reason to stay once staying is no longer expected.

### Leaving the Church Without Leaving Christ

The distinction the leavers draw is real, and the church should stop pretending otherwise. A person can leave an institution without leaving Christ. Grace Davie named the pattern in Britain in 1994 as believing without belonging, and Hout and Fischer found it among the American nones of the 1990s. In John 6, after a hard teaching, "many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him," and Jesus asked the Twelve, "Do you want to go away as well?" Peter's answer says nothing about the company: "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:66–68). Faith attaches to a Person before it attaches to a room.

But a distinction that holds in a single moment rarely holds across a life, and almost never across generations. David Voas and Alasdair Crockett tested Davie's phrase against British survey data in 2005 and titled their answer "Neither Believing nor Belonging." Belief, they found, falls along with belonging from one generation to the next. Berger's plausibility structures explain why. A belief no one around you shares, rehearses or sings becomes hard to hold, not because it has been refuted but because it has gone unspoken. The children of believers who don't belong are rarely handed the believing.

Hebrews names the same danger in its own terms. The letter is written to people tempted not so much to renounce Christ as to drift from him: "we must pay much closer attention to what we have heard, lest we drift away from it" (Hebrews 2:1). Its remedy is not private resolve. "And let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near" (Hebrews 10:24–25). Every verb runs between people. You can't stir yourself up in the sense the writer means, any more than you can tickle yourself. The neglect is called a habit, which is exactly how drift works, since nobody decides it. And the horizon is the Day, the return of Christ; the writer assumes that the nearer it comes, the more believers will need one another.

Paul's image in 1 Corinthians 12 goes further, because it treats belonging as a fact rather than a preference. "For just as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, though many, are one body, so it is with Christ" (1 Corinthians 12:12). A foot that decides it doesn't belong because it isn't a hand is still part of the body (12:15). But the passage cuts toward the church before it cuts toward the leaver: "The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you'" (12:21), and "If one member suffers, all suffer together" (12:26). Too many of the wounded were told, in effect, that the body had no need of them, and too few suffered with them. The church broke Paul's sentence first. That doesn't make the sentence untrue: "Now you are the body of Christ and individually members of it" (12:27). Membership, in Paul's grammar, is given in Christ. It isn't a subscription.

Cyprian of Carthage, writing around 251, argued that no one can have God for his Father who does not have the church for his mother. The line has been swung as a club, but in its setting it was a bishop's plea against schism in a church broken by persecution. The New Testament puts the same truth more tenderly and more severely at once: "Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25). A Christ held apart from the people he died for is not the Christ of the New Testament. He comes with a body attached.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote *Life Together* (1939) after the Gestapo had closed his underground seminary, and he supplies the word both sides need. The person who loves his dream of community more than the actual community, he warned, becomes its destroyer, and God's grace often arrives by shattering the dream. The warning falls first on churches that love their image more than their wounded members, and then on anyone waiting for a congregation that will never disappoint them.

### What Remains

The first obligation belongs to the church, and it is repentance, not a relaunch. That means telling the truth about abuse and handing abusers to the police even when the institution pays for it. It means refusing to let any party, right or left, write the sermon. It means making room for the questions the dones brought and the church sent away. None of it is a strategy for getting people back, and if it's done as one, the people who left will know. They have learned to recognize marketing.

To the reader who left and does not believe: you were owed honesty and often didn't get it. If you finish this unconvinced, I would ask only that you not mistake the church's failures for a verdict on Christ, since the prophets, and Jesus himself, saved their hardest words for religious people who misused God's name. To the reader who left and still believes: no one is asking you to walk back into the room that hurt you. There are other rooms, and some of them are quietly faithful. The question in front of you is whether the faith you are carrying can survive alone, and the witness of Scripture, of the church's long memory and of the sociology is that it rarely does.

It helps to remember how the church began. On the night Jesus was arrested, "they all left him and fled" (Mark 14:50). The first Christian community was a room full of people who had walked out on him, and the risen Christ came back to them, gathered them, and cooked breakfast on the shore for a boatload of them, the man who had denied him three times among them. Ezekiel's indictment of the shepherds ends the same way, with God doing what the shepherds would not: "Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out" (Ezekiel 34:11). Gathering the scattered is his work before it is ours, and that humbles every growth plan we have ever voted for. It also leaves a question sitting where it has always sat, inside the church's own house. If the Shepherd is out searching, and he brings someone home to us this Sunday, will they find a people worth staying with, or the same reasons they left?
