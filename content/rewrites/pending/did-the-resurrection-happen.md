### The one claim Christianity can't soften

Most people who ask whether the resurrection happened have already answered the question, one way or the other, without ever quite asking it. Some absorbed the answer in a pew, sung every spring alongside lilies and new dresses, a truth so familiar it stopped sounding like a claim about a corpse. Others absorbed the opposite answer from the air of modern life, where it goes without saying that the dead stay dead and that grown people who say otherwise are speaking in poetry. Both answers feel like conclusions. Very often they are inheritances. And the church has made the confusion worse, because we have preached the resurrection as a doctrine to be affirmed on one Sunday a year and then lived the other fifty-one as though death had the final word after all. I say that as someone who preaches it. The gap between the Easter sermon and the Tuesday that follows it is not only in the congregation.

Christianity is unusual among the world's faiths in refusing to let its central event drift into metaphor. You can read a creation poem as poetry and keep much of what it teaches. Paul would not allow the same move with Easter. Writing to the church at Corinth around the year 54, he stated the stakes with a bluntness that still startles: "And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14, ESV). A few lines later he went further: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17, ESV), and then, "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:19, ESV). He did not say the ethics would survive, or that the community would still be worth belonging to. He said the whole thing stands or falls on whether a particular man, publicly executed under Pontius Pilate, was dead on a Friday and alive on a Sunday.

The church has held Paul to that ever since. The creed hammered out at Nicaea in 325 and expanded at Constantinople in 381 confesses that "on the third day he rose again in accordance with the Scriptures," echoing Paul word for word. This belongs to the confessional floor, the small set of claims without which there is no historic Christianity to argue about. It isn't a second-order question like baptism or the millennium, where faithful churches disagree and remain brothers. It is the hinge on which the rest turns.

Two distinctions keep the question honest. The first is between resurrection and resuscitation. When Jesus called Lazarus out of his tomb, Lazarus came back to the life he had left, and he died again later. The New Testament claims something different about Jesus: "We know that Christ, being raised from the dead, will never die again; death no longer has dominion over him" (Romans 6:9, ESV). The claim isn't that a heart restarted. It is that a body passed through death into a life death can't touch, the first piece of the world to come arriving ahead of schedule.

The second distinction is between an event and a meaning. A generation of modern theologians tried to keep the meaning and release the event. Marcus Borg, debating N. T. Wright in *The Meaning of Jesus: Two Visions* (1999), argued that Easter's truth doesn't depend on anything having happened to Jesus' corpse; what matters is that his followers went on experiencing him as a living presence and that God vindicated his way of life. Borg was a generous and serious man, and he was trying to keep faith open to people who could no longer believe in miracles. But his proposal gives away the one thing Paul refused to give away. If nothing happened to the body, then the dead do stay dead, and the most the church can offer is an inspiring memory. Conservatives have their own way of emptying the claim, which is to shout it so loudly and so tribally that it becomes a password rather than a report. Neither the metaphor nor the slogan is what the first witnesses said.

So the question deserves to be asked as a historical question, the way one asks whether Caesar crossed the Rubicon. The claim of this essay is narrow, and it has to be earned. The resurrection of Jesus is not provable in the way a theorem is provable, and anyone who says otherwise is overselling. But it is the best available explanation of a set of facts that most serious historians of the period, including many skeptical ones, already grant. The rival explanations are real, and some of them are strong. Each one, pressed hard, explains a part of the evidence and has to borrow from the resurrection to explain the rest.
