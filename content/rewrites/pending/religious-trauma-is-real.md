People usually come to the words "religious trauma" from inside something. Someone left a church years ago and still can't sit through a sermon on judgment without her chest tightening. Someone was told at sixteen that his questions were a rebellious spirit and finds, at forty, that he can't make an ordinary decision without a dread he can't explain. Someone types "spiritual abuse signs" into a search bar late at night because a pastor's words from a decade ago still speak in his voice, and she wants to know whether what happened to her has a name, or whether, as she was told, the problem was her.

Some who read this are reading from inside harm, so several things belong at the top. This is a pastor's essay, not medical or psychological advice, and it can't tell you what you're carrying. A licensed counselor can, ideally one who understands religious communities and will respect your faith or your lack of it. If a church leader has sexually abused you or a child, that is a crime as well as a sin, and it belongs with the police, not only with the church that employs him. If the control has come into your home, with threats, violence, or someone who decides where you go and whom you may see, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233, and in immediate danger, call 911. If what you carry has turned toward thoughts of ending your life, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, tonight. None of this is a failure of faith.

Two words get run together in this conversation. "Religious trauma" is a clinical word about what happened inside a person. "Spiritual abuse" is a moral word about what someone holding spiritual power did to them. The church has spent much of the last fifteen years arguing about the first, whether religious trauma is a real diagnosis, and has often used that argument to avoid answering for the second. But Scripture never needed a diagnostic manual to condemn a shepherd who fed himself. I came to faith as an adult, from atheism, so I didn't carry a childhood version of this into the church. But I hold a pulpit, and I know how little effort it takes to use one against the person who questioned me. The people who most need to hear that this harm is real are often the people most invested in denying it, and on some days the pastor is one of them.

### Where the words came from

The concern is older than the vocabulary. In 1961 the psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton published *Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism*, a study of "thought reform" in Mao's China. Lifton wasn't writing about churches, which is part of what makes his work useful: he showed that a closed system of total meaning works the same way whatever it believes. Among the marks of such a system he named the control of what a person may hear and read, a demand for purity no one can meet, "loading the language" with phrases that end thought rather than start it, and a principle he called doctrine over person. Where a person's experience contradicts the doctrine, the experience must be wrong. Anyone who has been told that their doubt proves their sin has met that principle in a Christian accent.

For the next thirty years Americans mostly thought of religious control as something that happened elsewhere. The deaths of more than nine hundred people at the Peoples Temple settlement in Jonestown, Guyana, in November 1978 fixed "cult" as the frame, and the frame was comforting: abuse happened in a jungle under a madman, not in a brick church with a budget committee.

The change came in 1991, when David Johnson and Jeff VanVonderen, a pastor and a counselor, published *The Subtle Power of Spiritual Abuse*. Their contribution was to move the category out of the compound and into the ordinary congregation. They described spiritual abuse, in substance, as the mistreatment of a person who has come for help or spiritual strength, in a way that weakens and undermines the very strength they came for. They traced its marks in churches that would never have thought of themselves as cults: leaders preoccupied with their own authority, a culture of performance in which acceptance must be earned, and unspoken rules, above all the rule that the problem can't be talked about, because whoever names it becomes the problem.

A year later the psychiatrist Judith Lewis Herman published *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) and argued that prolonged, repeated harm under conditions of captivity, where the victim can't easily leave and depends on the one who harms her, produces something different from the trauma of a single terrible event. Among the settings of that captivity she named some religious cults, alongside prisons and certain families. Her proposal of a "complex" form of post-traumatic stress eventually entered the World Health Organization's ICD-11, adopted in 2019. In 1993 the psychologist Marlene Winell, who works with people leaving fundamentalist churches, published *Leaving the Fold*, and in the early 2010s she gave a name to the pattern she kept meeting in clients raised in authoritarian religious settings: Religious Trauma Syndrome. She described anxiety and depression, a lingering terror of judgment long after belief had changed, difficulty with trust and decisions, a damaged sense of self, and the loss of an entire social world at once. In Britain the researcher Lisa Oakley studied spiritual abuse empirically and, with the safeguarding specialist Justin Humphreys, wrote *Escaping the Maze of Spiritual Abuse* (2019), which treats it as a pattern of coercion and control in a religious setting: Scripture used to steer behavior, obedience demanded, secrecy required, and a leader who suggests his position is divinely protected from question. By the late 2010s, online communities of former evangelicals had made the phrase common.

Two things follow. First, Religious Trauma Syndrome is not a formal diagnosis. It appears in neither the American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5 nor the WHO's ICD-11. Someone whose religious history has left the symptoms of post-traumatic stress, or its complex form, can be diagnosed with that, and the religious setting is part of the story. "Religious trauma" names the setting of an injury, not a separate disease. Second, the idea has serious critics, and not all of them are defending abusers. The psychologist Nick Haslam, in a widely discussed 2016 paper, called the pattern "concept creep": the steady widening of terms like trauma and abuse, outward to new kinds of experience and downward to milder ones, until the words cover so much that they lose their force for the people who need them most. Some Christians worry that "religious trauma" has become a way of classifying ordinary belief as injury. They are not wrong to watch for it.

That is why the distinction matters. A person can be deeply wounded by a church without anyone having abused them; a death, a failed congregation, or a true word heard at the wrong time can all leave marks. A leader can abuse someone who never meets any clinical threshold, and a person can be harmed for years and never call it trauma. The absence of a diagnosis has sometimes been treated as if it settled whether anything wrong was done. It settles nothing of the kind. Whether a wound has an entry in the manual is a question for clinicians. Whether a shepherd fed himself is a question for the church, and the church has had the answer since Ezekiel.

### The pastor's fear, stated fairly

There's a pastor reading this with his arms folded, and he deserves to have his objection stated better than he'd put it himself. He's not defending abuse. He's afraid of a word that has no edges.

He has absorbed what the sociologist Philip Rieff saw in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966): a culture in which the old question, what is true and what does it demand of me, gives way to a new one, how do I feel and how can I feel better. In such a culture, the pastor fears, the only unforgivable act left is making someone feel bad, and he sees that the Christian faith makes people feel bad constantly, on purpose, because it tells them the truth about sin before it tells them about grace. Jesus himself taught in Capernaum until his hearers said, "This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?" (John 6:60), and John records that "after this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him" (John 6:66). Jesus let them go. He didn't soften the saying. If the measure of spiritual abuse is that a teaching caused distress, the pastor asks, then the Sermon on the Mount is abusive, the prophets were abusers, and a church that preaches judgment, repentance, sexual holiness, or hell is guilty before it opens its mouth.

He isn't imagining the possibility. Richard Dawkins devoted a chapter of *The God Delusion* (2006) to arguing that frightening children with hell can be a form of child abuse. He also knows that Scripture gives pastors real authority and real duties. "Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account" (Hebrews 13:17). Paul protects elders from careless accusation: "Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses" (1 Timothy 5:19). And the pastor has seen that an accusation can itself become an instrument of control. "You traumatized me" can end a conversation the way "you have a rebellious spirit" used to. A word that can't be answered is a weapon, whoever is holding it.

Most of that is true, and a church that forgets it will end up with a faith that consoles and never commands. But notice two things about the fear. The first is where it points. It points, almost entirely, toward protecting the one who holds the pulpit from being misjudged. That's a legitimate concern, and 1 Timothy 5:19 honors it, but the very next verse turns the other way: "As for those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear" (1 Timothy 5:20). Paul's protection of elders comes with a public accountability for elders, and the church has been far better at quoting the first verse than the second.

The second is what the record actually shows. The reports that have shaken the church in this century, which we'll come to, did not describe congregations too timid to preach hard truth. They described institutions protecting powerful men. The pastor's fear is real, but it isn't symmetrical with the harm, and treating the two dangers as equal is its own kind of dishonesty. The question isn't whether the church may say hard things. It must. The question is how to tell a hard truth from an abusive one, and Jesus answered it.

### A heavy burden and an easy yoke

Matthew's Gospel sets two sayings of Jesus twelve chapters apart, and each interprets the other. The first is an invitation: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:28–30). The second is an indictment, spoken in the temple in the last week of his life: "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger" (Matthew 23:4). Matthew uses the same Greek word for "burden" in both places. There are two ways of laying a load on a human being, and Jesus names both.

Jesus's burden is not light because it asks less. He tells his hearers that "unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:20), and then raising the bar on anger, lust, oaths, and enemies until no one listening could have thought themselves safe. He calls disciples to take up a cross. By any measure of demand, his yoke is heavier than the Pharisees'. The difference lies elsewhere, and Matthew 23 says where. The Pharisees lay burdens they won't lift a finger to carry. They do their deeds "to be seen by others" (23:5). Jesus, by contrast, calls himself "gentle and lowly," and his whole life is the carrying of the load he lays on others, all the way to the cross where he carries what no one else could.

So the line between hard truth and abuse doesn't run through the content of a teaching, at least not first. A church can preach judgment and hell and sexual holiness without abusing anyone, and a church can abuse people while preaching grace. The line runs through three questions. Is the one who lays the burden under it himself, carrying it alongside you, or standing above it? Does the teaching serve the one who hears it, or the one who gives it, his position, his reputation, his control? And are you free, free to ask, to disagree, to take time, even to leave, without being told that God has left with you? A hard word spoken by someone who kneels under it with you, for your good, while leaving you free, is the yoke of Christ, however heavy. A word of any weight that is laid on you from above, for the speaker's benefit, with your freedom removed, is the burden Jesus condemned.

Notice, too, what Jesus tells the crowds about their teachers. "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat, so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice" (Matthew 23:2–3). He doesn't tell them the law of Moses was the problem. He tells them the teaching and the teacher are different things, and that they must not confuse faithfulness to the one with submission to the other. Then he strips the teachers of the titles that made the confusion easy: "you have one teacher, and you are all brothers" (23:8), and "the greatest among you shall be your servant" (23:11). That is the principle Johnson and VanVonderen later found broken in abusive churches. The leader's reading of Scripture becomes indistinguishable from Scripture, and so disagreeing with him becomes disagreeing with God.

That's why good theology is no protection. Every mark of spiritual abuse the researchers describe is built out of something true. Scripture really does call Christians to submit to their leaders, so the controlling pastor speaks of "covering" and "authority" until a member can't choose a job or a spouse without his blessing. Scripture really does warn against division, so the member who asks where the money went is accused of "sowing discord." This is Lifton's loaded language: a true phrase used to end a thought rather than answer it, and in a church it carries God's own weight. Scripture really does command forgiveness, so the person who was harmed is told that her refusal to reconcile with the man who harmed her is now the only sin in the room. But forgiveness is not the same as restored trust, and neither is the same as restored office. A person can release a debt before God and still refuse to be alone with the one who incurred it, still testify, still call the police. The better the doctrine, the more effective the weapon, because the victim's own reverence for the teaching is turned against her. Lifton's doctrine over person is the last step. When her experience of harm contradicts the church's account of itself, her experience is declared the error.

Jesus reserves the word "woe" for the people who did this. "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you tithe mint and dill and cumin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matthew 23:23). The Pharisees weren't the careless. They were the serious ones, the people who studied Scripture most closely and cared most about holiness. The danger Jesus names lives nearest the people who take God most seriously, which is why the chapter belongs to pastors before anyone else.

### Shepherds who feed themselves

The prophet saw it six centuries earlier. Ezekiel was a priest carried to Babylon in the deportation of 597 BC, and his thirty-fourth chapter comes just after the news that Jerusalem has fallen. His shepherds are first of all Judah's kings and officials, whom the ancient Near East called the shepherds of their people. "Thus says the Lord GOD: Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" (Ezekiel 34:2). The charge is specific. "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought, and with force and harshness you have ruled them" (34:4). God's verdict is personal: "Behold, I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand" (34:10).

The New Testament applies the image to the church's own leaders, which is how a text about kings becomes a text about pastors. Paul, saying goodbye to the elders of Ephesus, tells them to "pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood" (Acts 20:28). Then he warns them where the danger will come from: "from among your own selves will arise men speaking twisted things, to draw away the disciples after them" (Acts 20:30). Peter tells elders to shepherd "not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock" (1 Peter 5:3). And the shortest letter in the Bible preserves a portrait of the problem in a first-century congregation: "I have written something to the church, but Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first, does not acknowledge our authority. So if I come, I will bring up what he is doing, talking wicked nonsense against us. And not content with that, he refuses to welcome the brothers, and also stops those who want to and puts them out of the church" (3 John 9–10). Diotrephes controls who belongs and punishes those who disagree. John's remedy is exposure: I will bring up what he is doing. The apostolic answer to a controlling leader wasn't a private word and a quiet transition. It was the light.

The church hasn't lacked for Diotrephes since. When the fourth-century church came to share the power of the Roman state, spiritual authority acquired a sword, and one of its greatest minds supplied the text. Augustine had once opposed coercing the Donatist schismatics. By about 408, in a letter to the Donatist bishop Vincentius, he defended imperial coercion, and he read the master's command in Jesus's parable, "Go out to the highways and hedges and compel people to come in" (Luke 14:23), as warrant for it. The reading outlived him by more than a thousand years; the Protestant philosopher Pierre Bayle was still writing a book against it in 1686. The Reformation recovered Luther's argument of 1520 that every baptized Christian is a priest, and it broke the claim of a clerical estate to stand between the believer and God. But it didn't break the temptation. Zurich drowned the Anabaptist Felix Manz in 1527, and Geneva burned Michael Servetus in 1553. The Reformers redistributed the authority. The heart that misuses it came along unchanged.

Our own century has supplied the evidence across every tradition. In January 2002 the Boston Globe began publishing its reporting on the Catholic Archdiocese of Boston: church officials had known for years that the priest John Geoghan was abusing children and had moved him from parish to parish. Within the year the reporting had exposed a pattern reaching far beyond one man, and in December 2002 Cardinal Bernard Law resigned. In May 2022 the firm Guidepost Solutions, commissioned after the Southern Baptist Convention's messengers voted for an independent investigation, released its report on how the convention's Executive Committee had handled abuse allegations. It found that for years a small group of leaders had met survivors with resistance and suspicion while concentrating on protecting the convention from legal liability, that staff had privately kept a list of ministers accused of abuse, and that reform had been resisted in the name of the autonomy of the local church. One leader's internal correspondence cast survivors' calls for reform as a satanic distraction from evangelism. And in November 2024 an independent review for the Church of England concluded that the church had failed for decades to act on what it knew about John Smyth, a barrister and leader of evangelical holiday camps who had beaten boys and young men brutally under the language of spiritual discipline. The Archbishop of Canterbury resigned within days.

Every one of those institutions confessed the creeds. What failed was the use of power, and the psychologist Diane Langberg, who has spent decades working with survivors of trauma, has given the church the language to see it. In *Redeeming Power* (2020) she argues that power is not evil in itself. It's a gift from God, entrusted to be spent for the good of others, and the incarnation shows what it looks like: all power, used in humility for the weak. Abuse is that trust inverted, power spent to serve the one who holds it, and it hides best behind the vocabulary of the very faith it betrays. She also insists that abuse is rarely one man alone. Systems form around powerful people and learn to protect them, and good people inside those systems learn not to see. Herman saw it from the clinic. The perpetrator, she observed, asks only that bystanders do nothing, while the victim asks them to share the burden of her pain. It is the dilemma of Matthew 23:4 exactly: the burden lies there, and most of us would rather not move it with a finger.

I pastor a Baptist church, and the autonomy of the local congregation is a Baptist conviction with real scriptural reasons behind it. So we Baptists have to reckon with the fact that the Guidepost report found that doctrine used as a reason not to act. Ezekiel's shepherds didn't need bad theology either. They needed only to keep the fat for themselves, and to rule "with force and harshness" people they had been given to heal.

### What was done in God's name is not God

The third commandment reads, "You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain, for the LORD will not hold him guiltless who takes his name in vain" (Exodus 20:7). We've mostly taught it as a rule about cursing. It reaches further. To take up God's name for an empty or false purpose, to lay it over one's own will so that resisting the man becomes resisting God, is what the controlling shepherd does every time he speaks. The commandment doesn't say God will overlook it. It says the opposite.

That matters most to the person who was harmed, because the deepest injury of spiritual abuse is that it borrows God's face. Ezekiel 34 answers that injury directly. Having condemned the shepherds, God announces what he'll do himself: "Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out" (34:11). Then he reverses their failures, item by item: "I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak" (34:16). In the one chapter of Scripture that most fully describes spiritual abuse, God isn't standing with the shepherds. He's out looking for the sheep they hurt. Jesus took up the chapter's image for himself: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The only authority he ever gave his church has the shape of that sentence.

So if you've been harmed, your recoil may be your conscience working correctly, not your faith failing. You don't owe any church your return on its timetable. Herman found that recovery begins with safety, and only after safety with remembering and grieving, and that reconnection comes last and happens in relationship, not alone. Distance from the room that hurt you can be faithful. And if you're the skeptic who left and doesn't believe any of this, I won't pretend the harm was imaginary. It proves the church can do what Ezekiel said shepherds do. The claim here is only that Scripture saw it coming and condemned it in God's name, and you're free to weigh that without anyone pressing you.

What the church owes is repentance, and repentance has a shape. It tells the truth in public, as John said he would about Diotrephes. It takes crimes to the civil authorities rather than handling them in a back room. It puts pastors under people they didn't choose and can't dismiss, and it welcomes outside review. It stops making forgiveness the price of belonging. It lets people leave without shunning them. And it keeps teaching the hard things, with the teacher standing under them first. Dietrich Bonhoeffer warned in *Life Together* (1939) that the one who loves his dream of Christian community more than the actual community becomes its destroyer, however sincere he is, and he contrasted a self-seeking love that binds others to itself with a spiritual love that sets them free to stand before Christ.

Matthew 23 doesn't end with a woe. It closes in lament: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (23:37). The one who spoke those woes grieved over the city, and then went out and carried to the cross the burden its teachers wouldn't touch. That is the only ground a pastor stands on.

Hebrews 13:17 tells the church to obey its leaders because they "will have to give an account." We've quoted that verse to congregations for centuries. It was always addressed to us as well. Ezekiel heard God say, "I will require my sheep at their hand." The sheep were never ours. They were bought with someone else's blood, and one day each of us who has held a pulpit will be asked what we did with them, and whether anyone was afraid to tell us the truth.
