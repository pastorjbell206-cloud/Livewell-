## The loan that covers the rent

Most people who walk into a payday lender already know it's a bad deal. They aren't confused about the fee. The rent is due on the first and the paycheck lands on the fifth, and the storefront on the corner is the only place in town that will hand over three hundred dollars in fifteen minutes without asking about a credit score. They sign because the alternative looks worse: a bounced check, a late fee, a shutoff notice. Two weeks later the loan comes due out of the same paycheck that was already too small, and the easiest way to pay it is to take out another one.

The church has said little about that storefront, and I'm inside that silence. I have preached about personal financial responsibility, the dangers of debt, the virtue of saving and the discipline of giving. I have not named the industry that profits from people who can't practice those virtues because they are already underwater. That silence was not neutral. It was a choice about whose comfort my preaching protected.

The question is simple enough: what does the Bible say about usury, and does any of it apply to a payday loan? The honest answer is longer than a verse. For well over a thousand years Christians held that lending at interest was sin, and from the sixteenth century on, more and more of them concluded that it wasn't, or not always. It would be easy to decide the old teaching was a medieval scruple the modern economy outgrew.

This essay argues that the church was right to change its mind about one kind of loan and wrong to forget the other. The distinction running through Scripture and the usury tradition is between the loan that serves an enterprise and the loan that feeds on a need. The merchant borrowing for a ship and the farmer borrowing for seed are using credit to build something, and a lender who shares the risk may fairly share the gain. The poor neighbor borrowing to eat is trying to get to next week, and the Torah forbade anyone to turn his getting there into income. The payday loan sits almost exactly on that line, which is why the Bible's oldest money laws speak to it more directly than to nearly anything else in modern finance.

Companion essays take up [money and wealth](/writing/what-the-bible-actually-says-about-money-and-wealth), [fair wages](/writing/theology-fair-wages), [the question of enough](/writing/theology-of-enough) and [the Jubilee's limits on debt](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded). This one stays with the loan. Nothing here is financial advice; if you're caught in a cycle of short-term loans, a nonprofit credit counselor or a local credit union can look at your actual numbers with you.

## What the law of Moses forbade, and to whom

The first law on lending in the Bible names its borrower before it names its rule. "If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor, you shall not be like a moneylender to him, and you shall not exact interest from him" (Exodus 22:25, ESV). A lender could take a pledge, but a poor man's cloak had to go back before sundown, and the reason is given in the voice of God: "for that is his only covering, and it is his cloak for his body; in what else shall he sleep? And if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate" (Exodus 22:27, ESV). The law knows the collateral is a man's night of sleep.

The word for interest is *neshek*, from a verb meaning to bite, and Leviticus sets the rule inside its chapter on the Jubilee. "If your brother becomes poor and cannot maintain himself with you, you shall support him as though he were a stranger and a sojourner, and he shall live with you" (Leviticus 25:35, ESV). Then the command itself: "Take no interest from him or profit, but fear your God, that your brother may live beside you" (Leviticus 25:36, ESV). The prohibition rests on the fear of God, not on a theory of money, and its goal is that your brother may live beside you. Two verses later the law gives its deepest reason, that the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt (Leviticus 25:38). A people who had been squeezed by a system were not to build a small Egypt among themselves.

Deuteronomy broadens the rule and then makes an exception. "You shall not charge interest on loans to your brother, interest on money, interest on food, interest on anything that is lent for interest" (Deuteronomy 23:19, ESV). And then: "You may charge a foreigner interest, but you may not charge your brother interest, that the LORD your God may bless you in all that you undertake in the land that you are entering to take possession of it" (Deuteronomy 23:20, ESV). The word for foreigner is *nokri*, not *ger*. The *ger* was the sojourner living among Israel, often poor; the *nokri* was the outsider passing through, most often a trader, doing business rather than seeking relief. Many commentators read the exception in that light: Israel could lend on commercial terms to a foreign merchant, but not to a neighbor in need. The line runs less between us and them than between trade and distress.

The next chapter keeps the lender out of the borrower's house altogether: "When you make your neighbor a loan of any sort, you shall not go into his house to collect his pledge" (Deuteronomy 24:10, ESV). He has to stand outside and wait, a rule that will matter when we come to how a payday loan gets repaid.

Psalm 15 asks who may dwell on God's holy hill, and part of the answer is the one "who does not put out his money at interest and does not take a bribe against the innocent. He who does these things shall never be moved" (Psalm 15:5, ESV). Ezekiel's righteous man "does not lend at interest or take any profit, withholds his hand from injustice, executes true justice between man and man" (Ezekiel 18:8, ESV). His son who does the opposite is judged in the hardest words of the chapter: he "lends at interest, and takes profit; shall he then live? He shall not live" (Ezekiel 18:13, ESV). The prophet puts the lender's profit in the same list as oppression and idolatry, not among the small sins.

Nehemiah shows the law broken and then enforced. After the exile, famine and the Persian tax had driven families to mortgage their fields and see their children taken into debt servitude. The creditors were the nobles and officials of Judah, and Nehemiah confronted them in public: "You are exacting interest, each from his brother" (Nehemiah 5:7, ESV). Then: "Moreover, I and my brothers and my servants are lending them money and grain. Let us abandon this exacting of interest" (Nehemiah 5:10, ESV). The governor put himself inside the accusation. Then he demanded restitution, not a change of heart alone: the fields, the houses and the percentage returned that same day (Nehemiah 5:11).

Jesus pushes the lender past the law's floor: "But love your enemies, and do good, and lend, expecting nothing in return" (Luke 6:35, ESV). Yet in the parable of the talents the master scolds the timid servant because he could at least have put the money with the bankers, "and at my coming I should have received what was my own with interest" (Matthew 25:27, ESV). The parable is not a lesson in banking, but it knows commercial interest as ordinary and does not treat every return on money as the bite. The distinction was already in the canon. The church would spend fifteen centuries trying to say it well.

## Fifteen centuries of arguing about interest

The early church read these texts as binding, and the medieval schoolmen would later read them alongside Aristotle, who had argued in the *Politics* that making money breed money was the most unnatural way of getting wealth. In 325 the Council of Nicaea, in its seventeenth canon, ordered that clergy who lent at interest were to be deposed. The canon cites the psalm and names the going rate, one percent a month. It bound clergy, not everyone, but it set the direction for a millennium.

Ambrose of Milan, in the late fourth century, read Deuteronomy's *nokri* as the enemy one might lawfully fight, concluding in effect that where there is a right of war there is a right of usury. Benjamin Nelson traced the result in *The Idea of Usury* (1949): a double ethic of brotherhood inside and permitted exploitation outside. Jews, barred in much of Europe from land and the guilds, were left with moneylending as one of the few trades open to them and then despised by Christians for practicing it. That belongs in any honest account. Nelson's larger thesis was that the early modern era dissolved the double standard by making every man at once a brother and an other, so that one rule applied to all and the special protection owed the poor brother thinned out.

Thomas Aquinas gave the classic argument in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, question 78). Money, like wine, is used up in its use, so to charge for the principal and again for its use is to sell the same thing twice, or to sell what doesn't exist. That argument did not survive the arrival of capital markets. Two other things Aquinas said in the same question have aged better. He granted that human law may leave some usury unpunished, not because it is just but because a law that suppressed every sin would hinder many goods. And he held that a person in real need may borrow from a usurer without sin, because he is not sharing in the lender's wrong but using it for a good end. The sin belongs to the one who sets the terms, not the one with no better choice. A sermon on payday lending that shames the borrower has already left the tradition it claims.

The church did not only prohibit. It built. In the fifteenth century Franciscan preachers in Italy founded *montes pietatis*, charitable pawn banks that lent small sums to the poor at a low charge meant to cover costs. The first is usually dated to Perugia in 1462. Dominican critics attacked them as usury in a friar's habit. In 1515 the Fifth Lateran Council settled it: the *montes* were lawful, because a moderate charge for real expenses, taken without profit, is not usury. It defined usury as gain sought from lending itself, without labor, cost or risk. The medieval church's answer to predatory lending was, in part, a better lender.

John Noonan's *The Scholastic Analysis of Usury* (1957) showed how the doctrine developed long before anyone announced a change. Theologians came to allow compensation for a lender's real loss and then for a real gain forgone, and partnerships that shared risk had always been permitted. By the time John Calvin wrote his letter on usury, usually dated 1545, the ground had moved. Calvin argued that the Mosaic prohibition belonged to Israel's civil polity and was not a timeless ban on all interest, and that a charge for the use of money in business is no more unjust than rent on a field. But he hedged it on every side: no interest from the poor, none beyond what law and equity allow, none that serves the lender alone. Calvin opened the door to commercial credit, and he tried to keep the poor on the protected side of the threshold.

Adam Smith, in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), defended a legal maximum rate a little above the lowest market rate, reasoning that a high cap would send money to reckless borrowers and crowd out careful ones. Jeremy Bentham answered him in *Defence of Usury* (1787): no free adult should be barred from borrowing on terms he judged worth it, and caps hurt the people who most needed credit. That exchange is the modern payday debate in eighteenth-century dress, and the church, which had argued the question for fourteen hundred years, had largely stopped taking part. The older question, what a loan does to a poor neighbor, went quiet.

## What a payday loan actually is

The product is simple. A borrower with a checking account and a pay stub writes a postdated check or authorizes the lender to withdraw from the account, and walks out with a few hundred dollars. On the next payday the loan and its fee come due in one lump. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has pointed out that a common fee of fifteen dollars for every hundred borrowed works out, over a two-week term, to an annual percentage rate of almost four hundred percent. Defenders reply, fairly, that an annual rate is a strange measure for a two-week loan. That objection holds only if the loan lasts two weeks.

It usually doesn't. The first of the Pew Charitable Trusts' *Payday Lending in America* reports (2012), built on a national survey, estimated that about twelve million American adults use payday loans in a year, and that the average borrower takes out eight loans of about $375 and pays about $520 in fees over the year. Most first-time borrowers used the money for recurring bills such as rent, utilities and food, not a one-time emergency. In 2014 a CFPB study of millions of loans found that more than four in five payday loans were rolled over or followed by another loan within fourteen days. The borrower who can't repay it all out of one paycheck pays the fee, takes a fresh loan, and starts the clock again. The fee recurs. The principal stays.

That is the debt trap, and the phrase is not a slogan. On the CFPB's own data, much of the lending goes to borrowers caught in long chains of repeat loans, so the lender's best customer is the one who can never leave. A loan that serves an enterprise is repaid out of what it helps build. A loan made against a paycheck that was already too small profits from exactly the gap it claims to bridge.

Now go back to Deuteronomy 24:10. The postdated check and the electronic authorization are the modern pledge, and they put the lender inside the house. On payday it doesn't stand at the door and wait; it reaches into the account and takes its money first, before the rent and the groceries, and when the account is short, repeated withdrawal attempts can pile overdraft fees on top. The Torah kept the creditor outside so the debtor could decide, with dignity, what to hand over. The modern arrangement puts the lender at the head of the household's line.

In 2006 Congress passed the Military Lending Act, capping the annual rate on payday and certain other high-cost loans to active-duty service members and their families at thirty-six percent (a protection the Defense Department later widened to most consumer credit), after the Pentagon reported that high-cost lenders were clustering around bases and harming readiness. The civilian poor received no such national protection. In 2017, under its first director, Richard Cordray, the CFPB issued a rule that would have required lenders to make a reasonable determination that a borrower could repay without reborrowing, and would have limited lenders to two failed attempts to withdraw from a borrower's account before seeking fresh permission. In 2020, under Director Kathleen Kraninger, the Bureau rescinded the ability-to-repay provisions, the heart of the rule, and kept the limits on repeated withdrawals, which litigation then held up for years. Meanwhile some states capped rates low enough that storefront payday lending effectively disappeared, and others allowed it with few limits. In 2016 South Dakota, hardly a progressive state, voted by roughly three to one to cap the rates its licensed lenders, payday lenders included, could charge at thirty-six percent.

## The best case for the storefront

The defense of payday lending deserves to be heard in the form its defenders would sign, because parts of it are true. The need for small, fast credit is real. A bank won't make a three-hundred-dollar loan to someone with a thin credit file, and the alternatives are often worse than the fee: overdraft charges, a late fee on the rent, a job lost for want of a car repair. Lisa Servon, a scholar of urban policy who worked behind the counter of a check casher and later a payday lender to study the business from inside, argued in *The Unbanking of America* (2017) that many customers choose these services with open eyes because mainstream banks have treated them worse, with hidden fees, holds on deposits and indifference. Her point is that the people who use them are not fools, and that critics who have never been refused by a bank should be slow to sneer at the only door on the block that was open.

The evidence from bans gives the argument its sharpest edge. Donald Morgan and Michael Strain studied what happened after Georgia and North Carolina outlawed payday lending in the mid-2000s. In *Payday Holiday* (2008), a Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report, they found that households in Georgia bounced more checks, complained more often to the Federal Trade Commission about lenders and debt collectors, and filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy at higher rates than households in states where payday credit was still available; North Carolina households, they reported, fared about the same. Their reading was that payday credit, for all its cost, beat the substitutes. Behind that finding stands Bentham's old argument: a cap doesn't abolish the need; it pushes it toward pawnshops, title loans that take the car, and unlicensed lenders. Better a legal bite than an illegal one, the argument runs, and better either than the lights going out.

That case is not stupid. It is also not the whole story, because the evidence is divided. Brian Melzer, comparing households by how close they lived to a state where payday loans were legal, reported in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (2011) that access to payday loans made it harder, not easier, for low- and moderate-income households to keep up with mortgage, rent and utility bills, and was associated with delays in needed medical care. Honest readers will hold the studies in tension rather than pick the one that flatters them.

The steelman also leans on a picture of the borrower that the product's own design doesn't support. A payday loan requires a checking account and a regular paycheck, so its customers are mostly not the unbanked but the banked and underwater, working people whose income is too thin for their bills. The one-time emergency is real, but Pew found it was the minority case. The loan priced for a crisis is sold, in practice, to cover the rent, again and again. And the claim that the terms are freely agreed runs into what Aquinas already saw: consent given under pressure of need is real consent, which is why the borrower does not sin, but it does not make the terms just. The poor man in Leviticus 25 would have agreed to interest too. The law forbade the lender to ask.

So the question has to be split, because it is really two. The first binds every Christian: the poor are not a market segment to the God of Scripture but his named concern, and to profit from a neighbor's desperation is what the Torah calls the bite, what Ezekiel lists beside oppression, what Nehemiah made his own class give back. No Christian may build wealth on that or preach a stewardship that blames the bitten for the bite. The second question is about mechanism, and here faithful Christians genuinely disagree. Whether a thirty-six percent cap protects the poor or strands them, whether the answer is underwriting rules, postal banking, small-dollar lending by credit unions, or charitable funds: these are questions of prudence, the kind Aquinas assigned to the legislator rather than the moralist. The reader who fears rate caps more than storefronts is not defying Moses; he is weighing consequences. What he may not do is let the difficulty of the second question excuse him from the first.

## One measure for the right and the left

Leviticus gives the measure in a single clause: "that your brother may live beside you." Apply it to the right first, since that is where my own failure lives. There is a conservative Christianity that treats every family underwater as a discipline problem, has absorbed a deregulatory instinct so deeply that naming a predatory industry sounds like socialism, and keeps the stewardship sermon away from the storefront three blocks from the church. That was me, preaching budgets to people whose problem was not a budget. The measure asks of that preaching whether the brother ended up living beside us, or whether we explained to him why he couldn't. It judges the 2020 rescission too, which removed the one provision that asked whether a borrower could repay. To call that liberty is to forget whose liberty the Torah was guarding. Not all conservative churches have been silent, though: in 2014 the Southern Baptist Convention adopted a resolution criticizing predatory payday lending, and the South Dakota vote showed the conviction runs deep in places no one would call progressive.

Now apply the measure to the left, because it cuts there too. There is a Christian politics that votes for the rate cap, shares the article about predatory lending, and has never once lent a struggling neighbor two hundred dollars at no interest. It hands the whole covenant obligation to the state and calls the handoff justice. The Torah didn't tell Israel to petition Pharaoh for a rate cap. It told the people of God, with their own money, not to exact interest, and to open their hand. A church that lobbies against usury while its benevolence fund couldn't cover a member's car repair has named the sin and declined the alternative. And a movement that celebrates a ban without asking where the borrowers went next hasn't reckoned with Morgan and Strain. The measure asks the activist what it asked the preacher: where is your brother living now?

The distinction between [charity and justice](/writing/charity-is-not-justice-the-difference) matters here, but the tradition never let either excuse the other. The church that condemned usury also built the *montes pietatis*, and the modern credit union has the same double root. In 1864 Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen, a Christian mayor in the Rhineland troubled by the rural poor's dependence on moneylenders, helped found a cooperative lending society in Heddesdorf. In 1900 Alphonse Desjardins, a devout Catholic in Lévis, Quebec, founded a savings and credit cooperative there, and in 1908 helped parishioners in Manchester, New Hampshire, organize St. Mary's Bank, generally counted the first American credit union. The credit union was, among other things, the church's answer to the loan shark, built with the parish's own savings.

That answer is still available. Federal credit unions can offer Payday Alternative Loans, small-dollar loans introduced in 2010 and expanded in 2019, with an annual rate capped at twenty-eight percent and an application fee of no more than twenty dollars. Not every credit union offers them, but they're built to be repaid, not rolled. Mehrsa Baradaran argued in *How the Other Half Banks* (2015) that something like the old Postal Savings System, which ran from 1911 to 1967, could return to serve people the banks have left, a contested proposal Christians can argue in good faith. Some congregations and church-founded nonprofits have gone further, with funds that refinance a member's payday debt at no interest and stay with the borrower while it is repaid. These are small things. So was the first *mons pietatis* in Perugia. What most congregations lack is not the means but the imagination to see that Leviticus 25 was addressed to them, and the humility to let a neighbor say out loud that he can't make the rent. Our silence about the lender has been matched by silence about what we could do instead, and the second silence is one we can end without waiting for Congress.

## The debt we asked to have forgiven

Every week, in churches that have never said a word about the storefront, Christians pray the prayer Jesus taught: "and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (Matthew 6:12, ESV). Matthew's word is the ordinary word for money owed. Jesus made the ledger speak of grace and bound the two releases together, so that whoever asks for his own debt to be forgiven confesses in the same breath that he has forgiven others. We have learned to pray the second half as a metaphor. The Torah never let it be one.

The gospel is not a lending policy. It is the news that the one who owed nothing paid what we could not. "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). Paul wrote that sentence in the middle of raising money for poor Christians in Jerusalem. He could not take up a collection without explaining it by the cross. A church that has received that grace and then treats its neighbor's need as a business opportunity, or as someone else's business, has misunderstood what it was given.

Repentance starts with the truth about ourselves. Most of us are not payday lenders, but many of us have looked away from them, preached around them, owned funds without asking what they held, and assumed the people in the storefront line had made worse choices than we had. Some of us have stood in that line and never told the church, because we didn't think the church would understand. Both silences have the same root. We made money a private matter, and the Bible never let it be one.

The history in this essay doesn't end with the church winning the argument. It ends with the church ceasing to make it. Christians argued about interest for fifteen centuries because they believed God cared whether a loan was love or predation. Calvin was right that money can work honestly in trade, and the Fifth Lateran Council was right that lending to the poor at cost is mercy; neither imagined a Christian people content to let the poor borrow at four hundred percent and call it the market. The line Leviticus drew has not moved. We have only stopped looking at it.

Nehemiah's assembly ended with an oath, and the people said amen and did what they had promised (Nehemiah 5:13). The storefront is still there on Monday, and so is the church three blocks away with its benevolence fund and its weekly prayer about debts. The question Scripture leaves is not whether we hold an opinion about interest rates. It is whether, when our brother cannot maintain himself, he will find a way to live beside us, and whether we will be the ones who made the way.
