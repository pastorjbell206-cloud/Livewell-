Ask a room of churchgoers what the kingdom of God is, and the answers usually fall into three piles. Some will say heaven, the place the soul goes after death. Some will say a better society, the justice we're supposed to build or the nation we're supposed to reclaim. Some will say it's something inward, the peace of God ruling a quiet heart. Each answer holds a piece of the truth, and each would have puzzled the people who first heard the phrase from Jesus. That matters, because the kingdom was the topic of his preaching. Mark's Gospel skips the manger and the genealogy and opens on a grown man walking into Galilee after John the Baptist's arrest with one announcement: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ESV). As Mark tells it, the first word of the public ministry is not love, or grace, or even God. It is kingdom.

We have spent a long time making that word smaller than he meant it. The skeptic has noticed that the Christians who talk most about the kingdom often mean their party's platform, or their own afterlife, or their own feelings, and he's right to suspect that a word stretched over all three may mean nothing. The doubting Christian has prayed "your kingdom come" thousands of times without being sure what she was asking for. Both deserve to know what Jesus meant, where the idea came from, and why the answer cuts against every comfortable version of Christianity, mine included.

The claim is simple to state and takes the rest of the essay to earn. The kingdom of God is God's own reign, promised through Israel's prophets and breaking into the world through Jesus of Nazareth. It is neither a place we go nor a society we build. It has come in the king and has not yet come in its fullness, and it arrives, to everyone's surprise, by way of a cross.

## What the Word Kingdom Actually Means

English misleads us here. "Kingdom" suggests a map, with borders and territory. The Greek word in the Gospels is *basileia*, and behind it stands the Hebrew *malkut* and its Aramaic cousin, the word Jesus most likely spoke. All three lean first toward reign rather than realm: the fact of someone ruling, and only secondarily the land ruled. George Eldon Ladd spent much of his career at Fuller Seminary pressing this point on a church that kept hearing real estate where the text meant sovereignty. When Jesus says the kingdom of God is at hand, he isn't giving directions to the afterlife. He is saying that God is about to act as king.

Matthew usually says "kingdom of heaven," and generations of readers have taken that as proof that the kingdom is a place above the clouds. Many scholars read it as a reverent Jewish way of speaking of God without naming him, the way we still say "Heaven knows." Whatever else Matthew's phrase carries, the parallel sayings show it names the same reality as Luke's. Matthew has "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:3, ESV), and Luke has "Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God" (Luke 6:20, ESV). Heaven in Matthew's phrase names the source of the reign, not the address of the reader's soul.

The other key word in Mark's sentence carried just as much freight. "Gospel," *euangelion*, was the empire's word before it was the church's. A calendar inscription set up at Priene in Asia Minor around 9 BC celebrated the birthday of Caesar Augustus as the beginning of good news for the world. When Jesus announces the gospel of God's kingdom in a province run by Caesar's governors, he is using the language of royal proclamation, the herald's cry that a king has taken the throne. His hearers would have heard a claim about who is in charge.

## The King Israel Was Waiting For

Those hearers did not come with empty ears. For centuries Israel had sung God's kingship. "The LORD reigns; he is robed in majesty" (Psalm 93:1, ESV). "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and your dominion endures throughout all generations" (Psalm 145:13, ESV). The enthronement psalms (47, 93 and 96 through 99) confess that the God of Abraham is king over the nations, not merely over Israel's hills.

The trouble was that history kept contradicting the hymnbook. In 587 BC Babylon burned Jerusalem and the temple. The exiles came home under Persian permission after 538, but to a province, not a kingdom. Alexander arrived in 332. In 167 BC Antiochus IV set up pagan worship in the temple itself. In 63 BC Pompey walked into Jerusalem, and Rome never really left. To sing "The LORD reigns" through those centuries was to sing against the evidence, and the question beneath Israel's prayers was when God would finally act as the king the psalms said he was.

The prophets answered with images the Gospels lean on. In Daniel 2, Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a statue of four metals, successive empires, and watches as "a stone was cut out by no human hand" and struck it, grinding it to dust, until the stone became a mountain filling the earth (Daniel 2:34-35, ESV). The interpretation is plain: "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed" (Daniel 2:44, ESV). The phrase that matters is "no human hand." God's kingdom does not rise out of the succession of empires as the next and better one. It comes from outside the succession and ends it. Daniel 7 makes the same point with beasts. Four monstrous creatures rise from the sea, and then "with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man," and "to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom" (Daniel 7:13-14, ESV). The empires are beastly. The kingdom God gives is human. It is no accident that "Son of Man" became Jesus' favored way of speaking of himself, or that before the high priest he claimed Daniel's vision outright: "you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:62, ESV).

The verse standing closest behind Mark 1:15 is addressed to exiles in Babylon: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace, who brings good news of happiness, who publishes salvation, who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns'" (Isaiah 52:7, ESV). Picture a runner crossing the hills toward a ruined city with news that the exile is over and God himself is returning to Zion as king. N. T. Wright argues in *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996) that this is the frame for Jesus' whole ministry: he announced, and then enacted, the return of Israel's God to reign and the defeat of the real enemy behind the empires. Some scholars fairly think Wright makes the exile theme carry more than the sources will bear, but the link to Isaiah 52 doesn't depend on his whole thesis, and it holds a detail that matters later. The very next passage, beginning at 52:13, is the song of the servant who was "pierced for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5, ESV). The herald's news that God reigns and the servant's suffering sit on the same page.

## A Century of Arguing Over One Sentence

Modern scholarship on the kingdom began, in effect, as an argument inside a German family. Late nineteenth-century Protestant theology, shaped by Albrecht Ritschl, understood the kingdom as an ethical community, humanity gradually united in love as people acted on Jesus' moral vision. Adolf von Harnack's hugely popular lectures, published in 1900 as *What Is Christianity?*, turned it inward: the kingdom came as God's rule entered the individual soul, and he gathered Jesus' teaching under three heads, among them the fatherhood of God and the infinite value of the human soul.

The first crack came from Ritschl's own son-in-law. Johannes Weiss argued in *Jesus' Proclamation of the Kingdom of God* (1892) that the kingdom Jesus preached was nothing like a moral society. It was apocalyptic, future and entirely God's act, a sudden intervention that would end the present age. Albert Schweitzer pressed Weiss's reading to its limit in *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906), in what came to be called consistent eschatology. Jesus, he argued, expected the end imminently. He told the twelve, "you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes" (Matthew 10:23, ESV), and when that did not happen he went to Jerusalem to force the end by his own suffering. In Schweitzer's famous image, Jesus threw himself on the wheel of the world to make it turn, and it turned and crushed him.

That position deserves its full strength. Schweitzer took seriously sayings comfortable readers skip, including "there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God after it has come with power" (Mark 9:1, ESV). Nor was he trying to destroy faith; the man who concluded Jesus had misjudged the timing went to Lambaréné in French Equatorial Africa in 1913 as a doctor, believing Jesus' call to follow still addressed him. The skeptic's question in its sharpest form is Schweitzer's: if the kingdom came, why does the world look like this?

C. H. Dodd answered from the opposite side. In *The Parables of the Kingdom* (1935) he argued for realized eschatology: in Jesus' ministry the end had not been postponed but had arrived. He read "at hand" as "has come," and leaned on sayings like "if it is by the finger of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20, ESV). He defined a parable as a vivid picture from ordinary life that leaves the mind in enough doubt about its application "to tease it into active thought," and he read the parables as addressed to the crisis of Jesus' own ministry. Dodd saw what Schweitzer could not, that the Gospels present the kingdom as already at work. His weakness mirrored Schweitzer's. The future sayings, and the plain petition "your kingdom come," strained against his system.

In America a Baptist pastor had meanwhile carried the kingdom out of Harnack's soul and into the tenements. Walter Rauschenbusch served a German immigrant congregation on the edge of New York's Hell's Kitchen from 1886 and concluded that a gospel which saved souls while leaving untouched the structures that crushed them had forgotten Jesus' central message. *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) launched the Social Gospel, and in *A Theology for the Social Gospel* (1917) he described the kingdom as humanity organized according to the will of God. His defenders rightly note that he was no naive optimist about human nature; he wrote of a "kingdom of evil," sin gathered into institutions that outlive the individuals who serve them. But the movement's confidence that the kingdom would grow through social progress did not survive the First World War, which was still being fought when he died in 1918. H. Richard Niebuhr, in *The Kingdom of God in America* (1937), summed up the liberal Protestantism that followed in one sentence: "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross."

A very different reading was spreading through evangelical churches. Dispensationalism, taught by John Nelson Darby from the 1830s and carried into millions of homes by the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), held that Jesus offered Israel the literal kingdom promised to David, that Israel refused it, and that it was postponed until his return, with the church age as a parenthesis. Its strongest form deserves respect. It refuses to dissolve God's promises to a particular people into metaphor, because "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29, ESV), and it guards against every human attempt to build the kingdom by insisting that only God brings it. Its cost was a kingdom pushed almost wholly into the future, apart from what its teachers called the kingdom's mystery form in the present age. Some classic dispensational teachers, Scofield's notes and Lewis Sperry Chafer among them, gave even the Sermon on the Mount its primary application in the coming kingdom age, while granting it a moral application to Christians now. Craig Blaising and Darrell Bock, in *Progressive Dispensationalism* (1993), moved the tradition toward reading the Davidic kingdom as already inaugurated in Christ's enthronement at the Father's right hand.

The synthesis most evangelical scholars now hold came from several directions. At Princeton, Geerhardus Vos argued as early as 1903 that Jesus spoke of the kingdom as both present and future, and in *The Pauline Eschatology* (1930) he drew his diagram of two ages: the age to come has arrived in Christ's resurrection while this age runs on until his return, and believers live in the overlap. Oscar Cullmann gave it its best-known picture in *Christ and Time* (1946): a war's decisive battle can be won on D-Day while the fighting continues until V-Day. Ladd's *The Presence of the Future* (1974) made the full case. Weiss and Schweitzer were right that the kingdom is God's own end-time act, not a human achievement. Dodd was right that it had truly arrived in Jesus. Each was wrong to deny the other.

This essay lands there, as both earlier versions of it did, and it helps to name the order of the question. That Jesus is king, and that "his kingdom will have no end," as the creed of 381 confesses with Luke 1:33, is the confessional floor every orthodox church shares. How the future unfolds, including the millennium of Revelation 20 that divides dispensational, premillennial, amillennial and postmillennial Christians, is a second-order question on which believers can disagree and remain brothers and sisters. And the already-and-not-yet reading has not fully answered Mark 9:1 and Matthew 10:23. Readings that find their fulfillment in the Transfiguration, the resurrection, Pentecost or the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 are each plausible, and none has closed the question. Schweitzer's challenge has been met better than he thought it could be. It hasn't been made to disappear.

## Seed, Leaven, and a Prayer

The best evidence for the already and not yet is the way Jesus chose to talk. He taught the kingdom mostly in parables, and they keep returning to one shape. In Mark 4 a sower scatters seed that mostly fails and then yields beyond reckoning. A farmer plants and "sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows; he knows not how" (Mark 4:27, ESV). A mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, becomes a shrub where birds can nest (Mark 4:30-32). Matthew 13 adds leaven that a woman "took and hid in three measures of flour, till it was all leavened" (Matthew 13:33, ESV), a field where an enemy's weeds grow up with the wheat until the harvest (Matthew 13:24-30), and a treasure worth selling everything to buy.

The kingdom is present, really in the ground and the dough, and it is hidden and small, its fullness waiting for a harvest no human hand brings. Jesus told the disciples that "the secret of the kingdom of God" had been given to them (Mark 4:11, ESV), and the secret seems to be exactly this: the reign Daniel saw filling the earth like a mountain had arrived as a seed. It came like a seed and not like an army. The parables answered Schweitzer's question long before he asked it.

The Lord's Prayer holds the same shape in one breath: "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10, ESV). The petitions interpret each other, as Hebrew poetry often does. God's kingdom comes wherever his will is done on earth as it already is in heaven. The prayer assumes the kingdom has not fully come, or we would not ask. It also asks for its coming here, on earth, so it is neither an escape prayer nor a program. Whoever prays it asks God to reign in the world, and in the same words consents to be reigned over.

Two sayings are often lifted out of this pattern, and both need their setting back. Asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom would come, Jesus answered, "The kingdom of God is not coming in ways that can be observed, nor will they say, 'Look, here it is!' or 'There!' for behold, the kingdom of God is in the midst of you" (Luke 17:20-21, ESV). The Greek, *entos hymōn*, can mean "within you," as the King James Version has it and as Leo Tolstoy titled his 1894 book, and that is the ordinary sense of *entos*. The case for "in the midst of you," which the ESV adopts while footnoting the alternative, rests on context: Jesus is speaking to Pharisees whose hearts he is hardly praising, and the passage that follows concerns the Son of Man's public appearing. The better reading is that the kingdom was among them because the king was standing among them. But even the reader who prefers "within" should notice that nothing in Luke turns the kingdom into a private mood. Wherever it is, it has a face.

The other is John 18:36, spoken to Pilate: "My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would have been fighting, that I might not be delivered over to the Jews. But my kingdom is not from the world" (ESV). The preposition is *ek*, "from" or "out of." Jesus names the source of his kingdom, not its location. It does not arise from the world's way of gaining power, which is why his servants don't fight. That doesn't make it indifferent to the world. It enters the world from elsewhere, like Daniel's stone, and refuses the world's weapons when it arrives.

Paul rarely uses the phrase, but when he does it cuts the same way. To Roman house churches split over food laws he writes, "For the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit" (Romans 14:17, ESV). In context it rebukes Christians who had made their side of a secondary dispute the badge of belonging, mistaking a faction's markers for the reign of God. Paul says believers have already been "transferred to the kingdom of his beloved Son" (Colossians 1:13, ESV), and he looks to the end, when Christ "delivers the kingdom to God the Father after destroying every rule and every authority and power" (1 Corinthians 15:24, ESV). Already transferred, not yet delivered.

## Two Ways to Shrink a Kingdom

The two great misreadings turn out to be closer relatives than their defenders would like. The first shrinks the kingdom into a private inner experience or the destination of a private soul, and it comes in liberal and conservative dress. Harnack's version was sophisticated: God's rule in the individual heart. The American evangelical version is simpler and far more common: the kingdom is heaven, the place you go if you've prayed the right prayer, and the present world is a waiting room. I held that version for years, even after I came to faith. The kingdom was my insurance policy, paid up and filed away, and I couldn't understand why a faith I believed was true left so little mark on how I lived. The reason was the theology. A kingdom that exists only inside me or only after me asks nothing of my Tuesday. What this reading gets right is real: the kingdom claims the heart, and there is a life to come. What it cannot account for is most of what Jesus did. He healed bodies, fed crowds, confronted the temple's economy and taught his followers to pray for God's will on earth.

The second turns the kingdom into a political program, and it is just as old. In 336 Eusebius of Caesarea delivered an oration for the thirtieth year of Constantine's reign that praised the emperor's rule as an earthly image of the heavenly monarchy. The empire had stopped persecuting the church, and the church's great historian came close to calling the empire the kingdom. The temptation has never left. On the right it takes the form of Christian nationalism, the conviction that a particular nation is God's chosen instrument and its laws and flag can carry the kingdom. A more rigorous cousin is R. J. Rushdoony's Christian Reconstruction, set out in *The Institutes of Biblical Law* (1973). Rushdoony distrusted the centralized state and looked for change through regeneration, family and church rather than seized power, but his program still measured the kingdom's advance by a society brought under the law of Scripture. On the left it takes the form of identifying the kingdom with a slate of progressive causes, so that "kingdom values" becomes a synonym for one party's platform and the Social Gospel's optimism returns, often without Rauschenbusch's own sense of how deep sin runs. Both respond to something true. The kingdom does concern bodies, bread, justice and power, and the prophets expected God's reign to touch the ground.

The same instrument judges both misreadings, and Jesus supplied it. When the crowd meant to "take him by force to make him king," he "withdrew again to the mountain by himself" (John 6:15, ESV). When his disciples argued about greatness, he told them the rulers of the Gentiles "lord it over them," but "it shall not be so among you," because "even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:42-45, ESV). The right and left versions of the kingdom-as-program both reach for the state's power to accomplish what they're sure God wants. The inward and heaven-only versions both retreat from the world God claims. Beneath the difference lies a common grammar. Each makes the kingdom something we possess or something we produce, an experience we have or an outcome we engineer, and in both cases the self stays quietly on the throne.

Scot McKnight names a version of this split in *Kingdom Conspiracy* (2014). He describes a "skinny jeans" reading that equates the kingdom with good work for justice in the public square and a "pleated pants" reading that equates it with God's saving rule in individual lives, and he argues that both have lost the word's concrete meaning, a king ruling a people. His answer is that the kingdom is the people of God under King Jesus, so kingdom mission is church mission. It guards well against baptizing every cause, but this essay stops short of it. Lesslie Newbigin's language in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) seems truer to the parables: the church is the sign, instrument and foretaste of the kingdom, a people living now under a reign the world can't yet see everywhere. But McKnight's objection should stand unanswered here. If the kingdom is larger than the church, what keeps the word from floating free and attaching itself to whatever we already wanted? The only guard I know is the one that answers both misreadings: the kingdom is defined by its king and by the way he chose to come.

## The Throne That Was a Cross

The crowds wanted a king who would do to Rome what Rome had done to them. They got one who let the empire kill him. Wright's deepest contribution may be his insistence that the crucifixion is not an interruption of the kingdom but the way it comes. On the cross the king fights the real enemy, which was never Rome. The real enemy is the sin and death that hold every human being in one grip, Roman and Galilean, skeptic and saint. That is why the announcement in Mark 1:15 carries a command. Repent is not religious throat-clearing. It is what you do when the true king arrives and you've been living as though the throne were empty.

Watch how the Gospels tell the end. The soldiers dress Jesus in purple, twist a crown of thorns and kneel in mockery (Mark 15:17-19). Pilate fixes a sign above the cross: "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" (John 19:19, ESV). It was meant as a joke at the expense of a beaten man, and the evangelists report it because the joke told the truth. This is how this king reigns. The herald of Isaiah 52 and the pierced servant of Isaiah 53 turn out to describe the same event. The kingdom meets the world's violence by absorbing it, exhausting it, and rising. "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV), and the empty tomb is the first square foot of the new creation.

That is also the New Testament's answer to the skeptic's question about the world's condition. "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9, ESV). The wheat and the weeds grow in one field because the harvest would end the season of mercy. Had the kingdom come in full the day Jesus announced it, the door would have closed on every one of us. That won't settle the argument for someone who doesn't believe, but it is a better answer than he has usually been given, and the parables show it wasn't invented later to cover an embarrassment.

It lands on the church before it lands on anyone else. If the church is the kingdom's sign, the world is entitled to ask whether anything in our common life looks like a place where God is actually king. I have to ask that of my own congregation and of myself, and the honest answer is rarely the one I'd like to give. We have offered the watching world a kingdom that looked like our afterlife plans, or our feelings, or our voting guide, and each time we shrank the reign of God to something we could manage, the shrinking was a way of keeping the throne. The empire I most need overthrown isn't in Washington or anywhere else. It's the one I run in my own chest.

So the kingdom of God is God reigning: promised to Israel in psalm and prophecy, announced in Galilee, shown in healings and parables, won on a cross and vindicated in an empty tomb, carried now by a smudged and forgiven people, and aimed at the day when every rule and authority is put under Christ's feet and the prayer is answered at last. The question it puts to us was never where we are going or what we can build. It was who reigns. Tomorrow morning someone will pray "your kingdom come" out of habit, and the words will still mean what they have always meant: a request that God take the throne, and the surrender of the one who asks. The king has come. The throne is no longer empty, and it was never ours.
