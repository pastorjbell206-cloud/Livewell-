Most of us meet December as a problem of logistics. The calendar fills, the money runs thinner than we planned, and underneath the errands a feeling moves that none of the errands touch. We call it stress. Sometimes it's grief, sharpened by an empty chair. Often it's harder to name, a sense that the season keeps promising something it never hands over, so that by the afternoon of the twenty-fifth, with the wrapping paper bagged at the curb, the house goes quiet in a way that feels less like rest than like a letdown.

The church has kept a season for that feeling for roughly fifteen centuries. It's called Advent, from the Latin *adventus*, a coming, the word the Latin church used for the Greek *parousia* that the New Testament gives to the return of Christ. Newcomers tend to assume Advent is a countdown to the manger. Then they hear its first Sunday's Gospel, Jesus speaking of the sun going dark and the stars falling, and wonder whether someone chose the wrong reading. I came to faith from atheism with no church calendar at all, and for anyone who arrives that way the season is awkward at first. But that reading is the most honest thing the church says about December.

**Advent is the season in which the church remembers the first coming of Christ in order to learn how to wait for the second, and the difference between those two kinds of waiting, one with a date on the calendar and one without, is the lesson a restless age most needs and least wants.** (The whole church year has its own essay, [What Is the Church Calendar?](/writing/why-the-church-has-a-calendar). This one stays with its first four weeks.)

## Where Advent Came From

Advent is younger than Easter and Christmas, and its beginnings are untidy. The feast of Christ's birth on 25 December is attested in Rome by the 330s. A season of preparation grew up around it later and unevenly. Thomas Talley's *The Origins of the Liturgical Year* (1986), long the standard account in English, separates what the sources say from what later piety wished they said, and shows that Advent has more than one root. A canon of a council at Saragossa in Spain, usually dated to 380, is reported as telling the faithful not to absent themselves from church but to attend daily from 17 December until Epiphany. Whether that describes anything like Advent, a preparation for Epiphany baptisms, or something else again is disputed. It's a sign, not a birth certificate.

Firmer ground lies in Gaul. Gregory of Tours, writing his *History of the Franks* near the end of the sixth century, lists the fasts that Perpetuus, bishop of Tours from about 460 to 490, had set for his church (Book 10, chapter 31), among them three fasts a week from the feast of St. Martin on 11 November until Christmas. The span roughly matched the forty days before Easter, and the season later came to be called St. Martin's Lent. A regional council at Mâcon, usually dated to 581, wrote a version of it into church law, directing fasts on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays between St. Martin's day and Christmas, though whether the canon bound the laity or chiefly the clergy is not certain. In Gaul, a people preparing to celebrate the birth of the Lord prepared by going without.

Rome came to the season by a different road and somewhat later. Roman Advent appears in the sixth century, and some of the oldest Roman service books provide for five Sundays of preparation. Tradition credits Gregory the Great, pope from 590 to 604, with settling it at four, and the sacramentary that carries his name has four; but sacramentaries were revised for generations, scholars still debate how much of that book is Gregory's, and the four-Sunday Advent became the Western norm only gradually. The attribution is a tradition, best held loosely. Rome's Advent was less a fast than a season of expectation, and over the following centuries the strands braided together, Roman structure with Gallic seriousness. Milan kept its own six-week Advent, as the Ambrosian rite still does, and the Eastern churches keep a forty-day fast before the Nativity.

The braid is still visible. The Gloria is held back through the season, saved for the angels at Christmas. The vestments turn violet, the color of penitence, and on the third Sunday, called Gaudete from its opening command to rejoice (Philippians 4:4), many churches lighten the violet to rose, as if the fast had caught sight of the feast. No council invented Advent and no pope decreed it whole. What unites the scattered evidence is that wherever the season appeared, it was a discipline. Nobody in the sixth century thought the weeks before the Nativity were for celebrating it early.

## Two Comings and the Four Last Things

If the history is tangled, the theology isn't. From its earliest Roman prayers, Advent looked two ways at once: back to the coming of Christ in the flesh, and forward to the coming the Nicene Creed confesses, that he "will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead." The church has always lived in the space between.

Gregory the Great knew that space from the inside. One of his *Forty Gospel Homilies*, on the signs in the sun and moon in Luke 21, was preached on an Advent Sunday to a Rome worn down by plague and war in the early 590s. Gregory believed the end was near and read the calamities around his hearers as its signs, but his sharper complaint was that they still loved the world: it had withered, he said in effect, and yet it went on flowering in their hearts. He summoned them to stop clinging to a world coming apart in their hands and to face the judge who was coming.

A custom long associated with the season, especially in English-speaking churches, gives the Sundays of Advent to the Four Last Things: death, judgment, heaven and hell. It was a custom, never a rule, but it named what the season had always known: the manger and the throne of judgment belong to one story. Thomas Cranmer's collect for the first Sunday of Advent in the 1549 Book of Common Prayer begins, in modernized spelling, "Almighty God, give us grace that we may cast away the works of darkness, and put upon us the armour of light." It goes on to set this mortal life, in which the Son came to visit us "in great humility," against the last day, when he will come again "in his glorious majesty" to judge the living and the dead, and it asks that we may then rise to the life immortal. Humility and majesty: the whole season in two nouns. Cranmer drew on the epistle for that Sunday: "The night is far gone; the day is at hand. So then let us cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light" (Romans 13:12, ESV).

Much of the modern church, liturgical and free alike, has quietly dropped the second half, and Advent became Christmas preparation. Fleming Rutledge, the Episcopal preacher whose sermons were gathered as *Advent: The Once and Future Coming of Jesus Christ* (2018), has pressed hard against that drift. She argues that Advent is first the season of the second coming, that it begins in the dark because the world is dark, and that Christian hope is credible only to someone willing to look squarely at evil. A skeptic can respect that, because it doesn't pretend the world is fine.

The lectionary still says it plainly. In the Revised Common Lectionary's year of Mark, the first Sunday of Advent reads from Mark 13, which begins with Jesus foretelling the temple's fall (Rome burned it in AD 70) and widens to the end of all things: "But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32, ESV). Then the command, the first word of Advent: "Be on guard, keep awake. For you do not know when the time will come" (Mark 13:33, ESV).

Jesus names the watches in which the master might return: evening, midnight, cockcrow, morning. Readers of Mark have long noticed that these are the hours of the passion that follows at once: the supper in the evening, Gethsemane in the night, Peter's denial at cockcrow, Pilate in the morning. The disciples who were told to stay awake fail within hours: "And he came and found them sleeping, and he said to Peter, 'Simon, are you asleep? Could you not watch one hour?'" (Mark 14:37, ESV). Mark leaves the discourse's last line pointed past the Twelve: "And what I say to you I say to all: Stay awake" (Mark 13:37, ESV).

That's where Advent puts us. Not with the faithful few who watched. With the sleepers, who were loved anyway and told again to keep awake.

## The Prophets Who Taught the Church to Wait

Advent's readings lean on Israel's prophets, and it's easy to hear them as predictions fitted to the manger like parts of a kit. Read in their settings, they're the words of a people who waited through catastrophe.

Isaiah 9 opens in Galilee, which the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III stripped from Israel around 733 BC (2 Kings 15:29). Into that humiliation the prophet speaks as though rescue had already come: "The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light; those who dwelt in a land of deep darkness, on them has light shone" (Isaiah 9:2, ESV). The verbs are past tense while the smoke is still in the air, because God's promise is as good as its keeping, and Matthew heard the verse fulfilled when Jesus began preaching in Galilee. Two chapters on, the house of David has been cut down like a forest: "There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse, and a branch from his roots shall bear fruit" (Isaiah 11:1, ESV). The prophet reaches behind the king to his father, an obscure man of Bethlehem, as if to say that when God renews a promise he starts from almost nothing.

Isaiah 40 speaks to exiles in Babylon after Jerusalem fell in 587 BC, and its first word is tenderness: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (Isaiah 40:1, ESV). Then a herald: "A voice cries: 'In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD; make straight in the desert a highway for our God'" (Isaiah 40:3, ESV). All four Gospels give that voice to John the Baptist, which is why he dominates the middle Sundays of Advent. The highway was for God's return to his people, and the Gospels say he came back walking.

The sharpest Advent text in Isaiah is a prayer, not a prophecy. Late in the book, a voice looking at the burned temple and the wilderness that Zion had become cries out: "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down, that the mountains might quake at your presence" (Isaiah 64:1, ESV). It's the prayer of people who have waited long enough to be angry. A few verses later the same voice turns the accusation inward: "We have all become like one who is unclean, and all our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment. We all fade like a leaf, and our iniquities, like the wind, take us away" (Isaiah 64:6, ESV). Advent prayer has that double edge. We ask God to come and set things right, and we confess that we're part of what needs setting right.

Malachi, the last book of the Old Testament in Protestant order, sharpens the edge. His hearers had a rebuilt temple, worship going through its motions, and a weariness with a God who seemed not to care. He promised them what they asked for, a messenger to prepare the way and the Lord they sought coming suddenly to his temple (Malachi 3:1). Then he asked what they hadn't thought to ask: "But who can endure the day of his coming, and who can stand when he appears? For he is like a refiner's fire and like fullers' soap" (Malachi 3:2, ESV). The book ends with a sunrise and a warning held together: "But for you who fear my name, the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2, ESV), and the promise of Elijah before the great and awesome day.

The church gathered these texts into one of the loveliest things it ever made. From 17 to 23 December, at evening prayer, the Roman rite sings a short antiphon with Mary's song, the Magnificat, each addressing Christ by a title from the prophets: O Wisdom; O Adonai, the Lord of the burning bush; O Root of Jesse (Isaiah 11); O Key of David (Isaiah 22); O Dayspring, the rising sun of Malachi; O King of the nations; O Emmanuel (Isaiah 7). They're attested by the ninth century, and their Latin initials read backward spell *ero cras*, "tomorrow I will be there," though whether by design is uncertain. Most English speakers know them through "O Come, O Come, Emmanuel," John Mason Neale's nineteenth-century translation of a Latin hymn built from several of them. In them a church that knows Christ has come prays in the voice of those who didn't yet know. The prophets taught the church to wait by waiting first.

## Luke's Waiting Room

Luke opens his Gospel in a room full of people waiting. An old priest named Zechariah has drawn the lot to burn incense in the temple, perhaps the only time in his life he'll do it, and outside the worshipers wait for him to come out and bless them. Luke records their impatience: "And the people were waiting for Zechariah, and they were wondering at his delay in the temple" (Luke 1:21, ESV). A crowd wondering at a delay describes the church in every century since.

An angel has told him that he and Elizabeth, old and childless, will have a son who fulfills Malachi's last promise: "And he will go before him in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, and the disobedient to the wisdom of the just, to make ready for the Lord a people prepared" (Luke 1:17, ESV). The last verse of Malachi had threatened utter destruction on a land where fathers and children were estranged. Luke's first promise of the new thing God is doing begins with the healing of that estrangement. For anyone who grew up without a father, that verse is not ornament. The coming of God, in Luke's telling, starts with the repair of the house. Zechariah doubted and was struck mute until the child was born, and when his tongue was loosed he sang of a dawn: "because of the tender mercy of our God, whereby the sunrise shall visit us from on high" (Luke 1:78, ESV). The man who couldn't believe the promise ends up singing it.

Mary's song is fiercer. Young, poor and carrying a child whose origin no one would believe, she sings, echoing Hannah in 1 Samuel 2, as if the world had already been turned over: "he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52, ESV), and "he has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich he has sent away empty" (Luke 1:53, ESV). Dietrich Bonhoeffer, preaching on the Magnificat in London on 17 December 1933, the third Sunday of Advent and less than a year after Hitler took power, told his hearers this was not the gentle, dreamy Mary of the paintings, and called her song the oldest Advent hymn and the most passionate, even revolutionary, one ever sung. The mighty were on their thrones in Berlin, and he would die at their hands.

Then Luke returns to the temple, where two people had waited their whole lives. Simeon was "righteous and devout, waiting for the consolation of Israel, and the Holy Spirit was upon him" (Luke 2:25, ESV). Anna, a widow of great age, had made her waiting a vocation: "She did not depart from the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" (Luke 2:37, ESV). When she sees the child, she speaks of him "to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem" (Luke 2:38, ESV).

Notice who does the waiting in Luke: an old priest, a barren woman, an unmarried girl, an old man near death, a widow. None had power, and none could make the promise come faster. But their waiting wasn't idle. Anna fasted and prayed, Simeon kept coming to the temple, and Zechariah served at the altar long after he'd stopped expecting anything. The Gallic bishops who set a fast before Christmas were closer to Luke than we are. Waiting for God is a practice, and it has a shape.

## Two Kinds of Waiting

Everything so far turns on a distinction that's easy to blur. Waiting for Christmas and waiting for the return of Christ are not the same act.

Waiting for Christmas is a countdown. The date is fixed, and it will arrive whether you pray or shop or do neither. There's nothing wrong with it; four candles lit one week at a time teach the body something argument can't. But a countdown asks nothing except patience, and when the date comes, the waiting is over. Waiting for the return of Christ has no date. Jesus refused to give one, and every attempt to supply one has ended in embarrassment. It can't be hastened or managed, and it's the arrival of the judge before whom, as Malachi asked, no one stands unaided. This waiting doesn't end on a date. It becomes a way of living, what Jesus called keeping awake.

Jürgen Moltmann named the distinction in *The Coming of God* (1995; English translation 1996), contrasting *futurum*, the future as what develops out of the present and can be forecast and produced, with *adventus*, the future as what comes toward the present from outside and can only be received. Christmas as the culture keeps it is *futurum*: we build it every year with our own hands. The return of Christ is *adventus*. It comes to us. Advent takes the one kind of waiting we're good at and uses it to train the kind we've nearly lost.

We've nearly lost it because we've built a world to abolish waiting, where any gap between wanting and having registers as a malfunction. December is that world's high feast. The retail season starts before Thanksgiving is cleared and ends on the twenty-fifth, just as the church's twelve days of Christmas begin. The market front-loads the celebration and ends in fatigue; the church begins in the dark and withholds the feast.

William Cavanaugh's *Being Consumed* (2008) explains why this is more than a quarrel about timing. Consumer culture, he argues, isn't mainly an excessive attachment to things but a kind of detachment, a desire trained to keep moving on because its rest would be bad for business. That's why the wrapping-paper afternoon feels flat: the season was built to renew the wanting, not satisfy it. Augustine named the deeper restlessness at the start of the *Confessions*, written around 400, when he told God that our hearts are restless until they rest in him. Advent refuses to anesthetize that ache, because it was always an ache for a Person.

So keeping Advent is a quiet resistance, and I'm implicated in what it resists. I've filled Decembers so full that no longing could get a word in, and called the noise the Christmas season. The resistance isn't a refusal of gifts or joy. It refuses the premise that desire must be satisfied the moment it appears, and it recovers the season's oldest form, the fast: something withheld, something given away, an hour of quiet the market can't sell back. Mary's song is the season's economics, the hungry filled and the rich sent away empty, sung in the one month when the economy most depends on our never feeling full.

Bonhoeffer knew waiting from a harder place. In a letter from Tegel prison to his friend Eberhard Bethge dated 21 November 1943, he likened life in a prison cell to Advent: one waits and hopes and busies oneself with things of no real consequence, and the door can only be opened from the outside. He was hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945, weeks before the war in Europe ended. His picture holds. We don't open the door. We keep watch by it.

The New Testament's answer to the ache of delay comes in 2 Peter, written to Christians already hearing scoffers ask where the promised coming was, since everything went on as it always had (2 Peter 3:4). Peter doesn't deny the delay. He reinterprets it: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9, ESV). The waiting that feels like God's absence is God's patience, and every day of delay is a day on which someone is still being sought.

That matters most to those for whom December is hardest, whose private grief the culture's brightness makes feel like failure. Advent begins in the dark and doesn't ask the grieving to pretend otherwise, and many churches hold a Longest Night service near the solstice for them. If the dark has become more than you can carry, don't carry it alone: in the United States you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour.

## Does a Christian Have to Keep Advent?

Many faithful Christians don't keep Advent, and their reasons deserve their strongest form. The Puritan objection to the calendar as a whole is traced in [the essay on the church year](/writing/why-the-church-has-a-calendar). The objection to Advent in particular runs like this. The New Testament commands watchfulness but never a season of it. "Stay awake" was spoken about every hour, and to give the return of Christ a season implies the rest of the year is off duty. The free-church Christian, Baptist or independent, would say the whole of life is Advent, and a liturgical season risks turning a daily readiness into an annual mood. Much of the modern Advent is also recent. The wreath is usually credited to Johann Hinrich Wichern, a Lutheran who ran a home for neglected boys in Hamburg and in 1839 hung candles on a cartwheel to answer the children's question of how long until Christmas. And the season has become merchandise, scented candles and calendars with a luxury product behind every door. Charles Spurgeon, the best-known Baptist preacher of the nineteenth century, put the principle plainly. Opening his sermon "Joy Born at Bethlehem" on the morning of 24 December 1871, he told his London congregation that he had no superstitious regard for times and seasons, and that he found no Scriptural warrant for keeping any day as the Saviour's birthday. Then he preached on the incarnation anyway, reasoning that it is lawful to meditate on it any day of the year and that other people's superstitions can't make such a meditation improper. A church that follows him isn't less faithful for it.

This is a second-order question. The creeds bind the church to confess that Christ came in the flesh and will come again in glory; they bind no one to a season in which to say so. Churches that keep Advent and churches that don't can recognize each other as faithful.

Each side has failed, and the failures deserve the same grief. The churches that kept Advent often kept the candles and lost the content, preaching nothing about the return of Christ that would disturb a suburban December. The free churches that ignored the season often preached the second coming more than anyone, and then turned it into a chart. William Miller's followers waited for Christ on 22 October 1844 and called the morning after the Great Disappointment, and the *Left Behind* novels, beginning in 1995, sold the return of Christ to millions as a thriller. One side domesticated the hope and the other sensationalized it. Both made it manageable, which is the one thing the return of Christ will not be.

Where this essay lands is where the essay on the calendar landed for the whole year: no church may bind a conscience to Advent, and no Christian who skips it is the poorer in faith for that alone. But most of us would be helped by keeping it, because we've lost the capacity to wait. The strongest objection hasn't been fully answered: a season for watching can become a substitute for watching. The only reply is that the season, kept honestly, points past itself. An Advent that ends on 24 December has failed. An Advent that sends people back into January more awake than it found them has done what it was for.

## Come, Lord Jesus

One of the oldest Christian prayers isn't in Greek. Paul ends his first letter to the Corinthians with a phrase he leaves in Aramaic, the language of the first believers in Jerusalem, *maranatha*, which the ESV renders "Our Lord, come!" (1 Corinthians 16:22, ESV). The *Didache*, a church manual usually dated to around the end of the first century, puts the same word into its prayers at the Lord's Supper. Before there were creeds or a single season of the church year, the church at its table was praying for its Lord to arrive. The Bible's last prayer is the same one: "He who testifies to these things says, 'Surely I am coming soon.' Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20, ESV).

It's worth asking whether we mean it. Malachi's question hasn't gone away: who can endure the day of his coming? A prayer for the arrival of the judge is a strange thing for sinners to pray, and much of our religion is arranged to keep him politely at a distance. We'd like him to come after we've set a few things right.

The answer to Malachi is the first advent followed to its end. The child didn't stay in the manger. He was handed over on the morning after the night his friends couldn't stay awake. The one who will come in glory to judge the living and the dead first came in humility and was judged, and on the cross he bore the judgment that makes his return something other than terror for those who belong to him. Then, on the first day of the week, the tomb was empty. The resurrection is the church's evidence that the second coming isn't a wish. He kept the first appointment, through death and out the other side, and he will keep the second.

That's why the church can pray *maranatha*, though not without trembling. We're asking the one who was judged in our place, who still bears the marks, to finish what he started: to set right what no election and no economy will set right, to raise the dead we've buried, and to bring the new heavens and new earth in which righteousness dwells.

Advent will come around again, with its violet and its prophets and its four candles, and it will end at a manger. The waiting it trains doesn't end there. In a world still dark, with the door opened only from the outside, the church lights one more candle and keeps its eyes on the door, not knowing the day or the hour. What we carry out of the season is not a feeling but a watch, kept through ordinary nights by people who have already failed it once and been told, again, to stay awake.
