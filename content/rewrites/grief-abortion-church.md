She may be sitting in the third row on Sunday morning, singing words she means, or teaching a Sunday school class, or rocking someone else's baby in the nursery. He may be the man two rows back who drove her to the clinic years ago, paid half, and has never said a word about it to anyone, God included. Every sermon on the sanctity of life and every offhand remark in the fellowship hall lands on them in a way the speaker never sees. They carry it into the one building that was supposed to be the place where nobody has to carry anything alone.

This essay is written to them first. If you've had an abortion, or were part of one, and have wondered whether there is forgiveness for it, the answer is yes, and it isn't a reluctant yes. But God's forgiveness doesn't work by telling you nothing happened. It takes what happened more seriously than you do, and then it does with it what you cannot do yourself.

If the weight of this has turned toward thoughts of ending your life, stop reading and call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, and tell a real person what you're carrying. Nothing here matters more than your staying alive. What follows is pastoral and theological writing, not medical or mental-health advice, and it can't replace a doctor or counselor who can sit with you in person.

## Why This Grief Has Nowhere to Go

Two answers dominate the public argument about abortion, and between them they leave almost no room for the person who lived through one.

The first says there is nothing to grieve. The pregnancy was tissue, the decision was health care, and sorrow is a leftover from a moral framework she has presumably outgrown. The instinct behind this answer deserves to be stated fairly: women have been shamed without mercy, and many who insist there is nothing to grieve are trying to shield them from a cruelty they have watched religious people inflict. The refusal of shame is a real good. But an account of abortion with no room for sorrow leaves the woman who does feel sorrow wondering what is wrong with her.

The second answer says the grief is deserved, the consequence of a moral failure and the proof of what the speaker already believed, so that comforting it would only soften the lesson. This too has a truth in it, since a conscience that aches is not always malfunctioning. But it turns a wound into a sentence and a person into evidence. The church, which ought to belong to neither camp, has too often joined the second, and sometimes, embarrassed by the second, the first.

The scholar of grief Kenneth Doka gave this kind of sorrow a name in *Disenfranchised Grief* (1989). He meant grief that cannot be openly acknowledged, publicly mourned or socially supported: the loss nobody sends a card for, because nobody is allowed to know it happened. Abortion fits his description almost exactly, and inside a church it fits twice over. There is no funeral, no meal brought to the door, no name spoken in the prayer time. The grief goes underground, and underground is where grief does its worst work. Nor is it rare in our pews. A 2015 Lifeway Research survey of women who had had an abortion found that roughly four in ten were attending church at least monthly when they ended the pregnancy.

The men are there too, usually more hidden still. Some pressured her, some paid, some waited in the car. Some weren't told until afterward, and some pleaded for the child and had no say. The culture tells a man it was never his business, and his own idea of manhood tells him to keep it that way, so his grief is disenfranchised twice. Whatever part he played, the forgiveness in this essay is addressed to him as fully as to her.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw the mechanism that keeps all of this hidden. In *Life Together* (1939), written after the Gestapo closed the underground seminary he led at Finkenwalde, he observed that a pious fellowship which permits no one to be a sinner forces everyone to hide their sin, so that people who pray and sing together stay alone in the very place they most need company. His remedy was confession spoken to a brother or sister, because a person left alone with his sin is alone in the deepest way there is. A church that preaches clearly about the sanctity of life and has no way to receive the person carrying an abortion has been theologically consistent and pastorally absent.

I have been that pastor. I have preached the conviction without building a church where the person most implicated by it could bring her actual life into the room, and I have led services where the political language was clear and the pastoral consequences were invisible to me. I didn't see the women, or the men, for whom those words were not abstract. The sermon may have been right. What was missing was a church in which someone could answer it honestly.

## What the Research on Abortion and Mental Health Shows

The research here has become a battlefield, each side accusing the other's studies of bias. In 2008 a task force of the American Psychological Association concluded that for an adult woman with an unplanned pregnancy, the relative risk of mental-health problems after a single elective first-trimester abortion is no greater than if she delivers that pregnancy. The same report said plainly that some women experience sadness, grief and feelings of loss afterward, and that some experience clinically significant depression or anxiety. Diana Greene Foster's *The Turnaway Study* (2020) followed nearly a thousand women over five years, comparing those who obtained an abortion with those turned away because they were past a clinic's gestational limit. On average the women who had abortions did not fare worse in mental health, most said years later that the decision had been right for them, and their feelings about it, relief and sadness and guilt alike, tended to fade over time.

Other researchers dispute these conclusions, and they are serious people. A long-running study of young people in Christchurch, New Zealand, led by David Fergusson, reported in 2006 higher rates of later mental-health problems among young women who had had abortions, even after adjusting for earlier circumstances. Priscilla Coleman's 2011 meta-analysis in the *British Journal of Psychiatry* reported a substantially elevated risk and drew sharp criticism of its methods. Critics of the Turnaway Study point to how many eligible women declined to take part and how many dropped out along the way. The proposed "post-abortion syndrome" has never been recognized as a diagnosis in the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual. Anyone who tells you the question is settled in either direction is telling you more than the evidence does.

Two things follow. A study reports averages, and you are not an average; neither a finding of no harm nor a finding of elevated risk tells you where you stand. And no one gets to tell you what you must feel. The church has sometimes told women they must be devastated, as though grief were the proof of repentance. The culture has sometimes told them they must be fine, as though sorrow were a symptom of someone else's propaganda. Both claims serve an argument rather than a person. Some feel relief for years until a due date stirs something they didn't expect; some grieve from the first day; some mostly feel numb. None of these is the correct response, and none puts anyone beyond grace.

The church also has a particular misuse to repent of. Women's grief has been drafted into the public fight, their suffering turned into a talking point for policy. A woman whose sorrow is borrowed to win a debate has not been served by the church. She has been used by it. Waving her grief away as false consciousness, because it complicates a political message, does the same thing from the other side. If you are living with depression, anxiety or intrusive thoughts after an abortion, see a physician or a licensed counselor; seeking that help is not a failure of faith. An essay can speak to the soul. It can't diagnose or treat anything.

## The Church Once Held the Conviction and the Door Together

The conviction is old. The Didache, a manual of Christian teaching from around the end of the first century, forbids killing a child by abortion in the same breath as it forbids killing a newborn, and in a Roman world where exposing unwanted infants was legal and ordinary, the first Christians were known for refusing both. The church did not discover this conviction in 1973.

But the early church did something with its conviction that we have largely forgotten. It built a way back. In 314, the year after Constantine's Edict of Milan, bishops gathered at Ancyra in Asia Minor and addressed women who had ended pregnancies and those who supplied the drugs to do it. An earlier rule had barred such women from communion until the hour of death; the council judged that too severe and set a discipline of ten years, after which the woman was restored to the table. Around 374 Basil of Caesarea, in a letter the Eastern church came to treat as canon law, treated deliberate abortion with the seriousness of homicide and refused to distinguish between a formed and an unformed fetus. In the same canon he held that the penance should not last for life but for ten years, and that a woman's restoration should depend on the character of her repentance rather than on the mere passing of time. Nobody should revive those penalties. But look at what they assume. The woman who had an abortion was never outside the reach of restoration. The discipline was a road home with an end to it, and at the end was the Lord's Table.

Across the medieval centuries that road hardened into a system in which forgiveness could seem to be earned by suffering, and in 1517 Martin Luther's first thesis answered it: when Christ said "Repent," he meant the whole life of the believer to be one of repentance. Luther was pulling apart penance, a payment made to settle an account, from repentance, the turning of the whole person toward a God who has already settled it. A great many people who carry an abortion are quietly doing penance. They have sentenced themselves to a grief they are not allowed to finish, as if their suffering might someday add up to enough. It won't, and it was never meant to.

After *Roe v. Wade* in 1973, and especially after Francis Schaeffer and C. Everett Koop released *Whatever Happened to the Human Race?* in 1979, American evangelicals joined Catholics in making abortion a defining public cause. They had serious reasons, and the conviction that unborn human life deserves protection is no partisan invention. But abortion became chiefly an issue, fought over in campaigns and courts, and far less often a sin confessed and forgiven in a pastor's study. The public certainty grew louder while the private door grew narrower. When *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* overturned *Roe* in June 2022, the politics changed again, and the woman in the third row still had nowhere to put what she carried.

The progressive wing of American Christianity made the mirror-image mistake. Rightly troubled by cruelty toward women, some churches stopped speaking of abortion as a moral matter at all, which left the woman who felt its weight in a congregation that couldn't help her name it. A church that says there is nothing to forgive cannot offer forgiveness. One side handed her a verdict without a door, and the other handed her a door with nothing on the other side of it. Both of us, in our different pews, forgot what the bishops at Ancyra knew.

## What Forgiveness Covers: David, the Psalms and Paul

Scripture's fullest portrait of a man with blood on his hands who was forgiven is one the church has sung for three thousand years. David took another man's wife and, when she conceived, arranged her husband's death in battle to cover it (2 Samuel 11). The prophet Nathan told him a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, let the king condemn the thief in fury, and said, "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:7). David answered, "I have sinned against the LORD." Nathan replied at once: "The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die" (2 Samuel 12:13).

The forgiveness is immediate and complete, but it doesn't undo the loss. The child falls ill, David fasts and lies all night on the ground, and on the seventh day the child dies. Then David rises, washes, worships and eats, and explains: "But now he is dead. Why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he will not return to me" (2 Samuel 12:23). The parallel with abortion is not exact, and it shouldn't be pressed. But the chapter holds forgiveness and grief side by side and lets neither cancel the other.

The psalms long associated with that season say what the narrative only shows. Psalm 32 is the psalm of the kept secret: "For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long" (Psalm 32:3). The body keeps the account the mouth refuses to give. Then comes the turn: "I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,' and you forgave the iniquity of my sin" (Psalm 32:5). The psalm opens by pronouncing happy "the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered" (Psalm 32:1). While David covered his sin, it crushed him. When he uncovered it, God covered it.

Psalm 51, whose heading ties it to the day Nathan came, refuses every euphemism: "Deliver me from bloodguiltiness, O God, O God of my salvation" (Psalm 51:14). David doesn't call what he did a hard choice. He calls it blood, and that is why the prayer can end in hope, because you can only be forgiven for what you are willing to name. "Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin!" (Psalm 51:2). "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Psalm 51:17). That is grace without minimizing. The sin is called by its right name, and the name doesn't get the last word.

Paul supplies the distinction that separates this from mere self-reproach: "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). The two can feel alike from the inside, but they run in opposite directions. Godly grief turns toward God, confesses, receives mercy and eventually rests. Worldly grief turns in on itself and never arrives anywhere, and people mistake it for piety because it hurts so much. The same line runs between guilt, which says I did something wrong and can be confessed and forgiven, and shame, which says I am something wrong and tries to hide. The gospel answers guilt with forgiveness and answers shame with a welcome.

The promise asks only confession: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9). *Just* is the word doing the work. Forgiveness is the just thing for God to do, because the debt was paid at the cross, and to keep punishing yourself after you have confessed is to say that Christ's death was not quite enough for this. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). That includes the verdict of your own heart, often the harshest judge in the room: "for whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything" (1 John 3:20).

If anyone still suspects that abortion puts a person beyond this, consider who wrote most of the New Testament's letters about grace. Saul of Tarsus watched the stoning of Stephen, and "Saul approved of his execution" (Acts 8:1). He never pretended it hadn't happened. And he wrote this: "The saying is trustworthy and deserving of full acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost. But I received mercy for this reason, that in me, as the foremost, Jesus Christ might display his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe in him for eternal life" (1 Timothy 1:15–16). Paul didn't think his past disqualified him from mercy. He thought it was the evidence.

## "Do You See This Woman?"

Luke tells of a dinner in the house of a Pharisee named Simon (Luke 7:36–50). A woman of the city, "who was a sinner" (Luke 7:37), comes in with an alabaster flask of ointment, stands behind Jesus weeping, wets his feet with her tears, wipes them with her hair and anoints them. Simon thinks to himself, "If this man were a prophet, he would have known who and what sort of woman this is who is touching him, for she is a sinner" (Luke 7:39). Simon has a category, and the category is all he can see.

Jesus tells him a story about two debtors, both forgiven, and asks which will love the lender more. Then he turns toward the woman and says to Simon, "Do you see this woman?" (Luke 7:44). Simon has looked at her all evening and hasn't seen her once. Jesus says to her, "Your sins are forgiven" (Luke 7:48), and over the murmuring of the other guests, "Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (Luke 7:50). In the whole passage she says nothing. Her tears are her confession, and Jesus treats them as enough. Luke never tells us what her sins were, which leaves room for anyone to find herself in the story.

Most of us, pastors first, would rather picture ourselves as the host who welcomed Jesus than as the man who couldn't see the woman at his feet. But the church has often been Simon's house: correct about the law, confident about who the sinner is, and quietly relieved that it's someone else.

There is a serious objection to all of this, and it deserves its strongest form. A church that opens its arms to the grief of abortion, the objection runs, risks teaching that abortion is simply a sad loss, like a miscarriage, rather than a moral act that calls for repentance. Comfort offered without a theological frame produces grief that never reaches repentance, and grief that never reaches repentance never quite heals. Some of that is true. The grief of abortion is not the grief of a miscarriage, and treating the two as identical erases a distinction that matters to the person herself. Repentance and healing are not rivals in the Christian tradition. Each is the road to the other.

But the objection gets the order wrong. A person in active grief can't receive a verdict before she has been received, and leading with the verdict produces defensiveness rather than contrition. The Gospels run the other way. The father in Jesus' parable sees his son "while he was still a long way off," and runs and embraces him before the young man gets through his rehearsed speech (Luke 15:20). The distinction the objection misses is the one between the church's conviction about abortion and the church's posture toward the person who had one. The first can stay where it has stood for two thousand years. The second is where we have failed.

Grace is not tolerance. Tolerance says it didn't matter much. Grace says it mattered enough that the Son of God died for it, and that it is forgiven. The space between approval and condemnation is wide, and nearly all real pastoral work happens there.

## Grieving a Child You Did Not Keep

Forgiveness settles the guilt. It doesn't always end the grief, and it isn't meant to. David was forgiven and still fasted and lay on the ground until the seventh day. Forgiveness answers the question of what stands between you and God. Grief answers a different question, which is what you lost, and love for what was lost doesn't stop because the debt is paid.

Scripture makes more room for that grief than most churches do. Not every psalm of lament resolves. Psalm 88 ends without a single note of relief: "You have caused my beloved and my friend to shun me; my companions have become darkness" (Psalm 88:18). The church kept that psalm in its prayer book as one of faith's forms, the faith that brings what is real before God without first making it presentable. The grief of an abortion, tangled with choice and regret and unanswered questions, rarely resolves into a tidy gratitude or a tidy contrition. The lament tradition was built to hold exactly that.

Many who carry this find that the grief grows more bearable once the loss is treated as the loss of someone. Some give the child a name. Some write a letter they will never send, mark the date that would have been a birthday, or ask a pastor to pray with them in a small, private service. None of this is required, and none of it earns anything. It is the ordinary work of mourning, which the church offers for every other death and seldom offers here. A loss that can be named can be mourned; a loss that can't be named tends to haunt. A man who was involved has as much right as anyone to name the child and grieve. "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb" (Psalm 139:13). Whatever else is true, the child was known.

The question many are most afraid to ask is where the child is now. An honest answer claims no more than Scripture does. The creeds bind no single answer about children who die before they can believe, and faithful traditions have spoken about it differently. What can be said with confidence rests on the character of God. Abraham's question stands over every such grave: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). David said of his child, "I shall go to him," and many Christians have heard hope in those words. The child is in the hands of a God more just and more merciful than we are, who loved that child before anyone else did. That is not a guarantee dressed up as doctrine. It is where trust rests when knowledge runs out.

Grief also keeps its own calendar, and when it returns at a due date or a baby shower, that doesn't mean the forgiveness failed. The woman still carrying this ten years later deserves the same patience from her church as the woman who told her story yesterday. So does the man.

## Where to Bring It

The first step out of a secret is usually small: telling one person. Confession to God comes first, and 1 John 1:9 means exactly what it says. But Scripture also says, "Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16). Bonhoeffer's point was that a sin spoken to another believer loses its power to isolate, because someone else now knows and hasn't left. You don't need to tell a congregation. You need one person who will keep your confidence and pray with you.

For many, that person will be a pastor. Choose one you trust, and it is fair to ask beforehand whether he keeps confidences; if your own pastor has made that feel unsafe, find another. A licensed counselor who respects your faith is often wise as well, especially if what you carry includes depression, anxiety or thoughts you can't shake. Many churches and pregnancy centers offer confidential post-abortion support groups, usually small and meeting for a set number of weeks, and some are specifically for men. A good one protects your privacy, is led by people with real training, and never presses anyone to tell her story in public or to serve a cause, so ask about those things before you go. None of this is medical or mental-health advice. If the weight becomes dangerous, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If someone pressured or forced you into an abortion and still threatens or controls you, that is abuse, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

Churches have harder work to do than preaching a sermon. It means saying out loud, in the same sermon that states the conviction, that there are people in this room who have had an abortion or been part of one, and that the grace of God is for them without an asterisk. It means ending the offhand contempt that teaches people what can't be said here, and training those who hear confessions to keep them. And it means pastors admitting that we don't always get this right. I am not always capable of being the person whose response does not wound, and I have said things in response to a confession that made the confession feel like a mistake.

None of this lightens the loss. A child did not live, and you may carry some form of that sorrow for the rest of your life without shame. But a sorrow you carry is different from a sentence you serve. The bishops at Ancyra knew it when they set an end to the discipline and a place at the table for the woman who came back. David knew it when he rose from the ground and went into the house of the LORD. The woman at Simon's table knew it when she walked back into the city with the words "go in peace" still in her ears.

The Christian hope doesn't end at forgiveness. It ends at resurrection, in a city where God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:4). That promise doesn't erase what happened. It says that what happened won't be the last thing. Until then, the grief is yours to carry in the open, and the forgiveness is yours to receive without paying for it. The question that remains belongs to the rest of us, in the pews and in the pulpit: whether the person in the third row, having finally heard that the door is open, would find us standing in it.
