The renderings usually come up on the screen near the end of the service. A new worship center, a lobby with a coffee bar and a wall of glass, a children's wing with secured check-in, a parking lot that will finally hold the Easter crowd. Someone from the building committee walks through the phases and the three-year pledge period. And somewhere in the season that follows, as dependably as the thermometer graphic in the foyer, comes a sermon on the widow's mite: the poor woman who dropped two small coins into the temple treasury and gave, Jesus said, more than all the rich. The application is rarely stated so baldly, but everyone hears it. If she could give everything, surely you can stretch.

I've sat on the receiving side of that arrangement. I'm a pastor, and I've sat in budget meetings where the discussion of the facilities line ran long and the discussion of the benevolence line ran short, and nobody in the room, least of all me, meant anything by it. I've preached the widow during a season of capital giving and felt the text standing quietly behind the budget, lending it approval. It took me longer than it should have to notice where Mark actually put her story. He set it between a warning about religious leaders who devour widows' houses and a prophecy that the building she was giving to would be torn down. The passage I used to encourage the gift was also, at the very least, asking a question about the institution that received it.

That question is the subject here. What Scripture says about a Christian's personal money is its own subject, and so is whether poverty is political. This essay concerns the church's own wealth: what a congregation owns, builds, keeps and spends on itself, weighed against the poor, who in the New Testament's accounting are the church's family and the Lord's own body. Buildings aren't sin. Christians have built for God from the tabernacle to Chartres, and some of that building was worship of a very high order. The harder truth is that every church holds what it has in trust, and an institution that starts treating the trust as its own is on its way to becoming the treasury Jesus sat across from and watched.

## Two coins and the frame around them

Mark tells us that Jesus "sat down opposite the treasury and watched the people putting money into the offering box" (Mark 12:41, ESV). Around him stood Herod's temple, begun around 20 BC and still unfinished; in John's Gospel Jesus's questioners say it has been forty-six years in the building (John 2:20). Josephus, who watched it burn, remembered in *The Jewish War* (c. 75) gold plates that threw back the morning sun until a man had to look away, and white stone that made the mount look from a distance like a hill of snow. Offerings went into trumpet-shaped chests, thirteen of them by the Mishnah's count (Shekalim 6:5), and it is easy to picture a large gift making a long noise.

Into that scene a widow drops two *lepta*, the smallest coins in circulation, and Mark converts the sum for his Roman readers: "And a poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, which make a penny" (Mark 12:42, ESV). The penny is the quadrans, a sixty-fourth of the denarius that paid a laborer for a day (Matthew 20:2). Jesus calls his disciples over: "Truly, I say to you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the offering box" (Mark 12:43, ESV). Then comes the reason: "For they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on" (Mark 12:44, ESV).

The church has mostly heard praise in those words, and the reading is strong. Jesus measures the gift by its cost rather than its size, an arithmetic that runs from the widow of Zarephath's last handful of flour (1 Kings 17) to the Macedonian churches whose "extreme poverty" overflowed in generosity (2 Corinthians 8:2, ESV). Elizabeth Struthers Malbon, in a 1991 article in the *Catholic Biblical Quarterly*, reads the widow as a model disciple whose total self-giving anticipates Jesus's own, paired in Mark's design with the woman who pours costly ointment on him two chapters later.

The second reading deserves the same care. Addison Wright made its case in "The Widow's Mites: Praise or Lament? A Matter of Context," in the same journal in 1982. Jesus, he observed, never tells anyone to imitate her and attaches no word of approval to the act. What surrounds her is the verse just before, a warning against scribes "who devour widows' houses and for a pretense make long prayers" (Mark 12:40, ESV), and the verse just after, when the disciples admire the temple's stones and Jesus answers, "Do you see these great buildings? There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down" (Mark 13:2, ESV). In that frame, Wright argued, Jesus is lamenting a woman taught by religious authorities to give her livelihood to an institution about to fall, and condemning the values that taught her. Ched Myers pressed the point in *Binding the Strong Man* (1988), reading Mark as a gospel set against the temple's economy. Luke builds the same frame, and just after the widow he has bystanders admire how the temple "was adorned with noble stones and offerings" (Luke 21:5, ESV). The offerings were part of the ornament Jesus said would fall.

This is an open question of interpretation, and no creed settles it. The praise reading has to reckon with a frame that two evangelists built on purpose. The lament reading has to reckon with a sentence that really does call her gift greater than the gifts of the rich and never calls her foolish; its grief has to be supplied, just as the other reading's approval does. Where this essay lands is that the two needn't compete. Jesus can honor the giver and judge the receiver in the same breath. Either way the ledger runs on cost, and either way the institution that took her coins is under scrutiny from the one who has just said its leaders eat widows' houses.

Mark's phrase for what she gave, *holon ton bion autēs*, means "her whole living," and it can also be heard as "her whole life." Within days Jesus will do what she did, handing over everything to men who will take the gift and remain what they were. The temple's finishing work wasn't completed until the early 60s, and in AD 70 Roman soldiers pulled it down. She gave her whole life to a building with about forty years left to stand, and her gift is still being told in every language the church speaks.

## What the first church did with its treasury

The first Christian community had a common fund almost at once, and Luke says what it was for. Believers who owned land or houses sold them and brought the proceeds, "and laid it at the apostles' feet, and distribution was made to each as any had need" (Acts 4:35, ESV). The result echoes the promise of Deuteronomy 15:4: "There was not a needy person among them" (Acts 4:34, ESV). The sharing wasn't compulsory; Peter tells Ananias the field was his own (Acts 5:4), and his sin was the lie. But the direction of the money is plain. The apostles held a treasury, and it existed to empty itself toward need. Two chapters later the church creates its first office below the apostles, and it does so because widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution (Acts 6:1–6). In the temple, the men trusted with widows' affairs were eating their houses. In the church, the first administrators were appointed to feed them.

Paul's collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem, which took years of his ministry and two chapters of 2 Corinthians, shows how an apostle thought about church money. He grounds the appeal in the incarnation rather than in an institution's needs: "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, ESV). His principle is fairness between churches: "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need, that there may be fairness" (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV). And he has men chosen by the churches travel with the money, explaining, "for we aim at what is honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man" (2 Corinthians 8:21, ESV). The apostle didn't consider himself above an audit.

None of those churches owned a building. They met in houses, rented halls and the open air, and the earliest securely dated church building archaeologists have found, a house at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, was remodeled for worship only in the 230s or 240s. The faith spread across the empire for the better part of two centuries largely without buildings of its own, and its credibility came from what it did with its common purse. That doesn't forbid a building. It locates the burden of proof. In the New Testament the church's money goes outward by default, and whatever a church later spends on itself has to be justified against that default.

## Gold vessels, captives and the naked Christ

The default began to shift after 313, for understandable reasons. With Constantine the church gained patrons, legal standing and land. He paid for basilicas at Rome, among them the Lateran and the first St. Peter's, and for the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, and within a generation churches held gold vessels and endowments. Justo González, in *Faith and Wealth* (1990), traces how the bishops of that age tried to keep the church's inherited teaching on sharing alive inside a church that was becoming rich. They didn't refuse the new wealth. They ranked it.

After the Goths destroyed a Roman army at Adrianople in 378, captives from the Balkan provinces were carried off and sold, and Ambrose of Milan, most likely in those years (he gives no date himself), had the church's sacred vessels broken up and melted to ransom captives. Accused of sacrilege by his Arian opponents, he answered in *On the Duties of the Clergy* (c. 390, book 2, chapter 28). His argument, in substance, is that the church has gold in order to spend it on those in need rather than to hoard it, that the sacraments don't require gold, and that if the Lord asked why the church let the needy starve and captives go unransomed while it kept its treasure, it would have no answer. Better, he said, to save the living vessels than the metal ones.

John Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch in the same years, spoke to people who gave beautiful things to the church and felt good about it. In the fiftieth of his homilies on Matthew he tells them that if they want to honor Christ's body, they shouldn't clothe it in silk in church while neglecting it outside, perishing from cold and nakedness, and he asks what good it does to load Christ's table with gold cups while Christ himself is starving. He stops short of forbidding the gifts. What he insists on is order: no one was ever condemned for failing to adorn a church, but neglect of the hungry brings the judgment of Matthew 25, and the brother in need is a more precious temple than the building.

The medieval church kept the argument inside its own walls. Around 1125 Bernard of Clairvaux's *Apologia* attacked the lavish churches of the great Benedictine houses, saying in substance that the church shines in its walls and goes without in its poor, clothing its stones in gold and leaving its children naked. Two decades later Abbot Suger rebuilt the abbey church of Saint-Denis, its new choir consecrated in 1144, and defended its gold and stained glass in writings gathered in Erwin Panofsky's edition, *Abbot Suger on the Abbey Church of St.-Denis* (1946): material beauty, rightly seen, lifts the mind to the invisible God. Suger's choir is where Gothic begins, and Bernard's letter is where the church's conscience about it begins. Both men were orthodox, and the church has been right to keep listening to both.

Francis of Assisi lived the argument. As his early biographers tell it, around 1205 he heard the crucifix in the ruined chapel of San Damiano tell him to repair the Lord's house, and he rebuilt the chapel stone by stone before coming to see that the house meant was the church of living people. Around 1206 he renounced his inheritance before the bishop of Assisi and gave the rest of his life to chosen poverty among lepers and beggars. He died in 1226. In 1228, the year he was canonized, work began on the great basilica in Assisi to hold his body, and his order divided for a century over whether the brothers could own anything; in 1323 John XXII's bull *Cum inter nonnullos* declared it heretical to say Christ and the apostles owned nothing, individually or in common. Whether the basilica was devotion or domestication, it was probably both.

## From St. Peter's to the suburban campus

Protestants tend to tell the next chapter as someone else's scandal. Julius II laid the foundation stone of a new St. Peter's in 1506, and Leo X renewed an indulgence in 1515 to help pay for it; in the territories of Albrecht of Brandenburg the proceeds were split between the building and the debt Albrecht owed the Fugger bank for his archbishopric. Luther's ninety-five theses of October 1517 are rightly remembered for what they say about grace, but a good number of them concern the building and the poor. Christians should be taught, he wrote, that giving to the poor is a better work than buying pardons (thesis 43), and that whoever passes a needy man in order to buy one purchases God's wrath (thesis 45). If the pope knew the extortions of the indulgence preachers, he would rather St. Peter's burned to ashes than see it built with the skin, flesh and bones of his sheep (thesis 50). And why, laypeople ask, doesn't a pope richer than Crassus build it with his own money rather than with the money of poor believers (thesis 86)? The Reformation began partly as an objection to a building campaign financed by the fears of the poor.

The rest of the story flatters Protestants less. When the English crown dissolved the monasteries between 1536 and 1541, their lands went overwhelmingly to the king and to the gentry who bought them. Historians argue over how much the monasteries actually gave away, but their gates, hospitals and almoners had been part of how the poor were fed, and over the rest of the century England built a system of statutory poor relief, codified in the Poor Law of 1601, to do some of what the church's charity once did. Stripping the church of its wealth mostly delivered that wealth to the powerful. Sometimes the institution was the mercy, and the poor noticed when it was gone.

American Protestantism began in plain meetinghouses, log churches and brush arbors. Prosperity, the suburbs and the postwar boom changed that, and by the late twentieth century the megachurch, conventionally a congregation of two thousand or more in weekly attendance, was building campuses with auditoriums, gymnasiums, cafés and acres of parking. Robert Schuller's Crystal Cathedral in Garden Grove, California, Philip Johnson's glass sanctuary dedicated in 1980, became the emblem. The ministry filed for bankruptcy in 2010, the Roman Catholic Diocese of Orange bought the campus in 2011 and 2012, and the diocese rededicated the building in 2019 as Christ Cathedral. Real people met Christ under that glass, and I don't tell the story to mock them. I tell it because it's the oldest story in this essay: a magnificent house outliving the ministry that built it.

The money tells the rest. Since the early 1990s John and Sylvia Ronsvalle of empty tomb, inc. have published *The State of Church Giving*, tracking member giving in a set of Protestant denominations back to 1968. Their series shows giving falling as a share of income while incomes rose, and the portion sent beyond the congregation shrinking relative to what stays home; their book *Behind the Stained Glass Windows* (1996) looked at how congregations and their leaders think and talk about money. Anyone who has read a church budget knows the shape. Salaries take the largest share, the building much of the rest, and what goes beyond the congregation is often a thin line near the bottom.

I have to stand inside that paragraph. Pastors' salaries are among those lines, and a pastor who preaches about church wealth is preaching about the budget that pays him. That doesn't cancel the sermon, but it takes away any right to preach it from above.

## The case for the building, at full strength

The defenders of church building have a better case than their critics allow, and it starts in Scripture. God gave Israel detailed plans for a tabernacle of gold and fine linen, and he said of its craftsman Bezalel, "I have filled him with the Spirit of God, with ability and intelligence, with knowledge and all craftsmanship" (Exodus 31:3, ESV). David prayed over the people's gifts for the temple, "For all things come from you, and of your own have we given you" (1 Chronicles 29:14, ESV). Haggai rebuked the returned exiles for neglecting God's house: "Is it a time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?" (Haggai 1:4, ESV). And two chapters after the widow, Mark tells of a woman at Bethany who pours ointment worth more than three hundred denarii, close to a year's wages, over Jesus's head. When some at the table say it should have been sold for the poor, Jesus answers, "Leave her alone. Why do you trouble her? She has done a beautiful thing to me" (Mark 14:6, ESV).

The theological case follows. Hans Urs von Balthasar opened *The Glory of the Lord* (first volume 1961) by warning that anyone who scorns beauty as the leftover ornament of a bourgeois past will find he can no longer pray, and before long can no longer love. Suger believed something like that about his windows. A room built with care says that God is worth our best and that something real happens here. The poor have often understood this better than their advocates. For centuries the cathedral was the one magnificent room the poorest laborer could enter as of right, and a congregation that puts its poorest members in a cinder-block room with a drop ceiling may be telling them they don't deserve beauty either.

The practical case is just as real. Most church buildings hold far more than Sunday worship: the food pantry, the recovery groups, the English classes, the winter shelter, the funeral for a family who couldn't afford one. Children need rooms that can be secured. Roofs leak, and a church that gives away its operating budget will soon have nothing left to serve anyone with. Christian hospitals, schools and universities exist because some generation built and endowed rather than spending everything at once. When Notre-Dame de Paris burned on 15 April 2019, donors pledged close to a billion euros within days. Critics in France asked why money appeared overnight for stone when the poor had been told there was none, and others answered that the cathedral belonged to everyone, the poor included. Both were saying something true.

So grant the case in full, and then read Mark 14 as carefully as its defenders do. The ointment was poured on the body of Jesus, present at the table and days from death, "for burial," as he says. And when he adds, "For you always have the poor with you, and whenever you want, you can do good for them" (Mark 14:7, ESV), he is echoing Deuteronomy 15, where the permanence of the poor grounds a standing command: "You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor, in your land" (Deuteronomy 15:11, ESV). After the ascension, where does a Christian pour ointment on Christ's body? Jesus had already answered in Matthew 25: "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40, ESV). Chrysostom only drew the conclusion. The body of Christ is at the altar, and it is also in the street.

One more text rarely appears in building campaigns. When Israel gave for the tabernacle, the craftsmen told Moses the people were bringing far more than the work required, and Moses sent word through the camp to stop (Exodus 36:5–7). It's the one sanctuary campaign in Scripture with a stated end, and it ended because someone in charge said *enough*. The modern case for the building seldom does. A better facility draws more people, who give more, who fund more ministry; there's truth in that, and crowds do need room. But the argument justifies every next building, and it rarely produces the meeting where a congregation decides it has built enough and the next dollar belongs to the widow rather than the lobby. An argument that always ends where the institution's comfort ends deserves suspicion. So does any building the prophets would recognize, raised by those "who build Zion with blood and Jerusalem with iniquity" (Micah 3:10, ESV) and then trusted, like the temple in Jeremiah's day, as proof of God's favor (Jeremiah 7:4).

The verdict isn't that beauty is wrong. Beauty has a place in an order, and the order is Chrysostom's: the living temple first, then the temple of stone.

## Steward or proprietor, and the widow in the pew

The distinction that settles most of this is older than any building committee. Scripture's word for the one who manages another's household is steward, and the steward's defining mark is that nothing he manages is his. "Moreover, it is required of stewards that they be found faithful" (1 Corinthians 4:2, ESV). Jesus described the office in a parable: "Who then is the faithful and wise manager, whom his master will set over his household, to give them their portion of food at the proper time?" (Luke 12:42, ESV). The manager exists to feed the household. The same parable imagines a manager who decides the master is delayed and starts beating the servants and getting drunk (Luke 12:45), and Ezekiel had already named that figure in Israel's leaders: "Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves! Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" (Ezekiel 34:2, ESV). A church that acts as steward holds its buildings, accounts and endowments in trust for purposes beyond itself: the worship of God, the forming of his people and the relief of the poor. A church that acts as proprietor has quietly become its own purpose. The scribes of Mark 12 probably never thought of themselves as devouring anyone; they managed estates, said long prayers and took their fees. The drift from trustee to owner happens one reasonable line item at a time.

A church willing to look can see it. It shows in who uses the building on a Tuesday afternoon, in whether the benevolence line grows when the budget grows or only when something is left over, in whether a capital campaign asks first whom the project will serve, and in whether the books are open to the people who fund them, as Paul's were. Vision statements say what a congregation hopes it believes. The budget is where that theology gets audited.

The failure runs in two directions, and both deserve the same grief. One is comfortable accumulation called stewardship: the campus that keeps expanding while the share sent beyond it shrinks, carried by an unspoken belief that a strong balance sheet and God's blessing are the same thing. That is the prosperity gospel in institutional form, and good taste doesn't make it less dangerous. The other is a performance of guilt: contempt for every building from people who depend on buildings others paid for, symbolic divestment that changes nothing for anyone who is hungry, denunciations of church wealth from people who've never had to meet a payroll or replace a roof. England's monasteries stand as the warning on that side. A church can sell everything for a press release and leave the poor exactly where they were.

Every congregation has a widow in it, sometimes several. She lives on a fixed income, gives faithfully and has never asked to see the budget, because she assumes the church is doing something worthy with what she gives. She is the reason the question can't be put off. A sermon that asks only whether the congregation is giving like her has asked half of Mark's question. The other half, the half he framed on both sides of her, is whether the institution receiving her gift is worthy of it. That belongs in every budget meeting and every capital campaign, asked aloud before the renderings go up, and asked most sharply by those of us who stand to benefit if the answer comes out wrong.

I can't answer it cleanly for the church I serve, and I'd distrust any pastor who says he can. What I know is where the answer has to start. It starts with repentance: for the years we let momentum decide, for widow sermons preached to raise money rather than to examine ourselves, for every time the benevolence line was discussed last and cut first. And it starts at the cross, because her two coins point there. She put in her whole life, and within the week he gave his, for people who took the gift and went on being what they were. Though he was rich, for our sake he became poor. The temple that received her coins is rubble. The temple he is building now is made of people, and Peter tells ordinary believers that "you yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5, ESV). The poor are stones in that house, and some of them are its most precious ones.

Every building we raise will come down, the beautiful ones and the vain ones alike. What lasts is what was given and what was done with it. This Sunday, in some church, a widow will put an envelope in the plate that holds more of her life than the largest pledge to the building fund. She'll trust that we're worth it. Whether we are isn't settled by anything written here. It sits in the plate with her envelope.
