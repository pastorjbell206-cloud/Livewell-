There are two readers this question usually reaches, and they rarely sit in the same room. The first grew up in a church where concern for the environment belonged to the other side. Earth Day posters and recycling drives were somebody else's religion, and "creation care" sounded like a political program that had borrowed a Sunday school word. The second reader has never belonged to that church, but she learned somewhere that Christianity is the reason the planet is in trouble: a faith that told human beings to subdue the earth got exactly what it asked for. Both readers are owed an honest answer, and the honest answer begins by admitting that each of them has seen something true.

I was formed in the first church. For years I treated environmental concern as a distraction from the real work of saving souls, and nothing I preached in those years questioned it. I no longer think that posture survives contact with the Bible. But the cure isn't to trade one tribe's reflex for the other's. What Scripture says about the earth is older than either party and deeper than either platform, and it turns on a distinction both sides tend to lose: the difference between dominion as domination and dominion as stewardship under a Creator who has never stopped owning what he made.

The Bible does not hand the earth to human beings as property. It sets them inside a world that belongs to someone else, gives them real authority there, and makes that authority answerable. Scripture has a word for rule that forgets whom it answers to, and the word is not a compliment. Where the church has forgotten this, the harm has not fallen evenly, and the people living downstream have usually been the poor.

## The Charge Lynn White Laid Against the Church

On 26 December 1966 a historian of medieval technology named Lynn White Jr. gave a lecture to the American Association for the Advancement of Science, and on 10 March 1967 the journal *Science* published it as "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis." White was no village atheist; he had spent his career on the plows, stirrups and water mills of the Middle Ages. Modern science and modern technology, he said, were both products of the Latin West, and the Latin West had been shaped at its root by Christian theology. When the two joined in the nineteenth century and gave human beings the power to wreck the planet, the attitudes steering that power came, he argued, from that root.

His case deserves to be put in the form his admirers would sign. Pagan antiquity, White argued, had filled the natural world with spirits. Every grove, spring and hill had its guardian, and a man who wanted to fell a tree or dam a stream had first to reckon with the spirit who lived there. Christianity emptied the groves. By teaching that God alone is holy and that nature is a creature rather than a divinity, it made the world available for use without scruple. By teaching that man alone bears God's image and that the rest of creation was made for his sake, it gave him a motive. White called Western Christianity "the most anthropocentric religion the world has seen." And if the union of science and technology had produced powers that were now out of control, he wrote, then "Christianity bears a huge burden of guilt."

This is not a cheap argument, and it should not be answered cheaply. White was right that ideas about God shape what people feel permitted to do with the world. He was right that the West developed an unusually aggressive posture toward nature, and right that Christians supplied much of its vocabulary. Anyone who has heard Genesis 1:28 used to bless a strip mine knows the reading he described is not imaginary. And he did not end in contempt. Because the roots of the crisis were religious, he argued, the remedy would have to be religious too, and he proposed Francis of Assisi as a patron saint for ecologists. The charge came from a man who thought Christianity still had the resources to repent.

The answer had two parts. The first was historical. Forests fell and soils washed away in civilizations that never heard of Genesis. Plato, in the *Critias*, already mourned the hills of Attica, stripped of their soil until only the bones of the land were left, and the irrigation farmers of ancient Sumer salted their own fields. Human beings have never needed a doctrine of dominion to exhaust the ground. The second answer was more pointed, and Richard Bauckham presses it in *Bible and Ecology* (2010). For most of Christian history, he points out, dominion was read as permission for the ordinary uses people already made of the world: farming, herding, fishing, building. The idea that humanity's calling is to master nature, to conquer it and bend it to human purposes without limit, came later, from the humanists of the Italian Renaissance and above all from Francis Bacon, whom Bauckham treats as the first to read dominion as a project of progressive technological conquest. Bacon argued at the close of the *Novum Organum* (1620) that the dominion over creation lost at the fall could be partly recovered by the arts and sciences. Genesis supplied the word. The laboratory and the factory supplied the meaning White found in it.

That answer is true, and it is not enough, because it clears Genesis without clearing us. Bacon was a Christian. The men whose mills fouled English rivers sat in English pews, and the reading of dominion White attacked is still preached from American pulpits. The honest verdict is that White misdiagnosed the text and correctly diagnosed a great many of its readers. The church doesn't get to hide behind a better exegesis it failed to practice.

## What Dominion Meant in Genesis

Start with the words White worried about. "Then God said, 'Let us make man in our image, after our likeness. And let them have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the heavens and over the livestock and over all the earth and over every creeping thing that creeps on the earth'" (Genesis 1:26, ESV). Two verses later the commission returns as a blessing, with a sharper verb added: "And God blessed them. And God said to them, 'Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the heavens and over every living thing that moves on the earth'" (Genesis 1:28, ESV). The Hebrew behind "dominion" is *radah*, a word for royal rule. The word behind "subdue" is *kabash*, and it is strong; elsewhere it describes bringing a land under control and even acts of violent force. There's no use pretending these are gentle words. They grant real authority.

The question is what kind of authority, and the text answers in its own setting. In the ancient Near East, kings set up statues of themselves in distant provinces to mark where their rule reached, and a king could be called the image of his god. Genesis takes that royal language and does something startling with it. It gives the image to every human being, and it places the image in a garden rather than a palace. J. Richard Middleton, in *The Liberating Image* (2005), reads the image of God as exactly this royal vocation: humanity is appointed to represent God's rule inside creation. That reading, widely held among Old Testament scholars, cuts against White rather than for him. A representative rules in another's name, and every act of his rule either tells the truth about the one he represents or tells a lie.

So what does God's rule look like? The Psalter answers with its portrait of the ideal king in Psalm 72, whose dominion stretches from sea to sea precisely because he defends the weak: "For he delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper" (Psalm 72:12, ESV). And the Torah uses the same verb, *radah*, to draw the boundary of rightful rule. When an Israelite has fallen into poverty and sold himself into service, his master is told, "You shall not rule over him ruthlessly but shall fear your God" (Leviticus 25:43, ESV). Ezekiel indicts Israel's shepherds in the same terms, charging that they fed themselves and ruled the flock with force and harshness (Ezekiel 34:2–4). Scripture already has a category for dominion gone wrong, and it builds that category out of the very word Genesis uses. Domination is not an overdose of the dominion God gave. It is the thing God forbade under the same name: rule that has forgotten it will be judged.

Genesis 2 tells the story again from the ground up. "The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it" (Genesis 2:15, ESV). *Abad* means to work or to serve. *Shamar* means to keep, guard or watch over, and it is the verb of the priestly blessing: "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Numbers 6:24, ESV). When the pair appears together again in the Pentateuch, it describes the Levites' service at the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7–8). Gordon Wenham, in "Sanctuary Symbolism in the Garden of Eden Story" (1986), read Eden as the first sanctuary partly for this reason, and whatever one makes of every detail of that reading, the vocational point holds. The first human being was set into the world as a gardener serving in a holy place. His authority was the authority of a servant who guards the thing he tends for the one who owns it.

The owner is named without embarrassment. "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof, the world and those who dwell therein" (Psalm 24:1, ESV). The law turns that confession into a rule of tenure: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). Notice the posture it assigns. Israel held real title to real fields, and it was still told that its deepest legal standing was that of a resident alien on God's land. Tenancy of that kind raises responsibility rather than lowering it, because the tenant answers both for what he used and for what he handed on.

The whole question turns on this distinction. The owner may consume what is his. The steward must give an account. Even stewardship has critics, Bauckham among them, who worry that it pictures human beings as managers standing over a creation that exists to be administered, when Scripture also places us inside a community of creatures that praise God without our help. The worry is fair. When God finally answers Job, he walks him through a world of wild goats, of rain falling on deserts where no one lives, of a war horse that laughs at fear, and almost none of it exists for Job's benefit (Job 38–39). Psalm 104 has the young lions "seeking their food from God" (Psalm 104:21, ESV), not from us. Dominion rightly understood is exercised by a creature among creatures, answerable to the one who feeds them all.

## How Christians Answered, and Where We Failed

The first serious evangelical answer came quickly. In 1970 Francis Schaeffer, founder of the L'Abri community in Switzerland, published *Pollution and the Death of Man*. Schaeffer granted White more than his readers expected, agreeing that Christians had often treated nature as worthless. But he refused White's cure. Pantheism, he argued, cannot actually raise the value of nature. By erasing the line between God and the world, it ends by lowering human beings to the level of everything else, and a world in which a person is worth no more than a tree gives no reason to protect either. The Christian alternative, in Schaeffer's account, is to treat each created thing with respect because God made it and values it, and to seek what he called substantial healing in the present: a real, if partial, foretaste of the renewal to come.

Wendell Berry, the Kentucky farmer and writer, gave the answer a different texture. In *The Unsettling of America* (1977), a book about agriculture with a theological spine, Berry drew a line between two characters, the exploiter and the nurturer. The exploiter asks how much can be taken from a place, how fast and at what profit. The nurturer asks what the place can bear and how its health can be kept for those who come after. In "Christianity and the Survival of Creation," collected in *Sex, Economy, Freedom & Community* (1993), Berry took up the charge White had made famous. He conceded that Christian institutions had been deeply complicit in the ruin of the natural world and had often blessed the economy doing it. But he denied that the Bible teaches that ruin; read with care, it teaches that the world is God's, that it is held as a gift to be used well, and that waste is contempt for the giver. Berry's exploiter and nurturer are domination and stewardship, translated into the language of a working farm.

Then the answer stalled, and not for exegetical reasons. Through the late 1970s and the 1980s, American evangelicals were sorting themselves into a political coalition, and environmental concern was sorted into the other one. The suspicion had real roots. Some strands of environmentalism really did treat humanity as a blight, and Paul Ehrlich's *The Population Bomb* (1968) opened by declaring the battle to feed humanity already lost and forecasting that hundreds of millions would starve in the 1970s. Famines came, but nothing on that scale, as crop yields rose faster than Ehrlich thought possible, and the book's willingness to entertain compulsory population control taught conservatives to hear every ecological warning as the prelude to someone controlling their lives. But the reason most of us gave for keeping our distance was not that we had read Genesis more carefully than the environmentalists. It was that we had noticed who else was in the room. A doctrine of creation was surrendered because it had become culturally inconvenient, and I spent years inside that surrender without seeing it.

The recovery, when it came, came in documents. In 1994 the Evangelical Environmental Network issued *An Evangelical Declaration on the Care of Creation*. It confessed that Christians had failed to care for creation, grounded that care in the lordship of Christ over all things, and named particular degradations, from the loss of species to the fouling of land, water and air. Six years later an interfaith coalition of religious conservatives, Protestant, Catholic and Jewish, offered a sharply different account in the *Cornwall Declaration on Environmental Stewardship* (2000), and it should be stated as its signers would state it. They affirmed that human beings are stewards accountable to God. But they argued that people are producers and not merely consumers, that human ingenuity can enrich the earth as well as exhaust it, that the era's great fears of destructive man-made warming, overpopulation and rampant species loss were unfounded or overstated, and that the environmental problems actually killing the world's poor were the local ones: poor sanitation, the smoke of wood and dung burned for fuel, primitive farming and industry. Poverty, on their account, is the deeper threat, and a clean environment is a costly good that growing prosperity makes affordable.

Both declarations are right about something. The 1994 statement is right that the church owed repentance and that Christ's lordship leaves no corner of creation outside the question of faithfulness. Cornwall is right that human beings are not a cancer on the earth, that poverty kills, and that the costs of environmental policy often land on the very poor it means to protect. Cornwall fails where its confidence that the warnings are overblown stops weighing evidence and starts defending a coalition. Parts of the creation-care movement fail where they treat one policy agenda as though it came bound into the doctrine of creation.

The fullest statement from the wider church came in 2015, when Pope Francis issued *Laudato si'*, subtitled "On Care for Our Common Home." Protestants will dissent from its ecclesiology, and many Catholics disputed its economic judgments. But its central move is one every Christian tradition can recognize. Francis rejected what he called a tyrannical anthropocentrism and admitted that Christians had at times read Genesis wrongly, while insisting that the text calls for tilling and keeping, not plunder. He proposed an integral ecology in which the cry of the earth and the cry of the poor are heard together, and he called the climate a common good, belonging to all, and access to safe drinking water a basic human right. He also said, in a line worth setting beside the rest, that the church does not presume to settle scientific questions or to replace politics. Whatever one makes of the policies the letter went on to favor, that is the right posture.

## The Air and Water We Hold in Common

The question stops being abstract when it reaches the lungs. Air and water are the plainest cases of what Roman law, and the Christian moral tradition that inherited it, counted among the things common to all: no one can own them outright, everyone depends on them, and anyone can spoil them for everyone else. The American story of both turns on disasters that forced the country to look. In October 1948 a temperature inversion trapped the smoke of the mills over Donora, Pennsylvania, and people died of it. In June 1969 the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland caught fire, and not for the first time. Within a few years Congress had passed the Clean Air Act of 1970 and the Clean Water Act of 1972. Garrett Hardin had already named the mechanism in "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968): when a shared resource belongs to no one, each user gains by taking a little more and bears only a sliver of the harm. Hardin thought the only exits were private property or state coercion. Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) showed otherwise, documenting Swiss alpine pastures and Spanish irrigation systems kept healthy for centuries by local rules and mutual watchfulness. The means of keeping a commons are prudential and various. The obligation to keep it is not.

Scripture knew the shape of this long before the economists did. Ezekiel, addressing the strong sheep who shove the weak aside, asks a question that could be carved over every discharge pipe: "Is it not enough for you to feed on the good pasture, that you must tread down with your feet the rest of your pasture; and to drink of clear water, that you must muddy the rest of the water with your feet?" (Ezekiel 34:18, ESV). The sin is fouling what others must still drink. In the American record, those others have not been chosen at random.

In 1982 residents of Warren County, North Carolina, a poor and predominantly Black county, lay down in the road to stop trucks carrying PCB-contaminated soil to a new landfill. Many were arrested, and many came out of churches. The landfill was built anyway, but a pattern had been named, and Benjamin Chavis of the United Church of Christ is usually credited with calling it environmental racism. In 1987 the church's Commission for Racial Justice published *Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States*, which found race to be the strongest single factor associated with where commercial hazardous waste facilities were located. The sociologist Robert Bullard traced the same pattern across the South in *Dumping in Dixie* (1990). The language was not invented in a faculty lounge. Christians with a theology of the poor named it, because they recognized its violation.

Then Flint. In April 2014, under a state-appointed emergency manager, the city switched its water to the Flint River without the corrosion control that would have kept lead inside the old pipes. Residents carried discolored water to public meetings and were told it was safe. In September 2015 a local pediatrician, Mona Hanna-Attisha, released data showing that blood-lead levels in the city's children had risen after the switch, and state officials disputed her until the evidence overwhelmed them. The city went back to Detroit water that October. Flint is a short drive from the church I serve, and while the water was still wrong I preached on other things. I didn't lack the facts. I lacked the willingness to spend the pulpit's credibility on children who weren't in my pews.

The strongest objection deserves its full weight. Polluting facilities, its defenders argue, follow cheap land, industrial zoning and rail and highway corridors, not the race of the neighbors; poor families sometimes arrive after the plant because housing beside it is what they can afford; much of the disparity may track poverty more than race. Some of that is true, and honest researchers still argue over the proportions. But the objection explains a mechanism without excusing an outcome, and a child's blood does not ask whether the lead came by malice or by price signal. The prices themselves were drawn by law, in the redlining maps of the 1930s and the covenants that kept Black families out of better neighborhoods. Scripture has never made intent the only moral category. Leviticus 4 provides an offering for the whole congregation when it comes to realize it has sinned without meaning to (Leviticus 4:13–14). A people can be guilty of a harm it never intended, and still be required to set it right.

## One Instrument for the Right and the Left

The church will not be heard on any of this if its criticism fires in only one direction. Psalm 24:1 supplies the one instrument for both: the earth belongs to the Lord, which means it is neither a commodity nor a god.

The right's characteristic failure is dismissal. It hears every environmental warning as a pretext for bigger government, files the subject under the other party, and sometimes reaches for an end-times scheme in which the earth will burn anyway, so why keep it. The promise of bodily resurrection has never licensed contempt for the body, and the promise of a renewed earth licenses contempt for the earth no more. Beneath the dismissal usually sits the quiet assumption that prosperity is the measure of blessing and whatever grows the economy must be what God wants. That treats the creature as a commodity.

The left's characteristic failure is apocalypse. Its alarm is often grounded in real science; a warming climate is not a hoax invented to control anyone. But the politics built on that alarm tends to borrow the shape of Christian eschatology while leaving out the grace. There is a fall, a coming judgment, a priesthood of experts, a set of rituals for staying pure, and no forgiveness anywhere. People formed by that story drift toward paralysis or scapegoats, and some decide that the most responsible thing they can do is have no children. That treats the creature as a god and the human being as the pest in its temple, which is the move Schaeffer saw coming in 1970. Most of us are capable of both failures in the same week.

So order the questions. First-order and binding: the earth is God's, human authority over it is answerable, and the neighbor's air and water fall within the command to love him. Second-order, where faithful Christians will differ: how much weight to give particular climate projections, whether carbon should be taxed, regulated or left to innovation, how to set cheap energy for the global poor against emissions, whether nuclear power belongs in the answer. Third-order: the particular rule, permit and price. The church has no mandate to draft the regulation. It does have a mandate to refuse any arrangement, public or private, that quietly sends its costs downstream to people with no voice in the decision. A Christian who reads the policy evidence differently is still a brother or sister. A Christian who says the poisoned child is none of the church's business has stepped off the first-order ground.

## A Creation Groaning Toward Its Maker

The deepest reason the question matters is where the story ends. Writing to Rome, Paul sets the whole creation inside the hope of the gospel: "For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God" (Romans 8:19, ESV). It was subjected to futility, he says, not by its own will but in hope. "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now" (Romans 8:22, ESV). Labor pains announce a birth rather than a burial, and Paul names what is being born: "that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God" (Romans 8:21, ESV). The earth's future is bound to ours.

Colossians carries the reconciliation all the way down. In Christ, Paul writes, all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell, and "through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross" (Colossians 1:20, ESV). The cross is the peace God has made with a world he never stopped owning. And Revelation, in its hymn of final judgment, lists among the things the Lord will do at the end "destroying the destroyers of the earth" (Revelation 11:18, ESV). In John's vision the destroyers are first the empire and its idolatries, the kind of power later judged for having "corrupted the earth" (Revelation 19:2, ESV), and the verb John uses in 11:18 means to corrupt as well as to destroy. The line names no party and spares none, and it tells us the owner keeps accounts.

There is one more garden in the story. The tomb where they laid Jesus was in a garden, and when Mary Magdalene met the risen Lord there she did not know him at first, "supposing him to be the gardener" (John 20:15, ESV). She was more right than she knew. The one who put the first man in Eden to serve and keep it came into a garden himself, took the curse of the ground into his own body, and walked out of the grave as the keeper of the new creation. He does not need us to save the planet. He asks us to stop treating it as ours.

So we carry this out with us. Somewhere downstream of what each of us burned, bought and threw away this week is a neighbor we will never meet, breathing what we left in the air and drinking what we left in the water. We don't know her name. The owner of the earth does, and he will ask us about her.
