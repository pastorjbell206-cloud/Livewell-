Every spring, at Jewish tables around the world, a child asks why this night is different from all other nights, and the adults answer with a story about slaves. Most Christians have never sat at that table. For many of us the Exodus survives as a Sunday school flannelgraph, a Cecil B. DeMille film from 1956, and a spiritual we can sing without knowing why it was first sung. The story also reaches us bruised. We've heard it borrowed by every side: preached as proof that America is God's chosen nation, preached as a charter for revolution, preached as a picture of the soul escaping sin with nothing at all to say about bodies. It's fair to wonder whether the Exodus means anything once everyone has finished using it.

It does, and it means more than any of the uses. The Exodus is the founding memory of the people of God. It was rehearsed at a family table before it was argued in a seminary, and Jesus took it up on the night he was betrayed. The argument of this essay turns on one small word. God did not bring Israel out of Egypt simply to be free *from* Pharaoh. He brought them out to be free *for* himself, and nearly every misreading of the story, on the right and on the left, begins by dropping that second preposition. And it is a memory that judges whoever claims it. The church has rightly read itself into the story as Israel for two thousand years. It has not always noticed when it was standing in Egypt.

## "Remember that you were a slave"

Start where the Bible starts its law. Before a single commandment at Sinai, God introduces himself with a piece of history rather than a list of attributes: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exodus 20:2). The commandments are not the terms of a rescue Israel might earn. They are the shape of life for people already rescued.

Deuteronomy turns that grammar into a habit of mind, grounding command after command in a memory and linking the two with "therefore." Why should an Israelite household give its servants a day off? "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. Therefore the LORD your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15). Why release a Hebrew slave in the seventh year, and not empty-handed? "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you; therefore I command you this today" (Deuteronomy 15:15). The same logic governs the sojourner, the widow and the orphan, treated more fully in the essays on [immigrants and refugees](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-immigrants-and-refugees-in-depth) and on [the Year of Jubilee](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded). Israel's ethics were built out of its autobiography. A people who remembered the brickyards were not permitted to build brickyards of their own.

The historian Yosef Hayim Yerushalmi argued in *Zakhor* (1982) that only in Israel was the duty to remember laid on an entire people as a religious obligation, and that it was not a call to historical curiosity but to live inside what God had done. To remember, in the Bible, is to let the past govern the present.

That is why the memory is handed down at home. The Passover is a household meal, and the Torah assumes the children will interrupt it: "And when your children say to you, 'What do you mean by this service?'" (Exodus 12:26). Deuteronomy scripts the parent's answer: "then you shall say to your son, 'We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt. And the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand'" (Deuteronomy 6:21). The father answering had never seen a brick kiln, and the Torah still tells him to say "we." Exodus goes further: "You shall tell your son on that day, 'It is because of what the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt'" (Exodus 13:8). For me.

The rabbis of the Mishnah, compiled around the year 200, drew this out. In the tractate on Passover they taught that in every generation a person is bound to regard himself as though he personally had gone out of Egypt, and that line passed into the Passover Haggadah, the order of the meal that Jewish families still read. That is a theology of the family table. Each generation is placed back at the edge of the sea.

A doubting reader will ask whether any of it happened, and the skeptical case deserves its full strength. No Egyptian record mentions Israel's slavery or its departure. The first mention of Israel outside the Bible, on Pharaoh Merneptah's stele around 1208 BC, places a people called Israel already in Canaan. Exodus counts "about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides women and children" (Exodus 12:37), a multitude that should have left some trace in the Sinai, and none has been found. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman argue in *The Bible Unearthed* (2001) that Kadesh-barnea, where the tradition sets Israel's long stay in the wilderness, shows no sign of occupation in the Late Bronze Age, that the earliest Israelite villages in the highlands look like their Canaanite neighbors, and that much of the story's geography fits the seventh century BC, when they believe it took written shape, better than the thirteenth. On that reading the Exodus is a national epic, not a memory.

Others read the same ground differently. James Hoffmeier argues in *Israel in Egypt* (1997) that the narrative's Egyptian background fits the New Kingdom too closely to be a late invention, and that royal inscriptions written to celebrate pharaohs were never likely to record the escape of their slaves. William Dever, no friend of easy apologetics, reads the archaeology in *Who Were the Early Israelites and Where Did They Come From?* (2003) as pointing to Israel's emergence largely within Canaan, with at most a smaller group carrying a memory of escape. This essay won't settle it, and nothing in the creeds hangs on the size of the crowd at the sea. But notice one thing. Peoples tell stories about heroic founders. Israel told its children, every Passover, that their ancestors had been Pharaoh's property.

## From the prophets to the upper room

The prophets used the memory as a claim on the present, most sharply against people who assumed it was on their side. Hosea remembers the Exodus as a love story: "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1). Micah makes it the evidence in a lawsuit, with the mountains as witnesses: "For I brought you up from the land of Egypt and redeemed you from the house of slavery, and I sent before you Moses, Aaron, and Miriam" (Micah 6:4). The requirement that follows, to do justice, love kindness and walk humbly, is what a redeemed people owes its Redeemer.

Amos is the hardest, and the one Christians most need. In the prosperous northern kingdom of the eighth century BC, the Exodus had become a warrant for confidence: God had chosen Israel, so God would protect Israel. Amos turns the logic inside out: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2). Election raises the standard of judgment. Near the end of the book he goes further: "Are you not like the Cushites to me, O people of Israel?" declares the LORD. "Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor and the Syrians from Kir?" (Amos 9:7). Israel's rescue was unique in its covenant purpose, but it was never a private claim on God's favor. Every nation that has since called itself the new Israel has needed that verse.

When Judah fell to Babylon in 587 BC, the Exodus became a promise. The second half of Isaiah tells exiles that the God who made a path through the sea will do it again: "Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it? I will make a way in the wilderness and rivers in the desert" (Isaiah 43:19). The essay on [why the Bible is one story](/writing/why-the-bible-is-one-story-not-66-books) traces how the Gospels walk through that open promise. Two moments matter here.

The first is the table. Jesus chose the Passover to interpret his death. "And he said to them, 'I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer'" (Luke 22:15). At the meal that rehearsed Israel's rescue, he changed the words. "This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). The command to remember is carried straight into the church's central act of worship. Paul saw it plainly: "For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7). The Supper is where the church is told, as Israel was, to say "for me" about a rescue it did not witness.

The second is the name. Writing to largely Gentile believers in Corinth, Paul calls the wilderness generation family: "For I do not want you to be unaware, brothers, that our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:1). Our fathers. Then Paul does what Amos did. Most of that rescued generation died in the desert, he reminds them, and the story was written down for a reason: "Now these things took place as examples for us, that we might not desire evil as they did" (1 Corinthians 10:6). The warning that follows belongs over every church door: "Therefore let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12).

Peter makes the adoption complete. At Sinai God had told the escaped slaves what they were for: "and you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). Peter hands Israel's Sinai titles to scattered, largely Gentile churches in Asia Minor: "But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9). The people is no longer defined by ancestry, as the Exodus itself hinted when "a mixed multitude also went up with them" (Exodus 12:38), but the titles carry their Sinai purpose. A priestly people exists to serve God and show him to the nations. Which brings us back to the preposition.

## "Let my people go, that they may serve me"

Ask most Americans what the Exodus is about and they'll say freedom. They're right, and incomplete in a way that matters. The demand Moses carries into Pharaoh's court has two halves, and we usually quote only the first. "Let my people go, that they may serve me" (Exodus 8:1). It is repeated before plague after plague. God is not asking Pharaoh to release his workforce. He is claiming it.

The Hebrew sharpens it. The verb *'avad* and its nouns describe the forced labor of Egypt in the book's first chapter, and the same root appears in God's demand, "that they may serve me," and again for the Passover itself, which Exodus calls a *service* when the children ask what it means. Israel moves from one *'avodah* to another, from servitude to Pharaoh to the service of the LORD. The law of Jubilee states it outright: "For they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt; they shall not be sold as slaves" (Leviticus 25:42). Israel cannot be sold again because it already has a master, the one master whose service is freedom.

The Jewish scholar Jon Levenson pressed this in an essay called "Exodus and Liberation," collected in *The Hebrew Bible, the Old Testament, and Historical Criticism* (1993). The Hebrew Bible, he argued, does not picture the Exodus as a passage from slavery to freedom in the modern sense. It pictures a change of lords. Neither the liberal's freedom from outside constraint nor the revolutionary's freedom from oppression is the destination. The question the text asks is less "liberation from what?" than "liberation for what?" And its answer is Sinai, where the rescued slaves receive a covenant, a law, a sanctuary and a calling.

This is the distinction the essay turns on, and it cuts every way. Freedom *from* is real. The Exodus is no metaphor floating above the brickyards: "God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exodus 2:24). A faith that turns the story into a picture of the inner life while ignoring the Pharaohs of its own day has not understood it. But freedom *from* is not the goal. A people released from Pharaoh and handed over to their own appetites have only been reassigned to a different taskmaster.

The wilderness shows what happens when the second half is lost. Within weeks of the sea, the rescued people are already longing for Egypt's pots of meat (Exodus 16:3), and after Sinai the homesickness becomes a menu: "We remember the fish we ate in Egypt that cost nothing, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic" (Numbers 11:5). They are remembering the wrong thing. The golden calf, made at the foot of Sinai, was less a return to Egypt's gods than an attempt to worship the God of the Exodus on their own terms, with an image they could make and control. The generation that crossed the sea did not enter the land. Getting a people out of Egypt took one night. Getting Egypt out of a people took forty years and a new generation.

The New Testament keeps both halves together. Jesus names the deeper Egypt: "Truly, truly, I say to you, everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin" (John 8:34). And then the Exodus promise: "So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36). Paul describes the result not as autonomy but as a new service: "But now that you have been set free from sin and have become slaves of God, the fruit you get leads to sanctification and its end, eternal life" (Romans 6:22). To the Galatians he closes the loop: "For you were called to freedom, brothers. Only do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh, but through love serve one another" (Galatians 5:13).

## Everyone wants to be Israel

American history is a laboratory of the story's uses. The Puritans who sailed for Massachusetts Bay in 1630 read their crossing through it: England was Egypt, the Atlantic was the sea, and New England was to be a covenanted commonwealth in the wilderness. In 1670 Samuel Danforth preached an election sermon published as *A Brief Recognition of New-Englands Errand into the Wilderness*, and Perry Miller borrowed the phrase for *Errand into the Wilderness* (1956). Miller showed the errand was double-edged: the colonists believed they had been sent and were haunted by the fear that they had failed, and their characteristic sermon was the jeremiad, a lament over a people fallen from the covenant. Give them this much. They knew that a people who claimed to be Israel would be judged as Israel.

The claim outlived the fear. When the Continental Congress appointed a committee in July 1776 to design a seal for the new nation, Benjamin Franklin proposed Moses lifting his hand over the sea while Pharaoh's chariots were swallowed, with the motto "Rebellion to Tyrants is Obedience to God." Thomas Jefferson proposed the children of Israel in the wilderness, led by a cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night. Neither was adopted. The political philosopher Michael Walzer argued in *Exodus and Revolution* (1985) that the story of a people leaving bondage, passing through a wilderness of discipline and covenant, and moving toward a promised land became the West's paradigm of revolutionary politics. His summary, paraphrased: wherever you live, it's probably Egypt; there is a better place; and the only way there runs through the wilderness, together.

But the same nation that told its founding as an Exodus was holding people in bondage, and they were reading the same book. The enslaved, as Albert Raboteau showed in *Slave Religion* (1978), found in the Exodus the clearest word Scripture had for them, and they understood what their masters did not want them to see: they were Israel, and the plantation was Egypt. The history of [the Black church in America](/writing/the-black-church-in-america) tells that story in full, from the hush arbors to "Go Down, Moses." It's enough to note how dangerous the text was thought to be. An abridged Bible printed in London in 1807 for the enslaved in the British West Indies simply left the escape from Egypt out, as the essay on [whether faith is just wishful thinking](/writing/is-faith-just-wishful-thinking) recounts. And on April 3, 1968, in Memphis, the night before he was killed, Martin Luther King Jr. told a crowd that he had been to the mountaintop and seen the promised land, and that he might not get there with them. He was standing where Moses stood on Nebo.

Then there is a reading American Christians have mostly declined to hear. In 1989 the Osage scholar Robert Allen Warrior published an essay called "Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians," arguing that the Exodus story does not end at the sea. It ends in the conquest of Canaan, where the people already in the land are not the heroes. Settlers pushing west often read themselves as Israel entering Canaan, which made the people already here Canaanites. Warrior asked liberation theologians to read the story from the Canaanites' side. That question is taken up in [did God command genocide](/writing/did-god-command-genocide). The story that freed the slaves also licensed the settlers, depending on who was holding the book.

The verdict of the history applies to every side at once. The Exodus has been read faithfully by the oppressed and self-servingly by the powerful, and the difference was rarely in the exegesis. It was in where the reader stood, and whether he would ask if he was Egypt. Amos 9:7 and 1 Corinthians 10:12 are the texts every claimant skipped. The Puritans at least remembered that the covenant could condemn them; their heirs turned "a city upon a hill" into a national compliment. Christian nationalism is, at bottom, an Exodus reading that has kept the promised land and lost Amos: America as the chosen nation, its enemies as Pharaoh, its wars as the Lord's. It is idolatry wearing Israel's clothes, and it is not only the fault of other people. Many of us learned to hear the story that way in churches that loved us, and some of us taught it.

## Liberation theology, heard at its strongest

No modern movement took the Exodus more seriously than Latin American liberation theology, and it deserves to be heard in the form its defenders would sign. In 1968 the Latin American bishops met at Medellín, Colombia, on a continent where land sat in few hands and vast numbers of baptized Catholics lived in grinding poverty, often under military governments that claimed to defend Christian civilization while torturing their critics. In *A Theology of Liberation* (1971), the Peruvian priest Gustavo Gutiérrez read the liberation from Egypt as a political act through which God's saving work first became visible in history, at once social and religious, the pattern of a God who does not stand neutral between Pharaoh and his slaves.

At its strongest the argument runs like this. The God of Exodus takes sides. He hears the cry of people their masters had every reason to ignore, and the Torah writes that hearing into law: "If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry" (Exodus 22:23), said of the widow and the orphan in Israel itself. A church that reads the story as a parable about the soul, while baptizing the landowners and burying the peasants, has not been neutral. It has chosen Pharaoh and called the choice spirituality. And Gutiérrez was more careful than his critics often allow. He described liberation at three levels, social and political, human and historical, and finally liberation from sin, which he named the deepest and the root of the rest. He denied that any historical liberation was the coming of the kingdom or the whole of salvation. He thought a gospel that never touched the brickyards was not the gospel of the God who came down to Egypt.

Much of that is simply true, and the northern churches that dismissed it often did so from comforts they had not examined; the essay on [why justice is theological, not political](/writing/justice-not-political-theological) presses the point. But the critique is serious too. Levenson's objection was twofold. The first is the preposition: a reading that ends at the sea cuts the story off before Sinai and turns a transfer of service into a generic emancipation. The second is particularity. The Exodus text says God acted because he "remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exodus 2:24), and Levenson warned that Christians who turn Israel's story into a universal symbol of the oppressed repeat, in a new vocabulary, an old habit of emptying the Jewish story of the Jewish people. The Vatican's *Instruction on Certain Aspects of the Theology of Liberation* (1984) made a parallel point in Catholic terms: the Exodus liberation is ordered to the founding of the people of God and the covenant worship at Sinai, and cannot be reduced to politics.

Some of this is first-order and settled. That God hears the cry of the oppressed is not a political opinion; it runs from the Exodus through the Prophets to the Magnificat. That salvation is God's act, from sin and death, through the cross and resurrection of Christ, is the confessional floor, and a reading that makes revolution the saving event has left it. Between those lie real second-order questions on which faithful Christians disagree: how directly the Exodus should shape policy, whether the church should borrow social analysis from traditions hostile to it, how its witness differs from a party's program. This essay lands where its sources did. The Exodus is not a political program. It is a theological orientation that no political program is allowed to escape, and it becomes more demanding, not less, when the New Testament extends it to the Gentiles. The strongest objection to that position, which Gutiérrez's heirs would press and which has not been fully answered, is that the churches most insistent on "freedom for" have often used it to postpone "freedom from" indefinitely. A theology always ready to discuss Sinai and never ready to leave Egypt shouldn't be surprised when the oppressed stop trusting it.

The instrument has to cut the same way on the right and on the left. The nationalist reading keeps the promised land and forgets Amos. The purely political liberationist reading keeps the sea and forgets Sinai. The therapeutic reading, most at home in suburban churches, keeps neither: Egypt becomes whatever holds me back, the promised land becomes my best life, and no one asks who made the bricks. All three put the reader in Israel's place without asking whether he has ever stood in Pharaoh's, and all three drop the purpose of the rescue, which was never the people's own agenda. It was God. "Let my people go, that they may serve me."

## When the children ask

So what does the Exodus mean for Christians today? First, that we have a story which is not about us and is nonetheless ours. The church hasn't replaced Israel, and it isn't an audience watching someone else's history. It has been grafted into a people whose memory begins in slavery, and at the Lord's Table it is told to say "for me" about a rescue it did not see. That changes how a household holds its faith. The first question the Torah expects from a child is "What do you mean by this service?" The answer it scripts is not a definition of God. It's a story in the first person plural.

Most Christian homes have no ritual that invites the question anymore. The Supper is one place, and a family that talks on the drive home about why the bread was broken is doing something very old. The Sabbath is another. Deuteronomy grounds rest in the Exodus: everyone in the house stops, the servant and the animal and the foreigner, because you were once the one who could never stop. A household that keeps a day when no one produces is practicing the Exodus in a culture that keeps rebuilding the brickyards and calling them opportunity.

Second, freedom has a direction. Our children will be told that freedom is the absence of every claim, and they'll find, as the wilderness generation did, that a self with no master soon acquires one. The doubting Christian often suspects that faith is the cage and leaving it would be the Exodus. It's worth sitting with the possibility that the story runs the other way: that career and comfort and self-invention are the Pharaohs, and the service of the living God is the only service that doesn't end in bricks.

Third, the story judges its claimants, the church first, and I'm inside that judgment. I have preached the Exodus from a place of safety to people who mostly live in safety, and from there it's easy to read yourself into the story as the slave and never once as the Egyptian. The American church has been both. Its people prayed in the hush arbors, and its people also owned the land around them, preferred a Bible without the Exodus, and preached Canaan to settlers who were taking it from the people already there. We don't choose our ancestors. We can choose whether our remembering produces Deuteronomy's "therefore" or only a warm feeling.

The last book of the Bible gives the Exodus its final shape. John sees the redeemed standing beside a sea of glass, and "they sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb" (Revelation 15:3). The oldest song of rescue and the newest are one song, and the lamb whose blood marked the doorposts has become the Lamb who was slain. The story does end in freedom, the freedom God intended from the burning bush on: a people from every nation, no longer anyone's property, doing in his presence what they were rescued to do.

We are not there. We're in the wilderness, which is where the Exodus leaves everyone who reads it honestly: out of Egypt, not yet home, still carrying Egypt in our appetites and our institutions and our politics. The generation that crossed the sea didn't enter the land, and King said on his last night that he might not either. What they handed on was not arrival. It was a memory, a direction and a God who hears. Some evening a child at your table will ask what all this means. The words are ready: we were slaves, and the LORD brought us out. Whether the child believes them will depend less on how well we say them than on whether our house looks like it has left Egypt, and whether the people who work for us, live near us or depend on us would say so too.
