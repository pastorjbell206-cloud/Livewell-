Most Christians meet this question in the wrong order. It arrives as a news cycle, already sorted into teams, and by the time anyone opens a Bible the reading has been settled by the vote. So the verses become ammunition: Leviticus 19 and Matthew 25 on one side, Romans 13 on the other. Neither side reads the other's verses slowly, and many congregations have quietly agreed not to read any of them together, because the pews hold both the man who spent his career in law enforcement and the family whose paperwork isn't in order, and a pastor would rather keep the peace than lose either one.

I grew up without a father, which means I grew up knowing what it is to belong somewhere conditionally, watching a household from its edge. That is nothing like crossing a border with a child and a plastic bag, but it's close enough to make me wary of how easily settled people talk about who belongs. I am a settled person now, holding the keys to a house I did not build. The Bible says a great deal about the stranger, and nearly all of it is addressed to people like me.

The church has confused two questions, and the confusion serves everyone's convenience. Scripture binds the church, without ambiguity, in how it regards and treats the foreigner: with the same justice owed the native, with a love modeled on God's own, and with a memory of its own strangeness it is forbidden to lose. Scripture does not hand a modern nation its visa quotas, its asylum procedures or its enforcement budget; those are matters of prudence on which faithful Christians disagree. Both halves of that sentence are harder than they sound. The binding half asks more of the right than it usually admits, and the prudential half leaves more room than the left usually allows.

## Three Hebrew words and an argument about them

Start with vocabulary, because much of the Christian argument now turns on it. The Hebrew Bible's several words for the outsider are not synonyms. The *ger* is the sojourner or resident alien, the person who has left home and settled among a people not his own, without the land and kin that gave an Israelite standing. The *toshav* is also a resident outsider, often paired with *ger* in a single phrase. Abraham calls himself both when he asks the Hittites for a grave for Sarah (Genesis 23:4), and God tells all Israel, "For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Leviticus 25:23, ESV). The *nokri* is the foreigner proper, someone who belongs to another people and remains attached to it; Israel could charge the *nokri* interest and did not release his debts in the seventh year (Deuteronomy 15:3; 23:20). There is also the *zar*, a word for the outsider that can carry a note of threat.

Scholars do not agree on how sharply these lines should be drawn. James K. Hoffmeier, an Egyptologist and Old Testament scholar, argues in *The Immigration Crisis* (2009) that the *ger* was a foreigner who had settled with the host community's permission and come under its law, something close to a legal resident alien, and that *nokri* and *zar* cover foreigners without that standing. Readers who share his view point to Jacob's sons asking Pharaoh's leave to settle in Goshen (Genesis 47:4). On this reading, applying the Torah's protections wholesale to people who entered a modern country unlawfully misuses the text. M. Daniel Carroll R., an Old Testament scholar writing in *Christians at the Border* (2008; revised 2013), reads the same material more broadly. He begins further back, with every human being made in the image of God, notes how much of the Old Testament is a story of people on the move, and cautions against reading modern ideas of documentation into ancient categories. What made someone a *ger*, on his reading, was displacement and exposure, not a status conferred by an office.

The ancient readers argued too. The Septuagint often rendered *ger* as *prosēlytos*, and later rabbinic tradition distinguished the *ger tzedek*, the full convert, from the *ger toshav*, the resident outsider who kept a minimal law without converting. The philology does not settle the modern question in either direction, and an honest reader should say so.

But notice how much survives the argument. Ancient Israel issued no visas and kept no quota; whatever gave a person the standing of a *ger*, it wasn't a consulate. The law's protections do not stop at the tidiest edge of the category. The wage law covers the hired worker "whether he is one of your brothers or one of the sojourners who are in your land within your towns" (Deuteronomy 24:14). The courtroom is one courtroom: "You shall have the same rule for the sojourner and for the native, for I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 24:22). Nowhere is even the *nokri* handed over to be abused. The book of Ruth makes the point by story. Ruth is a Moabite, from a people the law barred from the assembly "even to the tenth generation" (Deuteronomy 23:3), and when Boaz shows her kindness she falls on her face and asks, "Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?" (Ruth 2:10). The word she uses of herself is the feminine of *nokri*. She gleans under laws written for the sojourner and the poor, and she becomes the great-grandmother of David. Whatever the categories meant, the canon shows them bending toward the foreign woman who took refuge under Israel's God. The rabbis of the Talmud counted thirty-six places where the Torah warns against wronging the *ger*, and some said forty-six (Bava Metzia 59b); by their day they read the word chiefly of the convert, but the count is of the same verses. God repeats this command as though he knew which one his people would most want to forget.

## The case for the border, as its defenders would make it

The argument for enforced borders deserves its strongest voice, and a Christian who dismisses it as mere fear has not read the texts it stands on. It begins with the goodness of order. Paul told a small church in the capital of the empire, "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God" (Romans 13:1), and he called the magistrate "God's servant for your good" (Romans 13:4). A government that cannot decide who enters its territory cannot keep that charge. It continues with the legitimacy of nations. In Athens Paul said God "made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place" (Acts 17:26). Israel had borders, and when Moses asked the king of Edom for passage along the King's Highway and was refused, Israel turned away rather than force the road (Numbers 20:14–21).

Then comes the duty of a government to its own people, which the defender of enforcement grounds in the old tradition of ordered love. Augustine, in *On Christian Teaching* (begun around 397), held that every person is to be loved equally, but since no one can do good to everyone, we owe particular care to those joined to us by place, time and circumstance, as though they had been assigned to us by lot. Paul reasons the same way in miniature: "So then, as we have opportunity, let us do good to everyone, and especially to those who are of the household of faith" (Galatians 6:10). A state is not a church, but it is a trust, and the officials who hold it owe its citizens something they do not owe the whole world.

The strongest part of the case is the part its critics least like to hear. The costs of rapid, unmanaged migration fall on the working poor, who compete for the same wages and apartments, and not on the professionals who write about welcome from neighborhoods where nothing changes. An unmanaged system also rewards the young man strong enough to make the crossing and able to pay the smuggler over the widow who waits a decade in a camp doing everything lawfully. The rule of law is one of the few protections the weak have, and selective enforcement teaches everyone that law belongs to whoever can evade it. A restrictionist Christian can affirm every sojourner text in the Torah and still hold that those texts governed how persons and communities treat the stranger among them, not how many strangers a modern state must admit.

That is a case its defenders would sign, and much of it is true. Order is a creational good, and nations are not sin. A citizen may care about his neighbor's wages without being a bigot, and a church that answers his worry with contempt has failed to love him. But the case has two weak joints. The first is Romans 13 itself, read in its setting. The chapter division is a later addition, and a few verses earlier the same apostle wrote to the same congregation, "Contribute to the needs of the saints and seek to show hospitality" (Romans 12:13). The word behind *hospitality* is *philoxenia*, love of strangers. Paul never imagined the magistrate's sword relieving the church of it. The second weak joint is that an argument about how many may enter says nothing yet about how anyone already here must be treated. The case for the border, even at full strength, tells the state some true things about its job. It doesn't tell the church what to do when the stranger is standing in its foyer.

## The case for welcome, from Egypt to the empty tomb

Now the other case, which rests on a body of law rather than a mood. Israel's statutes about the stranger all stand on one ground, and the ground is memory. "You shall not wrong a sojourner or oppress him, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 22:21). A chapter later the law reaches inside the Israelite: "You shall not oppress a sojourner. You know the heart of a sojourner, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 23:9). Leviticus 19 is the chapter that gives us "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18), the line Jesus would call the second great commandment, and later in the same chapter it applies the same verb to the foreigner: "When a stranger sojourns with you in your land, you shall not do him wrong. You shall treat the stranger who sojourns with you as the native among you, and you shall love him as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 19:33–34). Deuteronomy grounds it deeper still, in God's own character: "He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing. Love the sojourner, therefore, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:18–19).

That last text changes the argument. Israel is not told to tolerate the stranger because God tolerates him. It is told to love him because God already does, and because Israel knows that love from the inside. The law built it into the economy through gleaning (Leviticus 19:9–10), the third-year tithe (Deuteronomy 14:29) and a Sabbath that covered "the sojourner who is within your gates" (Deuteronomy 5:14). The prophets treated the stranger as a test of whether a people knew God at all. Jeremiah's charge to the king was to "do no wrong or violence to the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow" (Jeremiah 22:3), and Malachi put "those who thrust aside the sojourner" in the dock beside the sorcerers and adulterers when God draws near for judgment (Malachi 3:5). Abraham Joshua Heschel argued in *The Prophets* (1962) that the prophets' anger over the treatment of the powerless was not one concern among many but a window into what he called the pathos of God, a God who is moved by what is done to the weak. The widow, the orphan and the sojourner appear together because all three stood at the city gate with no one to speak for them.

The story line runs the same way, from Abraham leaving Ur to Moses naming his son Gershom, "for he said, 'I have been a sojourner in a foreign land'" (Exodus 2:22), to Israel by the rivers of Babylon. Matthew's Gospel opens with a family fleeing by night after the angel tells Joseph, "Rise, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt" (Matthew 2:13). Herod's Judea was a client kingdom of Rome and Egypt a Roman province, so there was no passport, visa or border post in our sense, and a careful reader should say so. But Herod's reach stopped where Egypt began, which is why Egypt could be a refuge, and the child fled a king's violence into a foreign land and lived there until it was safe to come home.

The New Testament gathers all this into the church's own practice. Jesus places himself at the door in the judgment of the nations: "I was a stranger and you welcomed me" (Matthew 25:35). Interpreters have long debated whether "the least of these my brothers" (Matthew 25:40) means every needy person or Jesus' own disciples in their need, since Matthew elsewhere uses "brothers" for the disciples (Matthew 12:50; 28:10). The welcome case doesn't depend on winning that debate, because the letters command the same thing plainly: "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" (Hebrews 13:2). The empire noticed. Around 362 the emperor Julian, trying to revive the old religion, wrote to a pagan high priest in Galatia complaining that the Christians' kindness to strangers had done more than anything else to spread their faith, and that they fed the pagan poor as well as their own. Christine Pohl's *Making Room* (1999) traces how that welcome of strangers, once near the center of Christian life, was handed off to institutions and then shrunk into the entertaining of friends.

The strongest form of the welcome case, then, does not claim that Leviticus is a visa policy. It claims that the stranger is a neighbor by God's own definition, that God loves him before we decide what to do with him, and that a rescued people have no right to treat the stranger the way Egypt treated them. Matthew Soerens and Jenny Yang, who both worked with immigrants and refugees through World Relief, press that case in *Welcoming the Stranger* (2009) without pretending the law is simple. They explain how the American system actually works, argue that for many would-be immigrants there is no line to stand in, and call for a reform that takes both enforcement and a lawful path seriously.

## An American history of the stranger

The question did not start with the last election. The Naturalization Act of 1790 limited naturalization to an alien who was a "free white person." In 1844 riots in Philadelphia, sparked in part by a quarrel over which Bible would be read in the public schools, burned Catholic churches, and a decade later the Know-Nothing movement ran candidates on hostility to Irish and German Catholic newcomers. The strangers Protestant America feared then were fellow Christians, and their descendants now sit in the same pews as the descendants of the people who feared them.

In 1882 Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act, the first major federal law to restrict immigration by nationality. It drew real strength from white workers in California who believed Chinese labor was driving down their wages; the slogan of the day was "The Chinese must go." The wage worry was not imaginary, and that is exactly the lesson. A legitimate concern for working people was conscripted into racial exclusion, and the exclusion was not repealed until 1943. The Immigration Act of 1924, usually called Johnson-Reed, then set national-origin quotas at two percent of each nationality's share of the foreign-born population in the 1890 census, a year chosen to shrink immigration from southern and eastern Europe, and it effectively closed the door to Asia. The historian Mae Ngai argues in *Impossible Subjects* (2004) that the 1924 law made the "illegal alien" a central figure in American law and politics for the first time, because sweeping numerical restriction created a class of people whose very presence was unlawful. Illegality, in her account, is a status law produces, not a trait a person carries.

Fifteen years later the quota system met the *St. Louis*. In May 1939 the ship left Hamburg carrying 937 passengers, nearly all of them Jewish refugees. Cuba refused most of them. The United States refused them too; many had applied for American visas and were waiting on a quota that was already full. The ship sailed back to Europe, and by the count of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, 254 of its passengers were later killed in the Holocaust. Every argument used against them was lawful and orderly, and that reasonableness is what ought to haunt us. In 1951 the nations wrote the Refugee Convention out of the wreckage of that war.

In 1965 the Hart-Celler Act abolished the national-origin quotas in favor of family reunification and needed skills. It was a moral correction. It also placed the first numerical cap on immigration from the Western Hemisphere, arriving just after the Bracero program for Mexican farm labor ended in December 1964. Ngai, and the sociologist Douglas Massey with his colleagues in *Beyond Smoke and Mirrors* (2002), have argued that closing those legal channels while the demand for Mexican labor remained helped produce the large undocumented population of the decades that followed. That is a scholarly argument about causes, and it should be offered as one. The Refugee Act of 1980 wrote the international definition of a refugee into American law and built the modern resettlement system. The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, signed by Ronald Reagan, joined legal status for roughly 2.7 million people who had lived in the country for years to penalties on employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers. The legalization happened; the enforcement half was widely judged weak, and many restrictionists still remember 1986 as a promise the government did not keep. They are not wrong to remember it.

That history doesn't tell a Christian how to vote. It shows that the category of the unlawful stranger is partly the product of a nation's choices, so the church cannot treat the word "illegal" as though it described a person's soul. And it shows each side where its own failures lead. Restriction that did not guard its language and its motives ended in exclusion by race and a ship turned back at sea. Legalization that did not keep faith on enforcement bred a distrust that has soured nearly every attempt at reform since.

## What Scripture binds and what it leaves to prudence

The fight in most congregations is two people arguing at different altitudes without knowing it. Some things are binding on every Christian as a matter of obedience. The person at the border or in the next pew bears the image of God, and the image does not expire at a line on a map or depend on a document. The church may never speak of image-bearers as vermin, an infestation or a flood; that language does theological work, and the work is dehumanization. The stranger among us is owed the same justice as the native: honest wages, a fair hearing, protection from abuse, a day of rest (Deuteronomy 24:14; Leviticus 24:22). And the church, as the church, is commanded to welcome the stranger at its own door and to practice *philoxenia* (Romans 12:13; Hebrews 13:2), whatever his status and whatever the state is doing. No reading of Romans 13 releases a congregation from that, and neither does silence. A church that has held meetings about carpet and parking and never once asked what the immigrant families in its own town are afraid of has taken a position; it just hasn't been honest enough to name it.

In practice that obedience is unglamorous. It looks like a congregation that learns the difference between a refugee, who was vetted and admitted from abroad after fleeing persecution; an asylum seeker, who asks for the same protection at or inside the border and waits for a ruling; and a person without legal status, who entered without inspection or stayed past a visa. The categories change what help is needed, not anyone's worth. None of this is legal advice; a family facing immigration law needs a licensed immigration attorney or an accredited representative at a recognized nonprofit. It looks like a pastor who knows the local resettlement agency, and members who sit at the table long enough to learn names. Sentiment that never becomes a guest room, a ride to a hearing or a seat at a birthday party is not yet hospitality.

Other things are genuinely second-order, left to the prudence of citizens and rulers, and faithful Christians will land in different places. How many people a nation admits each year, how it designs asylum, how it enforces its border, whether it favors resettlement here or aid nearer the crisis, what path if any it offers those already present: Scripture does not hand a modern state these answers. The pastor who preaches an immigration bill as the gospel has confused the altitudes as badly as the one who preaches the border as sacred. A Christian may support stricter enforcement without sinning against Leviticus 19, and a Christian may support broader admission without despising Romans 13.

The ordering cuts both ways, and this essay lands where its sources landed. The second-order argument may never reach down and dissolve the first-order obligation. When "we need an orderly border" becomes a reason to treat people as a contamination, the position has left its own moral ground, and when "welcome the stranger" becomes a license to sneer at every neighbor who worries about order, the same thing has happened in the other direction. The church must be willing to say in public when a policy is crushing people it can name, and that sentence ought to cost it something on both flanks. If it only ever costs a congregation on one side, it is probably doing politics in theology's vocabulary.

Criticize both with the same instrument, because the same sin lies under both. On the right, the sojourner texts are explained away with a speed never applied to the verses about the tithe, Romans 13 is quoted as if it settled policy details it never mentions, and cruelty is applauded as strength. The cross gets conscripted into the flag, and that is idolatry before it is politics. On the left, the migrant becomes a mascot. There is an advocacy that speaks of immigrants constantly and knows none, treats every misgiving as hatred, has nothing but a lecture for the working-class citizen watching his rent rise, and borrows the prophets' language without the prophets' love for the whole people they indicted. One side makes the stranger a threat and the other makes him a symbol. Neither has to learn his name.

I have to put myself inside this. I have preached the Good Samaritan to a congregation I never asked to cross anything. I have preached Leviticus 19 in seasons when the news made it convenient and gone quiet in seasons when it didn't. I have weighed the political complexity against the textual clarity, let the complexity win, and called it prudence.

The strongest objection this essay has not fully answered is Hoffmeier's. If he is right that the Torah's protections were written for the lawful resident, then Israel's statutes give the modern advocate less direct warrant than advocates claim, and the weight shifts onto the image of God, Jesus' welcome and the church's commanded hospitality. That still carries every binding obligation named above; a church doesn't need a *ger* statute to be forbidden to despise a man God made. But what a Christian citizen owes when the law itself is doing the harm, and how far the church's duty at its door should shape its members' votes, is not settled here. Faithful readers will keep arguing about it, and they should, as people who have first agreed on what the stranger is worth.

## Outside the gate

There is one more reversal, and the comfortable reader, which is to say me, has to feel it. We cast ourselves as householders deciding whom to admit. Scripture turns the picture over. "So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). Before grace, Paul says, we were the ones outside, "strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12). We had no claim on the household and no standing to demand one. Citizenship in the kingdom was given to aliens who could not earn it. Every Christian is a naturalized foreigner, holding his place by grace and not by right.

And the naturalized are not allowed to settle in. Peter writes to the churches "as sojourners and exiles" (1 Peter 2:11). The second-century Epistle to Diognetus described Christians to a pagan inquirer as people who live in their own countries as though they were visitors, for whom every foreign land is a homeland and every homeland a foreign land. The church began as a people who kept their bags by the door, and a church that fears the stranger has forgotten its own baptism.

Beneath all of it is the cross. "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Hebrews 13:12). The one citizen with every claim on the Father's house was put out of the city and executed in the place of the unclean, so that those with no claim could be brought in. The chapter that opens by commanding hospitality to strangers closes on this: "Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured. For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Hebrews 13:13–14). The Christian who looks at the stranger from his own doorway is looking toward the place where Christ stood for him, outside the wall, where the welcome was bought.

None of this settles the next vote. It settles something harder: who we are when the stranger is standing in front of us, on the church steps, at the job site, in the fellowship hall after the service when nobody knows what to say. The law of Israel assumed the forgetting, which is why it kept legislating memory. *For you were sojourners in the land of Egypt.* We were strangers, and we were not left outside. Whatever the church argues about next, it has no right to forget the country it came from, or the gate it was carried through, or the price of the carrying.
