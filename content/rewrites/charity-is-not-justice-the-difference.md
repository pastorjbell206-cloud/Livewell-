## The people on the riverbank

There's a story that gets told in church basements, public health lectures and community organizing trainings, and it has been credited to so many people, Desmond Tutu among them, that nobody can say with confidence where it began. A handful of villagers are standing by a river when a body comes past in the current. They wade in and pull the person out. Then another comes, and another, and soon the whole village is organized around rescue: shifts on the bank, blankets and a clinic, a rota of the strongest swimmers. One day someone climbs out of the water and starts walking upstream. The others shout after her that they need every pair of hands. She calls back that she's going to find out why people keep falling in.

The story isn't an argument against rescue. Anyone who hears it as permission to stop pulling people out has missed the point. It's about what rescue can't do on its own. The river will keep delivering bodies for as long as whatever is upstream stays as it is, and a village can grow so proud of its blankets and its clinic that it forgets there is an upstream at all.

American churches are very good at the riverbank. We run pantries, benevolence funds and coat drives, and the help is real. A family that gets groceries on Thursday eats on Friday, and no theory of justice should ever make a Christian embarrassed about that. But when the same family comes back the next month, and the month after, the bag of groceries has done what it could and changed nothing about why it was needed. I've been part of that work, and I've felt the warm relief of having given, and mistaken the relief for righteousness.

The question underneath the parable is usually put as a contest. Is charity the same impulse as justice at a smaller scale, so that enough giving eventually adds up to a just world? Or are they different things, so that charity can quietly work against justice by relieving the pressure that justice needs? Both answers have their parties. Other essays here take up [what Scripture says about poverty and whether it is political](/writing/is-poverty-political-the-bibles-answer) and [what the Bible means by justice](/writing/justice-not-political-theological). This one asks the narrower question they leave standing: what is the actual difference between giving to the poor and doing justice for them, and why did the church once think it mattered so much?

The answer turns on a distinction older than any American party. Charity, in the sense most of us now use the word, is a gift: something that belongs to me, which I choose to hand to someone who has no claim on it. Justice is a debt: something that belongs to someone else, which I'm holding and must return. A long line of Christian teachers, from Ambrose in the fourth century to the Second Vatican Council in the twentieth, insists that a great deal of what we call charity is really the second thing wearing the clothes of the first. And that confusion is not harmless. When I give you what I owe you and let you thank me for it, I've gained something I had no right to, and you've lost something you had every right to keep.

## What the church once meant by "owed"

The word itself has shrunk. The Latin *caritas* was the church's word for the love Paul praises in 1 Corinthians 13, and the King James Version of 1611 still rendered that chapter's great word as "charity." Modern translations say "love," because in ordinary English "charity" has narrowed to mean almsgiving, or the institutions that handle it, or the posture of a comfortable person toward an unfortunate one. The narrowing has a cost. Paul himself warned that the two can come apart: "If I give away all I have, and if I deliver up my body to be burned, but have not love, I gain nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:3, ESV). A person can empty his accounts without love. Paul could imagine almsgiving with no charity in it, and we have learned to imagine charity as nothing more than almsgiving.

The early church did not make that mistake, and its preachers were blunt about why. Sometime around 389, Ambrose, bishop of Milan, wrote a treatise on the story of Naboth. The story is in 1 Kings 21. King Ahab wants a vineyard next to his palace for a vegetable garden, and its owner, Naboth, refuses to sell: "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1 Kings 21:3, ESV). Under the Torah the land was God's, held by families as an inheritance, and no king could buy a family out of its place in Israel. So Jezebel arranges false witnesses, Naboth is stoned, and Ahab takes the vineyard. Ambrose opens by observing, in substance, that the story is old but happens every day, and that Ahab is not one king but a whole class of people who can't stop adding field to field. Then he turns to his wealthy hearers with the line the tradition would never forget. When you give to the poor, he tells them in effect, you aren't making a gift out of what is yours. You're returning what is theirs, because the earth was given to all in common and you have taken more than your share of it.

John Chrysostom was preaching in Antioch in the same years, around 388, and his sermons on the rich man and Lazarus press the same point from the other side. The rich man in Jesus' parable is never accused of robbing Lazarus. He simply lived beside him. Chrysostom argues that this was exactly his crime: not to share one's goods with the poor is itself a kind of theft, since what the rich man held belonged to the needy before it belonged to him. Two centuries later, around 590, Gregory the Great gathered this conviction into the handbook that trained medieval bishops, *The Book of Pastoral Rule*. In the third part, where he explains how to counsel different kinds of people, he tells pastors to warn the generous against pride. When we give the poor what they need, Gregory says in substance, we're rendering them what is theirs rather than bestowing what is ours, and we're paying a debt of justice more than performing a work of mercy.

These men weren't socialists, whatever either the right or the left would like to make of them. They took private property for granted, preached to the consciences of the rich rather than drafting land reform, and believed a wealthy Christian could be saved, provided he learned that he was a steward and not an owner. But their language was the language of debt. The poor, in their preaching, have a claim on the surplus of the rich that doesn't depend on the rich feeling kind. The gift is real, and they praised it. It simply came second, after the debt had been paid.

The tradition kept that order into the modern period. In 1965 the Second Vatican Council's decree on the lay apostolate, *Apostolicam Actuositatem*, said in its eighth section that the demands of justice must be satisfied first, so that nobody offers as a gift of charity what is already owed in justice. That sentence could have been written by Gregory. It also names the exact temptation of a prosperous church: to keep a debt on the books, pay a small part of it, and call the payment generosity.

## Aquinas and the three kinds of giving

If the Fathers preached the distinction, Thomas Aquinas mapped it. In the second part of the second part of the *Summa Theologiae*, written around 1271 and 1272, he treated justice, charity and generosity as separate virtues, and the care with which he kept them apart is what the American argument has lost.

Justice, for Aquinas, is the steady and lasting will to render to each person what is his due, a definition he took over from Roman law (II-II, q. 58, a. 1). It is about the other person's right. It doesn't ask how I feel, and it isn't finished when I've meant well. Liberality, the virtue of open-handedness with money, is different. Aquinas asks whether it is a part of justice and answers that it isn't a species of justice, only a virtue annexed to it, because justice gives another what is his, while liberality gives another what is one's own (q. 117, a. 5). Liberality is a real virtue, lovely and worth praising. But it lives on the far side of the debt. It begins only where what I owe has been paid.

Then Aquinas complicates the picture, because he refuses to let "charity" mean "optional." Almsgiving, he says, is an act of charity working through mercy (q. 32, a. 1). Yet he goes on to ask whether it is a matter of command, and he answers that it is: to give out of one's surplus is required, and so is giving to someone in extreme need (q. 32, a. 5). Behind that answer sits his teaching on property. People may rightly own things and manage them, he argues, but in the use of external goods a person should hold them not as his own alone but as common, ready to share them with others in their need (q. 66, a. 2). He even concludes that in a case of urgent and evident need, where there is no other remedy, a person who takes what he must have to live is not committing theft, because in such need all things are common (q. 66, a. 7).

So Aquinas gives us not two categories but three. There is what is owed in strict justice: the wage honestly earned, the contract kept, the thing taken restored, the scale not tilted. There is what charity commands: the surplus that must go to meet the neighbor's need, which the giver doesn't own in the way he imagines. And there is the free gift beyond both, the liberality that can't be demanded and is beautiful precisely because it can't. The modern word "charity" has swallowed the second category into the third, so that nearly everything the rich give the poor feels voluntary, and anything voluntary feels like a favor. Aquinas would say we've moved a debt into the gift column and started thanking ourselves for paying it.

One more point keeps the distinction from hardening into a war between two virtues. Charity, in its full sense as the love of God and neighbor, is for Aquinas the form of all the virtues (q. 23, a. 8). Far from being justice's rival, it's what gives justice its life and aims it at God. Justice without love turns cold and grasping. Love that refuses justice is, on Aquinas's terms, a counterfeit. So the question that matters is whether the love we claim is large enough to pay what we owe before it starts giving what we don't.

Scripture had drawn the same lines long before. "Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in your power to do it" (Proverbs 3:27, ESV). The Hebrew behind "those to whom it is due" speaks of the good's owners, as though the good I'm holding already has a name on it that isn't mine. When John the Baptist preached repentance in the wilderness, the crowds asked what they should do, and he answered them first with a command to share: "Whoever has two tunics is to share with him who has none, and whoever has food is to do likewise" (Luke 3:11, ESV). Then tax collectors asked, and he didn't tell them to give; he told them to stop taking: "Collect no more than you are authorized to do" (Luke 3:13, ESV). In one sermon John laid out both halves: the one who has more must share, and the one who has power must stop taking. Neither command substitutes for the other.

Jeremiah pushed it further still. He indicted King Jehoiakim for building a spacious palace by making his neighbors work for nothing, and then he held up the king's father, Josiah, as the contrast: "He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Is not this to know me? declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 22:16, ESV). The prophet doesn't say that justice for the poor is a sign of knowing God, or its fruit. He says it *is* knowing God. That's a claim no theory of optional generosity can survive.

## The strongest case for charity

Before any verdict, the defender of charity deserves his strongest voice, and it is stronger than the people upstream usually admit.

Alexis de Tocqueville made the case in 1835, in a short essay now known as the *Memoir on Pauperism*, written after he had watched England's poor laws at work. What troubled him was that legal relief, handed out as a right by public officials, seemed to produce the very dependence it meant to cure. It gathered a permanent class of paupers who expected support and could not escape it, and it cut the moral bond between rich and poor, because a man who receives from a clerk owes the giver nothing and the giver sees no face. Private charity, Tocqueville admitted, was unreliable; it gave too little in hard years and depended on the mood of the fortunate. But it joined people. It created gratitude on one side and knowledge on the other. Five years later, in the second volume of *Democracy in America* (1840), he described with something close to wonder how Americans of every age and condition formed associations for every conceivable purpose, building hospitals, schools and relief societies with no permission from anyone. He believed that habit was one of liberty's deepest defenses, and that a government which took such work over would teach citizens to stop doing it.

Marvin Olasky brought the argument to American Christians in *The Tragedy of American Compassion* (1992). He reminded readers that compassion means suffering with, not writing checks from a distance, and he argued that the best nineteenth-century relief societies had worked because they were personal and demanding at once. He named its marks, from affiliation and bonding, which tied a needy family to community and to one faithful volunteer, through discernment and employment, to God, because the societies addressed the spirit as well as the stomach. Olasky's charge against the welfare expansion of the 1960s was that it replaced relationship with disbursement and treated the poor as a category rather than as neighbors with names. Catholic teaching gives the same instinct a principle. Pius XI, in *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931), stated it as the principle of "subsidiary function," now called subsidiarity: it is an injustice for a larger and higher association to take over what smaller and lower communities can do for themselves.

Put all that together and the case for charity is serious. Charity is personal where policy is abstract. It knows the name of the person it helps, and it can ask something of that person, which a program cannot do without humiliating him. It is immediate; the child is hungry tonight, and no statute passed next year will feed her. It is also humble in a way crusades seldom are. The advocate for structural change is always in danger of loving humanity in the abstract and resenting the actual people who get in the way of his plan. And much of what passes for justice in our moment costs the person who claims it nothing at all: a vote, a post, a phrase of solidarity, while the charity worker is at the pantry on a cold Saturday. The defender of charity asks a fair question. Which of the two actually touched a poor person this week?

## The strongest case for justice

The defender of justice has a reply, and much of it comes from people who worked among the poor rather than only theorizing about them.

Jane Addams moved into a house on Halsted Street in Chicago in 1889 and, with Ellen Gates Starr, founded Hull House among immigrant families. Ten years later she published an essay in *The Atlantic Monthly* called "The Subtle Problems of Charity." She described the charity visitor of her day, usually a comfortable woman sent by a relief society, who came into a poor family's home carrying her own class's virtues of thrift and sobriety and judging the family by them. Addams didn't mock her. She saw that the visitor was sincere, that the poor had moral codes of their own, generous to neighbors in ways the visitor couldn't see, and that the encounter taught both sides distrust. The problem wasn't a lack of goodwill. It was that the relationship itself put one person permanently above the other. The same societies, the Charity Organisation Society founded in London in 1869 and its many American imitators, were the ones that made the line between the deserving and undeserving poor into a working method. Olasky admired them. Addams lived next door to the people they sorted.

Reinhold Niebuhr added a harder analysis in *Moral Man and Immoral Society* (1932). He argued that philanthropy mixes real pity with a display of power, and that this is why the powerful are more inclined to be generous than to be just. A gift leaves the giver in control. He decides when, how much and to whom, and the arrangement that made him able to give and made the other person need is untouched. A just wage or a fair law does something a gift never can: it takes the decision out of his hands.

Then there's Dom Helder Câmara, who became archbishop of Olinda and Recife in Brazil's poor northeast in 1964, the year of the military coup, and spent two decades there under a regime that watched him closely. The line most associated with him circulates in several versions and its exact source is hard to pin down, though it has been credited to him for decades: when he fed the poor, people called him a saint, and when he asked why the poor had no food, they called him a communist. In *Spiral of Violence* (1971) he described the injustice built into an economy as the first violence, the one that provokes revolt and then the repression that answers it, and he argued that the only way out of the spiral was to confront that first violence without taking up arms. His point about the saint and the communist is the whole debate in two sentences. Feeding the hungry is praised because it threatens no one. Asking why they are hungry is feared because the answer has an address.

Janet Poppendieck's *Sweet Charity?* (1998) traced how emergency food, which was meant to be temporary in the early 1980s, became a permanent part of American life, and she warned that because charity satisfies the giver it can take the pressure off everyone else to ask why it's needed. (The essay on [hunger and the church's table](/writing/church-food-insecurity) follows her argument further.) The upstream parable tells the same story. Relief that never asks about causes turns into a permanent institution, and a permanent institution acquires an interest in the river staying the way it is.

## One instrument for both

Now the two cases have to be judged, and judged the same way. It won't do to measure charity by its best volunteers and justice by its worst activists, or the reverse. Deuteronomy supplies a better instrument. When it commands generosity to the poor, it keeps calling them your brother, and that word forbids a certain kind of relationship. Your brother isn't your client, your project, your cause or your ward. He's your equal before God, with a claim on the family's goods that doesn't depend on your mood.

So ask three questions of any response to poverty, whether a pantry or a policy. Does it treat the poor person as a brother, with a name and a claim, rather than as a category to be managed? Does it cost the people who hold power, or only those who have little? And does it pay what is owed before it offers what is spare?

Charity fails the test in a recognizable way. It fails when it keeps the giver permanently above the receiver, a small lord at a folding table whose kindness must be waited for and thanked. It fails when it gives only out of surplus and never asks whether the surplus was honestly come by. And it fails when it refuses to look upstream because the answer might implicate the donors. I've been part of churches with thriving benevolence ministries and no public word at all about the arrangements that produced the need, and I don't think that was an accident. The food pantry improves a church's standing in town. A question about wages or rents can cost a pastor his biggest givers.

But the cause of justice fails the same test in its own way, and it's just as recognizable. It fails when the poor become a category in a speech rather than neighbors with faces, so that the advocate knows the statistics and not a single name. It fails when it costs the advocate nothing but a vote and an opinion, while the actual work of sitting with a family in crisis is left to someone else. And it fails when it simply moves the small lord from the folding table to a government office, so that the poor now wait on a caseworker rather than a benefactor and are no more anyone's brother than before. Tocqueville and Câmara, from opposite sides of the world, were asking the same question: who is lord over the poor? One feared a paternal state, the other a paternal oligarchy. Both were right.

This is where the essay lands, and it's worth marking the order of the question. That Christians owe the poor both personal generosity and justice is not open; the Law, the Prophets, John the Baptist, Jesus and the apostles all bind it. That justice is owed first, and that a gift can't discharge a debt, is the tradition's settled teaching from Ambrose to the Council. How a pluralist society should arrange its wages, benefits and relief is a second-order question of prudence and evidence, and faithful Christians who agree on everything above may disagree about the minimum wage and remain brothers. The strongest objection this essay hasn't fully answered is a practical one: churches are rarely competent at structural analysis, and when pastors pronounce on policy they can bind consciences with their own economic opinions and call them the word of God. That danger is real. It argues for humility about remedies. It doesn't argue for silence about debts.

## The debt that never gets paid off

Luke tells of a man who did both. Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector in Jericho, rich from a system that let men like him take more than Rome required. When Jesus called him down from the sycamore and invited himself to dinner, Zacchaeus answered with two sentences that the church has too often read as one: "Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (Luke 19:8, ESV). The first sentence is charity, a gift to people who had no claim on him. The second is justice, restitution to people he had robbed, at the rate the Torah demanded for a stolen sheep. Jesus' reply takes in both: "Today salvation has come to this house, since he also is a son of Abraham" (Luke 19:9, ESV). Had he given half to the poor and kept his ledgers closed, the town would have praised his generosity and his victims would still be waiting. His repentance is taken up more fully in the essay on [personal and systemic sin](/writing/individual-sin-and-systemic-sin-explained); the point here is simpler. Grace didn't make him choose.

Scripture also refuses to make charity small. "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD, and he will repay him for his deed" (Proverbs 19:17, ESV). God counts the gift as a loan to himself. And the whole argument finally runs into a sentence of Paul's that dissolves the neat line between gift and debt. "Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed, revenue to whom revenue is owed, respect to whom respect is owed, honor to whom honor is owed" (Romans 13:7, ESV). That is justice, the rendering of what is due. Then, in the next breath: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (Romans 13:8, ESV). Paul takes love, the thing we like to think of as the free gift beyond obligation, and calls it a debt. Every other account can be settled and closed. This one stays open for life.

That's where the cross stands in the question, and it's why the Christian can't finally be either the proud benefactor or the cold litigant. God owed us nothing. If anyone could have stood above the poor as a pure giver, it was God. Instead the Son entered the field, had nowhere to lay his head, and took on himself a debt that wasn't his. Paul describes it as God "canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross" (Colossians 2:14, ESV). At Calvary justice wasn't waved away by kindness, and kindness wasn't swallowed by justice. The debt was honored and the debtor was forgiven in the same death. The man who knows that about himself can't stand at a folding table as a small lord, because he knows he was the one in the river. And he can't make the poor into a cause, because he knows he was loved by name.

None of that resolves the river. It will still be carrying people past the church on Thursday, and the church should still be in the water. But the parable's question doesn't go away because we're busy. Somewhere upstream are wages, rents, courts and rules, some of which benefit people in our pews and some of which we've never bothered to learn about, and the tradition says that part of what we hand across the pantry counter was never ours to give. The hard work isn't choosing between the bank and the walk upstream. It's learning to read our own ledgers honestly, to find the line in them where what we called generosity was only a partial repayment, and to pay the rest before we let anyone thank us. The debt of love doesn't end there. It only begins to be paid.
