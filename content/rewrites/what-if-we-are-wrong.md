The question rarely arrives in the middle of an argument. It comes when the house is asleep, or halfway through a hymn you've sung for thirty years, and it feels less like temptation than like honesty. What if none of this is true? What if the story we tell about a crucified and risen Jew is a beautiful consolation that frightened people built so they could bear the dark, and everyone inside the building is too invested to say so out loud? The thought feels like a curtain pulled back, which is what makes it frightening, and it's why so many people type some version of it into a search bar at two in the morning, half hoping for an argument strong enough to make it stop.

I came to faith from atheism, so this question isn't a foreign army to me. It was my first language, and it never entirely leaves. There are still mornings when, for a few seconds, the whole structure looks like wishful thinking, and I've learned not to panic at those seconds. No argument made the question disappear. I've simply come to think it deserves to be asked all the way down, and that most of us, believers and skeptics alike, stop halfway.

Three things are worth saying before anything else. Christianity invites the question: its founding documents name the event that would refute it, so asking "what if we're wrong?" takes the apostles at their word. The question also cuts in two directions, because there are two ways to be wrong about God, and neither the believer nor the doubter stands on neutral ground while the other takes all the risk. And asking whether you might be wrong is a different act from ceasing to believe. The church has confused the two for a long time, and the confusion has cost us some of our most honest people.

## Paul wrote the test into the creed

A great deal of religion, and nearly all of the spirituality sold in bookstores now, lives where no historian can follow: in an inner experience, a timeless teaching, a moral ideal that would stay true whoever first said it. Beliefs kept there are safe, because nothing that happened or didn't happen in the world can touch them. Christianity gave itself no such shelter. Around the year 54, writing to a church in Corinth where some members had begun to say that there is no resurrection of the dead, Paul didn't soften the claim to keep them. He followed their position to its end, one link at a time.

"And if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14). The word means empty. If the tomb stayed occupied, the apostles have announced nothing and the Corinthians have trusted nothing.

Then he turns the knife on himself. "We are even found to be misrepresenting God, because we testified about God that he raised Christ, whom he did not raise if it is true that the dead are not raised" (1 Corinthians 15:15). That is the skeptic's charge, false witness about God, and Paul brings it against himself before anyone else can.

"And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). No forgiveness, and no moral teaching left over as a consolation prize. "Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished" (1 Corinthians 15:18). The believers they had buried are simply gone. And then the sentence that ought to unsettle every comfortable Christian: "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:19).

Paul is writing to people who wanted to keep Jesus and lose the body, and he tells them there's no such option; a faith that outlives the refutation of its central event is, in his view, merely pitiable. A few verses later he adds what he personally stands to lose. He asks why he and his companions are "in danger every hour" (1 Corinthians 15:30), and he says that if the dead are not raised, the logic belongs to the revelers Isaiah heard in besieged Jerusalem, feasting when they should have been repenting: "Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" (1 Corinthians 15:32; compare Isaiah 22:13). Paul doesn't pretend the Christian life is a good bargain whether or not it's true. He says that without the resurrection it is a bad one.

In 1934 Karl Popper published the book that appeared in English in 1959 as *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, proposing that what separates a claim about the world from a claim that merely protects itself is falsifiability: a real claim tells you what would count against it. Popper had no interest in rescuing religion. But Paul's paragraph meets the spirit of his test more squarely than most of what passes for spirituality today. It names a place, a time, a body and living witnesses, and says in effect that if this didn't happen, the faith is over. Christianity hands you the question itself.

The evidence for the resurrection and the strongest skeptical replies are weighed in [Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen), and why Christianity stakes itself this way is the subject of [Why Christianity and Not Another Religion?](/writing/why-christianity). The question here is how to live with the possibility of being wrong, because for most people at two in the morning the problem is less a missing argument than a question that stays after the arguments are in.

## The wager, stated fairly

The most famous attempt to reason about being wrong comes from a man who had already met God before he wrote it. On the night of 23 November 1654, Blaise Pascal, a mathematician who had helped found the theory of probability, had an experience he recorded on a parchment sewn into his coat and found after his death in 1662. It sets down the single word "fire" and names the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, not the God of philosophers and scholars. His notes toward a defense of the faith were published in 1670 as the *Pensées*, and among them is the wager.

It deserves to be stated as Pascal stated it. He addresses a particular reader: a clever, worldly man who has concluded that reason cannot settle whether God exists. Pascal grants him that premise for the sake of argument. Then he points out that the man can't simply decline to answer, because his life is already a choice one way or the other. In Pascal's phrase, *vous êtes embarqué*: you are already on the boat. You have one finite life to stake. If God is, and you have sought him, you gain everything; if he is not, you lose nothing of consequence. If God is, and you have refused him, the loss is total. Where reason cannot decide, Pascal argues, prudence can, and prudence says to seek God.

The objections are old and serious. The first is that the wager proves too much. Denis Diderot made the point sharply in his *Pensées philosophiques* in 1746: an imam, he said, could reason exactly the same way. If the argument works for the Christian God, it works for any deity who promises an infinite reward, and the gambler is left facing a table with a hundred wheels. The second is that belief can't be chosen by calculation: I can decide to raise my hand, but I can't decide to find a claim true because it would pay. The third came from William James, who was no enemy of faith. He thought a belief adopted on such a calculation would lack the inner life of faith, and suggested, with a smile, that if he were God he might take a particular pleasure in excluding such believers from their reward. A God worth wagering on would see through the wager.

Pascal saw the second objection coming. His reader protests that he is made in such a way that he cannot believe, and Pascal tells him to learn from people who were once bound as he is, and to begin living as they did, because what stands in his way is his passions more than his reason. It can sound like autosuggestion. Read inside the whole *Pensées*, it's an observation as true of marriage and friendship as of faith: some things are known only from within a life that has begun to take them seriously.

Pascal never meant the wager as a proof of God, and never rested his own faith on it; the man with the parchment in his coat was not a gambler who had run the numbers. The fragment belongs to an unfinished apology whose weight was meant to fall on Christ, prophecy and history, and it has a narrow target: the reader who thinks indifference is a safe place to stand. That is the part that survives every objection, and it bears directly on two in the morning. The question usually arrives as though only the believer were exposed, as though doubt were the harbor and faith the open sea. Pascal's point is that everyone is at sea. The wager gives no one a reason to believe. It removes the excuse of neutrality.

## Two ways to be wrong

The sharpest modern form of the objection came from an English mathematician who thought the believer's risk was not only his own. In 1877 W. K. Clifford published "The Ethics of Belief," and its central sentence has lost none of its edge: "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." His point was moral. No one's belief, he argued, is private: beliefs steer actions that land on other people, and unwarranted belief trains the believer, and those who learn from him, in credulity. His famous shipowner is discussed in [Is Faith Irrational?](/writing/is-faith-irrational). What matters here is where the argument lands when a pastor reads it.

It lands on the pulpit first, mine included. If Christianity is wrong, a pastor hasn't merely held a mistaken private opinion. He has stood at gravesides and told grieving families that their dead will rise. He has told children that they are known and loved by a God who isn't there. He has asked people to give money, forgive enemies, keep costly promises and lay down their lives on the strength of a report about a tomb. Paul felt the same weight when he said the apostles would be found misrepresenting God. Turned where it belongs, Clifford's charge falls hardest on those who preach, and a Christian who has never felt its force hasn't yet understood what he is claiming.

The reply came in 1896, in "The Will to Believe," a lecture William James gave to philosophy clubs at Yale and Brown, and it noticed something Clifford had passed over. We have two duties as knowers, not one: to believe what is true and to avoid believing what is false. They sound like the same duty stated twice, and they aren't. Someone who cares only about avoiding error can be perfectly safe by believing nothing, and he will miss every truth that asks for commitment before the evidence is complete. Every real knower strikes a balance between the two duties, and logic doesn't dictate the balance. Temperament does: which loss one fears more.

Clifford's rule, James argued, is the temperament of a man whose deepest horror is being made a dupe: a respectable horror, but a choice, with costs Clifford never counted. Where a question is what James called a genuine option, live, forced and momentous, and the evidence can't settle it, our passional nature, as he called it, not only may but must decide. To wait for proof is to decide against, and to lose the truth if it's there. The skeptic who would rather believe nothing than risk believing a lie has not escaped the risk of being wrong. He's chosen which kind of wrong he'd rather be.

James's argument has a real weakness: taken loosely, it licenses believing whatever consoles, and his own religion was far thinner than the apostles'. That worry is taken up in [Is Faith Just Wishful Thinking?](/writing/is-faith-just-wishful-thinking). But James's central distinction stands. There are two ways to be wrong about God. You can believe in him when he isn't there, and you can refuse him when he is. The question that visits at two in the morning always presents the first bill and hides the second.

John Henry Newman gave the church language for living inside that tension. In his *Apologia Pro Vita Sua* (1864) he distinguished a difficulty, a question you can't yet answer, from a doubt, the actual withdrawal of assent, and insisted that no number of the first adds up to the second; that distinction is worked out in [What Is Faith Deconstruction?](/writing/deconstruction-is-not-destruction). To ask "what if I'm wrong?" is to feel a difficulty. It is not, by itself, to stop believing.

Six years later, in *An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent* (1870), Newman described how people actually come to be sure of things that can't be demonstrated like theorems. Nobody believes that Britain is an island, he observed, on the strength of a single proof. We believe it because a great many independent lines of evidence, none conclusive alone, converge on the same point, and the mind judges the convergence with a faculty he called the illative sense. That's how a jury reaches a verdict and a doctor a diagnosis. Newman faced the obvious objection that people have been certain and wrong, and answered that the failures of a faculty don't abolish it, any more than mistakes of memory abolish the right to remember. The possibility of error is a reason for care, not a reason for paralysis.

## Nobody asks from neutral ground

The question also assumes that doubt is the resting position and belief the leap, that the skeptic stands on bedrock while the believer hangs over the drop. That assumption is itself a belief, and a large one. To hold that the universe is without purpose, that love is a trick evolution plays to keep us breeding, and that justice is a preference with nothing behind it, is to hold claims as sweeping as anything in the Apostles' Creed. None of them can be proved. When I was an atheist I held them as a Christian holds the creed, except that I'd been taught my convictions were the absence of conviction, and that was the least examined thing about me.

Lesslie Newbigin, a missionary in India for most of four decades and a bishop there for much of that time, spent the last years of his life on this problem. In *Proper Confidence* (1995) he traced the modern craving for certainty to René Descartes, who resolved to doubt everything that could be doubted, and, drawing on the scientist and philosopher Michael Polanyi, argued that the program was confused from the start. You can't doubt anything except on the basis of other things you don't doubt. Every act of questioning rests on commitments that aren't being questioned at the same moment, so doubt is never the neutral floor beneath all belief. It's one committed position examining another. The honest question is never whether you are standing on faith. It's which faith, and whether it can bear what a life will put on it.

What Newbigin offered in place of Cartesian certainty was what his title calls proper confidence, a confidence that rests on the faithfulness of the God who has made himself known rather than on the believer's proof, a theme followed further in [Can You Have Faith and Doubt at the Same Time?](/writing/can-you-have-faith-and-doubt). For the question here, its value is simple. Such confidence can hear "what if you're wrong?" without panic, because it never rested on the believer's immunity from error.

Charles Taylor described the setting. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he argued that where unbelief was nearly unthinkable in the West of 1500, every position is now cross-pressured: the believer feels the pull of unbelief in the empty hour, and the unbeliever feels the pull of belief at a graveside. That settles nothing about who is right, but it humbles everyone in the room. The question at two in the morning is the weather everyone now lives in, and its arrival proves nothing about the state of your faith.

## What if it's only useful?

There is a softer answer, offered from pulpits and by people who don't believe the creed: even if Christianity is wrong, it's good for you. There is real evidence behind the claim. In 2016 the journal *JAMA Internal Medicine* published a study led by Shanshan Li, with Tyler VanderWeele of Harvard among the authors, that followed tens of thousands of women in the Nurses' Health Study for about sixteen years; those who attended services more than once a week had a substantially lower risk of dying during that time than those who never attended, after adjusting for many other factors. It was an observational study, which can show an association but not prove a cause. In *American Grace* (2010), the political scientists Robert Putnam and David Campbell found that religious Americans are, on average, better neighbors by several measures than their secular counterparts: more generous with money and time, secular causes included, and more involved in the civic life of their communities.

A believer should receive findings like these gratefully, and a skeptic should take them seriously. The weekly gathering, the practice of forgiveness and the meal taken to a neighbor after a funeral appear to be good for human beings, which is what the church would expect if human beings were made for them.

But the argument can't carry the weight people put on it, for three reasons. The first is in the data. Putnam and Campbell found that what predicted the good neighborliness of religious Americans was less what they believed than whom they belonged to: friendships formed inside a congregation mattered more than theology or private devotion. That's worth knowing about congregations, but it's no evidence that anything they confess is true. Any close community with high expectations of its members might show many of the same effects.

The second reason is that a faith kept for its usefulness becomes a different faith. In *Soul Searching* (2005), Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton gave a name to the religion they found to be the dominant faith of American teenagers, whatever their denomination: moralistic therapeutic deism. God exists, wants people to be nice, wants them to feel good about themselves, and stays at a distance until needed. That is precisely the religion the usefulness argument produces. It keeps what helps, lets go of what costs, and has forgotten to ask whether the consolation is true.

The third reason is Paul's, and it's decisive. He doesn't say that without the resurrection the Christian life remains a healthy habit. He says that believers who hope in Christ for this life only "are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:19). Paul had been beaten, shipwrecked, stoned and imprisoned for this message, and by any reckoning without an empty tomb his life was a catastrophe. The New Testament offers a crucified man who was raised, and tells you that if he wasn't, you should stop.

In 1844 Karl Marx called religion the opium of the people, a thing that soothes those who ought to be awake. The usefulness argument concedes his point and simply prefers the opium. The Christian answer to Marx can't be that the drug works. It has to be that the report is true. A faith worth keeping only because it helps is worth nothing if it's false.

## Living with the question open

If the question can't be banished by an argument or medicated by a benefit, it has to be lived with, and the church has more practice at that than its Sunday confidence suggests. The psalms are the oldest evidence. Psalm 42 is the prayer of someone cut off from the house of God, taunted by people who find his faith ridiculous: "My tears have been my food day and night, while they say to me all the day long, 'Where is your God?'" (Psalm 42:3). The worst of it is that the taunt has reached inside him, and he argues with himself: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?" (Psalm 42:5). What he doesn't do is stop talking to God while he feels this way. He carries the taunt into the prayer, and Israel put the song in its hymnbook, so that generations of believers have sung the skeptic's question in worship.

The modern witness most people know is Mother Teresa. When her private letters were published in 2007 as *Come Be My Light*, readers learned that for most of the half century she spent among the dying in Calcutta, she felt almost nothing of God's presence, and that she wrote to her confessors about an emptiness so complete that at times she felt she had no faith at all. Skeptics read the letters as proof that she had stopped believing and kept up the performance, and the letters are dark enough to deserve that hearing. But the reading has to account for what she did with the dark: she kept praying into it, addressing her complaints to the one whose absence she felt, and kept her hands on the dying for decades. That is what the wager looks like when it's made by a whole life rather than a clever mind. Her story is told more fully in [Can You Have Faith and Doubt at the Same Time?](/writing/can-you-have-faith-and-doubt).

Dietrich Bonhoeffer left a smaller record of the same thing. In the summer of 1944, in a Berlin prison cell, he wrote a poem that asks who he really is. Others, he says, see a man who steps from his cell calm and cheerful. He knows himself as something else: restless and sick with longing like a caged bird, thirsty for a kind word, too weary even to pray. He doesn't settle which man he is. The poem ends by handing the question to God and saying, in effect, that whoever he is, he belongs to God. It was later published in *Letters and Papers from Prison*. Bonhoeffer was hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945, weeks before the war in Europe ended, with the question still open and the belonging intact.

None of them found a formula. But what they did has a shape. They didn't try to settle the largest question of their lives in the worst hour of the night. They spoke it aloud to God and to other people. They listened to the strongest voices on both sides, not the most embarrassing. And they kept doing the next faithful thing while the question stood, which isn't hypocrisy but the only way anyone learns whether a trust will hold.

One more thing has to be said plainly, because the question has a darker cousin. "What if Christianity is wrong?" can slide, without your noticing, into "what if nothing matters, including me?" When doubt comes tangled with sleeplessness, hopelessness or a heaviness that is plainly larger than any argument, it may not be a theological problem at all, or not only one. Depression and grief can wear the clothes of a philosophical question. Tell someone you trust, and talk to a doctor or a counselor; this essay is not medical advice, and getting help is not a failure of faith. If the dark has come to include any thought of ending your life, call or text 988, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, today. The feeling that there's no ground under you is real. The conclusion despair draws from it, that there's no way through and no one who would want you to find one, is false.

## To whom shall we go?

Much of the fear around this question is the church's doing, and pastors carry most of it. We taught people that a question was a crack and a crack a collapse. We answered honest doubts with quick arguments or a worried look, and people learned to keep their questions somewhere else, and many took them out the door. We lost them because we wouldn't let them ask among us, and that is ours to repent of before it is anyone else's.

John records a day when the question emptied the room. After a teaching many found intolerable, "many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him" (John 6:66). Jesus didn't soften what he'd said. He turned to the twelve: "Do you want to go away as well?" (John 6:67). Peter's answer is not a claim to certainty. The same man would later deny knowing Jesus. It's a comparison, made by someone who had looked at the alternatives: "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:68). Peter didn't have every answer. He had nowhere else to take the question, and he was honest enough to say so.

Paul asked the question more ruthlessly than any skeptic at two in the morning. He followed the possibility of being wrong through every link, to the empty preaching, the false witnesses, the unforgiven sins and the dead who stay dead, and then he wrote the sentence the whole chapter turns on: "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). His "what if" was a man looking straight at the worst case because he was convinced it hadn't happened.

You may not be there tonight. The question may still be sitting at the end of the bed, and no essay will send it away. But it was never only one question. What if we're wrong has a twin, and the twin is just as heavy: what if we're right? What if a man really did walk out of a grave outside Jerusalem, and everything you have been afraid to hope is not too good to be true but simply true? You don't have to answer either question before morning. You only have to decide which way you'll carry them, and to whom, and whether you'll keep asking until you find out if the stone stayed where they put it.
