Listen to how people talk about their bodies and you'll hear an odd picture, repeated so often it passes for common sense. We say the body "let us down," as if it were a colleague who missed a deadline. We track it with a watch and speak of hacking it, overriding it and upgrading it, and a few talk seriously about copying a mind off its failing tissue into something that doesn't age. The body, in all of it, is equipment. The real person sits somewhere behind the eyes, looking out the way a driver looks through a windshield.

The philosopher Gilbert Ryle named that picture in *The Concept of Mind* (1949), and he named it in order to criticize it. He called it the ghost in the machine, the view he traced to René Descartes's *Meditations* (1641) that a person is a thinking thing lodged in an extended thing, a mind that happens to operate a body. The phrase stuck because it describes what most of us assume without having decided to.

The modern world offers two ways out of the ghost story, and neither works. The hard materialist says there's no ghost, only the machine, chemistry that has learned to talk. The far more common answer keeps the ghost and demotes the machine: the self is the inner chooser, and the body is raw material it may use, alter or ignore. The first leaves the body with no one inside it; the second leaves it with no meaning of its own. Christianity says something neither can hold, and a great many Christians no longer quite believe it either.

**Your body is not a cage your real self is serving time inside, and it isn't a machine with no one home. It was made on purpose and called good, God took one up in Christ and has never set it down, and it is headed for resurrection. You don't merely have a body; in a real sense you are one, though never only one.** How we answer that shapes how we grow old, fall sick, eat, sleep and die.

## The Oldest Heresy Is Still the Newest

None of this is new. Plato, writing after the execution of Socrates in 399 BC, has his teacher describe philosophy in the *Phaedo* as a lifelong practice for dying, the soul training to be free of the body that clouds its sight, and elsewhere, in the *Gorgias* and the *Cratylus*, his Socrates passes on an older saying that plays on the likeness of *soma* and *sema*, body and tomb. The picture was noble in its way. It honored the mind and met death with composure. It was also the most serious rival to the Christian account of the person that the early church ever faced.

In the second century it came back inside the church. The teachers we loosely call Gnostics, among them Valentinus, who taught in Rome around the middle of that century, held that the material world was the botched work of a lesser power and that salvation was a secret knowledge by which the spirit of the enlightened few escaped the flesh. Some concluded that Christ only seemed to have a body, the view called docetism. Since a jar of their codices was dug up near Nag Hammadi in Egypt in 1945, we can read them in their own words. The New Testament had already named the test: "By this you know the Spirit of God: every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (1 John 4:2, ESV).

Irenaeus, who became bishop of Lyon after the persecution of 177, answered them in the five books of *Against Heresies* around 180. He saw that the argument about the body was an argument about God. If matter is a mistake, its maker is a fool or a villain, and the God of Israel who called the world good can't be the Father of Jesus. So Irenaeus insisted on one God who made everything with his own two hands, the Son and the Spirit, and who in Christ gathered up and restored the whole human story from the inside, flesh included. His best-known line is usually quoted as "the glory of God is a human being fully alive," which is loose. What he wrote comes closer to this: the glory of God is a living human being, and the life of a human being is the vision of God. God's glory is not a spirit that has shed its body but a whole creature, dust and breath, alive in his presence.

Tertullian of Carthage, in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* early in the third century, compressed the point into a phrase the church has kept: the flesh is the hinge of salvation. He argued it from the church's own practice. The flesh is washed, anointed, signed with the cross, overshadowed by the laying on of hands and fed with the body and blood of Christ, so that the soul may be cleansed, consecrated, strengthened, enlightened by the Spirit and filled with God. A faith that reaches the soul through the body can't treat the body as a wrapper. The essay on [why Christians share bread and wine](/writing/why-faith-uses-physical-things) follows that thought to the Lord's Supper.

It would be comforting to say the church won that fight and moved on. It didn't, quite. Peter Brown's *The Body and Society* (1988), a patient history of Christian renunciation in the early centuries, shows how tangled the story became. The desert ascetics were not, in Brown's account, simple haters of the flesh; many believed the body could be changed and drawn into the life of the Spirit. Yet the prestige of renunciation carried a drift, and in places the ordinary life of the household, the field and the table began to look like second-class discipleship. Paul had already warned against that slide: "These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (Colossians 2:23, ESV).

The modern form of the drift is quieter. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), calls it excarnation: the long movement, through the reforming movements of the late medieval and early modern church and on into the Enlightenment, by which faith was carried out of feast and fast, pilgrimage and gesture, and resettled in the head as belief and inner sincerity. Those of us in evangelical churches should feel that. We built services for listening minds in padded chairs and spoke of "saving souls" as if the rest of the person were packaging. When the wider culture told people their bodies were raw material for the self, many Christians had no answer, because they half believed it already. The hardest questions about the body and identity are taken up in the essay on [what the church owes transgender people](/writing/faith-and-gender-identity); what matters here is the root they share with our pieties. Secular self-making and pious soul-talk assume the same thing: the real you lives somewhere other than your body. Scripture doesn't begin there.

## Formed From the Dust

Genesis begins somewhere else. "Then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature" (Genesis 2:7, ESV). God doesn't make a soul and then issue it a body, the way a pilot is assigned an aircraft. He forms a body first, with the verb a potter uses for clay, and then breathes into it, and the man *became* a living creature, *nephesh chayyah*, the same words Genesis has just used of the animals. He doesn't receive a *nephesh* as a part living inside the clay. He becomes one.

Hans Walter Wolff's *Anthropology of the Old Testament* (1973; English translation 1974) showed at length that the Hebrew Scriptures don't carve the person into a precious inner part and a disposable outer one. The *nephesh* hungers and thirsts, the heart thinks, and the whole creature prays, weeps and eats. The breath comes from God, so there is more to a person than chemistry. But the Bible's first picture of a human being is clay God decided to love and bring to life, not a ghost piloting it.

The man, *adam*, is taken from the ground, *adamah*: the earthling from the earth. You are kin to the soil, rooted by your body in a creation God called good six times before he made you, and then the verdict changed: "And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good. And there was evening and there was morning, the sixth day" (Genesis 1:31, ESV).

Here a distinction matters: finitude is not the fall. Some of the body's limits belong to creation itself. Adam slept and ate before anything had gone wrong. To be tired at night, hungry at noon and in only one place at a time is simply what it means to be a creature and not God. "For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14, ESV). The psalmist says it as comfort. Decay, disease and death came later, as intruders, and the curse names them in the language of the ground: "By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19, ESV). Death there is not the soul going home. It's the potter's work coming apart. The Gnostic confused the two, treating creaturely limits as a prison and death as a release. We confuse them the other way, treating our limits as enemies to be hacked and our mortality as a design flaw.

Wendell Berry saw what that confusion costs. In "The Body and the Earth," an essay in *The Unsettling of America* (1977), he argues that once the body is split from the soul and ranked beneath it, it becomes a tool to be driven, fueled and repaired, and that the same habit of mind treats land as a mine. A people that uses its bodies up will use its fields up too. He reminds his readers that *health* belongs to a family of words that includes *heal*, *whole* and *holy*. To be healthy is to be whole: at peace with one's limits, with other bodies, with the ground and with the God who formed it. By that measure most of us aren't healthy even when our numbers are good. We override tiredness with caffeine, treat sleep as lost time and eat at a desk without tasting, and then we're surprised when the body files its complaint. None of us writes from outside that indictment.

The psalmist took the other view. "I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Wonderful are your works; my soul knows it very well" (Psalm 139:14, ESV). He's speaking of being knit together in his mother's womb, the most bodily of beginnings, and his response to his own frame is praise, not management.

## The Word Became Flesh

Then comes the claim that made the Gnostics recoil, and that ought still to stop us. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14, ESV). John doesn't say the Word appeared to be flesh or borrowed a body for a season. He says the Word *became* flesh, and he chooses the bluntest word available, *sarx*, the word for meat and mortality. The verb translated "dwelt" means to pitch a tent. The God who once filled the tabernacle took up residence in a body that hungered in the desert, slept in the stern of a boat, wept at a friend's grave and bled on a Roman cross.

Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria from 328 and the great defender of Nicaea, wrote *On the Incarnation* in the first half of the fourth century (older scholarship dates it near 318, many recent scholars to the mid-330s), and it remains one of the clearest accounts of why the body had to be taken up. Human beings, made from nothing, had turned from God and were sliding back toward nothing, and the corruption had taken hold in the body. A pardon spoken from heaven wouldn't reach the damage. So the Word, never confined by any body, took a mortal body of our kind, to offer it in death for all, undo corruption from the inside and raise it clothed in incorruption. Athanasius compares it to a king who lives in one house of a great city: the whole city is honored, and the bandits keep away. Hebrews says the same: "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14, ESV).

The council at Chalcedon in 451 confessed Christ truly God and truly human, with a rational soul and a body, of one substance with the Father in his divinity and with us in his humanity. The body was part of what the council refused to let anyone subtract.

Look, then, at how much of Jesus's ministry happened through the body. A man with leprosy knelt before him, a man the law required to live apart and cry out his own uncleanness. Jesus could have healed him with a word. Instead: "Moved with pity, he stretched out his hand and touched him and said to him, 'I will; be clean'" (Mark 1:41, ESV). Touching a leper made a person unclean. Here cleanness ran the other way, and the touch was part of the cure, since it gave the man back the human contact his disease had stolen. For a man born blind, Jesus spat on the ground, made mud and spread it on his eyes (John 9:6). He fed a hungry crowd with bread and fish. When he raised the daughter of Jairus, taking her by the hand, his next instruction was almost comically practical: "And he strictly charged them that no one should know this, and told them to give her something to eat" (Mark 5:43, ESV). A faith that thought the body was the problem would have produced a savior who kept his distance from flesh. Ours couldn't stop touching it, feeding it and healing it, and in the end he gave his own.

And he didn't give it back. He rose in the body, ascended in the body, and sits at the Father's right hand as a man. We have our ways of forgetting that. We speak of "asking Jesus into my heart," a true thing said imprecisely, and it can leave a believer imagining a savior who lives only in the interior and has little to say about hands and stomachs and aching backs. The Christ of the Gospels has hands, and he used them on sick people.

## The Body You Will Have Forever

Here many Christians expect the comforting turn: when we die, the soul goes to heaven, free at last of the flesh. That belief deserves a fair hearing, because it isn't simply wrong. Believers who die are with Christ. Paul, weighing his own death, wrote, "I am hard pressed between the two. My desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better" (Philippians 1:23, ESV). A grieving family that says its mother is with the Lord is saying something true.

But it's the middle of the story, and the distinction between the middle and the end is the whole matter. Theologians call the first the intermediate state. The final hope is the resurrection of the body, which is where the Apostles' Creed ends: "the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting." N. T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* (2008) presses this home. Heaven, Wright argues, is real but not the final destination; the Christian hope is what he calls life after life after death, the raising of the dead into a renewed creation. When the church shrinks that hope into "going to heaven when you die," it keeps the comfort and loses the claim.

Paul spells out the claim to Corinthians who found a bodily resurrection crude or impossible. He answers with the image of a seed and the plant that grows from it, and then sets out four contrasts. "So is it with the resurrection of the dead. What is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable" (1 Corinthians 15:42, ESV). "It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power" (1 Corinthians 15:43, ESV). "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body. If there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44, ESV).

We read that last phrase wrongly almost every time. "Spiritual body" sounds like a body made of spirit, thin and ghostly. Wright points out that the Greek adjectives Paul uses, *psychikos* and *pneumatikos*, don't describe what something is made of but what animates it. The "natural" body lives by ordinary human life; the "spiritual" body is animated and governed by the Spirit of God. Paul's contrast is between a body that is dying and a body that can no longer die, not between the physical and the nonphysical. If anything, the risen body will be more solid than the one you have now.

The risen Jesus is the pattern. When his frightened disciples thought they were seeing a ghost, he answered with his body: "See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me, and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have" (Luke 24:39, ESV). Then he ate broiled fish in front of them, and later cooked them breakfast on a beach. He still bore his wounds, a detail the essay on [disability and the image of God](/writing/disability-dignity-and-the-image-of-god) follows to its pastoral end. Paul calls him "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20, ESV), the first sheaf of a harvest that includes all who belong to him: he "will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body, by the power that enables him even to subject all things to himself" (Philippians 3:21, ESV).

Thomas Aquinas, commenting on this chapter, put it in a sentence that still startles readers raised on soul-only hope: my soul is not I. He believed the soul survives death, but he held that a soul without its body is an incomplete human being, and that if only souls were saved, no one, strictly, would be saved. The dead in Christ are waiting for something.

That changes how you hold your body now, especially the parts that are failing. "And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23, ESV). Not redemption *from* our bodies. Redemption *of* them. The Christian doesn't look at a stiffening knee or a tremor and see a prison wall giving way to let the real self out. She sees a seed sown in weakness, which God has promised to raise in power. The grief is real, but it's the grief of someone waiting for a harvest, not for release.

## What This Changes on an Ordinary Tuesday

The verse most Christians know about the body is also the one most often pulled out of its setting. "Or do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God? You are not your own" (1 Corinthians 6:19, ESV). The next verse completes the sentence: "for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20, ESV). It gets quoted as a case for cutting sugar or working out, which isn't quite what Paul meant.

Read it where it sits. A few verses earlier Paul quotes a Corinthian slogan: "Food is meant for the stomach and the stomach for food" (from 1 Corinthians 6:13, ESV). The logic was thoroughly Greek. The body is temporary, so what we do with it doesn't reach the real self. Paul's immediate subject there is a particular kind of bodily conduct, which isn't this essay's concern, but his answer rests on a premise that is: "And God raised the Lord and will also raise us up by his power" (1 Corinthians 6:14, ESV). Because the body has a future, it has a present. The "your" in the temple verse is plural, spoken to the whole congregation, but the body is each person's own. The Spirit doesn't merely visit the church's meetings. He lives in the flesh of its members.

That reading draws a line the fitness slogan misses. A temple is holy because of who lives in it, not because of the condition of the stonework. The tabernacle was a tent, dusty and carried on poles, and the glory of God filled it. A believer whose hands shake or whose strength is gone is not a lesser temple than a healthy one. The verse doesn't ask us to perfect our bodies. It tells us they aren't ours, which is harder: we receive the body as a trust, neither neglected nor worshiped.

Take aging. Our culture treats it as a slow humiliation to be delayed and disguised. The church has often answered with its own evasion, telling the old that the body doesn't matter because the soul is what counts. Scripture takes neither road. "So we do not lose heart. Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day" (2 Corinthians 4:16, ESV). Paul doesn't pretend the wasting isn't happening, and he doesn't sneer at the outer self. The law of Israel honored the aging body outright: "You shall stand up before the gray head and honor the face of an old man, and you shall fear your God: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:32, ESV). In that verse a gray head is something to rise for.

Take illness. The ghost story says sickness is the cage wearing thin, and some pious talk quietly agrees. The prosperity preacher says the opposite, that a faithful Christian ought to be well and lingering sickness shows weak faith, and that teaching has broken people who were already carrying enough. Christianity calls sickness an intruder in a good creation and names death without euphemism: "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26, ESV). That is why the church has cared for sick bodies and not merely prayed over them. Basil of Caesarea built a great complex for the sick and the poor outside his city around 370, and Christians have been founding hospitals ever since. Going to the doctor honors a body God made and means to raise.

Take fatigue. We treat tiredness as a weakness to override, and some of us wear exhaustion as proof of seriousness. Scripture is gentler. When Elijah fled into the wilderness after Carmel, so spent and low that he asked God to take his life, God didn't answer first with a rebuke. "And he lay down and slept under a broom tree. And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, 'Arise and eat'" (1 Kings 19:5, ESV). Then he let him sleep again. The first ministry to a despairing prophet was bread and rest. "It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep" (Psalm 127:2, ESV). Receiving sleep is a small nightly confession that God, not we, holds the world together.

## Dust That God Loves

The same conviction reaches the smallest things, the ordinary pleasures many of us were half taught to feel guilty about: the taste of bread, sun on a cold morning, the good ache after honest work, a hand held in a hospital room. They're gifts to a creature made to receive them through a body. When teachers in Ephesus demanded abstinence from certain foods as a mark of holiness, Paul told Timothy, "For everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4, ESV). Even the Preacher, who had looked hard at the vanity of nearly everything, said, "Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart, for God has already approved what you do" (Ecclesiastes 9:7, ESV). The Gnostic feels a flicker of shame at bodily delight, and the glutton feels no limit to it. The Christian gives thanks, and the thanks keeps the pleasure in its place, received from a Giver rather than seized as a right.

None of this makes the body's troubles small. Bodies break, and they break people, and the gospel doesn't ask anyone to smile through a diagnosis. What it offers is a different account of what is happening. The early Christians buried their dead with a care that puzzled their neighbors and called their burial grounds *koimeteria*, sleeping places, the origin of our word *cemetery*. They didn't think they were disposing of a shell. They thought they were planting.

We have told ourselves in a hundred quiet ways that we are ghosts in machines, minds that would be freer without the flesh, and the church has told itself a pious version of the same story. Repentance starts with admitting that we believed the old lie and called it spirituality. The truth is humbler and better. You are dust that God shaped with his own hands and filled with his own breath, the kind of creature he thought worth becoming. He touched lepers, fed the hungry and told a little girl's parents to give her something to eat. He went into the grave in a body, came out of it in a body, and has not set that body down.

So the body you are living in this week, tired or aging or sick or strong, is not a cage. It's the part of you God stooped to form, the part he took up to save you, and the part he refuses to leave in the ground. The ache in your back won't have the last word, and neither will the grave. What you carry out of this page is not a technique for managing your body but a way of seeing it: held, owed and promised, sown in weakness and waiting, with the rest of creation, to be raised.
