## The war you were told about

Most people who believe that science and faith are at war never decided to believe it. They absorbed it. The story arrives with its scenes already cast: Galileo on his knees before the Inquisition, medieval monks insisting the earth was flat, a Tennessee courtroom full of Bible-waving rubes, and Darwin quietly making God unnecessary. Its moral is that knowledge advances, belief retreats, and every honest person must pick a side. A young person raised in church often meets it in her first serious biology class, and nobody at church ever told her it had a history. When David Kinnaman published Barna's research on young adults who had drifted from church as *You Lost Me* (2011), the sense that churches are antagonistic to science was among the reasons they gave.

I believed the story when I was an atheist, and I believed it partly because nobody in the church had given me a reason not to. What I didn't notice then was how convenient it was. I had taken the friction that really exists at a few points and inflated it into total opposition, because total opposition flattered the side I had already chosen. I never checked where the story came from. Very few of us do.

The conflict deserves its strongest form first. Science tests its predictions against the world without mercy and has been right about an astonishing amount. The church has more than once put its weight behind claims about nature that proved false, and has sometimes punished people for saying so. Each time a mechanism was found for something once credited directly to God, the room left for God seemed to shrink. A skeptic who sees an army in retreat isn't being foolish. Some of the pattern is real.

But the story in its familiar form, a war that has always been fought and must be fought to the end, has a birth date. In 1874 John William Draper, an English-born chemist teaching in New York, published *History of the Conflict between Religion and Science*. Writing in the wake of the First Vatican Council's 1870 definition of papal infallibility, he aimed chiefly at the Roman Catholic Church. In 1896 Andrew Dickson White, co-founder and first president of Cornell University, published the two volumes of *A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom*. White had helped found Cornell as a nonsectarian university, clergy attacked it as godless, and his book grew out of the lectures he gave in its defense. He was no atheist; he believed he was rescuing true religion from dogmatic theology. These were books of advocacy by men with institutions to defend, and they supplied the scenes nearly everyone still pictures.

## What the historians found

For half a century historians of science have been taking those scenes apart. Ronald Numbers, a historian at the University of Wisconsin who does not write as a believer, edited *Galileo Goes to Jail and Other Myths about Science and Religion* (2009), in which specialists take one piece of folklore per chapter and check it against the sources. The greatest myth of all, Numbers says, is that science and religion have been in constant conflict. John Hedley Brooke had already concluded in *Science and Religion: Some Historical Perspectives* (1991) that the real relationship is so varied that no general thesis, war or harmony, survives contact with the evidence. The point of this complexity thesis is not that everyone got along but that the story is always particular: this theologian and that astronomer, under that pope, with these politics in play.

Peter Harrison went deeper in *The Territories of Science and Religion* (2015). He argues that "science" and "religion," as two bounded territories that might go to war over a border, are modern inventions. For a medieval thinker, *scientia* was a trained habit of reasoning from causes and *religio* an inner virtue of reverence, not rival institutions; William Whewell coined the word *scientist* only in the 1830s. The warfare story could be told only after the territories were drawn, and then it was projected backward over centuries that had never thought in those terms.

The flat earth shows the mechanism most clearly. Educated medieval Christians knew the earth was a sphere. Bede taught it in 725, the standard university astronomy text of the thirteenth century assumed it, and Thomas Aquinas used it as a routine example on the first page of his *Summa Theologiae*: the astronomer and the natural philosopher both prove the earth is round, one by mathematics and the other by reasoning about matter. The quarrel over Columbus concerned the earth's size, not its shape, and his learned critics were right that it was larger than he claimed. The legend owes much to Washington Irving's romantic life of Columbus (1828), and Draper and White gave it scholarly cover, as Jeffrey Burton Russell showed in *Inventing the Flat Earth* (1991). The myth survives because it flatters us.

Galileo is harder, and he has to be told straight, because the church was genuinely in the wrong. The trouble came not with his telescope, whose findings Rome's Jesuit astronomers confirmed in 1611, but when he claimed the earth truly moves, and when his *Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina* (1615) told theologians how to read Scripture. He repeats there a saying he credits to Cardinal Baronius, that the Holy Spirit meant to teach us how one goes to heaven, not how the heavens go. Cardinal Robert Bellarmine replied, in a letter that April to the Carmelite Paolo Foscarini, that if the earth's motion were ever truly demonstrated, the church would have to reinterpret the passages that seemed to deny it and admit it had not understood them. He didn't believe such a demonstration existed, and on the evidence then available he was not being obtuse. Tycho Brahe's earth-centered alternative fit the telescopic data as well as Copernicus did, stellar parallax would not be measured until 1838, and Galileo's favorite proof, from the tides, was wrong.

Still, in February 1616 the Holy Office's theological consultants declared the sun-centered cosmos formally heretical, and Bellarmine, on the pope's instruction, admonished Galileo in person to abandon the view. In March a decree of the Congregation of the Index pronounced the earth's motion false and contrary to Scripture and suspended Copernicus's book until corrected. When his admirer Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII in 1623, Galileo thought he had room to write, and his *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632) argued for Copernicus with little pretense of neutrality, putting an argument dear to the pope in the mouth of a character named Simplicio. In 1633 he was tried, found vehemently suspected of heresy, and made to abjure. He was threatened with torture under interrogation but not tortured, and he was never held in a cell: he spent the trial at the Tuscan embassy and, for some weeks, in an official's apartment inside the Holy Office's building, and his sentence of imprisonment was commuted to house arrest, which he served from the end of that year at his villa at Arcetri until his death in 1642. Maurice Finocchiaro's chapter in the Numbers volume draws the right conclusion: the legend of the tortured martyr is false, and the condemnation was real. A man was silenced for telling the truth about the sky because churchmen let one reading of verses like "He set the earth on its foundations, so that it should never be moved" (Psalm 104:5, ESV) outrank the evidence and the principle Bellarmine himself had stated. John Paul II acknowledged the error in 1992. The affair is not a parable of faith against reason. It is a parable of an institution mistaking its interpretation for the text, and that temptation has never belonged to Rome alone.

## Believers at the bench

The warfare story has to keep one fact quiet. The people who built modern science were, with few exceptions, Christians, and many thought their faith gave them reason to expect a world worth studying.

Johannes Kepler trained in theology at Tübingen to become a Lutheran pastor, and when he turned to astronomy instead he told his teacher Michael Maestlin, in a letter of 1595, that he could now see God being honored through astronomy. He fought Tycho's observations for years until the orbit of Mars yielded an ellipse, published in 1609. Robert Boyle, a founder of the Royal Society, left money in his will for lectures defending the Christian faith against unbelievers. Michael Faraday, who discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831, served as an elder among the Sandemanians, a small and strict Scottish sect. James Clerk Maxwell, whose equations united electricity, magnetism and light, was a devout Presbyterian, and when the Cavendish Laboratory opened in Cambridge under his direction, a psalm was carved in Latin over its door: "Great are the works of the LORD, studied by all who delight in them" (Psalm 111:2, ESV).

Isaac Newton belongs here, with the qualification the list usually leaves off. He wrote more on theology and prophecy than on physics, and in the General Scholium he added to the *Principia* in 1713 he argued that the system of sun, planets and comets could only have come from the counsel of an intelligent and powerful Being. He also privately rejected the Trinity. Newton is evidence that belief in a Creator drove scientific work, not evidence for Nicene orthodoxy, and Christians who enlist him as though he were do their cause no favors.

The candor has to go further. Noah Efron's chapter in the Numbers volume takes apart a myth Christians are fond of, that Christianity single-handedly gave birth to modern science. Alfred North Whitehead's argument in *Science and the Modern World* (1925), that faith in the possibility of science was an unconscious inheritance from medieval theology, holds some truth, but the Scientific Revolution also drew on Greek, Arabic and Jewish learning. The honest claim is narrower. The founders of modern physics expected nature to be lawful because they believed a faithful God made it, and intelligible because they believed human minds bore his image. Francis Bacon urged in *The Advancement of Learning* (1605) that no one could study too far in either of God's two books, his word and his works. The image is rooted in the psalm these men loved:

> The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork. Day to day pours out speech, and night to night reveals knowledge. (Psalm 19:1–2, ESV)

The psalm's shape matters. The heavens speak without words, and at verse 7 the psalm turns to the law of the Lord, which speaks in words. Before that turn, the sun, which Israel's neighbors worshiped as a god, comes out "like a bridegroom leaving his chamber, and, like a strong man, runs its course with joy" (Psalm 19:5, ESV). It is a creature doing its appointed work. A world emptied of gods is a world that can be studied without sacrilege, and that was the doctrine of creation's quiet gift to science long before the telescope.

Georges Lemaître carried the pattern into the twentieth century and showed its proper limit. A Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, he showed in 1927 that Einstein's equations allowed an expanding universe, two years before Edwin Hubble's measurements. In 1931 Arthur Eddington confessed in *Nature* that the notion of a beginning to the present order of nature was philosophically repugnant to him, and Lemaître answered in the same journal that the universe might have begun as a single atom holding the mass of the whole, the idea he soon called the primeval atom and others later nicknamed the Big Bang. When Pope Pius XII suggested in 1951 that modern cosmology had confirmed the creation of the world, Lemaître was uneasy and worked quietly to keep the pope from saying it again. He held that his theory stood outside any metaphysical or religious question. The priest who found the universe's beginning refused to make his equations a proof of God.

## Darwin, Dayton and the hardening

The war's second founding scene is 1859 and *On the Origin of Species*, followed by the 1860 Oxford debate in which Thomas Henry Huxley is supposed to have humiliated Bishop Samuel Wilberforce. The contemporary accounts of that exchange are thin and inconsistent, and the triumphant version was polished decades later by Huxley's allies. The real reception was mixed.

Darwin opened the *Origin* with epigraphs from two Christian writers: Whewell observing that God works in the material world not by isolated interventions but by general laws, and Bacon on the two books. His most important American ally was Asa Gray, the Harvard botanist and a devout Christian, whose essays in *Darwiniana* (1876) argued that natural selection was compatible with a designing God working through natural process. At Princeton the response divided along a line worth noticing. Charles Hodge asked *What Is Darwinism?* (1874) and answered, "It is atheism." But his target was not the development of species over time; it was Darwin's exclusion of design, the claim that the process was undirected. B. B. Warfield, whose defense of biblical inerrancy became the conservative Protestant standard, was open through much of his career to evolution as a means providence might use, provided the special creation of the human soul was kept, and in 1915 he described Calvin's account of the world's development after the first creation of matter as a pure form of evolutionism. As the collection Mark Noll and David Livingstone edited, *B. B. Warfield: Evolution, Science, and Scripture* (2000), shows, the man who defined inerrancy for American evangelicals did not think it required rejecting evolution.

What hardened the lines was the 1920s. In March 1925 Tennessee's Butler Act forbade public schools to teach that man descended from a lower order of animals. The American Civil Liberties Union advertised for a test case, Dayton's boosters supplied a young teacher named John Scopes, and that July Clarence Darrow defended him against a prosecution joined by William Jennings Bryan. When Darrow put Bryan on the stand as an expert on the Bible, Bryan readily conceded that the days of Genesis might have been long periods of time. He was not a young-earth creationist, and neither were most fundamentalist leaders of his day. Scopes was fined a hundred dollars, the verdict was set aside on a technicality, and Bryan died five days after the trial. Edward Larson's *Summer for the Gods* (1997) tells it with the care it deserves.

Bryan deserves his steelman. The textbook at issue, George William Hunter's *A Civic Biology* (1914), taught evolution alongside eugenics, ranking the races and treating the "unfit" as a burden society should keep from reproducing. Bryan had watched Darwin's name invoked to justify the strong crushing the weak, and his lecture "The Menace of Darwinism" (1921) was as much moral protest as science. On eugenics he was right, and many of the educated men who mocked him were wrong. His mistake was to fight a moral philosophy smuggled in with a biological theory by attacking the biology rather than the smuggling. When young-earth creationism revived, it came through the flood geology of the Seventh-day Adventist George McCready Price, recast for evangelicals by John Whitcomb and Henry Morris in *The Genesis Flood* (1961), as Numbers traced in *The Creationists* (1992). Belief in a young earth is old, as its defenders rightly say. But the flood geology that carries it today, and the conviction that it is the only faithful position, have a twentieth-century origin, just as the war story does.

## Reading Genesis 1 among its neighbors

Here Christians disagree among themselves, and the order of the question matters. The confessional floor is that God made heaven and earth out of nothing, that creation is good, that human beings bear his image, and that the world has fallen and will be redeemed. The age of the earth and the process by which living things came to be are second-order questions. They divide faithful churches. They do not divide the faith.

Young-earth creationists read the days as ordinary days because the text marks them that way: "And there was evening and there was morning, the first day" (Genesis 1:5, ESV). The fourth commandment grounds the Sabbath in that week, "For in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them, and rested on the seventh day" (Exodus 20:11, ESV). Their deepest concern is theological. Paul writes that "sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12, ESV), and they ask how hundreds of millions of years of death and predation before anyone sinned could be the creation God called "very good" (Genesis 1:31, ESV). They add that most Christians through history read the days this way, and that letting one century's science set the meaning of Scripture leaves the church at the mercy of the next. It is a serious case, made from reverence for the text.

Old-earth creationists answer that the Hebrew *yom* has a wide range, and that Genesis itself uses it for the whole creation week: "in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens" (Genesis 2:4, ESV). The sun doesn't appear until the fourth day, and the seventh day has no evening and morning. Augustine, in *The Literal Meaning of Genesis* in the early fifth century, did not take the days as ordinary days at all, so such readings are no surrender to modern geology. Many old-earth Christians accept the scientific age of the universe while holding that God acted directly at decisive points, above all in making the first humans.

Evolutionary creationists, whose view Francis Collins defends, accept both the age of the universe and the common descent of living things, and hold that God created through the process biologists describe, much as Warfield allowed. Collins led the Human Genome Project and, by his own account, came to faith from atheism as a young doctor. In *The Language of God* (2006) he sets out the genetic evidence that persuades him, argues that God is not diminished by working through a process, and rejects intelligent design as a god-of-the-gaps strategy bound to shrink as science advances.

How, then, should Genesis 1 be read? John Walton, an Old Testament scholar at Wheaton, argues in *The Lost World of Genesis One* (2009) that it must be read as an ancient Near Eastern text first. Its first hearers shared their world's picture of waters above an "expanse" (Genesis 1:6, ESV) and waters below. In that world, Walton argues, creation accounts were about assigning order and function rather than manufacturing materials. He reads the seven days as the inauguration of the cosmos as God's temple, and the seventh day's rest as God taking up his reign, as the Lord says of Zion, "This is my resting place forever; here I will dwell" (Psalm 132:14, ESV). Critics who share his high view of Scripture reply that he divides function from material too sharply. But the setting he recovers is hard to deny. Israel's neighbors worshiped sun and moon as gods. Genesis 1 names no rival gods and does not even name the sun and moon, calling them "the greater light to rule the day and the lesser light to rule the night" (Genesis 1:16, ESV). The chapter's polemic is aimed at idolatry, not geology. Calvin saw part of this in his commentary on Genesis (1554): Moses wrote in a popular style about what ordinary people could see, while astronomers, whose study Calvin refused to condemn, know that Saturn is larger than the moon.

This essay lands where its sources land. Genesis 1 is not a science textbook and never claimed to be, and the question of its timeline stays open. It tells an ancient people, surrounded by rival gods, who made the world and what they are for. The strongest objection this reading hasn't fully answered is the one young-earth readers press hardest: what to make of Adam, and of death before the fall, if Romans 5 is read as history. Collins offers more than one possibility and does not claim to have settled it. A Christian can hold any of these three views and confess the creeds without reservation. What none of us may do is tell a sixteen-year-old that the gospel stands or falls with our reading of the days, and then act surprised when she chooses the laboratory. Augustine warned that when unbelievers hear Christians talk nonsense about the natural world in Scripture's name, they conclude the biblical writers held such opinions, and then ask why they should trust those books about the resurrection of the dead and the hope of eternal life.

## What the instrument cannot weigh

Beneath the history lies a distinction the war story depends on blurring. Methodological naturalism is a working rule of science: investigate nature by looking for natural, repeatable, measurable causes, and don't write "a miracle occurred" into the middle of an equation. Metaphysical naturalism is a claim about reality: nature is all there is. The first is a method; the second is a philosophy. A Christian can practice the first with a clear conscience, and the founders of modern science did, because they believed God ordinarily governs the world through the regular order he made. Whewell's epigraph to Darwin's book says as much. The second can't be derived from the first, any more than a metal detector's silence proves there is no wood in the ground.

The war story makes the leap silently. Carl Sagan opened *Cosmos* (1980) with the sentence "The Cosmos is all that is or ever was or ever will be." It is a beautiful sentence, and it is not a finding of astronomy. No telescope observed it. Richard Dawkins wrote in *The Blind Watchmaker* (1986) that Darwin "made it possible to be an intellectually fulfilled atheist," a claim about what a theory permits, and honest about being one.

So the distinction this essay turns on is this. Science answers how nature works. It cannot, by itself, answer why there is a nature at all, or why nature should be intelligible to the minds that study it. These aren't questions science hasn't reached yet; they are of another kind. Every experiment presupposes that the world is orderly and its order readable, and no experiment can establish that without first assuming it. The deeper remainders, consciousness and the reliability of reason and the balance of the physical constants, belong to [a companion essay](/writing/what-secular-explanations-still-have-to-explain). The point here is simpler. The instrument that measures wavelengths cannot weigh a duty, and it cannot tell you whether the world it measures was meant.

The mistake of my old atheism was to take the silence of science on those questions as the answer no. Because the laboratory couldn't find God, I concluded there was nothing there, without noticing that the laboratory can't find justice or beauty or obligation either, and I hadn't stopped believing in those. I had exempted the values I liked from the standard I used to dismiss the God I didn't want. That isn't skepticism. It is selective skepticism, which is a polite name for prejudice.

Paul's claim is pitched with care: "For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse" (Romans 1:20, ESV). Creation shows not a mechanism or a date but power and deity. And Paul isn't offering a seminar proof. He is diagnosing why people who see do not honor what they see, and within a chapter he has widened the charge to the religious judge who thought himself exempt. The churchmen who silenced Galileo and the churches that told their children to choose between Genesis and genetics were doing the same thing, protecting an interpretation by refusing to look.

## The one in whom all things hold together

The New Testament's boldest statement about nature isn't in Genesis. It is in a letter to a small church in the Lycus valley: "For by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible ... all things were created through him and for him. And he is before all things, and in him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:16–17, ESV).

Paul's claim is that the lawfulness that let Kepler find his ellipse and Maxwell write his equations is not a brute accident. It is held, and the one holding it is personal. Science can neither confirm nor refute that, because it isn't a claim about a mechanism inside the world but about why there is a world with mechanisms in it. You don't need it to do science. But the assumption science can neither prove nor do without, that the universe is orderly and its order knowable, is exactly what the doctrine of creation supplies, and exactly what a purposeless cosmos has no obvious reason to grant.

Then the passage does something no philosophy of science would predict. The one in whom all things hold together is named in the next breath "the beginning, the firstborn from the dead" (Colossians 1:18, ESV), and the reconciliation of all things comes "by the blood of his cross" (Colossians 1:20, ESV). The Word who holds the atoms together is the man nailed to wood outside Jerusalem and, the church confesses, raised bodily on the third day. That is where the Christian claim finally rests: not on a gap in the physics or a reading of Genesis that science must be bent to fit, but on an event in history, to be weighed by its witnesses. Methodological naturalism can't rule it out, because it studies what nature regularly does, and the resurrection is the claim that nature's Author once did something new.

The repentance belongs to us first. The church handed the skeptic his best scenes. Churchmen silenced Galileo, and no other church has standing to point at Rome, because we told a generation of students they had to choose between the fossil record and Christ, and many took us at our word. We have been more afraid of what a microscope might find than confident in the God who made what it looks at. Maxwell could put a psalm over a laboratory door because he did not expect anything inside to threaten the Lord of the works. Our faith has too often been smaller than his.

If you don't believe, nothing here asks you to. It asks only that you stop letting a nineteenth-century polemic make the decision for you. The choice you were told to make, between faith and evidence, was sold by two men with institutions to defend. The choice that remains is older, and no laboratory can make it for you: whether the order you study points beyond itself, whether the world is authored or accidental. Science hands you a magnificent description of the house. It is constitutionally unable to tell you whether anyone lives there. I once pretended the microscope had settled that question, and the longer I pretended, the more I was hiding a decision I had already made from the evidence I claimed to be following. The heavens are still pouring out speech without words. Whether you hear a voice in them was never a question for better instruments. It is a question of whether you are willing to be addressed.
