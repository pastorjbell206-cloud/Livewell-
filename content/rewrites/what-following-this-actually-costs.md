Before I believed, I took it for granted that faith was a kind of anesthetic. I came to Christianity out of atheism, and the man I was then assumed that people held their religion because it dulled something, that church existed to keep the dose steady, and that God was mostly a way of feeling agreed with. I couldn't respect that. A belief whose whole claim on you is that it makes you feel better has no claim on you at all once you notice what it's doing. If that were what Christianity is, a thoughtful person would be right to walk past it.

The skeptic who suspects as much is asking a fair question. Every offer worth distrusting hides its price until the papers are signed. So it's reasonable to ask what following Jesus actually costs, and whether anyone tells you before you begin. The surprising answer is that Jesus did. He named the price to a crowd that was growing, which is the moment every recruiter lowers it, and he let the ones who did the arithmetic walk away. That is strange behavior for a movement that wants members.

One distinction carries the whole argument, so it belongs at the start. Christianity insists that grace is given free, that no one earns forgiveness or buys a place with God. It also insists that following Jesus costs everything. Those claims aren't in tension, because the cost of discipleship is not a fee paid for grace. It is what happens to a life when its center moves. You don't pay for the gift. The gift rearranges you, and the rearranging is what hurts.

## The crowd Jesus turned to

Luke places the hardest words about cost at a particular point in the story. Since the ninth chapter Jesus has been walking toward Jerusalem: "When the days drew near for him to be taken up, he set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51, ESV). The reader knows what waits there. The crowds following him mostly don't.

Just before the passage on cost, Jesus tells a parable about a great banquet (Luke 14:15–24). The invited guests make excuses, so the host sends his servant into the streets for "the poor and crippled and blind and lame" (Luke 14:21, ESV). The invitation costs the guests nothing. They bring nothing, deserve nothing, and the table is full because the host wants it full. Then the tone changes completely.

"Now great crowds accompanied him, and he turned and said to them" (Luke 14:25, ESV). The crowd is large, the momentum is good, and Jesus turns around to face it with words that sound like a man trying to shrink his audience.

> "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters, yes, and even his own life, he cannot be my disciple." (Luke 14:26, ESV)

A modern reader hears *hate* as an emotion, and the sentence becomes monstrous. But the word is working in a Semitic idiom of preference, where "love" and "hate" rank two loyalties. Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah" (Genesis 29:30, ESV), and in the next verse the narrator says that "the LORD saw that Leah was hated" (Genesis 29:31, ESV). Jacob didn't despise Leah; she lived on the losing side of his preference. Matthew gives the saying of Jesus in plainer terms: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me, and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me" (Matthew 10:37, ESV). Elsewhere Jesus rebukes teachers who invented a loophole to excuse a man from supporting his aging parents (Mark 7:9–13). He isn't commanding contempt for family. He is ranking allegiances, and he puts himself first.

That reading is accurate, and it can also be used to take the edge off the passage. In first-century Galilee a person's family was his economy, his security, his religious identity and his name. To rank anything above it would look, from inside the family, exactly like hatred. Jesus chose the strong word because the ranking would feel that strong to the people on the losing side of it. Then, after a word about the cross that we'll come to, he tells two small stories.

> "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?" (Luke 14:28, ESV)

The man who lays a foundation and runs out of money becomes a joke, and everyone who passes begins to mock him, "saying, 'This man began to build and was not able to finish'" (Luke 14:30, ESV). In an honor culture, the half-built tower is a public monument to a man who didn't think before he started.

The second story raises the stakes from a building to a war. A king with ten thousand soldiers sits down to deliberate whether he can meet another king coming with twenty thousand. "And if not, while the other is yet a great way off, he sends a delegation and asks for terms of peace" (Luke 14:32, ESV). Interpreters read this two ways. On one reading it doubles the first story: assess your resources before you commit. On the other, the listener is the weaker king, the one coming with twenty thousand is God, and the wise thing is to send for terms while there is still time. Both readings land in the same place, because Jesus draws the conclusion himself.

> "So therefore, any one of you who does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple." (Luke 14:33, ESV)

Put the banquet and the tower side by side and the distinction from the start of this essay is there in Luke's arrangement. The invitation is free; the table is full of people who brought nothing. Following the host down the road to Jerusalem costs everything. Jesus doesn't hide the second behind the first. He tells the crowd to sit down, the posture of slow arithmetic, not of someone being rushed to a decision.

## A cross is not a bad knee

Between the hard word about family and the two parables sits a single verse: "Whoever does not bear his own cross and come after me cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:27, ESV). We have worn the image smooth. In ordinary speech a person's cross is a chronic illness or a difficult in-law. Those are real burdens, but they aren't what the word meant to the people who first heard it.

Martin Hengel's *Crucifixion in the Ancient World and the Folly of the Message of the Cross* (1977) gathered the ancient evidence. The cross was a punishment chiefly for slaves, rebels and the lowest classes, deliberately public and designed to shame as much as to kill. Hengel points to Cicero, who argued in a speech of 63 BC that the very word should be kept away from a Roman citizen's thoughts, eyes and ears. A Galilean crowd didn't need it described. Josephus reports in *The Jewish War* (c. 75) that after the unrest following Herod's death in 4 BC, the governor Varus crucified two thousand men. When Jesus said that a disciple carries his own cross, he was describing a man already condemned, walking to his execution while the town watched.

Mark sharpens the setting. Peter has just confessed that Jesus is the Christ, and Jesus begins to teach that "the Son of Man must suffer many things" (Mark 8:31, ESV), be killed and rise again. Peter rebukes him, Jesus rebukes Peter in the harshest words he ever speaks to a friend, and only then does he call the crowd over.

> "And calling the crowd to him with his disciples, he said to them, 'If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me.'" (Mark 8:34, ESV)

> "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake and the gospel's will save it." (Mark 8:35, ESV)

Jesus names his own cross before he names ours. The disciple's cross isn't a separate ordeal invented to test people; it is what happens to anyone who walks behind a man headed where he was headed. Self-denial here isn't the small renunciation of a diet. It means the self stops deciding where the road goes.

Matthew records the same logic when Jesus sends the Twelve out, and he spells out what Luke 14 leaves implicit.

> "Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth. I have not come to bring peace, but a sword." (Matthew 10:34, ESV)

The sword isn't a weapon anyone is told to pick up. Jesus has come "to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law" (Matthew 10:35, ESV), and "a person's enemies will be those of his own household" (Matthew 10:36, ESV). He is quoting Micah 7:6, the prophet's picture of a society so corrupt that trust had failed even at home, and saying that his own claim will produce the same division. He doesn't want families broken. But a claim that ranks above family will divide those who accept it from those who don't. The passage closes on the paradox Mark also records: "Whoever finds his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matthew 10:39, ESV).

No modern writer has made this language harder to evade than Dietrich Bonhoeffer. His *Nachfolge* appeared in 1937, the same year the Gestapo shut the Confessing Church seminary he led at Finkenwalde. English readers know it as *The Cost of Discipleship*, and newer translations call it *Discipleship*. Its best-known sentence comes from R. H. Fuller's translation in the 1959 edition: "When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die." Bonhoeffer was arrested in 1943 and hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945, less than a month before Germany surrendered.

His diagnosis of *cheap grace*, grace preached as a principle that asks nothing, is treated in [What Is the Gospel?](/writing/what-the-gospel-actually-is). The point to carry from him here is narrower. Bonhoeffer insisted that the cross is not the ordinary misfortune every human life contains. It is the suffering that comes to a person because he belongs to Christ. A cross is the thing you wouldn't be carrying if you hadn't answered the call, and the call, in his reading, is to a person rather than a program. Levi leaves his tax booth; the fishermen leave their nets. The cost isn't a set of rules added to belief. It is the shape of a life that walks behind a crucified man.

## The self that wants to be the point

For most readers the cost won't arrive as a literal cross. It arrives in quieter places, and they're worth naming, because a price described only in grand terms is easy to admire and never pay.

Start with money. Christianity doesn't mainly ask you to give more than a generous atheist already gives. It asks you to stop treating what you earn as simply yours. "What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you boast as if you did not receive it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7, ESV). A steward is accountable for what passed through his hands, not the owner of what landed in them, and that overturns the assumption that a balance in an account is a score or a defense against the future. I have five sons, and I know the instinct to build a wall of resources between my family and whatever is coming. The faith keeps telling me that the security I'm building can become a smaller god than the one I claim to serve. It is right, and I keep building anyway.

Then the body. The culture most of us live in treats the body as private property and desire as its own justification. Christianity says the body is not yours in that way. "You are not your own" (1 Corinthians 6:19, ESV), Paul told the Corinthians, "for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20, ESV). It treats sex as a covenant language that makes a promise with the body whether or not the mouth intends one, and marriage as [a covenant rather than a contract](/writing/covenant-vs-contract-what-marriage-is), which closes the exit a contract keeps open. That costs the freedom to treat another person as an experience, and, inside marriage, the right to leave when leaving would be easier and everyone would understand. (That covenant has never meant enduring abuse. Anyone in danger at home should reach for help: in the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233.)

Notice Paul's phrase, though. *Bought with a price.* The price has already been paid, and not by the reader. The cost of following is not the purchase price of grace. It is what it means to belong to someone who paid it.

Then politics, the cost that embarrasses every side. People often come to faith having already decided which half of the country is the problem, and they expect the faith to bless the arithmetic. If you lean right, the cross will convict you wherever the nation has become an idol, wherever the flag has been wrapped around the crucifix until it's hard to tell which one you are kneeling to, wherever the vulnerable are treated as a threat rather than a neighbor. If you lean left, it will convict you wherever the church's ancient moral vision has been filed under bigotry and the hard sayings of Jesus your side dislikes have been quietly dropped. Honest people will weigh those sins differently in different seasons. But the move is the same: a tribe recruiting Jesus instead of following him. The real thing costs you the comfort of a clean enemy.

Beneath all of these lies the cost of being the point. Each of us is quietly the main character in our own account of the world. Christianity's most offensive claim is that you are not the center of the story. You are a creature in it, loved beyond reason, but not central. Jesus warned against practicing righteousness "before other people in order to be seen by them" (Matthew 6:1, ESV), and the warning lands hardest on people whose work is public. My name is on books. I know the applause isn't the reward, and I still check to see who noticed. I'm inside this indictment.

These four aren't a list of fees. They are four places where the self that insisted on being the point is asked to step down. That's what Jesus meant by denying yourself. The costs aren't charges against your account. They are the account being handed to someone else.

## What it costs when the culture stops agreeing

The first Christians paid the social price in full, and much of the record comes from people who disliked them. Around 112, Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia and Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan about the Christians being denounced to him (*Letters* 10.96). He asked the accused three times whether they were Christians, with a warning of punishment, and ordered those who persisted to be executed, apart from Roman citizens, whom he sent on to Rome, reasoning that their stubbornness deserved punishment whatever their creed. Those who denied it could prove it by offering incense and wine before the emperor's image and cursing Christ, things he had heard genuine Christians could not be forced to do. Tacitus, describing Nero's attack on the Christians of Rome after the fire of 64, wrote that they were condemned less for arson than for hatred of the human race (*Annals* 15.44). Jesus had told his followers to "hate" father and mother in the sense of ranking him first. The empire returned the word to them in its plain sense.

Why did ordinary neighbors find them so offensive? Larry Hurtado's *Destroyer of the gods* (2016) argues that the answer lies in what made early Christianity distinctive. In the Roman world the gods of the household, the city, the trade guild and the empire were honored as part of being a decent member of each, and most people added gods freely and dropped none. Christians refused them all, and the refusal cut through family meals, civic festivals and guild banquets. To their neighbors it looked like impiety, even treason against the common life.

For most of modern Western history the arithmetic ran the other way, and the current account of that change deserves a fair statement. Aaron Renn, in a 2022 essay in *First Things*, proposed a rough periodization: a positive world, until about 1994, in which being known as a churchgoer was a modest social asset; a neutral world, from about 1994 to 2014, in which faith was a private preference that cost little either way; and a negative world since, in which Christian moral commitments, especially on sexuality, are more often treated as a liability. Renn's thesis concerns social standing, not martyrdom. It is also a sketch with limits: the positive world was never positive for Black Christians in the Jim Crow South, who lived under a public Christianity that many white churchgoers invoked to defend segregation. But most readers will recognize the shift, in a workplace where some convictions are safer unsaid or a family where a newly serious faith is treated as a symptom.

American Christians have been bad at the distinction this requires. The loss of social advantage is not persecution. It is closer to [losing home-field advantage](/writing/the-end-of-home-field-advantage) than to losing one's life. We've been tempted in two directions. Some of us call every slight a martyrdom and sound like a displaced ruling class. Others pay for respectability by quietly dropping whatever convictions have become expensive, until the faith costs nothing because it asserts nothing. Neither wants to pay the price Jesus named: the first insists the world should pay it for us, the second lowers it until it disappears.

For a large share of the world's Christians, meanwhile, the cost never stopped being literal. In many countries a person who leaves his family's religion for Christ can lose his inheritance, his marriage and his work, and in some places he faces legal penalties or violence. The details vary and deserve careful reporting rather than a slogan, but Matthew 10:36 is a lived experience wherever conversion closes the family table.

Nabeel Qureshi's memoir *Seeking Allah, Finding Jesus* (2014) shows the cost falling even in a free country. Raised in a devout Muslim family in the United States, he came to faith in Christ after years of argument with a friend. What he describes most painfully isn't danger from any state. It is the grief his decision brought to the parents he loved. The cost came from exactly the place Jesus said it would.

## The particular bill for someone who didn't believe

Every Christian pays some version of all this. The person who comes to faith out of unbelief receives an additional bill, and churches that treat such converts as trophies rarely mention it.

The first thing surrendered is a self-image. For many thoughtful atheists, not believing is part of who they are: the one who saw through it and could face a universe without consolation. To come to faith is to admit, often in public, that you were wrong about the largest question you ever had an opinion on. It means joining people you once found faintly ridiculous and discovering that you are now one of the hypocrites. Friends may assume you've gone soft or been captured by grief. Your own former contempt, which you remember well, is now aimed at you.

C. S. Lewis described his surrender to theism, which he dated to 1929, in *Surprised by Joy* (1955). He knelt and prayed and called himself "the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England." The comfort-product theory predicts that people come to God because they want to feel better. Lewis's account is of a man who wanted above all to be left alone and gave in because he couldn't honestly keep holding out. That doesn't prove his conclusion. It is evidence that his motive wasn't the one skeptics usually assign.

Rosaria Champagne Butterfield's *The Secret Thoughts of an Unlikely Convert* (2012) tells a more recent version. She was a tenured professor of English at Syracuse University, a lesbian and a scholar of queer theory, who began reading the Bible in order to criticize the Christian right and was befriended over a long period by a local pastor and his wife. Her conversion, as she tells it, cost her nearly everything that had made up her life: the community she loved, her place among colleagues, the identity she had built. Readers will weigh her convictions differently. What her book makes plain is that the price was paid first in relationships, before any consolation arrived.

Paul moved from one zealous faith to its fulfillment rather than from unbelief, but his ledger is the clearest in the New Testament. He lists the credentials he had once counted as gains, and then he redraws the columns.

> "But whatever gain I had, I counted as loss for the sake of Christ." (Philippians 3:7, ESV)

> "Indeed, I count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord. For his sake I have suffered the loss of all things and count them as rubbish, in order that I may gain Christ" (Philippians 3:8, ESV)

Paul doesn't pretend the losses weren't losses. He says he suffered them. What changed is the value of the other column. That is the honest shape of every convert's account.

I know this from the inside, having come to faith from atheism. One more cost belongs here. The questions don't vanish at conversion; some objections keep their force for years. Part of the price of an honest faith is carrying unanswered questions inside it rather than leaving in order to keep them, a subject taken up in [Can You Have Faith and Doubt at the Same Time?](/writing/can-you-have-faith-and-doubt). Anyone who promises the convert a mind at rest by the end of the first year is selling something. The Psalms and Job never promised that.

## What the price is not, and what Jesus promised

Honesty about the price requires honesty about what it isn't. It isn't a pressure sale. American revivalism refined the techniques of closing, from Charles Finney's anxious bench in Rochester in 1830 and 1831 to the hand raised while every head is bowed. Grace has reached people in those moments, and no one who met God at an altar should be mocked for it. But manufactured urgency contradicts the passage it claims to obey. Jesus told the crowd to sit down first. When a rich man asked what he must do to inherit eternal life, Mark records that "Jesus, looking at him, loved him" (Mark 10:21, ESV), and told him to sell what he had and follow. "Disheartened by the saying, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:22, ESV). Jesus didn't run after him or offer a discount. He loved him and let him go.

Nor is the price a down payment on prosperity. Teaching that promises health and wealth in exchange for faith reverses the passage; Jesus promised a cross. And it isn't a remedy for a bad mood. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, in *Soul Searching* (2005), found that the working faith of many American teenagers, learned from the adults around them, was what they called moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be nice, who exists chiefly to help them feel good about themselves, and who stays out of the way unless needed. That faith costs nothing, and it is the one the skeptic is right to despise. Churches like mine have taught it whenever we promised comfort before we mentioned the cross.

The gains, then, named without a pitch. Right after the rich man leaves, Peter says, "See, we have left everything and followed you" (Mark 10:28, ESV), and Jesus answers him.

> "Jesus said, 'Truly, I say to you, there is no one who has left house or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or lands, for my sake and for the gospel," (Mark 10:29, ESV)

> "who will not receive a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life.'" (Mark 10:30, ESV)

Two words keep that promise honest. The first is *now*. The hundredfold isn't only deferred to heaven; the new household of brothers and sisters and mothers and children is the church, strangers who become kin, a table that opens when another one closes. The second is *with persecutions*. Jesus sets the cost in the middle of the reward, so no one could hear it as a deal. The gain doesn't cancel the price. It comes alongside it. Elsewhere he compares the kingdom to treasure hidden in a field: "Then in his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field" (Matthew 13:44, ESV). From outside he looks like a man selling everything for a patch of dirt. He is the only one who knows what's buried there.

Which leaves the one question the price itself can't settle. Paul didn't hedge it: "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15:19, ESV). If Jesus did not rise from the dead, none of this is worth a cent, and the reader who walks away is simply keeping what is his. If he did rise, the arithmetic reverses, and those of us still holding tight to our money, our reputations and our tribes, and I am one of them, haven't yet understood what we are being offered.

Mark never tells us what became of the rich man. He leaves him walking away sorrowful, still holding what he couldn't release. Jesus' own road went on to Jerusalem, where the one who had named the price to the crowd paid it himself, and no one lowered it for him either. That is the strangest fact about the cost of discipleship: the man who asks for your life gave his first. He still waits for an answer rather than pressing for one. The crowd is large. He has turned around to face it, told it to sit down and count, and he hasn't lowered the price.
