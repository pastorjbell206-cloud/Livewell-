It is a quarter past eight. Someone is crying, someone has slid under the table, and a parent is reading from a devotional book in a voice that doesn't sound like his own, trying to reach the application question before the four-year-old comes apart. This is family devotions, the practice the parenting books promised would bind a household to God, and most parents who try it know the sinking feeling that comes with it. It feels like theater. So it gets dropped in the second week of a hard month, restarted in guilt after a sermon, run badly out of exhaustion, and dropped again in shame. The cycle itself teaches the children something, though not what anyone meant to teach: that God is the part of the day that makes the grown-ups tense.

The people typing "how to do family devotions" or "how to teach my kids to pray and read the Bible" into a search box at eleven at night are rarely lazy. They are usually the conscientious ones, carrying two burdens at once: the real conviction that a child ought to grow up knowing God, and an inherited picture of what that should look like, which no actual house with actual children has ever matched. I am a pastor, which gives me a particular way to fail here. Pastors pray in public for a living, and the temptation to pray at home the way I pray from a pulpit is strong and quietly deadly, because a child who only hears polished prayer learns that prayer is a skill adults have and he doesn't.

**The distinction this essay turns on is between performance and practice. A performance is judged by how it went tonight, and it needs an audience. A practice is judged by whether it continues, and by what it slowly makes of the people who keep it, and it is allowed to go badly. Most family devotions fail because they were built as performances. Scripture, and the church's long memory of household worship, describe a practice, and a practice is something a tired family can actually keep.**

## A House Built to Remember

The founding text is Deuteronomy 6, and it is worth reading slowly because its order is the instruction. "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might. And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise. You shall bind them as a sign on your hand, and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes. You shall write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates" (Deuteronomy 6:4–9).

Notice where the children come in. They come after the parent's own heart. Before Moses says a word about teaching, he says the words shall be *on your heart*, and only then that you shall teach them. The passage assumes the parent is a worshiper before he is an instructor. Then notice the hours it names: sitting, walking, lying down, rising. That is not the schedule of a service. It is the whole day, the table and the road and the bed, which is why observant Jews to this day recite the Shema at night and in the morning, taking the "lie down" and "rise" of the verse literally. And finally notice the doorposts. The house itself is made into a reminder, so that a child walks past the words a dozen times a day before anyone has explained them. Deuteronomy does not describe a fifteen-minute slot in which God is discussed and then put away. It describes a home that has been arranged to remember.

Psalm 78 shows what the remembering is for and how long it is meant to last. "We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD, and his might, and the wonders that he has done" (Psalm 78:4). The psalmist counts four generations in the space of two verses: the fathers who were commanded, the children who were taught, "the children yet unborn," who will "arise and tell them to their children" (78:5–6). And the purpose is stated with startling honesty: "so that they should set their hope in God and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments; and that they should not be like their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation, a generation whose heart was not steadfast, whose spirit was not faithful to God" (78:7–8). The story Israel handed its children included the parents' own failure. The household was not asked to present itself as the model. It was asked to tell the truth about God, which meant telling the truth about itself, and then to hope the children would do better.

That candor matters because the most famous household verse in the Bible is usually read without it. "But as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD" (Joshua 24:15) hangs on a great many kitchen walls. In context it is spoken at Shechem, at a covenant renewal near the end of Joshua's life, and the people answer with enthusiasm that they too will serve the LORD. Joshua's reply is the part that never gets framed: "You are not able to serve the LORD, for he is a holy God" (Joshua 24:19). He isn't discouraging them. He is refusing to let a vow be mistaken for a capacity. The Book of Judges then records what happened next: "And there arose another generation after them who did not know the LORD or the work that he had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10). The generation that made the most famous household vow in Scripture did not manage to hand it on. That is no reason for despair, but it is every reason for humility. The resolve on the wall is not the life in the house.

The New Testament keeps the household at the center, and it does so in ways that should comfort parents whose homes look nothing like the picture. Paul reminds Timothy of "your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well" (2 Timothy 1:5), and later urges him to continue in what he has learned, "knowing from whom you learned it and how from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (2 Timothy 3:14–15). Luke tells us something Paul leaves unsaid: Timothy was "the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer, but his father was a Greek" (Acts 16:1). The most celebrated case of a faith handed down in the New Testament passed through a grandmother and a mother, in a house where the father apparently did not share it. The word translated *sincere*, *anypokritos*, is the Greek word for a stage actor with a negating prefix: literally, unhypocritical. It describes a faith without play-acting, which is to say a faith that was not performed.

Later in the same chapter, when the gospel reaches the household of the jailer at Philippi, it arrives at midnight, after an earthquake, in a man who had just drawn his sword on himself. "Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household," Paul and Silas tell the jailer at Philippi (Acts 16:31). "And they spoke the word of the Lord to him and to all who were in his house. And he took them the same hour of the night and washed their wounds; and he was baptized at once, he and all his family. Then he brought them up into his house and set food before them. And he rejoiced along with his entire household that he had believed in God" (16:32–34). Christians have long argued over what those household baptisms imply for children. Paedobaptists read them as the covenant sign extended to the whole house, as circumcision once was; credobaptists answer that the word was spoken to all in the house and all rejoiced in believing. That second-order question divides faithful churches without dividing the faith, and this essay doesn't need to settle it. What both sides can see in the text is the shape of one of the first Christian households in Europe: the word spoken, wounds washed, a meal set out in the small hours, and joy shared across the whole house. None of it was tidy. All of it was real.

## The Little Church at the Hearth

For most of Christian history, a child's first school of faith was the house. The Didache, a church manual usually dated to the late first or early second century, told believers to pray the Lord's Prayer three times a day, which placed the church's central prayer inside the ordinary hours of the home long before any family owned a printed Bible. The Reformation took that inheritance and gave it written form. Luther wrote his Small Catechism in 1529, after the Saxon parish visitations had shown him how little ordinary Christians, and many of their pastors, understood of what they professed. He arranged it for the head of the household to teach, and its sections open with directions for how a father should set each part plainly before his family. The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 asks a child what his only comfort is in life and in death, and answers that he is not his own but belongs to his faithful Savior. The Westminster Shorter Catechism of 1647 begins with a question and answer that generations of children knew before they could spell: "What is the chief end of man? Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever."

The catechism was a family tool before it was a classroom one, and it rested on an insight modern parents often resist. Children can be given words before they understand them, and the words can wait. An answer learned at seven is a room built in advance, and the question that arrives at seventeen finds it already furnished. The danger is that the answer becomes a recitation performed for an approving face, and the Reformers knew that danger well, having just watched a continent perform a faith it didn't understand. But their remedy for recitation was never silence. It was a parent who meant the words.

The Puritans gave the arrangement its name. William Gouge, in *Of Domesticall Duties* (1622), described the family as a little church and a little commonwealth, a household with its own worship and its own order under God. The Scottish church wrote it into church order. In 1647 the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland approved a *Directory for Family Worship* that expected each household to gather for prayer and praise, for the reading of Scripture, for catechizing in plain terms, and for conversation about what had been read, with the head of the family answerable for seeing it done. The practice sank so deep into Scottish life that more than a century later Robert Burns could build a poem around it. In "The Cotter's Saturday Night" (1786), a farm laborer's family gathers at the end of the week while the father takes down the great family Bible that had been his own father's pride, chooses a passage, and leads them in a psalm and in prayer. Burns idealized the scene, and he knew exactly what its counterfeit looked like, because he also wrote "Holy Willie's Prayer," a merciless portrait of a church elder whose prayers display his own righteousness. Between those two poems Burns drew the line this essay is about.

In England, Matthew Henry preached *A Church in the House* (1704), taking his title from the phrase Paul uses of Priscilla and Aquila, "the church in their house" (1 Corinthians 16:19), and arguing that every Christian family ought to be such a church, with daily worship at its center. In New England, Jonathan Edwards ordered his house in Northampton around morning and evening prayer with Scripture. George Marsden's *Jonathan Edwards: A Life* (2003) describes a household of eleven children in which worship was as regular as meals. When Edwards's congregation dismissed him in 1750, his farewell sermon urged the families he was leaving to make each Christian home a little church, consecrated to Christ and governed by his rules.

The skeptic reading this has a fair objection, and it should be granted in full. The little church was also a little commonwealth, and its order was often hierarchical in ways Scripture doesn't require and sometimes in ways Scripture condemns. The same Marsden biography records that Edwards bought an enslaved woman named Venus in 1731. The household that gathered for prayer in his home included a woman held there against her will, and the prayers did not correct it. In many homes since, family worship has been an instrument of control, a nightly display of a father's authority rather than a father's submission to God. That history is real, and the church should say so before anyone else has to. If religion in a home is being used to frighten or control a spouse or a child, that is abuse, not devotion, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) is a place to start; where a child may be at risk, call the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline at 1-800-422-4453. But the abuse of a practice is an argument against the abuse. The same apostle who told parents to teach their children wrote, "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). A household worship that frightens children has already broken the text it claims to obey.

## How the Home Handed It Off

Family worship did not decline because anyone argued against it. It was quietly handed off. In 1780 Robert Raikes began gathering the children of Gloucester's working poor on Sundays, the one day they weren't at work, to teach them to read, with Scripture as the text. The Sunday school movement he helped launch did great good for children whose homes could not teach them, and over time it became, in many churches, the place where the children of Christian homes were sent to learn what their parents no longer taught. The twentieth century finished the handoff. Young Life was founded in 1941, Youth for Christ rallies drew crowds by the middle of the decade, and after the war, as the American high school became a world of its own, the professional youth pastor became an ordinary fixture of the American congregation. Thomas Bergler's *The Juvenilization of American Christianity* (2012) traces how methods designed to hold teenagers, built on excitement, belonging and a faith pitched to adolescent feeling, gradually reshaped the adult church in their image.

The people who built those ministries deserve their steelman, because they were answering real needs, and some of them were needs no household could meet. A great many teenagers had no believing parent at all. Many who come to faith from unbelief, and many raised without a father, had no family altar to inherit. For a child like that, the church's ministry to the young is not outsourcing. It is the only household of faith he has, and the psalmist praises a God who "settles the solitary in a home" (Psalm 68:6). The church should never apologize for being that home.

But in homes where parents were present and believing, something else happened. Formation migrated from the house to the building, and parents came to believe, often because the church told them so, that teaching their own children the faith was specialist work for which they were unqualified. The sociologist Christian Smith, with Melinda Lundquist Denton, reported what that produced in *Soul Searching* (2005), drawn from the National Study of Youth and Religion. Most American teenagers were not hostile to religion. They were inarticulate about it, and the faith they could describe amounted to what Smith and Denton named moralistic therapeutic deism: a distant God who wants people to be nice and to feel good about themselves, and who can be called on when there's a problem. The finding that ought to keep Christian parents awake is a different one. Smith and Denton concluded that parents remain the most important religious influence on their teenagers, and that most teenagers reflect the faith of the adults who raised them. The home had not stopped forming children. It had stopped noticing what it formed.

Kenda Creasy Dean, who teaches youth ministry at Princeton Theological Seminary, pressed the point from inside the profession in *Almost Christian* (2010). The thin faith of American teenagers, she argues, is not a youth problem. It is a portrait of the churches and homes that raised them, which had themselves traded a faith that costs something for a faith that helps. Young people mostly catch faith from adults for whom it is visibly consequential, and they learned precisely what they saw. That verdict implicates everyone who has stood at the front of a church, and I include myself. We built programs that told parents their part was to deliver the children on time. Many parents believed us, some with relief, and a good deal of the guilt they carry now is guilt we handed them after we had taken away their confidence.

## Every House Already Has a Liturgy

James K. A. Smith argues in *You Are What You Love* (2016) that we are formed less by what we are told than by what we repeatedly do. Our loves are trained by liturgies, embodied rituals that aim the heart before the mind catches up, and Smith gives particular attention to the home. His point cuts deeper than most parents want. Every household already has a liturgy. The rush to the car, the screens at dinner, the calendar built around the travel team, the tone a family uses when someone fails: these are rituals, repeated daily, and they are teaching children what matters. The question is never whether a home forms. It's what it forms.

That cuts against the dutiful parent too. A devotion that is dreaded, rushed and resented is also a liturgy, and repeated a few hundred times it teaches with great reliability that God is the dull part of the evening, the thing endured before release. Jesus warned about prayer staged for an audience: "And when you pray, you must not be like the hypocrites. For they love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners, that they may be seen by others" (Matthew 6:5). A family devotion can drift into exactly that, with the children as the audience, or with the parent's own conscience as the audience, checking the box that proves he is a good Christian father.

Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) supplies the sharper term. A practice, in his account, is a shared activity whose real goods are internal to it, goods available only by doing the thing itself over time, as distinct from external goods like reputation or reward. A child slowly acquiring the language of prayer is an internal good; a parent's sense of having done his duty is an external one. The household chasing the external good will grade every evening by how it went, and most evenings will fail. The household pursuing the internal good can have a terrible night and still have practiced, the way a child plays her scales badly and has still practiced the piano.

Seen this way, teaching a child to pray is less like instruction and more like language. Children learn to speak by being soaked in speech long before they understand it, and they learn to pray the same way. The disciples asked to be taught only after watching: "Now Jesus was praying in a certain place, and when he finished, one of his disciples said to him, 'Lord, teach us to pray, as John taught his disciples'" (Luke 11:1). So the first question about a child's prayer life is whether he has ever heard a parent pray in his own voice about real things: the money that isn't there, the person he can't forgive, the test results. A child files the prayer said because he is watching in a different drawer from the prayer that escapes a parent when the news is bad. Only the second will hold when his own ground moves.

For young children, short and regular beats long and heartfelt. Three sentences at bedtime in the same shape every night will do more than a moving twenty minutes once a month. The repetition isn't deadening. The church gave its children the Lord's Prayer and its fixed hours because a set prayer is a gift to the person who will one day be too tired or too devastated to compose his own. And teach them to lament. A large share of the Psalter is complaint, "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1), and Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* (1984) shows these psalms of disorientation to be the prayers of a faith honest about pain, not a faith that has failed. A child told that only grateful prayers are acceptable learns that God can take him only at his best, and when his life breaks he will go silent.

Scripture needs the same honesty. Children's Bibles that turn the flood into a cheerful boat ride and David into a lesson in self-esteem set a child up to meet the real book at fifteen and feel deceived. Move children toward the text itself as they grow, a few verses read slowly rather than a chapter checked off, and when a passage disturbs them, admit that it disturbs you too. The Bible doesn't flatter its heroes, and a child who notices that has found one of the first reasons to trust it.

## The Parent Who Cannot Add One More Thing

The objection deserves its strongest voice. A single mother working a second shift, a father whose child's disability turns every evening into triage, a parent married to someone who doesn't believe, a family whose teenagers are home together for ninety minutes in a whole week: for these parents the counsel to keep family worship lands as one more standard they are already failing. And they can make a scriptural case. No text commands a nightly devotional. The *Directory* of 1647 assumed households with settled days, and Burns's cotter had his Saturday night. A program added in guilt to a life already at its limit will be kept in resentment, and a resentful liturgy forms resentment. Some of these parents also remember a childhood devotion that was a weapon, and they would rather give their children nothing than give them that.

Nearly all of this is true, and it should be conceded plainly. The form is not commanded. What Deuteronomy commands is saturation, the words on the parent's heart and talk of them in the hours the family already has. So the answer to the exhausted parent is not a smaller program. It is attaching the practice to what already carries weight in the day. You already put them to bed; that is when you lie down. You already drive; that is walking by the way. You already eat, even if it's fast and interrupted. A prayer welded to something the family already does survives a hard month, because it doesn't need a slot the family doesn't have. Some nights it will be two minutes at the edge of a bed and a whispered Lord's Prayer. That counts. It is exactly what Moses meant by "when you lie down."

For the parent without a believing spouse, the New Testament has already written your household into the canon. Timothy's faith came through a grandmother and a mother, with a Greek father in the house, and Scripture did not treat that home as a lesser case. It held it up. The families who wait for ideal conditions will wait forever. The families who pray badly in the cracks of an ordinary evening are the ones who actually pray.

## What the Table Was For

The central practice Jesus gave his people was a meal, and the first time it was kept, the people at the table argued. "A dispute also arose among them, as to which of them was to be regarded as the greatest" (Luke 22:24). He fed them anyway, and washed their feet. The chaos at the table did not disqualify the table. A household gathered to pray is a small copy of that room: people who love and irritate one another, gathered around a grace none of them produced.

Which means the most formative act of family worship may not be a reading at all. It may be repentance. Psalm 78 handed children the story of their fathers' failure so that they would not repeat it, and a child who hears a parent say "I was wrong to speak to you that way; will you forgive me?" and then hears that parent confess the same thing to God has been taught the gospel in its native grammar. A house that only displays its competence teaches that God is for people who have it together. A house that confesses teaches that God is for sinners, which is the only God any of us has ever needed.

Then the outcome has to be released. "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:6). Joshua's generation made the vow and did not hand it on; Lois and Eunice handed it on through a house the world would have called incomplete. The growth was never the parent's to produce, and that is a mercy, because no child was ever saved by the quality of his family devotions. For a man raised without a father, the verse that carries the most weight in the whole Bible is the one where a son comes home in disgrace and "while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). That is the Father our children need to meet, and no household can produce him. It can only live near enough to him that the children learn where to look.

The question a parent carries away from the table, then, is not whether tonight went well. It is what the house is saying to God, and about him, in the hours no one thinks to count. The children have been listening to that liturgy since before they could speak. They are listening now.
