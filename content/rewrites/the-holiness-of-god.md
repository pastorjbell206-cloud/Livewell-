Most of us use the word holy the way we use the word nice, only more so. A holy person is a kind person with steady habits, and a holy place is quiet and has good light. When we say God is holy we usually mean that he is very, very good, the top rung of a ladder the rest of us are climbing more slowly, an improved version of ourselves. It's a comfortable picture, and one reason many thoughtful people have concluded God isn't worth much trouble. A being who is merely a better version of us might deserve admiration but not worship, and he doesn't explain the fear the Bible keeps describing in the people who meet him.

For other readers the word isn't soft at all. They grew up in churches where holiness was measured in hemlines and forbidden music, and where the word arrived most often as a verdict on somebody else. For them holy means cold, watchful, and faintly contemptuous, and many of them left because they couldn't find love anywhere near it. That wound is real, and this essay will come back to it.

Both pictures miss what Scripture means. To say God is holy is first to say that he is other: not the highest being in our category but the one who has no category, the Creator who is not a creature. That otherness is frightening, and the Bible never pretends it isn't. But the strangest thing Scripture says about the holy God is not that he keeps his distance. It's that his holiness comes near, burns, and makes the unclean clean. I came to faith out of atheism, out of exactly that flattened world, assuming any God worth the name would be one I could examine and assess and approve. It never occurred to me that the one actually there might be the kind who undoes the examiner.

## What the Hebrew word qadosh does and doesn't tell us

The Hebrew adjective is *qadosh*. Preachers have long said its root means to cut or to separate, so that the holy is whatever has been cut away from common use. There is something to that, but the etymology is debated. Scholars have tied the root both to separation and to an Akkadian word meaning to be pure, and the evidence doesn't settle it. James Barr, in *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961), warned biblical scholars against reading a word's meaning off its supposed root. Words mean what they do in use, and even if the root once meant "cut off," that wouldn't tell us what Isaiah meant by it centuries later. The better question is how the Bible uses *qadosh*.

The use is clear enough. Things become holy by belonging to God. "So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it God rested from all his work that he had done in creation" (Genesis 2:3, ESV). The ground at the bush is holy because the LORD is there (Exodus 3:5). Vessels, priests, a city, and finally a people are holy the same way, and their holiness is borrowed from the one who alone is holy in himself. Hannah prays it: "There is none holy like the LORD: for there is none besides you; there is no rock like our God" (1 Samuel 2:2, ESV). In God, holiness is first incomparability rather than a grade on the moral scale: he isn't the largest item in the universe's inventory but the one on whom the whole inventory depends.

The anthropologist Mary Douglas added something in *Purity and Danger* (1966). Reading the Levitical laws, she argued that holiness in Israel's world is bound up with wholeness and completeness, with things kept true to their kind. Her reading has been debated, but it caught something the slogan "set apart" misses. Jacob Milgrom's commentary on Leviticus (its first volume appeared in 1991) makes a related case: the impurities that barred a person from the sanctuary cluster around death, and holiness stands on the side of life. The holy is the whole and the fully alive, and not merely the distant.

That gives us the distinction the rest of this essay depends on. Holiness is separation *for* before it is separation *from*. Israel is set apart from the nations in order to be a priestly people for them. And the God who is holy in himself does not treat his otherness as a reason to stay away. Hosea hears him say so at the moment judgment on Ephraim seems certain: "I will not execute my burning anger; I will not again destroy Ephraim; for I am God and not a man, the Holy One in your midst, and I will not come in wrath" (Hosea 11:9, ESV). He spares Ephraim not in spite of his holiness but because of it. His otherness is the ground of his mercy.

## The year King Uzziah died

The clearest window onto all of this is the vision that undid a prophet. "In the year that King Uzziah died I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up; and the train of his robe filled the temple" (Isaiah 6:1, ESV). On the usual reckoning that is around 740 BC. The Chronicler records that at the height of his strength Uzziah grew proud, walked into the temple to burn incense himself, a task reserved for the priests, and was struck with leprosy on the spot. He lived out his days in a separate house, cut off from the house of the LORD (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). The king who presumed on holiness died unclean. In the year of his death, in the same temple, Isaiah sees who the real King is.

"Above him stood the seraphim. Each had six wings: with two he covered his face, and with two he covered his feet, and with two he flew" (Isaiah 6:2, ESV). The name most likely means burning ones. They aren't sinners, and still they cover their faces. Then they speak:

> "And one called to another and said: 'Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory!'" (Isaiah 6:3, ESV)

Hebrew often intensifies by repetition where English would reach for a superlative. The seraphim say it three times, and no other attribute of God is sung that way in Scripture; the only echo is the same song around the throne in Revelation 4:8. And the second line matters as much as the first: this otherness already fills the whole earth. The holy God isn't absent from the ordinary. We are the ones who can't see it.

Isaiah's response is collapse. "And I said: 'Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!'" (Isaiah 6:5, ESV). This is a prophet, very likely the most religious person in the building, and holiness first takes away his confidence in his own goodness. He names his lips, which is exact, because a prophet's instrument is his mouth. And he doesn't separate himself from his people. Before the holy, he can no longer be the good man in a bad nation.

Then comes what neither the soft nor the harsh picture of holiness expects.

> "Then one of the seraphim flew to me, having in his hand a burning coal that he had taken with tongs from the altar." (Isaiah 6:6, ESV)

> "And he touched my mouth and said: 'Behold, this has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away, and your sin atoned for.'" (Isaiah 6:7, ESV)

Isaiah can't clean himself up. The initiative comes from the throne, and it comes from the altar, the place of sacrifice, so that cleansing is tied to cost from the beginning. Holiness doesn't lower its standard to meet Isaiah; it burns away what can't stand before it and leaves the man alive and forgiven. Then the Lord asks who will go, and the prophet who a moment ago confessed his unclean lips answers with them, "Here I am! Send me" (Isaiah 6:8, ESV). The holy God undoes, cleanses, and sends, in that order.

## The trembling Rudolf Otto named, and what he couldn't see

Part of the reason the modern word went soft lies at the end of the eighteenth century. In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), Immanuel Kant described holiness as the complete conformity of a will to the moral law, a perfection no finite creature reaches. Holiness became the top of the moral scale, and religion became, at its best, morality with a transcendent guarantee.

Rudolf Otto's *The Idea of the Holy* (published in German as *Das Heilige* in 1917) was in part a protest against that reduction. Otto, a Lutheran theologian, argued that beneath serious religion lies an experience that is neither moral nor rational but prior to both. He called its object the *numinous*, from the Latin *numen*, a divine presence, and he described the creature-feeling it produces: the sense of being dust before something more real than oneself. His phrase for it is *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*. It is a mystery that makes us tremble and draws us in at the same moment, so that we want to flee and can't look away. Otto called it the wholly other.

Read Isaiah 6 with Otto beside you and the covered faces make sense. They aren't reactions to a very good person but what a creature feels at the edge of the uncreated. Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) later traced how the modern West came to experience the world within what he calls the immanent frame, a self-explaining natural order that can be lived as closed, with nothing pressing in from beyond it. A flattened world produces a flattened God. Nobody argued us out of trembling; we built a way of living with nothing left to tremble before, and then wondered why worship felt thin.

Otto has limits, though, and they need stating fairly. He was describing human experience, and a description of an experience can't establish that anything answers to it. Ludwig Feuerbach had argued in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) that the gods are human nature projected onto the sky, and a feeling of awe fits that account as easily as it fits Isaiah's. The skeptic deserves that point granted plainly. Awe doesn't prove God. What it shows is that the flattened picture of reality isn't the only one human beings have lived inside.

Otto's method also makes morality look like a later layer over a nonrational core. He didn't deny the ethical, but in Isaiah the dread and the guilt arrive together. The prophet doesn't only feel small. He feels unclean.

The deepest limit is that Otto starts from a general category of the sacred found across religions and reads the God of Israel as one instance of it. John Webster's short, demanding book *Holiness* (2003) pushes against exactly that move. Webster insists that Christian theology begin not from a general human sense of the sacred but from what God has made known of himself, and what God has made known is a holiness enacted in his turning toward creatures to make them holy. Holiness, on that account, is something God does: he sanctifies, sets apart a people for himself, and dwells among them. Otto helps us feel the edge of the cliff. Webster reminds us that the Holy One of Israel doesn't stay on the far side of it.

## Holiness at the edge of a field

If holiness were only otherness, the call to be holy would make no sense, since no creature can become uncreated. The command's most famous form shows what God has in mind. "Speak to all the congregation of the people of Israel and say to them, You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2, ESV). Scholars since the late nineteenth century have called Leviticus 17 through 26 the Holiness Code, and chapter 19 is its heart.

Most of what follows is about neighbors. When you reap your harvest, don't cut the field to its edges or go back for the gleanings, and don't strip your vineyard bare; leave them for the poor and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9–10). Pay the day laborer before nightfall (19:13). Keep honest weights (19:35–36). And in the middle of it all:

> "You shall not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:14, ESV)

The deaf man can't hear the curse, and the blind woman can't see who set the stone in her path. The only one watching is God, so the law places the fear of God exactly where no human witness reaches. Holiness is what you do when nobody can catch you. A few verses later comes the line Jesus would call the second great commandment: "You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against the sons of your own people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:18, ESV). Before the chapter closes, that love reaches the foreigner, to be loved as oneself, "for you were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34, ESV).

The refrain "I am the LORD" ties each of these obligations to God's own holiness: a people who belong to him will look like him in the field, the wage, and the market. Here Kant and Leviticus touch and then part. Kant was right that holiness is moral; Leviticus 19 won't let anyone separate the holy from the just. But this holiness isn't conformity to an abstract law that reason discovers for itself. It is the character of a particular God who brought a particular people out of slavery, and it takes the shape of his care for the powerless. The motive is history, not philosophy: you were strangers in Egypt. So the distinction worth holding is between holiness that is moral and holiness that is moralism. Moralism measures itself by the rules it keeps and the people it avoids. Holiness in Leviticus 19 measures itself by the edge of the field it leaves uncut. A religion that calls itself holy while cutting the field to the edges has misunderstood the God it names.

## The texts that make us flinch

Any honest account of holiness has to face the stories that make readers put the Bible down. The first is Uzzah. David is bringing the ark of the covenant to his new capital on a new cart. "And when they came to the threshing floor of Nacon, Uzzah put out his hand to the ark of God and took hold of it, for the oxen stumbled" (2 Samuel 6:6, ESV). Then: "And the anger of the LORD was kindled against Uzzah, and God struck him down there because of his error, and he died there beside the ark of God" (2 Samuel 6:7, ESV). The second story is older. On the day Israel's priesthood is inaugurated, after fire has come from before the LORD and consumed the offering (Leviticus 9:24), Aaron's sons Nadab and Abihu offer "unauthorized fire before the LORD, which he had not commanded them" (Leviticus 10:1, ESV). The same fire that accepted the sacrifice consumes them.

The skeptic's case isn't foolish. A man reaches out on instinct to keep a sacred object out of the mud, and he dies for it. Two priests get a ritual wrong on their first day, and they are burned. Whatever this God is, he looks petty and easily offended, more concerned with protocol than people, and on that reading moral progress consists largely in leaving him behind. The text itself feels the force of this. "And David was angry because the LORD had broken out against Uzzah" (2 Samuel 6:8, ESV), and then he was afraid (6:9). Scripture doesn't hide the protest. It records it.

An answer won't make the stories comfortable, but context changes their shape. The law specified that the ark be carried on poles by Levites and not touched, on pain of death (Exodus 25:14–15; Numbers 4:15). A cart was how the Philistines had moved it when they sent it back (1 Samuel 6:7–8). David's procession, however joyful, borrowed the method of people who didn't know the LORD. The Chronicler has David himself confess afterward that the disaster came because they had not sought God according to his rule (1 Chronicles 15:13), and the second procession, done properly, ends in dancing.

R. C. Sproul pressed further in *The Holiness of God* (1985). Uzzah's assumption, Sproul argued, was that his hand was cleaner than the ground, that the ark would be defiled by mud but not by a sinner's touch, when the earth had never rebelled against God. Our shock, on Sproul's account, is itself the symptom, because we've quietly assumed we belong in God's presence as we are. The astonishing thing in Scripture, he argued, isn't that God judges sin but that he lets any of us live. A skeptic can hear that as special pleading, and it becomes special pleading whenever it is used to end the conversation rather than to deepen it.

Nadab and Abihu fit the pattern, though the text never says exactly what made their fire unauthorized, and interpreters have guessed for centuries. What it says is that they offered what the LORD "had not commanded them." It gives Moses' word to their father: "Then Moses said to Aaron, 'This is what the LORD has said: "Among those who are near me I will be sanctified, and before all the people I will be glorified."' And Aaron held his peace" (Leviticus 10:3, ESV). Nearness raises the stakes, because worship on our own terms is always an attempt to handle God.

The honest thing is to admit these stories still don't resolve cleanly, and the skeptic isn't wrong to feel their weight. What changes things is where they sit in the canon. The same ark that killed Uzzah rested three months in the house of Obed-edom, and the LORD blessed his whole household (2 Samuel 6:11). And the Bible will not let the story end with a man struck down beside the holy thing. It ends with the Holy One struck down. Isaiah writes of a servant whom onlookers esteemed "stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted" (Isaiah 53:4, ESV), and then says it was the will of the LORD to crush him (53:10). Uzzah touched the holy and died. The Gospels tell what happened when the holy touched us.

## Two ways to get holiness wrong

The first way, the more common in American churches, keeps the word and drains it. In *The Kingdom of God in America* (1937), H. Richard Niebuhr summed up the liberal Protestantism of his day: "A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross." Nearly seventy years later, Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton drew on a national study of American teenagers for *Soul Searching* (2005) and found the same God across denominations, conservative as well as liberal. They called it moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who wants people to be good and nice, whose purpose for their lives is that they be happy and feel good about themselves, and who otherwise stays out of the way until a problem needs solving. It would be comfortable to pin that on the other tribe, and it can't be done. Progressive churches have subtracted the offense until nothing costs anything, and conservative churches have sold the same subtraction under brighter lights, with a God whose chief attribute is that he is for you. He is for us. But the one who is for us is the one before whom the seraphim cover their faces, and without that, "for you" turns into flattery.

The second way, holiness as withdrawal, deserves a fair hearing first. The Methodist and Holiness traditions took the call to be holy with a seriousness much of American Christianity had lost. John Wesley's *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection* (1766) insisted that grace doesn't merely pardon but changes a life down to its loves. In New York, Phoebe Palmer's Tuesday meetings and her book *The Way of Holiness* (1843) helped set a movement going that reached working people and defended the right of women to preach, and the Wesleyan Methodists who formed their own connection in 1843 did so largely in protest against slavery. Those churches believed God actually makes people different, and they were right.

Many of their standards began as costly witness, plain dress against display and abstinence against the saloon that was wrecking working families, and a tradition deserves to be judged by why it drew its lines as well as by what they later became. But holiness can harden into a fence, and not only in Holiness churches; fundamentalist, Reformed, Pentecostal and many other congregations have built the same one. The rules multiplied: dress, hair, music, movies, the right friends and the wrong ones. Holiness came to be measured less by love of neighbor than by distance from sinners, and the fastest way to feel holy was to notice someone who wasn't. Some who read this grew up there. They remember being taught to fear their own bodies and suspect every question. Some remember leaders who used holiness to control people or to hide their own sins. That grief is legitimate, and you don't owe anyone a quick recovery from it. If what you lived through was abuse, it has a name, and the name isn't holiness. Telling someone safe outside that system, a trusted friend or a counselor, and the police where a crime was done, is a good and faithful thing to do.

Scripture names this distortion with unusual heat. Through Isaiah the LORD describes people who sacrifice in gardens and eat what his law forbids, and then say, "Keep to yourself, do not come near me, for I am too holy for you." His verdict follows: "These are a smoke in my nostrils, a fire that burns all the day" (Isaiah 65:5, ESV). Jesus tells of a Pharisee who thanked God he was not like other men, and of the tax collector beside him who went home justified instead (Luke 18:9–14).

The contempt isn't confined to one side. There is a progressive holiness too, a purity kept by public denunciation, with its own lists of the unclean and its own pleasure in not being like those people. Whether the fence is built from hemlines or vocabulary, the posture is the same: holiness as a possession that sorts the world into clean and contaminated, with ourselves on the right side. We all reach for it, and if we're honest, every one of us has felt its warmth. Neither the flattened version nor the contemptuous one begins where Isaiah began, undone before a holiness that belongs to God alone and finds every one of us unclean. From there, niceness is impossible and contempt is absurd.

## The Holy One who touches the unclean

Late in the Old Testament, Haggai has the priests confirm the ordinary logic of purity: holiness doesn't pass to what it touches, but uncleanness does (Haggai 2:11–13). Holiness is fragile, uncleanness is contagious, and most of us still live by that logic.

In Mark's Gospel the first to call Jesus the Holy One of God is an unclean spirit (Mark 1:24). Soon after comes a scene the law had tried to make impossible. A man with a skin disease, required to live alone outside the camp and cry "Unclean, unclean" to warn people off (Leviticus 13:45–46), comes close enough to kneel.

> "And a leper came to him, imploring him, and kneeling said to him, 'If you will, you can make me clean.'" (Mark 1:40, ESV)

> "Moved with pity, he stretched out his hand and touched him and said to him, 'I will; be clean.'" (Mark 1:41, ESV)

> "And immediately the leprosy left him, and he was made clean." (Mark 1:42, ESV)

Jesus could have healed him with a word. He touches him first. By Haggai's logic the touch should have made Jesus unclean, and instead the current runs the other way: holiness turns out to be the contagious thing. King Uzziah presumed on holiness and died a leper in a separate house. In the Gospel, the Holy One reaches into the separate house.

Mark tells it again in another key. A woman who had bled for twelve years, which under the law made her unclean and made unclean whatever she touched (Leviticus 15:25–27), pushes through the crowd to touch his cloak, hoping to be healed unseen. Jesus stops until she comes forward trembling and tells him everything, and in front of everyone he calls her daughter. "And he said to her, 'Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease'" (Mark 5:34, ESV).

This is the deepest answer to the skeptic's question about Uzzah, and to the church that fenced its holiness. God's holiness was never fragile and never needed protecting from sinners. It needed sinners to stop imagining they could handle it on their own terms, and then it came to them. Hebrews puts the whole movement in a sentence: "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Hebrews 13:12, ESV). Outside the gate is where the lepers lived. The Holy One went there to make a holy people, and the coal from Isaiah's altar turns out to have been a cross.

That is the setting in which Peter repeats the command of Leviticus to scattered, slandered churches in Asia Minor: "but as he who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct, since it is written, 'You shall be holy, for I am holy'" (1 Peter 1:15–16, ESV). He grounds it at once in what their holiness cost: a ransom paid not in silver or gold but in the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18–19). And the holiness he asks for isn't withdrawal. Within a few verses it becomes earnest love for one another, and within a chapter, honorable conduct among the pagan neighbors who speak against them (1 Peter 1:22; 2:12). A holy people are set apart in the middle of the city, to live there differently.

None of this makes holiness safe. Hebrews still says, "let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:28–29, ESV). The fire is still fire. What has changed is that it has come near, and what it burns it also heals. If you don't believe, the most this essay can hope is to have set before you a God who is neither the bellhop nor the scold you rightly refused. If you were wounded by a church that called its contempt holiness, the hope is that you might hear the word again from the one who touched lepers. And for those of us who have grown comfortable with a God who fits, the seraphim have not stopped singing, and they still cover their faces. The coal is still on the altar, and it is pressed only to lips that have first said, "Woe is me." What we carry out of the room is whether we have ever said it, and whether, once touched, we are willing to be sent outside the gate where he went.
