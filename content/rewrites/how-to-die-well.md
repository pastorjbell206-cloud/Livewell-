Many families meet the end of a life in a small conference room off an intensive care unit. There's a box of tissues on the table, a whiteboard nobody has erased, and a physician who has had this conversation many times and is trying not to let it sound that way. What the family hears is that they're being asked to decide something no one ever taught them how to decide, about a person they can't imagine losing, in a vocabulary (code status, intubation, comfort measures) they learned yesterday. Some reach for the only instruction they have, which is to do everything. Others, after weeks of watching a parent suffer, begin to wonder quietly whether the kind thing would be to make it stop, and to make it stop sooner than the disease would.

First, a plain word. This essay is written by a pastor, not a physician or a lawyer, and nothing in it is medical or legal advice. The decisions at the end of a life belong to the dying person where they can still make them, to the people who love them, to the doctors who know that body and that disease, and to a pastor who knows their name. If you face those decisions now, call a palliative care team, a hospice, your own physicians and your pastor. And if you're reading this because you want to die, not as a bioethics question but tonight, please call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, or tell someone in your house. You shouldn't carry that alone.

The two reflexes in that conference room look like opposites, but they grow from a common root. Both assume death is something a person must master, by refusing it to the last machine or by scheduling it. The Christian tradition knew a third way, older than either. It called dying well an art, and it taught that art to people who weren't yet sick. The distinction this essay turns on is simple to state and hard to live: a Christian may stop fighting death without ever reaching for it. We don't cling to life at any cost, and we don't seize control of its end. We receive our dying, as we received our living, from someone else's hands.

## An art the church once taught

The Black Death reached Europe in 1347 and in four years killed something like a third of its people, and plague kept returning for a century after. Around 1415, in the circle of the Council of Constance, an anonymous writer (very likely a Dominican friar) produced a short Latin handbook, the *Tractatus artis bene moriendi*, the treatise on the art of dying well, drawing on earlier counsel from Jean Gerson of the University of Paris. By midcentury a shorter version was circulating with woodcuts, among the earliest printed picture books in Europe, and the *ars moriendi* became one of the most widely circulated devotional books of its age.

The woodcuts show a deathbed as a battlefield. Demons crowd one side and angels the other, and the dying man faces five temptations in turn: to abandon his faith, to despair of mercy, to grow impatient and bitter under suffering, to trust complacently in his own goodness, and to cling to his possessions and the people he's leaving. The longer text adds questions for the attendants to put to him, among them whether he trusts to be saved by Christ's death rather than his own merits, along with prayers for those around the bed and counsel to pray as Christ prayed from the cross.

The book assumed several things we have forgotten. Dying is something a person does, and not only something that happens to him. The last hours are spiritually contested, and fear, despair and grasping can be named in advance and resisted. And the community has a job at a deathbed: to be present, to pray, and to keep the dying person's eyes on Christ. Dying was a corporate act, rehearsed long before it arrived.

The Reformation didn't abolish the art. It moved its center. In 1519 Martin Luther wrote *A Sermon on Preparing to Die* for a man who had asked him how to face his end. Luther tells the reader first to put his worldly affairs in order, so his family won't quarrel over what he leaves, and to forgive and seek forgiveness from anyone he has wronged. Then he turns to the soul: the dying shouldn't stare at death, sin and hell in themselves, where they only grow larger, but look at them in Christ, who has taken all three into himself. The medieval deathbed was a battle to be won; Luther's is a promise to be believed.

In 1651, in the upheaval of the English Interregnum, the Anglican priest Jeremy Taylor published *The Rule and Exercises of Holy Dying* as a companion to his *Holy Living*. His governing conviction is that holy dying is the harvest of holy living. He warns against putting off repentance until the sickbed, where pain and weakness leave a person least able to attend to it. So the preparation is a whole life of daily self-examination, patience and charity.

Then, within a single lifetime, the art disappeared. The twentieth century moved death from the house to the hospital, and the hospital acquired astonishing powers. The Copenhagen polio epidemic of 1952 pushed physicians to keep patients breathing by hand for weeks, and out of that emergency came the modern intensive care unit; closed-chest resuscitation followed in 1960. The French historian Philippe Ariès, surveying a thousand years of Western attitudes in *The Hour of Our Death* (1981 in English), traced the movement from what he called a tamed death, familiar, public and ritually prepared, to an invisible one, hidden in institutions and managed by professionals.

Atul Gawande, a surgeon, gave the most widely read account of the cost in *Being Mortal* (2014): medicine is trained to fix, has little idea what to do when nothing can be fixed, and so keeps fixing, while patients spend their last weeks in treatments that neither heal them nor let them live the time they have. Rob Moll made the church's version of the charge in *The Art of Dying* (2010). The church, he argued, handed dying over to medicine and lost the practices that let Christians die as Christians. That charge includes pastors like me. We have often arrived at the hospital as guests of a process we didn't understand, offering a prayer at the end of a conversation we were never part of. The loss wasn't medicine's fault alone. We gave the room away.

## What Scripture does with death

The ninetieth Psalm is headed "A Prayer of Moses," and it reads like a prayer from the wilderness, where a whole generation died short of the promised land (Numbers 14). It sets our brevity against God's everlastingness, and then it asks for something.

> "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom." (Psalm 90:12)

Moses asks to be taught, because knowing that we will die doesn't produce wisdom by itself; it can as easily produce panic or distraction. We have become very good at the arithmetic of death: the staging, the median survival, the percentages. What we haven't learned is a life ordered around the fact of its end.

Scripture's first word about death is that it is an enemy. Oscar Cullmann made the contrast unforgettable in a lecture published in 1958 as *Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?* In Plato's *Phaedo*, Socrates drinks the hemlock calmly, even gladly, because for him death releases the soul from the prison of the body. In Gethsemane, Jesus trembles.

> "And he said to them, 'My soul is very sorrowful, even to death. Remain here and watch.'" (Mark 14:34)

For Socrates death is a friend. For the New Testament it is "the last enemy to be destroyed" (1 Corinthians 15:26), an intruder into a good creation, tearing apart what God joined when he breathed into the dust. A Christian who is afraid of dying isn't failing at faith. Jesus stood at his friend's grave and "Jesus wept" (John 11:35), though he knew he was about to call Lazarus out of it.

But the enemy is a defeated one. At the end of his long argument for the bodily resurrection, Paul picks up a line from Hosea and turns it into a taunt.

> "O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?" (1 Corinthians 15:55)

In its setting the taunt looks ahead to the day "when the perishable puts on the imperishable" (1 Corinthians 15:54). Christ's rising makes the victory certain; its fullness is still to come. So the Christian lives between two verses of the same chapter, the last enemy not yet destroyed and the taunt already on his lips, and Hebrews says Christ died precisely to free those who all their lives were enslaved by the fear of death (Hebrews 2:15).

Hold the two together and a distinction appears that the modern options pull apart. The person who clings to life at any cost treats death as the final evil, so every hour must be defended. The person who seizes control of death treats it as a solution, a door to open when the room becomes unbearable. Scripture calls death an enemy, which refuses the second, and a defeated one, which refuses the first. It is neither final nor friendly.

Paul lived inside that tension, and his letters let us watch. Writing from prison with his verdict still undecided, he tells the Philippians how he weighs the two outcomes.

> "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain." (Philippians 1:21)

> "I am hard pressed between the two. My desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better." (Philippians 1:23)

He goes on to say that remaining is more necessary for their sake. Paul isn't clutching his life, and he isn't choosing his death. The verdict belongs to a Roman court and, behind it, to God, and Paul is content either way because Christ is on both sides of the door. In what reads as his last letter, the waiting is over.

> "For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come." (2 Timothy 4:6)

> "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith." (2 Timothy 4:7)

> "Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day, and not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing." (2 Timothy 4:8)

The drink offering was wine poured over a sacrifice at the altar (Numbers 15). Paul reads his execution as an offering rather than a defeat, and as a departure, a word used for a ship loosing its moorings. And his last gesture is outward, toward everyone waiting for the same appearing.

Above every other death in the canon stands the death of Jesus, and Allen Verhey argued in *The Christian Art of Dying* (2011) that any Christian art of dying has to be learned there before anywhere else. Jesus doesn't pretend to want death. In the garden he asks for the cup to pass, and then he submits.

> "And he said, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.'" (Mark 14:36)

He doesn't cling: he tells Peter to put away his sword and declines the help he could summon (Matthew 26:52–53). And he doesn't seize. When he speaks of laying down his life of his own accord, he adds in the same breath that this is a charge from his Father (John 10:18), obedience offered for others and not an escape from pain. At the end he prays a line from a psalm.

> "Then Jesus, calling out with a loud voice, said, 'Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!' And having said this he breathed his last." (Luke 23:46)

The psalm is the prayer of a man surrounded by enemies who trusts God with his life anyway: "Into your hand I commit my spirit; you have redeemed me, O LORD, faithful God" (Psalm 31:5). Jesus adds one word, *Father*. The verb means to place something in another's keeping, as a deposit is entrusted to someone faithful. That is the shape of a Christian death. Not held onto, not taken. Handed over.

## Why letting die is not killing

If death is neither final nor friendly, then a Christian has permission to do something the "do everything" reflex forbids. He may stop. The Preacher says there is "a time to be born, and a time to die" (Ecclesiastes 3:2), and a faith built on an empty tomb has less reason than anyone to treat every death as a defeat to be resisted with every machine in the building. The tradition turned that permission into careful distinctions, worth knowing before you need them, because they are exactly what gets blurred in the conference room and the public argument alike.

The first is the difference between killing and allowing to die. The Methodist ethicist Paul Ramsey argued in *The Patient as Person* (1970) that once a patient is dying and treatment can no longer serve recovery, the duty changes shape into what he called only caring for the dying. The treatment may be abandoned; the patient never is. Pain is still treated, the body is still washed and turned, the person is still spoken to. What stops is the war against a death that has already come.

The strongest objection deserves a hearing. In 1975 the philosopher James Rachels argued in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, in "Active and Passive Euthanasia," that the distinction is morally empty. He imagined two men who each stand to inherit if a young cousin dies. One drowns the child in the bath; the other comes to do the same, finds the child has slipped and gone under, and simply watches, ready to push the head back down if need be. Nobody thinks the second man innocent. Rachels argued that killing and letting die therefore differ in no morally important way in themselves, and that where a doctor has already decided to let a patient die, a slow death by withdrawn treatment may be crueler than a quick one by injection.

Rachels is right about his two men, and his example shows why he is wrong about the hospital. Both men want the child dead. The Christian distinction was never about acting versus refraining; it's about what a person aims at and what actually causes the death. A family that stops a ventilator for a patient whose lungs won't recover is aiming at relief from a burden, and the disease ends the life. The test is what would count as success. When Karen Ann Quinlan's parents, devout Catholics reasoning from this tradition, won the right in 1976 to have her ventilator removed, she kept breathing and lived nine more years, and nobody's plan had failed. A lethal injection that left the patient alive would have failed completely. (By the same test, withholding insulin from a diabetic to be rid of him is killing.) In *Vacco v. Quill* (1997) the Supreme Court declined to erase the line, treating the difference between letting a patient die and making a patient die as well established in medicine and law.

The second distinction is between ordinary and extraordinary means. It reaches back to sixteenth-century Spanish Dominicans, Francisco de Vitoria among them, who asked whether a sick man was bound to undergo agonizing surgery or ruinously costly remedies, and answered no. On 24 November 1957, Pope Pius XII gave it modern form in an address to anesthesiologists who had asked him about the new techniques of resuscitation: a person is normally obliged, he said in substance, only to use means that don't impose a grave burden on himself or others. The Vatican's *Declaration on Euthanasia* (1980) refined this into the language of proportionate and disproportionate means, and Protestant ethicists such as Ramsey and Gilbert Meilaender reason along the same lines.

"Extraordinary" has nothing to do with how advanced the machine is. A ventilator can be ordinary care for a twenty-year-old after a car accident and a disproportionate burden for a ninety-year-old in the last stage of lung disease. What's weighed is a treatment's real prospect of benefit for this patient against its real burden. Meilaender's principle, in paraphrase, belongs in every hospital: we may ask whether a treatment is useless or excessively burdensome, but never whether a life is worth living. Kathryn Butler, a former trauma and critical care surgeon, brings this to the bedside in *Between Life and Death* (2019), explaining what resuscitation, ventilators and feeding tubes can and cannot do, so families don't choose between "everything" and "nothing" in ignorance. Most ethicists also hold that withholding a treatment and withdrawing it are morally equivalent, which means a time-limited trial, begun in hope and stopped when it fails, doesn't trap anyone.

The third is the principle of double effect. Thomas Aquinas, discussing self-defense in the *Summa Theologiae*, observed that one act can have two effects, one intended and the other outside the intention. Later moralists shaped this into conditions: the act must be good or neutral in itself, the bad effect foreseen but not intended and not the means to the good, and the reason proportionate. At the bedside it means morphine may be given to relieve pain or the terror of breathlessness even with some risk of shortening life, as Pius XII taught in an address on analgesia to Italian anesthesiologists in February 1957, nine months before the one on resuscitation. The aim is relief, never death.

The fourth question is the hardest, and here faithful Christians disagree. It concerns food and water given through a tube. One view, stated by John Paul II in March 2004 and argued by Meilaender for decades, holds that nutrition and hydration are basic care owed to every human being rather than medical treatment, and are obligatory as long as they nourish and relieve suffering; withdrawing them from someone not otherwise dying aims at death. The other view, held by many Protestant ethicists and physicians, treats tube feeding as a medical intervention with benefits and burdens like any other. In advanced dementia or the final days, the body often can't use nutrition, and a tube can add infection and aspiration without adding comfort; declining it there is allowing to die. Both agree no one may aim at death, and that at the very end feeding may give way to mouth care and sips for comfort. This is a second-order question that divides people who share the creeds, to be held with charity. The Terri Schiavo case in 2005 showed how fast a family's anguish can become a national political fight. A family facing it needs a physician and a pastor, not a cable panel.

## The strongest case for choosing the hour

If the tradition already permits stopping, what remains is the last step, a prescription written so a patient can end his own life. The case for it deserves to be stated as its defenders would. Many came to it at a bedside, watching someone they loved take weeks to die in suffering, and concluded that when medicine can only lengthen a dying, lengthening it is cruelty. Beneath the compassion lies a claim about ownership. Ronald Dworkin argued in *Life's Dominion* (1993) that people have critical interests in how their lives end, because death is the last chapter and shapes the meaning of the whole, and that forcing someone to die in a way that contradicts his deepest convictions, for the sake of convictions he doesn't share, is a form of tyranny. Behind it is a fear no honest person dismisses: ending among efficient strangers, wired to machines that pull you back from every exit.

Oregon's voters approved the Death with Dignity Act in 1994, and after court challenges and a failed repeal it took effect in 1997, permitting a physician to prescribe lethal medication to a competent adult with six months or less to live. The Supreme Court held in *Washington v. Glucksberg* (1997) that the Constitution guarantees no such right, leaving it to the states, and by 2026 twelve more states and the District of Columbia had followed Oregon. Oregon's own annual reports show that patients most often name loss of autonomy, a decreasing ability to take part in the activities that made life enjoyable, and loss of dignity as their end-of-life concerns, with fear of burdening family and caregivers common and inadequate pain control, or worry about it, further down the list. Canada legalized medical assistance in dying in 2016 for those whose deaths were reasonably foreseeable, and within five years removed that requirement.

The Christian answer begins where the disagreement actually lies, which is ownership. If my life is simply mine, Dworkin is very nearly unanswerable. Paul told the Corinthians, "You are not your own" (1 Corinthians 6:19), and he wrote to Rome in words that cover the deathbed as much as the marketplace:

> "For if we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord. So then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's." (Romans 14:8)

A gift is received, cared for and finally handed back; it isn't ours to dispose of. The same conviction frees us to stop burdensome treatment and forbids us to aim at death, which is why the Christian position isn't a compromise between the two panics but a different place to stand.

Oregon's reasons are chiefly not pain, which medicine can usually relieve, but loss of control, the loss of what made life enjoyable, and the fear of being a burden, which a community can answer and a lethal prescription only confirms. Disability advocates, many of them on the political left, founded Not Dead Yet in 1996 to say what Christians should have said louder: in a society that counts some lives as costly, a right to die slides toward a duty to die. The offer becomes a question an old woman asks herself about her children. I feel the pull of that fear. I have five sons, and I know that one day the father who carried them may need carrying, and the thought of costing them their years is the exact fear this argument is built on. The answer isn't to despise the fear. It's to refuse to let it write the ending, because being carried is not beneath a dignity I never supplied.

That answer is credible only from a church that does the carrying. Christians who fight these laws at the statehouse and then leave the dying and their exhausted caregivers alone have answered with half a gospel, and Christians who have quietly adopted the language of autonomy have forgotten whose they are. Both failures are ours. And a person who isn't dying but wants to die isn't facing a bioethics question at all. That is a wound that needs care now: call or text 988, or tell someone near you today.

## Learning the art before the hour

The better answer to a medicalized death was built by a Christian. In 1967 Cicely Saunders, a nurse turned physician, opened St Christopher's Hospice in London on the conviction that the dying suffer what she called total pain, of body, mind, relationships and spirit, and that all of it can be met even when the disease can't be cured. The United States created a Medicare hospice benefit in 1982. Palliative care, the broader discipline, can begin at any stage alongside treatment. When Jennifer Temel and colleagues randomly assigned 151 patients newly diagnosed with metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer to receive early palliative care alongside standard cancer care or standard care alone, in a trial published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2010, those given palliative care reported better quality of life and mood, received less aggressive care at the end, and, in a secondary finding, lived longer: a median of 11.6 months against 8.9. Asking for palliative care is not giving up.

Gawande's most practical counsel is to have the conversation early, at a kitchen table rather than in an ICU. The questions he learned from palliative clinicians are simple: what do you understand about where things stand, what are you most afraid of, what matters most to you, and what would you be willing, or unwilling, to go through for more time? Then put the answers in writing. A living will records your wishes, a health care power of attorney names who will speak for you, and the seriously ill can ask their physicians about portable medical orders. Laws differ by state, and none of this is legal or medical advice; your doctor and an attorney are the ones to help with the documents. Luther's counsel to settle your affairs so the family won't quarrel has aged well.

Many hope to die in their own bed with their family around them, as Jacob did, giving his last charge to his sons before he "breathed his last" (Genesis 49:33), and hospice makes dying at home possible for many. It isn't always possible or best, and an inpatient hospice can be a good place to die. What matters is that the dying aren't left alone. Here the church has work no hospital can do: meals, nights of relief for exhausted caregivers, Scripture read aloud, hymns sung to someone past speaking, the Lord's Supper brought to the bed, and children allowed in the room.

Then comes grief, which doesn't end at the funeral. Paul didn't forbid it; he wrote "that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). Christians grieve with hope, which is still grief. If it swallows the ability to live, see a doctor or counselor, and call or text 988 if thoughts of death come.

## Into your hands

Jeremy Taylor's warning stands over all of this: the last hour asks only what the life has practiced. For centuries the church has prayed the words of Psalm 31:5 every night at Compline, the office before sleep, so that the commendation Jesus spoke from the cross would be the most rehearsed prayer a Christian knew.

I came to faith from atheism, and the logic of that position is easy to state. If there is no one to receive us, there are really only two options, the two panics this essay began with: cling, because this is all there is, or take control, because no one else will. The third way depends entirely on someone being there to receive what we hand over. It stands or falls on an empty tomb outside Jerusalem.

So the art of dying begins tonight, with what you can do while well: the conversation you've postponed, the person who should speak for you, the grudge you meant to release, the prayer you'll want then, prayed now. Someday "into your hands" will be the last thing you say, or the last thing someone says over you. Whether the words sound like a stranger's or like your own will depend on how many nights you said them.
