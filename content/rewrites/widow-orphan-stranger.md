## Three people at the city gate

There is a man who sleeps under an overpass a few blocks from our church. I have driven past him on my way to preach about him. I know roughly where he'll be and I don't know his name. It's the honest place to begin, because the question of what the Bible says about widows, orphans and strangers is rarely a problem of interpretation. The problem is who we have trained ourselves not to see, and how comfortably a church can read these passages year after year without any of the people they name ever sitting down beside it.

Count them through the canon and the pattern is hard to miss. The widow, the fatherless child and the sojourner appear together in the covenant code of Exodus, again and again in Deuteronomy, in the Psalms, in the prophets from Isaiah to Malachi, and they are still there in the letter of James. Sometimes the poor or the Levite joins them, but the cluster returns too often to be coincidence. The philosopher Nicholas Wolterstorff, in *Justice: Rights and Wrongs* (2008), calls the group "the quartet of the vulnerable," counting the poor as its fourth member, and shows how often the Old Testament's calls for justice come back to them. They are the standing measure by which Israel's faithfulness is weighed.

That raises two questions the church usually skips. The first is historical: why these three? Israel had the sick, debtors and captives too, yet these three are named as a unit, and God attaches his own name to their cause in language he uses for almost no one else. The second question is ours: who stands in their places now, and why are so few of them in the room when we gather to read about them?

The answer to the first turns on a distinction between a patron and a kinsman. A patron protects the weak out of his own greatness; his kindness is real, but it flows downhill, and no one has a claim against him if he withdraws it. A kinsman protects the weak because they are his own; their loss is his loss, their shame falls on his name, and the duty doesn't wait on his mood. The ancient world was full of kings who styled themselves patrons of the widow and the orphan. Israel's Scriptures say something stranger: that God has made himself the next of kin of the people who have none, and has bound his whole people to the same kinship. On the Bible's terms the widow, the orphan and the stranger are not the church's charity cases. They are God's family, and whoever ignores them is dealing with their Redeemer.

## Why these three, and not others

The triad makes sense only inside an Israelite village. Israel was an agrarian society built on the household and the family plot. Land passed through the male line, and it was more than property; it was the household's place in the covenant people, the inheritance allotted to each clan. Legal standing ran through the same channels. Disputes were settled by the elders at the city gate, and the one who spoke there was the head of a household or a male kinsman acting for someone who couldn't. Christopher Wright, in *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* (2004), builds much of his account of Israel's economic law on this bond between God, the people and the land held in families. The system protected people by rooting them in a kin group with land of its own, which means the people most at risk were those who had fallen out of it or never belonged.

The widow had fallen out. For her, the Hebrew *'almanah*, a husband's death could mean losing access to the land, to the household's income and to anyone obliged to speak for her at the gate. If she had grown sons, they might carry her. If she didn't, or if her husband's family chose not to, she could find herself outside every structure that had held her. Naomi comes home from Moab saying she went away full and the LORD has brought her back empty (Ruth 1:21). That is grief, and it is also exposure.

The orphan often stood in the same house. The word *yatom* is better rendered fatherless, as the ESV usually has it; many of these children had a living mother, which is one reason the widow and the fatherless so often appear in the same breath. A fatherless boy had no one to defend his family's field, and a stronger neighbor could simply move the boundary stone. Proverbs imagines exactly that: "Do not move an ancient landmark or enter the fields of the fatherless" (Proverbs 23:10, ESV).

The sojourner, the *ger*, had never belonged to the arrangement at all. He was a foreigner settled among Israel, sometimes fleeing famine or war, living in the towns without an ancestral allotment and without the web of kin that made an Israelite safe. The Hebrew vocabulary for outsiders and the modern debate are treated at length in [What Does the Bible Say About Immigration and Refugees?](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-immigrants-and-refugees-in-depth). The point here is narrower. The sojourner belongs in the triad because, like the widow and the fatherless, he had no one at the gate.

So the three are chosen for what they lack, not for what they deserve. Nothing in the law suggests widows were more virtuous than wives or foreigners better than natives. The categories describe position, and that is why the covenant code's warning is so severe and so personal. "You shall not mistreat any widow or fatherless child." (Exodus 22:22, ESV). "If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry" (Exodus 22:23, ESV). "and my wrath will burn, and I will kill you with the sword, and your wives shall become widows and your children fatherless." (Exodus 22:24, ESV). The symmetry is terrible. The man who exploits a widow because she has no protector will learn what it is to leave one behind. The law doesn't appeal to his generosity. It tells him someone has taken up her cause.

The verse just before sets the stranger in the same place: "You shall not wrong a sojourner or oppress him, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt." (Exodus 22:21, ESV). The cry God hears from the widow is the cry he heard from Israel's brick kilns, when he told Moses at the bush that he had seen his people's affliction and heard their cry (Exodus 3:7). Israel knows what it is to have no one at the gate, because it once had no one but God.

## What Israel's neighbors said, and what Israel said instead

A skeptical reader will object here, and the objection deserves its full weight. Concern for the widow and the orphan was not Israel's invention. It was common coin across the ancient Near East, and some of the texts are centuries older than Moses. Around the twenty-fourth century before Christ, Uruinimgina (older books call him Urukagina), ruler of the Sumerian city of Lagash, recorded reforms and said he had pledged to his city's god not to hand the orphan and the widow over to the powerful. The prologue to the laws of Ur-Nammu, around 2100 BC, boasts that the orphan was not delivered to the rich man nor the widow to the mighty. Hammurabi of Babylon, in the eighteenth century BC, says in his prologue that the gods called him to make justice appear in the land so that the strong would not oppress the weak, and in his epilogue that he set up his inscribed stone so that the orphan and the widow might have justice. The Egyptian tale of the Eloquent Peasant has its hero praise a high official as father to the orphan and husband to the widow. In the Ugaritic tale of Aqhat the just ruler Danel sits at the gate judging the widow's case and the orphan's cause, and in the epic of Keret a son tries to push his ailing father off the throne for no longer doing so.

F. Charles Fensham gathered this material in a well-known article, "Widow, Orphan, and the Poor in Ancient Near Eastern Legal and Wisdom Literature" (1962), and concluded that protecting these groups was common policy across the region, a virtue expected of gods, kings and judges alike. Moshe Weinfeld, in *Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East* (1995), argued that Israel's paired phrase "justice and righteousness" answers to a similar Mesopotamian pair, and that royal edicts of release stand in the background of Israel's own laws of release. So the skeptic is right as far as he goes. Israel spoke a moral language its neighbors understood, and the Bible's defenders gain nothing by pretending otherwise. The conscience that recoils at a widow being robbed isn't the private property of Israel or the church.

But notice who is speaking in those older texts. Nearly all of them are royal. Protecting the widow is a king's boast carved on a monument, a quality a courtier praises in his lord, a test of whether an old king is still fit to reign. It is the patron's virtue, and it legitimates the patron's power. Whether any widow in Lagash or Babylon was actually helped the inscriptions don't say. What they announce is that the king is great enough to be kind.

Israel's law takes that inherited language and moves it in three directions. The first is upward. In Deuteronomy the widow's defender is God himself, named in the middle of the book's grandest description of him: "For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, the mighty, and the awesome God, who is not partial and takes no bribe." (Deuteronomy 10:17, ESV). Then, with no change of tone: "He executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner, giving him food and clothing." (Deuteronomy 10:18, ESV). The Lord of lords is described by what he does for a fatherless boy. When Israel got kings, they stood under this standard rather than being its source. Psalm 68 makes the move in worship: "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation." (Psalm 68:5, ESV). The God enthroned in his sanctuary takes the title the Egyptian peasant gave a steward.

The second move is outward, from the throne into every household's economy. "You shall not pervert the justice due to the sojourner or to the fatherless, or take a widow's garment in pledge" (Deuteronomy 24:17, ESV). The forgotten sheaf, the olives left on the branch and the grapes left on the vine follow in the next verses, each assigned to the same three people (Deuteronomy 24:19–21). The triad is now in the farmer's field, the lender's pledge and the court's verdict, and at the covenant ceremony the whole people say amen to a curse on themselves: "'Cursed be anyone who perverts the justice due to the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow.' And all the people shall say, 'Amen.'" (Deuteronomy 27:19, ESV). The pairing of widow and orphan was familiar across the region. Deuteronomy fastens the resident foreigner to them again and again, so that the one outside the bloodline shares the protection of those inside it.

The third move is inward, and no royal inscription could make it. Israel's motive is memory rather than greatness. "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt; therefore I command you to do this." (Deuteronomy 24:22, ESV). The king helps the weak from above. The Israelite is told to help them as someone who has been one of them.

Beneath all three lies the word that turns patronage into kinship. In Israel's family law the *go'el*, the redeemer, was the nearest male relative, whose duty was to buy back a kinsman's lost land, redeem him from debt slavery and stand up for him when no one else would. How that office worked in Leviticus 25 and in Ruth is traced in [the essay on the Jubilee](/writing/jubilee-debt-and-the-economy-god-commanded). What matters here is that Scripture gives the title to God, first at the exodus: "I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with great acts of judgment." (Exodus 6:6, ESV). The verb is the kinsman's verb. Then Proverbs finishes its warning about the fatherless child's field: "for their Redeemer is strong; he will plead their cause against you." (Proverbs 23:11, ESV). The Hebrew is their *go'el*. The boy whose father is dead has a kinsman after all, and the neighbor eyeing his boundary stone has picked a fight with God.

That is the difference, and it isn't small. A patron can be generous. Only a kinsman is wronged in the person of the one he protects. When Scripture calls God the widow's redeemer, it means an injury to her is an injury to his own household, and that is why the warnings about her carry a heat the inscriptions never do.

## From the prophets to the letter of James

The rest of the Old Testament keeps the ledger open. The Psalms sing the triad as a description of God: "The LORD watches over the sojourners; he upholds the widow and the fatherless, but the way of the wicked he brings to ruin." (Psalm 146:9, ESV). The prophets use it to test whether Israel's worship means anything (Isaiah 1:17; Malachi 3:5). The fuller case that biblical justice reaches courts and statutes as well as hearts is made in [What Does the Bible Say About Poverty, and Is It Political?](/writing/is-poverty-political-the-bibles-answer).

One prophetic text deserves slower attention, because it was spoken to people who had already lost everything once. Zechariah preached to the community back from Babylon, and some of them asked whether they should keep the fasts that mourned Jerusalem's fall. God's answer was to remind them what the earlier prophets had said before the fall. "Thus says the LORD of hosts, Render true judgments, show kindness and mercy to one another" (Zechariah 7:9, ESV). "do not oppress the widow, the fatherless, the sojourner, or the poor, and let none of you devise evil against another in your heart." (Zechariah 7:10, ESV). Then the history: "But they refused to pay attention and turned a stubborn shoulder and stopped their ears that they might not hear." (Zechariah 7:11, ESV). In Zechariah's reading the exile came to a people who kept the calendar and forgot the widow, and the lesson of the ruins is the triad.

When the Redeemer comes in person, he acts as the kinsman the law described. At the gate of a town called Nain, Jesus meets a widow following the bier of her only son, which means she is burying her last protector (Luke 7:11–17). Nobody asks him to act. He has compassion on her, raises the young man and, in words that echo Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:23), gives him back to his mother. He gives her back her household. The same Jesus warned against religious leaders "who devour widows' houses and for a pretense make long prayers. They will receive the greater condemnation." (Mark 12:40, ESV). And on the night he was betrayed he made the orphan's promise to his own: "I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you." (John 14:18, ESV). He does not help the fatherless from outside. Fatherlessness is the condition he rescues all his people from.

Then comes James. "Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained from the world." (James 1:27, ESV). The verb translated *visit* carries more than a social call. It is the word Zechariah the priest used when his son John was born: "Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for he has visited and redeemed his people" (Luke 1:68, ESV). In the Greek Old Testament it is a common word for God coming to look upon his people. So when James says pure religion is to visit the widow and the orphan, he asks the church to do toward them what God did toward Israel: come near, look closely, take up their cause as a kinsman would. And he joins it to keeping oneself unstained from the world, a stain that in his letter includes seating the rich man in the good place and telling the poor man to stand (James 2:1–4). James won't let a church choose between personal holiness and care for the exposed. The two halves of the verse are one discipline.

## What the first churches built

The earliest church began, almost at once, to organize around the triad. Its first recorded internal crisis is about widows. "Now in these days when the disciples were increasing in number, a complaint by the Hellenists arose against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution." (Acts 6:1, ESV). The verse assumes there already was a daily distribution. And the neglected widows were the Greek-speaking ones, the women furthest from the church's center of gravity, which is where neglect always settles. The apostles didn't treat the complaint as a distraction from the word of God. They created an office and filled it with seven men whose names are all Greek, very likely putting the care of the overlooked into the hands of people from the overlooked group.

A generation later the practice needed rules. "Honor widows who are truly widows." (1 Timothy 5:3, ESV). The church kept a roll of widows it supported, and Paul limits it to women at least sixty, known for good works, with no family able to carry them. He is just as firm that families come first. "But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever." (1 Timothy 5:8, ESV). "If any believing woman has relatives who are widows, let her care for them. Let the church not be burdened, so that it may care for those who are truly widows." (1 Timothy 5:16, ESV).

That passage carries the strongest form of an objection many conservative Christians feel in their bones, and it deserves a fair hearing. Paul doesn't want the church to replace the family or generosity to become a way for relatives to shed their duties. Care too easily given can dissolve the bonds it means to honor, and a church that forgets this will build programs that quietly teach sons they needn't look after their mothers. But notice how the passage ends: the limits exist "so that it may care for those who are truly widows." Paul restrains the church's care in order to concentrate it on the most exposed, not to excuse it.

The generations after the apostles kept the triad at the center of their self-description. Around 110 Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to die in Rome, told the young bishop Polycarp not to let the widows be neglected and to be, after the Lord, their protector. That is the theology of the *go'el* in a phrase: God is the widow's first kinsman, and the bishop stands second. In the first half of the second century the Athenian apologist Aristides told the emperor that Christians don't turn away the widow, that they rescue the orphan from whoever treats him harshly, and that when they see a stranger they take him under their roof and rejoice over him as a brother. Around 155 Justin Martyr explained that the money collected at the Sunday gathering went to the one who presided, who used it to help orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and the strangers staying among them. All three appear in one sentence describing ordinary worship. Tertullian's *Apology* (around 197) lists boys and girls without means or parents, and old people confined to their houses, among those the common fund supported.

Adolf von Harnack surveyed this evidence in *The Mission and Expansion of Christianity in the First Three Centuries* (1902) and counted this organized love among the forces behind the church's spread. On this point, whatever one makes of his liberal theology, the sources bear him out. The first Christians did not run a program for the vulnerable. They were a household that had taken the vulnerable in. The forms grew more institutional over the centuries, and some orphanages became places of real harm that later generations were right to close. What held was the conviction that a church which calls God the Father of the fatherless can't leave the fatherless outside.

## Who stands at the gate now

A fair objection waits here. Israel's categories were agrarian, and ours are not. Many widows now have Social Security, the state takes custody of children with no one to raise them, and a foreigner can hire a lawyer. Stretch the words far enough and they mean nothing. Some of that is true, and it's a useful discipline. But the law never gave Israel a complete list of who matters. It named the people with no land to fall back on and no one to speak for them at the gate, and every society produces its own. The test is standing, not sentiment.

Apply it and the faces come quickly. The widow is often the elderly woman whose income is adequate and whose days are empty, who no longer drives and whose children live three states away. In 2023 the Surgeon General, Vivek Murthy, issued an advisory, *Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation*, treating social disconnection as a public health concern rather than a private sorrow. Her poverty is of presence, which is exactly what James's verb asks the church to supply. The fatherless are the children in foster care, and the young people who age out of it with no family behind them. They are also the grown children who carry the absence. I was raised without a father, and I know something of growing up with no one to speak for me in the rooms where decisions got made about my future. The stranger is the refugee family whose paperwork is in order and whose isolation is not. And the man under the overpass has no gate at all.

Most of them are not in our pews, and not by accident. The service time, the dress, the small talk of the coffee hour, the assumption of a car and a stable job all tell the vulnerable, accurately, whom the room was built for. Donald McGavran observed in *Understanding Church Growth* (1970) that people like to become Christians without crossing barriers of race, language or class. His concern was missionary and honorable: that no one should have to cross a cultural barrier the gospel doesn't require. A generation of church planters turned the observation into a strategy. The essay on [building a multiethnic church](/writing/building-the-multiethnic-church) weighs that principle fairly. Applied to class, its fruit is plain enough: congregations of the comfortable, reaching people like themselves and wondering why the widow and the man with the cart never come. The host in Jesus's parable did not wait for them to come. "Go out quickly to the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor and crippled and blind and lame." (Luke 14:21, ESV).

Both of our political instincts find ways around the visit. One keeps the vulnerable at the distance of a donation, treats talk of justice as suspect and the stranger as a threat. The other turns the triad into a platform plank and lets a vote discharge the debt, speaking of the vulnerable constantly and knowing none of them by name. The difference between giving and owing is worked out in [Charity vs. Justice](/writing/charity-is-not-justice-the-difference). What both instincts share is that neither has to cross the room. And the churches I have led have been easier for the comfortable to enter than for the vulnerable, in ways I didn't always see and in ways I saw and left alone because changing them would have cost more than I was ready to pay. I have known that for years, and I have mostly not said it from the pulpit.

## Their Redeemer is strong

Proverbs meant its sentence as a warning, and it hasn't expired: "for their Redeemer is strong; he will plead their cause against you" (Proverbs 23:11, ESV). The widow, the orphan and the stranger are not left to our kindness. They have a kinsman, and he is keeping the record.

He also kept the promise. At the cross, with his own breath running out, Jesus looked down at his mother. The Gospels never say that Joseph had died. He simply disappears from the story after Jesus's boyhood, and the church has long inferred from that silence, and from this scene, that Mary was a widow by then. "When Jesus saw his mother and the disciple whom he loved standing nearby, he said to his mother, 'Woman, behold, your son!'" (John 19:26, ESV). "Then he said to the disciple, 'Behold, your mother!' And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home." (John 19:27, ESV). The dying son acted as her kinsman and gave her into the keeping of his disciple. And by that same death the Son did for us what the *go'el* did for a ruined brother. "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4, ESV). "to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons." (Galatians 4:5, ESV). Every Christian is an orphan who was adopted and a stranger who was brought in, and none of us found the door on our own.

That is why the triad can't be filed under outreach. A church that forgets the widow has forgotten its own rescue, and repentance starts there, with confession before strategy and a willingness to be changed by the people we go to find. The Redeemer still goes out to the gate at Nain. The question is whether his household will follow him there.

The man under the overpass will likely be there again tonight. I still don't know his name. The text has done its work, and it will keep asking, every time we read it aloud in a warm room, who is missing from the room and whether we mean to go and find him.
