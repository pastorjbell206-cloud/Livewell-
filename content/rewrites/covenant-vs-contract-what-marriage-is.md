Almost nobody who ends a marriage says it became wicked. What people say, and usually say honestly, is that they aren't happy anymore, that they've grown apart, that the marriage stopped giving them what it once gave. They are rarely lying about their own interior weather. What almost no one examines is the account of marriage that makes the report sound like a verdict. If a marriage exists to make two people happy, one that no longer does has failed at its one task, and a sensible person walks away from a failed instrument. The logic is clean. The question is whether the premise is true.

Couples argue about money and chores and whose family gets Christmas, but rarely about what kind of thing a marriage is, because they assume they already agree. Often they don't. Two people can stand at the front of a church, say identical words, and mean different documents. One is making a covenant, a binding of two lives under a promise sworn before God that doesn't depend on the benefit. The other is signing a contract, an agreement for mutual benefit that may be dissolved when the benefit fails. On every easy day the two look the same. They only come apart in the dark.

I should say where I'm standing. I'm a pastor, married to Susanna, and we're raising five sons. I also came to faith out of atheism, and I haven't forgotten how reasonable the contract view sounds from outside, or how often the church's talk of covenant sounded like a way of keeping people in rooms they had every right to leave. The Christian claim about marriage is very large, the history of its misuse is real, and I won't defend the first by pretending the second away.

## The case for the contract

Start with the other side in the form its best defenders would sign. For most of Western history, the argument runs, the language of permanent union didn't protect women; it absorbed them. When William Blackstone summarized English law in his *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (1765), he described the doctrine of coverture in words that sound almost like a sermon on Genesis: "By marriage, the husband and wife are one person in law: that is, the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage." That is "one flesh" turned into legal machinery, and what it meant in practice, in England and in the American states that inherited its common law, was that a married woman generally could not hold property in her own name, make contracts, or keep her own wages. The unbreakable bond was a real cage, and pulpits defended it.

The contract view, on this account, isn't a moral decline. It's a moral repair. Two adults enter freely, set the terms of their life together, and keep the dignity of exit. Consent is what gives the union its worth, and a love that couldn't leave wouldn't be love at all but custody. Add the pluralist argument: a state whose citizens disagree about God can't enforce a theological account of marriage, so the law has to treat it as an arrangement between consenting adults. And add the argument from experience. Many people have left cruel or hollow marriages and found life on the other side, and a theology that can only call their leaving a failure hasn't met them.

Robert Bellah and his colleagues gave the underlying sensibility a name in *Habits of the Heart* (1985). They called it expressive individualism, the conviction that each person has a unique core of feeling that must be expressed if a life is to be real, and they heard it in the way Americans described love and marriage: a relationship is good insofar as it serves the growth of the selves inside it. It is not shallow; it is serious about honesty and the refusal to live a lie.

I grant most of it. There are marriages that ought to end, and a theology that can't say so has made an idol of an institution and fed people to it. Covenant worth the name protects the vulnerable before the institution. What I don't grant is the conclusion, because the contract offered as the cure doesn't deliver the dignity it promises.

## What a contract protects, and what a covenant binds

A contract exists to protect each party from the other. That is its genius. When I sign one, I'm preparing for the day the other party fails me, specifying in advance what I'm owed and what I'll do if I don't get it. Every contract is written with one eye on the exit, and in commerce that is exactly right. But a contract keeps the parties two. It marks where you end and I begin, and it guards the self. A marriage built on that logic will keep two selves intact and adjacent, each watching the ledger, each checking whether the benefit still exceeds the cost. When such a marriage ends, the contract hasn't failed. It kept two people safe from each other right up to the moment it released them from each other. What it promised on top of that, intimacy with an exit, is a contradiction, because intimacy is the surrender of exactly the self-protection a contract exists to preserve.

A covenant runs on another logic, and Scripture shows it in one of the strangest scenes in Genesis. God has promised Abram land and descendants, and Abram asks how he can know. At God's command he cuts animals in two and lays the halves opposite each other. The Bible explains the rite itself centuries later, when Jeremiah warns the men of Jerusalem who broke their oath that God will "make them like the calf that they cut in two and passed between its parts" (Jeremiah 34:18, ESV). To walk that path was to call down on yourself the fate of the animals if you broke your word. The Hebrew idiom for making a covenant, *karat berit*, is literally to cut one. But in Genesis 15 Abram doesn't walk. "As the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram" (Genesis 15:12, ESV), and then, "When the sun had gone down and it was dark, behold, a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces" (Genesis 15:17, ESV). God passes through alone. He takes the self-curse on himself, and the covenant rests on his faithfulness before Abram has done a single thing to keep it.

That is the distinction this whole essay turns on. A contract is an exchange of goods between parties who remain separate and are protected against each other's failure. A covenant is an exchange of selves, sworn before a witness, in which the one who swears accepts the cost of keeping faith when the other does not. A contract says, "I will, as long as you do." A covenant says, "I will," and calls God to hear it.

Marriage enters Scripture before the word covenant is ever attached to it. "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him" (Genesis 2:18, ESV). The man names the animals and finds among them no counterpart, and when the woman is made he speaks the first poetry in the Bible: "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (Genesis 2:23, ESV). Then the narrator steps back from the scene to say what it means: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24, ESV). The verb for hold fast, *dabaq*, is the one Deuteronomy uses for Israel clinging to the LORD (Deuteronomy 10:20) and the one used when Ruth clings to Naomi (Ruth 1:14). It's the language of loyalty, not appetite. And in a world where a man's father's house was his economy, his name and his protection, the text subordinates even that loyalty to the new bond. The covenant begins with deliberate vulnerability; the contract begins by keeping every protection in place.

"One flesh" belongs to the same grammar. The Hebrew *basar* doesn't name the body as opposed to the soul; it names the whole creaturely person in his frailty. Two whole lives become a single new thing, a union that includes sex without being reducible to it. The next verse tells you what the union was for: "And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25, ESV). The covenant was the place where exposure could be safe. After the fall, the first thing the man and woman do is sew coverings, and every marriage since has been partly the long labor of taking them off.

The later Scriptures name what Genesis enacts. Proverbs warns against the woman "who forsakes the companion of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17, ESV), and Malachi indicts the men of Judah on the same ground: "the LORD was witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been faithless, though she is your companion and your wife by covenant" (Malachi 2:14, ESV). Notice who is in the room. A contract has two parties. A marriage, in Scripture, has a witness, and the witness is God. When the Pharisees test Jesus on divorce, he goes back behind Moses' concession, "from the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8, ESV), and names the true agent of the union: "So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matthew 19:6, ESV). The couple consents. God joins.

## From sacrament to contract

We didn't arrive at the contract in 1969. The movement took sixteen centuries, and each stage answered a real problem, which is why it's so hard to see.

Augustine wrote *De bono coniugali*, On the Good of Marriage, around 401, to defend marriage as a genuine good against those whose zeal for virginity made it sound like a concession to weakness. He named three goods of marriage: *proles*, offspring; *fides*, fidelity, the keeping of the marriage bed; and *sacramentum*, the sacred bond itself, which for Augustine held even when the spouses separated, so that neither could remarry while the other lived. He prized the companionship of husband and wife too, even in old age, when children are no longer possible. But happiness is not one of the goods. The goods are things a marriage *is* and *does*, not feelings it supplies. For more than a thousand years, that list was the Western grammar of marriage.

The medieval church kept the grammar and sharpened it. By the twelfth century the theologians of the schools counted marriage among the seven sacraments, and canon law settled a principle that matters more than it looks: consent makes a marriage. The couple's free exchange of vows, not the priest or the parents, created the bond. So the contract view's great truth, that marriage must be freely chosen, is older than modernity. The difference lay in what consent was thought to create: a bond no longer under the couple's control. The Council of Trent, in its twenty-fourth session in November 1563, defined marriage as one of the seven sacraments and, in the decree *Tametsi*, required that marriages be celebrated before a parish priest and witnesses, ending the secret marriages that left abandoned spouses with no proof they had ever been wed.

The Reformers broke the sacramental system and, with it, the church courts' hold on marriage. Luther argued in *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) that marriage is not a sacrament, and he came to treat it as a worldly estate under the civil authority rather than canon law. That was no demotion. In *The Estate of Marriage* (1522) he defended married life as God's own ordinance, holier in its ordinary duties than the monastery, and in 1525 he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun. Calvin and the Reformed tradition spoke of marriage as a covenant God himself witnesses, Malachi's word. John Witte Jr. traced what followed in *From Sacrament to Contract* (1997): Western marriage law passed through sacramental, Lutheran social, Reformed covenantal and Anglican commonwealth models before the Enlightenment recast it as a private contract between consenting individuals. The Reformers didn't make that last move. But once marriage belonged to the state, it could be redescribed in whatever terms the state preferred, and the modern state came to prefer contract.

Then love arrived as a revolution. Stephanie Coontz argues in *Marriage, a History* (2005) that for most of recorded history marriage was chiefly about property, alliance, labor and heirs, and that the ideal of marrying for love, with marriage at the center of emotional life, spread only in the late eighteenth century. Her most unsettling point is that the ideal which made marriage more satisfying made it more fragile. Once love was the reason for marrying, the absence of love became a reason for leaving. Industrialization pulled production out of the household, and the home became a refuge from the market, what Christopher Lasch called, in the title of his 1977 book, a haven in a heartless world, charged with supplying all the warmth the economy had drained from daily life. Wendell Berry, in "Feminism, the Body, and the Machine" in *What Are People For?* (1990), mourns what that did to marriage: a household that makes nothing together leaves a couple little to hold them but their feelings, and feelings were never meant to carry that load alone.

Andrew Cherlin, in *The Marriage-Go-Round* (2009), tracks the last stages: after the companionate marriage of the mid-century came an individualized marriage, judged by whether it serves each person's growth, with the spouse expected to be lover, best friend and confidant at once. Americans, Cherlin argues, hold a high ideal of marriage and a high ideal of personal fulfillment at full strength at once, and the collision produces a pattern of marrying, divorcing and re-partnering faster than in comparable nations. The soulmate model is its popular form. It asks one mortal to be what a village, a vocation and a God used to be.

The law caught up rather than led. California's Family Law Act, signed by Governor Ronald Reagan in 1969 and effective in 1970, allowed divorce on the ground of irreconcilable differences, without proof of fault. The reformers had honest motives: fault-based courts had become theaters of perjury, where couples staged or invented adultery to satisfy the statute. Nearly every state followed within about fifteen years; New York, the last, in 2010. Louisiana tried to legislate the older idea back in 1997 with an optional legal status called covenant marriage, and Arizona and Arkansas followed. Few have chosen it. A statute can raise the cost of leaving. It cannot make the promise.

Then came *Obergefell v. Hodges* in 2015, in which the Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires the states to license and recognize marriages between two people of the same sex. Christians disagree about what the civil law ought to be in a pluralist republic, and that question deserves careful argument. But whatever one thinks of the holding, its reasoning is revealing. The final paragraph of Justice Kennedy's opinion opens with the sentence, "No union is more profound than marriage, for it embodies the highest ideals of love, fidelity, devotion, sacrifice, and family." The words are noble. The account of marriage beneath them, a union defined by the couple's love and commitment, was not invented in 2015. It had been the working definition in American law, and in practice in a great many churches, for decades, built by men and women in the church as much as outside it, people who believed the covenant language at the altar and lived the contract at home.

The verdict of the history cuts in every direction. The conservative church that dates the fall of marriage to 2015 has to account for the divorces in its own pews and its quiet acceptance of the soulmate model. The progressive church that dissolved covenant into affirmation has to account for what it subtracted. We didn't lose marriage in a courtroom. We lost it at our own kitchen tables, when we began to believe marriage was for the self and kept the old words for the ceremony.

## The vow and the stranger

The shift is buried inside one word. A vow is an odd kind of speech. When you vow, you are not predicting your future feelings. You are binding your future self in advance, against the day your feelings argue otherwise, precisely because you know they will. The traditional vows say so bluntly: for better, for worse, in sickness and in health. We hear poetry, but those are terms, addressed to the diagnosis, the lost job, the year you don't like each other. The vow exists for the day the contract would have ended. A preference, unlike a vow, is revisable the moment it stops preferring, and once marriage became a preference, its ending no longer required any explanation at all.

Stanley Hauerwas put the problem in a sentence he was willing to call a law: we always marry the wrong person. He made the point in the *Christian Century* in 1978, and it wasn't cynicism. No one can know, at the altar, who they are marrying, because neither person is finished, and the one you wake beside in year fifteen is someone the one at the altar never met. A contract assumes you can know your counterparty and price the deal. A covenant admits you can't and binds you to the stranger anyway, which is the only way two people are ever really changed. The old captivity was the inability to leave. The new captivity is the inability to stay.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw this from a prison cell in Berlin. In May 1943 he wrote a sermon for the wedding of his niece Renate Schleicher and his friend Eberhard Bethge, a wedding he could not attend, and in it he wrote the sentence that ought to be read at every Christian wedding: "It is not your love that sustains the marriage, but from now on, the marriage that sustains your love." That reverses the modern assumption exactly. We think the feeling holds up the bond. Bonhoeffer says the bond, the vow God witnesses, holds up the feeling through the seasons when it can't hold itself.

Timothy Keller develops the same claim in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011), written with his wife Kathy. He argues that in Scripture the promise comes first and love grows inside its protection, and that the deepest thing a marriage offers is to be fully known and still fully loved, which is a small picture of being loved by God. Only a covenant makes room for that, because only a covenant tells the other person that the worst thing they reveal will not cancel the bond. Here the contract view's great value, consent, finds its true place. Consent is the door into marriage, not the house. The freedom of the one who vows is not lost when the vow binds him. It is spent, given away on purpose, the way God freely binds himself in Genesis 15, and a freedom that was never spent on anything has never loved anyone.

None of us gets to point at this from outside. Most married people keep a ledger in small ways and on ordinary evenings, measuring what they've given against what they think they're owed, and the ones who insist they don't are usually keeping it most carefully. The ledger is the contract living quietly inside a covenant. It appears as the silence after an argument that neither person will break first, as the fair division of chores that has replaced any gift, as a marriage that stays technically compliant while two people become strangers in the same bed. And we pastors have often made it worse, spending premarital hours on budgets and communication skills while barely explaining what a vow is, or who is listening when it's made.

## When the covenant is broken

All of this can be turned into a weapon, and has been. So, plainly: a covenant is not a sentence to endure abuse, and nothing in Scripture or in this essay requires anyone to stay in a home where they are being harmed. The spouse who beats, terrorizes or controls by fear has already broken the covenant, and shattered the one flesh long before the other said a word about it. To tell a battered spouse that the vow obliges them to remain inside the violence is the institution feeding on the person it was meant to shelter, and the church has sometimes done exactly that and called it faithfulness. If you are in danger, safety comes first. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, and if you are thinking of ending your life, call or text 988. A person in that situation also needs an advocate and often a lawyer; nothing here is legal advice.

Beyond the question of danger, the grounds for divorce and remarriage are contested among faithful Christians, and this is a second-order question. It divides churches that share the creeds; it doesn't divide the faith. The permanence view, rooted in Augustine, holds that the bond endures until death, so that separation may be permitted but not remarriage. The historic Protestant view, stated in the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), holds that adultery, and such willful desertion as cannot be remedied, dissolve the bond and free the innocent party to remarry, reading Matthew 19:9 and 1 Corinthians 7:15 together. A third reading, argued by David Instone-Brewer in *Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible* (2002), sets those texts against their Jewish background, including the duties of Exodus 21:10–11, and concludes that material and emotional neglect and abuse also break the covenant.

The argument of this essay leads toward the Reformation view, with persistent abuse read as a form of desertion, the abuser having abandoned the covenant from inside the house. The strongest objection to that conclusion, and to the third view more so, comes from the disciples themselves. When Jesus finished answering the Pharisees, they said to him, "If such is the case of a man with his wife, it is better not to marry" (Matthew 19:10, ESV). His teaching struck them as harder than any rabbi's, and every exception we add risks domesticating a shock he meant them to feel. Nothing here fully answers that. A Christian who holds the permanence view is still a brother or sister, and is guarding something true.

What the three views share matters more than where they part. None treats marriage as a contract. Each asks what breaks a covenant, not what ends a contract. A contract ends when the benefit fails. The covenant is broken only by betrayal, and even then Scripture grieves it as a death rather than filing it as a default.

## What the marriage was always pointing at

The Christian claim is not finally that covenant works better. It is that marriage was never mainly about the two people in it, and the prophets saw it first. God told Hosea, "Go again, love a woman who is loved by another man and is an adulteress, even as the LORD loves the children of Israel, though they turn to other gods and love cakes of raisins" (Hosea 3:1, ESV). A prophet's broken marriage became the sermon, and the sermon was that God does not leave: "I will betroth you to me forever" (Hosea 2:19, ESV). Jeremiah names the covenant at Sinai as one "that they broke, though I was their husband, declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 31:32, ESV). The whole history of Israel is a marriage kept by one party.

Paul takes the one flesh of Genesis and opens it all the way. "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25, ESV). Then he quotes Genesis 2:24 and says what it was about from the start: "This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32, ESV). God's covenant is the original, and every marriage is a small enacted picture of it. The smoking fire pot passing alone between the pieces was a promise, and the promise was kept on a cross, where the one who swore took the curse of the broken covenant on himself. "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). He wrote no contract listing the conditions of his departure. He held fast, all the way down.

This takes an impossible weight off marriage as well as laying a holy one on it. Jesus said that "in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage" (Matthew 22:30, ESV), and the last book of the Bible ends with a wedding, "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and his Bride has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7, ESV). Every human marriage is a sign that will give way to the thing it signifies. The soulmate model asks a spouse to be heaven. The gospel says your spouse is a fellow sinner who points toward it, which is far more than a contract could hold.

So the cross works inside an actual marriage as a pattern practiced on an ordinary Tuesday. You swore to hold fast, and holding fast will cost you the self-protection you've spent your life building. There will be mornings it asks of you a death you don't feel like dying. Repentance is the only door through it: confessing the ledger, apologizing without waiting for the other person's apology, turning back toward a spouse you had quietly begun to treat as a counterparty. No grace skips that. But the God who asks you to die to yourself in your marriage died first, and he does not ask you to walk a path he refused to walk. Resurrection is on the far side of it, which is why couples who have come through a real death together often carry a gravity the easy marriages don't, and it doesn't come from their own strength.

Most of us made a promise we didn't understand. That was never the failure. A covenant is always larger than the people who make it, and the understanding comes slowly, usually on the day it costs what you were sure it never would. The failure is what we did afterward, shrinking the covenant down to the size of a contract we could manage. A contract asks what you're owed. A covenant asks what you swore, and before whom. That second question will be waiting in your kitchen tonight, and it doesn't ask whether you're happy. It asks whether the One you made the promise before has ever, even once, walked out on you, and what it would mean to keep faith the way he has.
