## The question in the hospital room

The question of miracles rarely arrives as philosophy. It arrives in a hospital corridor in the week between the scan and the results, when someone who hasn't prayed in twenty years finds himself bargaining with a God he isn't sure is there. It arrives when a friend forwards a video from a crusade on another continent and asks, half hoping and half embarrassed, whether any of it is real. And it arrives, most painfully, in families who were told by a confident preacher that the cancer would retreat if they believed hard enough, who believed as hard as they knew how, and who buried the person anyway. They now carry a second grief on top of the first: the quiet suspicion that somebody's faith was too small, and that it may have been theirs.

I have prayed for healing beside hospital beds and watched it not come, and I have watched things I cannot explain. I hold both, and this essay is written from between them. Few words carry more baggage. For the skeptic, "miracle" summons credulous crowds, faith healers and the suspension of rational thought, a man in a white suit pushing people backward into the arms of ushers. For the believer it summons parted seas and an empty tomb. Both pictures need setting down long enough to ask the real question, and anyone who has stood in that corridor knows how badly people want God to act there, and how much pressure that longing can put on the truth.

Whether a reasonable person can believe in miracles at all, after David Hume argued in 1748 that no testimony could ever establish one, is taken up at length in [Can a Reasonable Person Believe in Miracles?](/writing/are-miracles-believable). This essay starts where that one ends. If the door isn't locked from the inside, what have Christians actually believed about what comes through it? Three questions follow, usually confused with one another. What were the miracles of Jesus for? Does God still work them, and if so, how should a church expect them? And what does the church owe the person who prayed and wasn't healed?

Most of us carry an unexamined answer to the first question, and it quietly governs the other two. We picture a miracle as a credential, a flash of divine identification that ends an argument: look what I can do, so believe me. Once a miracle is a credential, a church can be proud of it and put it on display, which is how the healing line and the offering bucket end up side by side. Or it can be ashamed of it and quietly stop presenting it, which is how you get the congregation that recites the Creed on Sunday and would be alarmed if God did anything on Monday. One is greedy and the other is embarrassed, and both have misread what the Gospels say a miracle is.

## Signs of a kingdom, not credentials

The biblical writers didn't think of the world as a machine God occasionally overrides. They thought of it as his creation, held in being moment by moment, in which he is free to act in ordinary and extraordinary ways. So the word John reaches for is *sēmeia*, signs, and he uses it with care. A sign isn't the destination. It points past itself to something larger. John numbers the first two as if he wants the reader to keep count. After the water becomes wine at a village wedding in Cana, he writes that this was the first of the signs by which Jesus "manifested his glory" (John 2:11, ESV). After an official's dying son is healed from a distance, he adds, "This was now the second sign that Jesus did when he had come from Judea to Galilee" (John 4:54, ESV). The book is built around a handful of these acts, and each is followed by a long discourse explaining what it signified: bread in the wilderness and the bread of life, a blind man's eyes and the light of the world, a dead man's grave and the resurrection and the life.

What is striking is how little confidence John places in a miracle's power to compel belief. When the official first begs for his son, Jesus answers with something close to a complaint: "Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe" (John 4:48, ESV). The day after the five thousand are fed, the crowd chases him around the lake, and he tells them plainly what brought them: "Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves" (John 6:26, ESV). They had eaten the miracle and missed the sign. Near the end of the public ministry John gives his own summary: "Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him" (John 12:37, ESV). If the miracles were credentials, John is documenting their failure. He isn't troubled by it, because he never thought they were credentials. A sign can be seen and refused. It offers itself to be read, and it leaves the reader free.

What the signs point to is the subject Jesus preached about more than any other, the kingdom of God. He didn't mainly announce that souls could go to heaven after death. He announced that the reign of God, the long-promised setting right of a world gone wrong, was breaking into the present through him. When he was accused of casting out demons by the power of Satan, he answered, "But if it is by the Spirit of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Matthew 12:28, ESV). The exorcism was evidence of a change of rule. The strong man's house was being plundered because someone stronger had come.

The clearest instance comes when John the Baptist, in Herod's prison and facing death, sends to ask whether Jesus is really the one they have been waiting for. Jesus doesn't answer with a claim about himself. He points at what is happening: "Go and tell John what you have seen and heard: the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, the poor have good news preached to them" (Luke 7:22, ESV). The list is built from Isaiah's picture of the day God comes to save his people: "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped" (Isaiah 35:5, ESV). The answer to John's question isn't "look how powerful I am." It is "look what time it is." And notice where the list ends. Good news for the poor stands beside the raising of the dead as if it belonged to the same order of wonder, because to Jesus it did. Both are the kingdom reaching the people the present age had given up on.

N. T. Wright argues in *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996) that the healings were not displays designed to impress but enacted announcements that Israel's God was returning to restore his people, and many of them did something a modern reader skims past: they restored outcasts to the community. A leper healed could go back to the synagogue and the family table. A woman whose bleeding had made her ritually unclean for twelve years could be touched again. The miracle mended a body and reopened a door, which is why the kingdom Jesus previewed looks less like a tidier version of the present order than a reversal of it.

Sometimes the sign points past the body entirely. When friends lower a paralyzed man through a roof, Jesus first tells him that his sins are forgiven, and when the scribes object that only God can forgive sins, he heals the man's legs "that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10, ESV). The visible miracle is in the service of the invisible one. The kingdom doesn't only mend bodies. It goes after the thing that broke the world in the first place.

C. S. Lewis, in *Miracles* (1947), distinguished miracles of the old creation, which do quickly and locally what God does slowly everywhere, as at Cana, from miracles of the new creation, such as the walking on water and the resurrection, which give a glimpse of what matter will be when God has finished remaking it. Either way, the miracle isn't God breaking into a world that belongs to someone else. It is the owner acting openly in one place as he means one day to act everywhere.

The miracles were signs of a kingdom that has truly arrived and has not yet fully arrived. George Eldon Ladd, in *The Presence of the Future* (1974), gave evangelical theology its standard account of this shape: the kingdom is already present in Jesus's ministry, death and resurrection, and not yet consummated until he returns. The signs belong to the "already." Their limits belong to the "not yet." Lazarus walked out of his tomb still wrapped in grave clothes, and in time he died again, as did every leper cleansed and every child raised. Every healing in the Gospels was a real mercy and a provisional one, a first taste of a meal not yet served. Jesus wept at Lazarus's grave (John 11:35) moments before calling him out of it, which tells you that even the sign did not make death small to him. Hold on to that tension. The church's two opposite errors about miracles today both come from collapsing it.

## The bishop who changed his mind

The most instructive witness in the church's memory of miracles is a man who held one view and then corrected it in writing. Around 390, a few years after his conversion, Augustine wrote a short book called *Of True Religion*. In it he argued that the miracles of the apostolic age had not been allowed to continue into his own time. His reasoning was pastoral rather than skeptical: had they continued, he thought, the soul would keep seeking visible things, and the human race would grow cold toward wonders it had grown used to. It is a thoughtful position, and in substance it is the one many Protestant cessationists would sign fifteen centuries later.

Then Augustine became the bishop of Hippo, a harbor town on the North African coast, and spent the next thirty-five years among ordinary sick people. Late in life he reviewed everything he had written and published his corrections, the *Retractations*, around 427. Of that earlier sentence he wrote, in substance, that it must not be taken to mean that no miracles happen in Christ's name now. When he wrote it he already knew of a blind man given his sight at Milan in 386, when the bodies of the martyrs Gervasius and Protasius were found, and he had been in the city that year. He had meant only that not every kind of apostolic sign continued, such as the tongues that came with the gift of the Spirit or the sick healed as Peter's shadow passed over them.

The fullest evidence of his change is in the final book of *The City of God*, completed around 426. In book 22, chapter 8, Augustine sets down a long catalog of healings from his own region and his own congregation. Relics associated with Stephen, the first martyr, had recently come to Hippo, and Augustine began asking those who were healed to write out an account so it could be read aloud to the church. He tells of Innocentius of Carthage, facing a second painful operation for a fistula, who prayed the night before with such weeping that Augustine, who was in the room, never forgot it; in the morning, he says, the surgeons found the wound healed. He tells of Paulus and Palladia, a brother and sister who had wandered the empire with a violent trembling, healed at the shrine a few days apart in the Easter season, and of the congregation's shouting when they stood before it. Augustine's tone is not triumphant. He complains, more than once, that such things happen and are barely known even in the town where they occur, because nobody writes them down.

Augustine's witness cuts both ways. He changed his mind toward testimony as a careful thinker, not a credulous one, keeping his caution about those who seek visible things while refusing to let a theory override what he had investigated in his own pews. But the healings he records cluster around relics and shrines, a devotional world the Reformers would later reject for reasons they considered weighty, and a Protestant reader should say so plainly rather than quote Augustine selectively. The honest summary is that the fathers give neither side a clean witness. They give a church that never stopped praying for the sick, grew cautious about spectacle, and kept being surprised.

The modern institution nearest to Augustine's habit of writing things down is a Catholic one. After a peasant girl named Bernadette Soubirous reported visions of the Virgin Mary at a grotto in Lourdes, in the south of France, in 1858, the town became one of the world's great pilgrimage sites. In the 1880s a medical bureau was set up there to examine claimed cures, later joined by an international committee of physicians. The standards are strict: a serious, well-documented illness, and a cure that is sudden, complete, lasting and unexplained by treatment or the natural course of the disease. Only after the physicians declare a cure medically unexplained does the bishop of the healed person's home diocese decide whether to call it a miracle. Thousands of cures have been claimed. When the healing of a French nun, Sister Bernadette Moriau, was recognized in February 2018, it became the seventieth, and the official count has stayed close to that number since.

A Protestant will disagree with much of the theology that surrounds the grotto, and a skeptic will want the files. But the number itself teaches something both of them should hear. About seventy, out of the multitudes of sick people who have made the trip since 1858. That isn't the arithmetic of a church trying to manufacture wonders. It is the arithmetic of a church that expects most of its sick to go home unhealed, and prays for them anyway, and holds the word "miracle" back for the case it cannot otherwise explain. Whatever you conclude about those cases, that restraint rebukes every Christian, Protestant or Catholic, who has called a recovered cold or a found parking space a miracle and so cheapened the word for the people who most need it to mean something.

## Three answers, each at its strongest

Whether God still works miracles is not the question of whether he can. Every orthodox Christian confesses that God raised Jesus bodily from the dead; that sits on the creedal floor. The question is how he ordinarily works now, and whether the miraculous gifts Paul describes, including "gifts of healing" (1 Corinthians 12:9, ESV), continue in the church. No ecumenical creed settles it; it divides congregations, not the faith. It is a second-order question, and faithful Christians stand on every side of it. (The exegetical case on each side, gift by gift, is laid out in [What Are the Spiritual Gifts?](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-spiritual-gifts). What follows concerns healing and the miraculous.)

The cessationist starts from purpose. If signs were given to confirm new revelation, they should gather around the moments when revelation was new, and in Scripture they do: the exodus under Moses, the ministries of Elijah and Elisha during Israel's apostasy, the ministry of Christ and the founding of the church. Hebrews speaks of a salvation declared by the Lord and passed on by those who heard him, "while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (Hebrews 2:4, ESV). B. B. Warfield of Princeton put the classic Protestant form of the case in *Counterfeit Miracles* (1918): the extraordinary gifts were part of the apostles' credentials, and they passed away with the apostolic age. Richard Gaffin restated it in *Perspectives on Pentecost* (1979) and as the cessationist voice in *Are Miraculous Gifts for Today? Four Views* (1996), edited by Wayne Grudem, arguing from Ephesians 2:20 that the apostles and prophets were the church's foundation, and a foundation is laid once. None of this makes the cessationist a man who thinks God has stopped healing. Warfield wrote against the faith-cure movement of his day while affirming that God answers prayer for the sick, ordinarily through his providence and the means he has given. What the cessationist denies is a continuing gift of miracle-working in particular persons, and his deepest motive is pastoral: he has watched people hunger for a fresh wonder more than they trust a finished word, and watched the sick blamed when the wonder didn't come.

The continuationist starts from the text as it stands, and the text sets no expiry date on any gift. The signs in Acts don't stay with the Twelve: "And Stephen, full of grace and power, was doing great wonders and signs among the people" (Acts 6:8, ESV), and Stephen was no apostle but one of seven chosen to see that the widows were fed. Paul's lists of gifts are written to ordinary congregations. The verse most often read as setting an end date, "but when the perfect comes, the partial will pass away" (1 Corinthians 13:10, ESV), is taken by many interpreters, cessationists among them, to point to Christ's return rather than to a completed canon. Grudem made the exegetical case at length in his *Systematic Theology* (1994), and in the *Four Views* volume Sam Storms argued the "third wave" form of it and Douglas Oss the Pentecostal and charismatic form. Jack Deere, who had taught Old Testament at Dallas Theological Seminary, made it as a confession in *Surprised by the Power of the Spirit* (1993): his own cessationism, he concluded, had rested less on Scripture than on the absence of anything in his experience to contradict it. Craig Keener's two-volume *Miracles* (2011) gathered a very large body of contemporary healing accounts, many from the Global South and some with medical documentation, and he argued that the Western academy's confidence that such things don't happen reflects one educated minority rather than the world's church. A 2010 study in the *Southern Medical Journal*, led by Candy Gunther Brown, measured hearing and vision in rural Mozambique before and after close-contact prayer and reported significant improvement. It was small, it can't say who or what acted, and suggestion and other natural explanations aren't ruled out. What it shows is that such claims can be tested rather than waved away.

Robert Saucy argued a third position in the same volume, and it deserves a hearing of its own rather than treatment as a truce. He found no text that switches the miraculous gifts off, so he would not say God has stopped giving them. But he read the New Testament as describing a special concentration of signs around the founding of the church, and nothing in it as promising the same density in every age, so he saw no warrant for making them the expected norm of every congregation or for organizing a church's life around seeking them. Its defenders would say it is simply the shape of the biblical record: wonders thick around the exodus, the prophets and the apostles, and thin across the long centuries between, when God's people prayed, waited, and were sometimes surprised.

Each is a live option among Christians who share the creeds, and each presses an objection nobody has cleanly answered. The continuationist asks how a church obeys the command to "earnestly desire the spiritual gifts" (1 Corinthians 14:1, ESV) while expecting nothing. The cessationist asks why, if these gifts were meant as the church's standing equipment, they grow so faint in the record for long stretches of its history. The open but cautious posture is pressed from both sides at once: its critics call it a dodge, and a door held open in principle can become one nobody expects God to walk through. This essay doesn't choose among them, because its sources didn't. What they held, and what it holds, is narrower and binds all three: the honest posture toward any particular report is neither credulity nor closed-mindedness. Take testimony seriously without accepting it uncritically, pray for healing knowing it is not guaranteed, and refuse to decide in advance what God may do. On the second-order question itself, a reader can close this page in any of the three positions and remain a brother or sister to those standing in the other two.

## When healing is sold

The worst thing to happen to the doctrine of miracles in the last century wasn't skepticism. It was the healing business. In 1986 the magician and investigator James Randi and his team brought a radio scanner into the crusades of the televangelist Peter Popoff, who was famous for calling out the names, addresses and illnesses of strangers in the crowd as if by revelation. The scanner picked up the source of the revelation: Popoff's wife, reading from prayer cards the audience had filled out before the service and transmitting the details to a receiver in his ear. Randi played the recordings on *The Tonight Show* that year and told the story in *The Faith Healers* (1987). Popoff's ministry went bankrupt the following year. He later returned to television, selling "miracle spring water."

Fraud is only the crudest form of the problem. The Minnesota surgeon William Nolen followed up people declared healed at a Kathryn Kuhlman service in Minneapolis and reported, in *Healing: A Doctor in Search of a Miracle* (1974), that he could not confirm a single cure of organic disease among them. The more common failure is sincere: a theology traced by Kate Bowler in *Blessed* (2013), her history of the American prosperity gospel, and taught most influentially in the Word of Faith movement associated with Kenneth Hagin: that faith is a force, that spoken words release it, and that healing, like prosperity, is a right the believer claims. On those terms, a healing that doesn't come has an explanation ready to hand, and the explanation always points at the sick person. The logic is airtight, which is exactly what makes it cruel. (The wider teaching is examined in [What Is the Prosperity Gospel?](/writing/prosperity-gospel-is-not-the-gospel).)

The New Testament saw this coming. When Simon, a magician in Samaria, watched the apostles and offered them money for the power to confer the Spirit, Peter's answer was immediate: "May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money!" (Acts 8:20, ESV). Jesus warned that "false christs and false prophets will arise and perform great signs and wonders, so as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect" (Matthew 24:24, ESV). Scripture never treats a wonder as proof of itself. It asks whether the wonder points to Christ or to the one performing it.

But the same instrument has to be turned the other way. The respectable congregation that would be mortified by a healing has made the huckster's mistake in reverse, treating the miracle as a credential it is too embarrassed to present. And the embarrassed church has its own cruelty, subtler but real: the raised eyebrow at the immigrant believer who says God healed her daughter, the assumption that the global church's testimony is a symptom of its lack of education. Those of us who prize a reasoned faith are most prone to it. We flinch first and listen second, and the reflex makes us poor neighbors to Christians whose experience of God is louder than ours.

## What we owe the one who wasn't healed

Every theology of miracles is finally tested at a bedside, by what it lets you say to the person the miracle passed by. Here Scripture is plainer than the crusades. When the disciples saw a man blind from birth and asked whose sin had caused it, "Jesus answered, 'It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him'" (John 9:3, ESV). The man at the pool of Bethesda was healed before he even knew who Jesus was: "Now the man who had been healed did not know who it was" (John 5:13, ESV). A father whose son had suffered since childhood cried out, "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24, ESV), and the boy was healed through faith that confessed its own weakness. And the apostle through whom God worked extraordinary miracles wrote in his last letter, "I left Trophimus, who was ill, at Miletus" (2 Timothy 4:20, ESV), and carried his own thorn after asking three times for its removal, hearing instead, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, ESV). The New Testament never once tells a sick believer that the illness stays because the faith was small.

So the church must never say it. Kate Bowler, the historian whose book on the prosperity gospel is cited above, was diagnosed with stage IV colon cancer in 2015, at thirty-five, and in *Everything Happens for a Reason* (2018) she describes being on the receiving end of the explanations she had spent years studying. She closes the book with a short list of things never to say to people in terrible times. Pray for healing, boldly and by name, as James tells the elders to do (James 5:14). Keep the doctors. Luke, who wrote down so many of Jesus's healings, was himself "the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14, ESV), and nothing in this essay is medical advice or a reason to stop treatment. If the waiting has turned into despair, say so to someone: your pastor, your doctor, or the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988).

The healings of Jesus were the kingdom arriving early, in one room, on one afternoon. They were real and provisional, and the one miracle that isn't provisional is the one the rest were pointing toward, the body that came out of the tomb and will not die again (the evidence is weighed in [Did the Resurrection Happen?](/writing/did-the-resurrection-happen)). Paul says that we "who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23, ESV). A healing now is a firstfruit, and so is the grace that holds a person who isn't healed. Neither is the harvest.

That leaves the church standing where Jesus stood at Lazarus's grave, weeping at a death he knew he would reverse, with this difference: we don't know which graves will open this side of his return. We pray as people who believe he still can, and we sit with the unhealed as people who know he has already promised them more than we asked. Some of us will see a sign. All of us are waiting for the day John saw, when "death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore" (Revelation 21:4, ESV). Until then the question isn't whether you can make yourself believe in wonders. It is whether you will stay in the room.
