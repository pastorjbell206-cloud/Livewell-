The most effective way to keep the church silent on poverty is to convince it that poverty is a personal problem.

Once poverty is primarily a moral category — a consequence of poor choices, bad values, weak discipline — the appropriate response is charity and evangelism: help people make better choices, convert them to better values, and poverty will sort itself out. The church can maintain its comfort, its donors, and its political alliances without ever asking the harder question. The harder question is this: who benefits from the arrangement that produces so much poverty, and why has the arrangement persisted?

That question is political. I do not mean partisan. I mean that it concerns polis — the ordering of common life, the distribution of power, the rules by which society decides who gets what. Poverty is political because the conditions that produce and sustain it are produced and sustained by policy choices, not merely by the personal failures of poor individuals.

The prophets knew this without the vocabulary of modern economics. Amos watched the wealthy merchants of the eighth century B.C. north kingdom manipulate grain weights, use legal mechanisms to dispossess small farmers of their ancestral land, and trade debt bondage as a commodity — all within the bounds of a system designed to make such extraction possible. He did not say: those poor farmers should have worked harder. He announced the word of the Lord against the system itself. "You trample on the poor and take a levy of grain from them" (Amos 5:11). The trampling is structural. The levy is extracted through economic and legal power. The sin is embedded in the arrangement, not merely in the hearts of individual transactors.

Proverbs 13:23 observes that "the fallow ground of the poor would yield much food, but it is swept away through injustice." The poor have productive capacity. What they lack is access — to land, to credit, to legal protection, to the basic infrastructure through which that capacity could be exercised. Injustice sweeps it away. That is not a personal failing. That is a system working as designed.

I have preached on the parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) as a story about afterlife consequences, which it certainly is. I have been slower to notice that Lazarus was not merely unlucky. He was lying at the gate of a man with enormous resources and receiving nothing. The gate — again the gate, as in Amos — was the place where transactions, legal claims, and distributions were settled. Lazarus was dying at the junction point between wealth and power, and the arrangement between them produced his death. Jesus does not explain why Lazarus was poor. He simply shows us a man who had resources and a man who had none, and announces where God stood.

The steel-man argument deserves a hearing. Personal behavior does affect economic outcomes. Marriage, work, education, and deferred gratification are real factors in individual economic trajectories. To deny this entirely is dishonest. But these factors operate within systems that either amplify or constrain their effects. The same behavioral profile — the same work ethic, the same education level, the same family structure — produces wildly different economic outcomes depending on where you were born, what neighborhood you grew up in, what schools were available to you, what credit was accessible, and what legal protections existed for your wage claims. The behavior is the same. The system is not. And the system is a political product.

The church that only addresses the behavior without ever addressing the system has chosen the easier conversation. Charity is easier than justice. Charity requires my surplus. Justice might require my vote, my voice, my willingness to name what the comfortable people in the room do not want named.

The prophets were not primarily social workers with a better methodology. They were messengers of a God who cared — with specific, named specificity — about the arrangements of power that crushed the poor. To preach what they preached is to accept that sometimes the most faithful thing the church can say is not "here is how to help the poor" but "here is why there are so many of them, and what God thinks about it."

That is political. That has always been political.

The only question is whether we have the courage to say it.
