If your only exposure to Christianity is the internet, you would be forgiven for concluding that Christians agree on nothing. They argue about politics, about which music is holy, about how to baptize and when, about the end of the world, about whether the universe is six thousand or fourteen billion years old, about wine and women and worship styles, and they do it with a viciousness that makes the whole thing look less like a faith than a permanent family feud with no referee. From the outside, it looks like chaos. It looks like a religion with two billion members and two billion versions.

So here is something that should surprise you, and it surprised me when I came in from unbelief. Underneath all that noise there is a center that has not moved in seventeen hundred years. A Christian in Lagos and a Christian in Seoul and a Christian in rural Kentucky and a Greek Orthodox monk on a mountain and a Coptic believer in Egypt — people who would argue furiously about nearly everything else — will all stand and say the same creed, and mean the same things by it. The creed is short. That shortness is not an accident. It is a decision.

**The genius of the creeds is not what they include but what they leave out. They draw a small, fierce, non-negotiable center — and then deliberately refuse to legislate the enormous territory around it, leaving Christians free to differ. Knowing where that line falls is the difference between a faith that can hold the whole world and a faith that splinters over everything.** Most of the bitterness you see online comes from people who have lost the line — who have promoted their opinions to the level of the creed, or demoted the creed to the level of their opinions.

## What the creed actually says, and what it pointedly does not

Read the Apostles' Creed and notice how much it does not tell you.

It tells you that God is the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth. It does not tell you *how* he made it, or how long it took, or what to do with the fossils. It tells you that Jesus Christ is his only Son, conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the virgin Mary, suffered under Pontius Pilate, crucified, dead, buried, risen on the third day, ascended, and coming again to judge. It does not tell you the mechanics of exactly how his death saves you — there are several ways Christians have understood the cross, and the creed names none of them as required. It tells you to believe in the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic (meaning universal) church, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and life everlasting. It does not tell you how to organize a church, who may lead it, how often to take communion, or what the music should sound like.

Look at what got left on the cutting room floor. Every question that has split denominations — church government, the precise timing of the end times, the age of the earth, predestination versus free will, the right mode of baptism, political allegiance — none of it is in the creed. Not because those questions do not matter. Some of them matter a great deal. But because the church, in its founding centuries, made a costly judgment about which truths you cannot be a Christian without, and which truths sincere Christians can hold differently while remaining brothers and sisters. The creed is the list of things worth dividing over. Everything not on it is, by implication, not.

## The oldest test for the center

How did the church decide where to draw the line? In the fifth century a monk in southern France named Vincent of Lérins gave the church a rule of thumb that has been quoted ever since, and a newcomer can carry it in a pocket. He said the true faith is what has been believed "everywhere, always, and by all" — *ubique, semper, ab omnibus.*

Take the test seriously and you see how strict it is. Everywhere: not just in your country or your culture, but across the whole church in every place. Always: not a recent innovation, not something the last fifty years discovered, but the faith held down the centuries. By all: not the special insight of one teacher or one movement, but the shared confession of the whole people of God. Run any belief through that filter and the screen catches almost everything except the creed. Your particular church's distinctive doctrine — the thing that makes it different from the church down the street — almost by definition fails the test, because it is not believed everywhere, always, and by all. That is not an insult to it. It is just a way of seeing that it belongs in the outer ring, not the center.

Vincent's rule is not magic, and historians have noted that the church's actual beliefs did grow and clarify over time rather than sitting perfectly still. Jaroslav Pelikan, the historian who spent his life mapping how doctrine developed, showed that the tradition was a living thing — it deepened, it answered new questions, it grew up. But it grew the way a tree grows, adding rings around a trunk that does not change, not the way a fashion changes by abandoning last year's shape. Vincent's test is best understood as naming that trunk: the part that has held while everything else grew around it.

## "Mere Christianity" and the hallway

C. S. Lewis gave the modern world its most useful picture of this, and it is the picture I most needed as a newcomer suspicious of joining any one camp. In the preface to *Mere Christianity*, Lewis says he is not trying to convert anyone to his own denomination. He is trying to describe the faith that nearly all Christians have held at nearly all times — the central hall, he calls it, off which all the particular rooms open. The hall is the creed. The rooms are the denominations, each with its fires lit and its chairs and its particular way of doing things.

And then Lewis says the thing that should govern how any of us behaves online and in person. The hall is a place to wait, he writes, a place from which to try the various doors, not a place to live in. The rooms, not the hall, are where there is food and warmth. You are meant to enter a room. But — and this is the rule that would heal half the church's public ugliness — when you are in your room, you must be kind to the people in the other rooms, and to those still standing in the hall trying to decide. The man in the hall is closer to your true brother than you think, even if his eventual room will not be yours. The error is not having a room. The error is mistaking your room for the whole house.

## Why the center has to stay small

Here is why this matters, and why getting it wrong does real damage.

If you make the center too big — if you treat your view of the end times or your politics or your worship style as though it were the creed — you will excommunicate most of the global church and most of Christian history, and you will teach new believers that following Jesus means signing onto a hundred-item checklist most of which has nothing to do with him. That is how you produce a faith that is brittle, suspicious, and exhausting, a faith that splits and splits and splits. I have watched it happen. I have watched churches divide over things the creed never mentions, each side certain it was contending for the gospel, when it was contending for a preference.

But if you make the center too small — if you decide that nothing is non-negotiable, that the resurrection is a metaphor and the deity of Christ an opinion and the whole creed a set of inherited feelings you can keep or discard — then you no longer have Christianity. You have a mood. Paul, writing to Corinth, refused to let one truth slide into the optional ring: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain... If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (1 Corinthians 15). He spends an entire chapter insisting that the resurrection is not negotiable, that the whole structure stands or falls on it. Some things are walls. Take them down and the roof comes with them.

The wisdom is in telling the walls from the wallpaper. The creed is the church's answer to exactly that question, worked out over centuries by people who paid for the answer.

## How the line keeps a person from two opposite cliffs

Knowing where the line falls does something for an individual believer, not just for the church as an institution, and it took me years to feel both edges of it.

On one side is the cliff of the anxious heart. If you do not know which beliefs are the center and which are the field of freedom, every disagreement feels like a threat to your salvation. You meet a Christian who reads Genesis differently than you, or votes differently, or baptizes differently, and some alarm goes off that says *one of us is not really a Christian.* That alarm is exhausting, and it is wrong, and it has driven more thoughtful people out of the faith than almost anything else. They were taught that to doubt the young-earth timeline or the end-times chart was to doubt the gospel, and when they could no longer hold the timeline, they thought they had to let go of Christ. They were never told that those things sit in the outer ring, that you can lose them and lose nothing of the creed. The line is mercy for the anxious. It tells you exactly how much you are actually required to hold, and it is far less, and far more solid, than the pile of opinions you were handed.

On the other side is the cliff of the dissolving heart. The opposite danger, just as real in our moment, is the slow erosion that keeps the warm feelings of faith while quietly emptying the content — Jesus as a good teacher rather than the risen Lord, the resurrection as an inspiring symbol of new beginnings rather than a corpse that walked out of a tomb, God as the name we give to our highest values. This version is comfortable and costs nothing and dies young, because there is nothing solid in it to hold a person up when life leans its full weight on them. The line guards against this too. It says: here are the walls; you do not get to quietly take them down and keep calling the building a house. The creed is as much a protection against the faith dissolving into a mood as it is against the faith hardening into a checklist.

The same line, then, saves a person from two opposite errors — the brittle certainty that fights over everything and the soft sentiment that believes nothing in particular. It tells the anxious believer he is freer than he feared, and it tells the dissolving believer he is more accountable than he hoped. That is what a good boundary does. It is not a cage. It is the edge of solid ground.

## The freedom on the other side of the line

And here is the part that should feel like fresh air to a newcomer afraid that joining means surrendering every opinion to a committee. On the far side of the line — outside the small fierce center — there is enormous freedom, and the Bible insists on it. Paul devotes a whole chapter to it. There were Christians in Rome fighting over food and holy days, each side sure it was the faithful one, and Paul's verdict is bracing: "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another?... Let each be fully convinced in his own mind" (Romans 14). On the matters that are not the center, he says, stop condemning each other. Hold your conviction. Hold it sincerely. And let your brother hold his.

That is the shape of a faith that can hold the whole world: a granite center surrounded by a wide field of freedom. Paul gives the architecture in a single sentence to the Ephesians, and it is worth memorizing as the map of the whole thing: "There is one body and one Spirit... one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all" (Ephesians 4:4–6). Count the *ones*. That is the center. That is the part you do not get to renovate, the part that makes a Lagos believer and a Kentucky believer actually one thing and not two. Everything outside those *ones* is the field where Christians may, in good conscience, disagree and still kneel together.

There is a discipline hidden in all of this, and it is harder than it looks. It is the discipline of holding your secondary convictions with full sincerity and zero contempt. The temptation is to think that the only way to take a belief seriously is to treat everyone who disagrees as an enemy of the faith. The creed teaches the opposite. You can be deeply, thoughtfully convinced about baptism or church government or how to read the first chapters of Genesis — you should be, these questions deserve real thought — and still recognize that the person across the aisle who landed elsewhere is your brother, bought by the same blood, confessing the same Lord. Strong conviction on the secondary matters; open hands toward the people who hold them differently. That combination is rare, and it is exactly what the line makes possible. Lose the line and you get one of two failures: people with strong convictions and no charity, or people with endless charity and no convictions. The creed lets you have both at once, which is the only way a church full of opinionated people has ever managed to stay one church.

I came into this expecting to have to swallow a thousand certainties to be allowed in. What I found instead was a short, hard creed and a wide, generous field — a center small enough to say in ninety seconds and strong enough to have held for seventeen hundred years, and around it room to breathe. The miracle is not that Christians argue. Every large family argues. The miracle is the line that has never moved, the handful of things that two billion quarreling people will all stand up and say together, in one voice, and mean the same thing. The creed is short on purpose. The shortness is the mercy.
