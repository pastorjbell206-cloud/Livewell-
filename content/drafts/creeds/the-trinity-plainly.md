Let me start with the objection, because I used to make it, loudly, and I had not earned it.

One God. Three persons. Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, each fully God, and yet not three Gods but one. To an outsider — and I was an outsider, an atheist who thought he had seen through the whole thing — this looks like a confession that the church cannot count. It looks like the moment where Christianity quietly admits that its central claim does not add up and asks you to stop asking. One plus one plus one is three. Everyone knows that. So either the math is wrong or the words mean nothing.

Here is what took me years to see, and what I want to hand to anyone standing where I stood. **The Trinity was never an answer to a math problem. It is the answer to a different and far more interesting question: what do you do when the one God you believe in shows up in person, calls another person his Father, and then sends a third who is unmistakably also God — and you refuse to give any of them up?** The doctrine is not the church being confused. It is the church being faithful to its own evidence, refusing to round off the strange shape of what actually happened.

## Christianity did not start with the Trinity. It got cornered into it.

This is the part newcomers almost never hear, and it changes everything.

The first Christians were Jews. Their deepest, most fiercely guarded conviction — the line they would die rather than cross, the line many of them did die rather than cross — was that there is one God. They said it every day: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one." They had watched the surrounding nations stack gods like firewood, and they knew that the whole dignity of their faith hung on the oneness of the one who made everything. These were not people looking for an excuse to add divine beings. These were the last people on earth who would invent a doctrine that multiplied God.

And yet. They met a man named Jesus. He forgave sins, which only God can do. He accepted worship, which a faithful Jew would sooner be killed than accept. He spoke of God as his own Father in a way that got him executed for blasphemy. The opening of John's Gospel says the unsayable thing flatly: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:1–14). *With God* and *was God* in the same breath. Then this Jesus is killed, rises, and before he leaves he tells his followers to baptize "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19) — three names, one name, said in one breath as the single identity into which a person is now baptized. And then the Spirit comes, and the Spirit is no force or influence but someone who teaches, grieves, decides, and is God.

So the first Christians were caught. They could not stop saying *one God* — their whole faith depended on it. And they could not stop saying that the Father is God, the Son is God, the Spirit is God — their whole experience depended on it. The Trinity is not a clever idea someone added on top of the Bible. It is the shape forced on the church by its refusal to let go of either truth. They did not reason their way to a three-in-one God. They were driven to it, against every instinct they had, by what they could not deny.

## The fight at Nicaea was about whether Jesus is worth your life

It came to a crisis in the early fourth century, and the crisis is worth knowing because it shows what was actually at stake — and it was never arithmetic.

A teacher named Arius offered the church a way out of the strain. What if the Son is not quite God? What if he is the highest, first, most glorious of all created things, but still made, still with a beginning, still a notch below the Father? This preserved the math beautifully. One God, the Father; and beneath him an exalted Son. It was tidy. It was popular. And a young deacon named Athanasius saw that it was fatal, because it cut the nerve of the whole gospel. If Jesus is a creature, then when you worship Jesus you are worshipping a creature, which is idolatry. And if Jesus is a creature, then it was a creature who died for you, and a creature cannot reach down to where God is and lift you up to him. Only God can save you, and only a man can die for men. Take away the full deity of the Son and the cross becomes a tragedy instead of a rescue.

So in 325 the council at Nicaea drew the line, choosing one Greek word that has held for seventeen centuries: the Son is *homoousios* with the Father — of the same being, the same divine substance, not a similar one, not a lesser one. Athanasius spent the rest of his life defending that word, exiled five times for it, because he understood that it was not a quibble over terminology. It was the difference between a Savior who can save and a hero who cannot.

## Then the Cappadocians gave the church the words to say it

Defining that the Son is fully God left a question Nicaea had not finished: how do you say *three* and *one* without contradiction? Three brilliant friends and family in the region of Cappadocia worked it out in the decades after — Basil the Great, his brother Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus, the one they later called "the Theologian." Their move was simple and enormous. They distinguished what God is from who God is. There is one *what* — one divine being, one essence, one God. There are three *whos* — Father, Son, and Spirit, three persons who are not three pieces of God and not three masks God wears, but three who eternally are the one God in the fullness of relationship.

Gregory of Nazianzus described it as a kind of dance of light, the mind no sooner resting on the One than it is carried to the Three, no sooner grasping the Three than it is drawn back to the One. He was not embarrassed that it could not be flattened. He thought the inability to flatten it was a clue that it was true — that the living God would of course exceed the size of our categories, the way the ocean exceeds the cup you dip into it.

## The analogies all fail, and that is fine

Every newcomer, sooner or later, gets handed an analogy meant to make the Trinity click, and every one of them is wrong in an instructive way. The honest thing is to admit it up front.

Water, people say, is one substance in three forms — ice, liquid, steam — so God is one in three. Except water is not all three at once; it is one form at a time, and a God who is Father, then Son, then Spirit, in turns, is not the Trinity at all. That is an old error the church already named and rejected; it called it modalism, the idea that the three are just masks the one God wears in sequence. The clover, people say, has three leaves on one plant. Except the three leaves are three separate parts, three thirds of the clover, and a God who is three parts adding up to one whole is not the Trinity either; that error has a name too. The sun, people say, is flame and light and heat. Closer, but still three aspects of one thing, not three persons in full relationship.

The analogies fail for a reason worth sitting with. There is nothing else like God. The Trinity is not an unusually complicated version of something we already know; it is the one reality that has no parallel, because everything else that exists was made, and God was not. We reach for the clover and the water because we want God to be an instance of a category we can already handle. He is not. Gregory of Nazianzus said that the moment he tried to picture the One he was caught up into the splendor of the Three, and the moment he grasped the Three he was carried back to the One, and he never apologized for the dizziness. He treated it as appropriate. A God you could fully diagram would be a God the size of your diagram, which is to say an idol. The failure of the analogies is not the Trinity falling short of our logic. It is our logic falling short of God, exactly as you would expect if the doctrine were true.

So the right posture for a newcomer is not "I will believe it once someone explains it in a way I can picture." You will not get that, and you should be suspicious of anyone who offers it. The right posture is the one the church actually took: hold both ends of what God has revealed — he is one, and the three are each fully God — refuse to drop either, and let the mystery stand. Mystery, in the Christian sense, does not mean a problem we have failed to solve. It means a reality too large to fit inside us, the way the sea does not fit inside the shell you hold to your ear, though what you hear in the shell is genuinely the sea.

## Why it was never math

So return to the objection. One plus one plus one equals three. Yes — when you are counting identical things in the same category. Three apples. Three dollars. But the Trinity never claimed that the Father, Son, and Spirit are three instances of the category "God," three divine items you could line up and total. The one God is not a member of a class that has three members. The oneness and the threeness are not at the same level, so they do not collide. God is one in *being* and three in *persons*. Those are different questions, and only by confusing them do you get a contradiction. The church did not flunk arithmetic. The church refused to let arithmetic be the measure of God.

Fred Sanders, in his 2010 book *The Deep Things of God*, makes the point that should reframe the whole thing for a newcomer: the Trinity is not a puzzle Christianity is stuck with, a piece of fine print you tolerate to get the good parts. It is the good part. It is the gospel itself, stated at full depth. Because the Trinity means that God did not become loving when he made the world, did not need creation in order to have someone to love. Before there was anything at all, the Father loved the Son in the Spirit. **Love is not something God does. Love is what God eternally is**, three persons pouring themselves out into one another from before time. A solitary god could acquire love only by making something to love. The God of the creeds *is* love, all the way down, with no beginning.

Look again at the last verse Paul wrote to the Corinthians, the one Christians have said over congregations for two thousand years: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of God and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all" (2 Corinthians 13:14). Grace, love, fellowship — three persons named in one blessing, not as a riddle but as the source of everything good being asked down on a struggling church. And listen to how Jesus prays the night before he dies, in the seventeenth chapter of John: he speaks of a glory he shared with the Father "before the world existed," he asks that his followers would be one "even as we are one," he speaks of the Father in him and him in the Father. This is not a man working out a theory. This is a Son talking to his Father out of a relationship older than the universe, and inviting us into it.

## What it gives you that no other God can

Here is why this matters for a person who is only now looking at Christianity and wondering whether any of it is worth the trouble.

A God who is merely one — a single, eternal, solitary power — can be magnificent. He can be just. He can be terrifying. But he cannot, in himself, be love, because love requires an other, and before creation a solitary god had no other. Such a god needs the world in order to have something to love. He is lonely at the center, and his love is a thing he picked up along the way.

The God of the creeds is not lonely at the center. The center is communion. And the staggering claim of the gospel is that this communion has thrown its doors open — that through the Son who became one of us and the Spirit who is poured into us, we are invited not merely to obey God or admire God but to be drawn inside the love the three persons have always had for one another. That is what salvation finally is. Not a transaction. Not a ticket. An adoption into the family life of God.

This is also why the Trinity is not a doctrine you graduate from. Some teachings you learn once and file away. This one you spend a life going further into, because it is not information about God so much as the shape of God, and the shape is infinite. The early teachers said the more you contemplate it the larger it grows, never the smaller — the opposite of a puzzle, which shrinks once solved. A newcomer should not expect to feel they have mastered it and may be relieved of that expectation entirely. You are not meant to master the ocean. You are meant to be taken out into it.

I made the math objection for years, and I thought it was devastating. It turned out I had been objecting to a cartoon — a god who was three of the same thing, which no one has ever taught. The real doctrine is harder to draw and impossible to outgrow. The God I refused to believe in was small enough for me to refute. The God of Nicaea is not. He is one, and he is three, and he is, in the deepest reach of what those words protect, love that had no beginning and will have no end — and that was making room for you before the world was made.
