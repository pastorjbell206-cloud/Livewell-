I have been wrong about politics for most of my adult life. Not wrong in the way each side thinks the other side is wrong. Wrong in an older way — wrong because I had picked a team, and the picking had cost me the ability to see what the team was costing me. Both teams cost something. The third option is the only option a Christian can finally take, and it costs more than either of the others, and it is worth the cost. This essay is about why.

I want to start by saying what I am not arguing. I am not arguing for centrism. Centrism is the average of two errors, and the average of two errors is not theology. I am not arguing for non-engagement. Christians have a duty to be in public life, including in the partisan tools of public life, including in voting. I am not arguing that the parties are equivalent or interchangeable. They have different histories, different errors, different goods, and a Christian making a particular voting decision will rightly weigh those differently in different elections.

What I am arguing is this. A Christian who has committed himself to one of the two American political teams in the way most American Christians have — who has identified his Christianity with the program of one of the parties, who has treated political opposition as theological opposition, who has allowed the partisan identification to colonize his pastoral judgment and his pulpit and his small group — that Christian has, whether he knows it or not, undermined his discipleship. He has confused political alignment with covenantal allegiance. He has subordinated the gospel to the platform. The subordination has happened in both directions, on both teams, in both halves of the American church. I have done it in my own. I am writing this essay as a man trying to repent of it.

## What the left gets right

I want to start with the left because most of my pastoral colleagues, including most of my own pastoral history, have been on the right, and the harder work of honesty is the work of granting what the other team has been right about.

The left has been right that justice is a fundamental category of Christian moral reflection. The prophetic tradition in scripture — Amos, Micah, Isaiah, Jeremiah — is a tradition of insistent concern for the conditions of the poor, the widow, the orphan, the immigrant. The early church, as I argued in the third essay of this series, took in the abandoned, freed slaves at higher rates than the surrounding culture, cared for plague victims, fed the hungry. The Christian social tradition has been justice-concerned for two thousand years, and the conservative American evangelical movement of the past forty years has, in many cases, lost this part of the tradition.

The left has been right that the church's witness on race, in the white American context, has been catastrophically deficient. The white evangelical tradition has been complicit in slavery and in segregation and in the post-Civil-Rights backlash, has often failed to recognize its complicity, has consistently been late and partial in its repentance, and has often blamed the conditions of Black Americans on the Black themselves rather than reckoning with the structural realities the white tradition itself helped create. Jemar Tisby's *The Color of Compromise* (2019) is the documentation; Esau McCaulley's *Reading While Black* (2020) is the constructive theological correction. The left's insistence that this part of the conversation cannot be skipped is correct. The conservative evangelicalism that has tried to skip it has been wrong.

The left has been right that the structural conditions of poverty are not adequately addressed by individual moral correction alone. The biblical tradition includes Jubilee — Leviticus 25 — a structural reset of land ownership and debt that has no analog in modern American policy. The biblical tradition includes the prophetic critique of land accumulation by the wealthy. The biblical tradition includes Christ's reading of his own ministry through Isaiah 61 — good news to the poor, freedom for the captives, the year of the Lord's favor. The structural concern is in the tradition. The conservative American tradition has often suppressed it. The left's insistence on putting it back is correct.

The left has been right that the modern church has often failed in its care for the marginalized. The disabled, the elderly without resources, the immigrant, the refugee, the LGBTQ person who has not yet figured out where to land theologically and has been treated as an enemy rather than a neighbor — the church has often been late to all of these, has often been worse than the surrounding culture, has often added shame to the suffering. The left's insistence that the church meet these people with care, regardless of how the deeper theological questions resolve, is correct.

These are real goods. I want to grant them in full before I name where the left has gone wrong.

## What the left gets wrong

Where the left has gone wrong is in the philosophical foundations, not the practical concerns.

The left has gone wrong in the autonomy axiom. The progressive moral tradition assumes that the human self is sovereign, that the highest good is the self's expression of its own identity, that any interference with self-expression is unjust. The Christian tradition does not assume this. The Christian tradition assumes that the human self is created, fallen, redeemed, and called to obedience to God — and that the obedience is, in many cases, an obedience that constrains self-expression in the name of a higher good. The autonomy axiom and the Christian anthropology are not compatible. The progressive Christian tradition has often tried to hold both, and the holding has produced incoherence.

The left has gone wrong in the sexual ethic. The progressive moral tradition's revision of Christian sexual ethics — premarital sex normalized, marriage redefined, the body's design treated as malleable to identity — is not a development of the Christian tradition. It is a departure from it. Scripture, on the sexual questions, is more consistent than the progressive Christian tradition has allowed. The departure has costs, and the costs are visible in the data on marriage, family stability, the mental health of young people, and the moral coherence of the progressive Christian institutions themselves.

The left has gone wrong in the state-as-redeemer assumption. The progressive tradition tends to assume that the state is the primary agent of justice, that the political program is the locus of redemption, and that the church's job is to align itself with the political program. This is not the Christian tradition. The Christian tradition assumes that the church is the primary agent of mercy, that the political program is at best a limited instrument of justice, and that the church's job is to be itself — a community of disciples doing the works of mercy — rather than aligning itself with any political project. The progressive Christian tradition has often reduced the church to a political auxiliary, and the reduction has cost the church its distinctiveness.

The left has gone wrong in the optimism about human nature that often underlies its political projects. The historic Christian doctrine of sin is more pessimistic than progressive politics generally allows. The historic Christian recognition that human beings, including the well-intentioned ones, are prone to corruption and self-deception is more rigorous than progressive politics generally accepts. The left's confidence that the right policies, administered by the right people, will produce a substantially better society is not, in my reading, a confidence the Christian tradition supports. The tradition supports incremental and humble work for the common good, with no expectation that political projects will produce salvation.

These are real errors. They are not the same kind of errors as the right's, but they are not smaller errors. They are different errors.

## What the right gets right

The right has been right about subsidiarity. The recognition that the household, the local community, and the church are prior to the state and have their own proper authority — that the state is not the basic unit of human flourishing — is closer to the Christian tradition than the progressive tendency to default to state action. The Catholic social tradition's articulation of subsidiarity in *Quadragesimo Anno* (1931) is the classic statement; the Reformed and evangelical traditions have their own versions.

The right has been right about the importance of the family. The progressive deconstruction of family forms has consequences. The recognition that children flourish when raised by their married biological parents, that the household is the basic site of formation, that the breakdown of marriage and the family has measurable costs on the next generation — these are correct, and the right has, on the whole, defended them more consistently than the left has.

The right has been right about religious liberty. The classical liberal commitment to religious liberty, which the modern American right has, on the whole, defended more vigorously than the modern American left, is closer to the Christian tradition's recognition that the conscience cannot be coerced. The left's recent willingness to subordinate religious liberty to other progressive concerns is a worrying development. The right has been correct to defend the older commitment.

The right has been right about the limits of utopian state projects. The conservative anthropology, which recognizes the corruptibility of human beings and the danger of consolidating power, is closer to the historic Christian doctrine of sin than the progressive confidence in expertise-driven state action.

The right has been right about a particular reading of the historic Christian sexual and family ethic — that the tradition's commitments on marriage, sexuality, and the meaning of the body are not arbitrary historical residues to be revised, but rather are connected to deep theological claims about creation, the body, and the meaning of human flourishing. This is correct.

These are real goods. I want to grant them in full before I name where the right has gone wrong.

## What the right gets wrong

Where the right has gone wrong is in the willingness to subordinate justice to order.

The right has been willing, repeatedly across American history, to defend orders that were unjust. The conservative tradition was used to defend slavery in the antebellum South. It was used to defend segregation through the mid-twentieth century. It is being used now to defend a set of arrangements around immigration, race, and economic inequality that produce real injustice to real people. The conservative instinct to defer to existing arrangements is not, on its own, sufficient. It has to be checked by the prophetic concern for justice that the right has often suppressed.

The right has been wrong in its willingness to baptize particular economic arrangements as Christian. The identification of free-market capitalism with biblical economic teaching, which I take up in a separate essay in this series, is not justified by careful reading of scripture. The biblical economic tradition is more critical of accumulation, more attentive to the conditions of the poor, more skeptical of inherited wealth than the modern American conservative tradition has been. The right has, on the whole, written its preferred economic order back into the Bible and called the order Christian. The order is not Christian. The order may be defensible on other grounds. The Christian endorsement is not honest.

The right has been wrong in the racial blindness that has marked it for most of its history. The unwillingness to take Black Christian theological work seriously, to learn from the prophetic tradition of Howard Thurman and James Cone and Esau McCaulley, to confess and repent of the white evangelical tradition's complicity in slavery and segregation — this has been one of the great failures of the American right. It is being only partly addressed now. The addressing is overdue and incomplete.

The right has been wrong in the recent absorption of Christian nationalism. I have written a separate essay on this; I will not repeat it here. The willingness to align the church with a political project that conflates national identity with redemptive identity is the deepest theological error of the recent American right. The error is not small. It is, on the historical analysis I find most persuasive, the same kind of error the German Christians made in the 1930s. The Confessing Church got it right. The American evangelical right, on the whole, has not.

The right has been wrong in the prosperity-adjacent theology that has crept into its economic preferences. The willingness to read the Bible as endorsing wealth, to treat the poor as morally deficient, to suppress the explicit biblical concerns about debt and the conditions of laborers — this is not the historic Christian tradition. The right has produced its own version of theological accommodation to its political preferences, and the accommodation is, in some respects, as worrying as the left's accommodations on sexuality.

These are real errors. They are different errors than the left's. They are not smaller.

## The third option

The third option is not centrism. The third option is not the recognition that both teams have a point and the wise course is to split the difference. The third option is the gospel, applied to the political questions, in a way that judges both teams by a standard neither team accepts. The third option is what scripture does to the political imagination of the Christian who reads it seriously. The third option is uncomfortable because it does not let the Christian stay on either team. The third option is sustainable because the standard it is measuring by is not a team's program but the kingdom of God, and the kingdom of God is not a political project of any party.

The third option requires the Christian to be informed about politics, engaged in voting and other civic acts, attentive to the policy questions, and refused — refused, finally, in the deeper allegiance — to either team. The third option requires the Christian to be in conversation with people on both sides, to learn from both, to disagree with both, to refuse the tribal identification that both sides demand. The third option requires the Christian to be useful to no one's political coalition in the way coalitions need useful members. The third option requires the Christian to be lonely, politically, for most of his adult life.

I am writing this as someone who has been a coded conservative for most of my pastoral career and has, over the past decade, been slowly extracted from the coalition by my own theological reading. The extraction has cost. I have lost friends in both directions — friends to my right who think I have softened, friends to my left who think I have not gone far enough. The third option is, in some real sense, the position of someone who has agreed to lose friends in both directions, because the gospel demands a particular kind of independence from the political teams that scripture does not endorse.

## The verdict

Pick the gospel. Watch what the gospel does to your political instincts. It will not let you stay on either team. The not-letting is uncomfortable. The not-letting is the only honest theology. The Christian who has stayed on his team is a Christian who has not yet let the gospel finish its work. The Christian who has been driven into the third option is a Christian whose theology has begun to do what theology is supposed to do. The third option is hard. The third option is right. The third option is what the faithful church in our generation will be holding, and the holding will cost most of us most of what we currently call our political identities. That cost is what the next essay takes up — what it costs when pastors, in particular, get pulled into the partisan tools. That cost is real. The cost is mine. The cost is the next essay. Stay for it.
