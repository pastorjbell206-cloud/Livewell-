There is a kind of ministry that looks like faithfulness from the outside and is hollow from the inside. I have practiced it. I know what it feels like to preach a sermon I no longer believed at the level of the gut, to sit with a grieving widow and speak the words of comfort while my own soul was somewhere else entirely — competent, practiced, professionally present, inwardly absent. Nobody knew. That is the most dangerous kind of drift.

The ancient desert fathers called it *acedia* — the noonday demon, the spiritual condition in which the monk goes through the motions of prayer while the interior of his life grows arid and cold. Evagrius of Pontus [cite — confirm] catalogued it in the fourth century as the most destructive of the spiritual vices precisely because it is not dramatic. It is not scandal. It is erosion. The pastor who falls into sexual immorality makes headlines. The pastor whose interior life quietly dries up for a decade while his church grows takes a long time to name what has happened, and by then the damage is wide.

The interior life of the pastor is not a private luxury. It is the aquifer from which the congregation drinks.

Paul, writing to Timothy, does not say "manage your congregation well" as the first commandment of pastoral formation. He says, "Watch your life and doctrine closely" (1 Timothy 4:16). Life first. Doctrine second. The order is not accidental. The theological content you preach is only as durable as the life from which it emerges. A man can preach justification by faith while practicing justification by performance in every corner of his interior world, and the congregation will eventually feel the gap even if they cannot name it.

What does a pastor's interior life actually require? Not more productivity disciplines. Not a faster morning routine. Something harder: the willingness to be with yourself in the presence of God, which is another way of saying the willingness to stop performing even in private.

I have discovered — slowly, with resistance — that the most threatening spiritual discipline for me is not fasting or extended prayer. It is silence. The fasting ends and I feel righteous. The prayer fills time. But silence requires that I sit still long enough for the actual condition of my soul to surface, and what surfaces is not always what I want to see. The pride. The exhaustion dressed up as dedication. The low-grade resentment toward a congregation I claim to love. Silence does not produce these things. It reveals them. That is exactly why we avoid it.

The pastoral office compounds this. Ministry is relentlessly outward. There is always someone who needs something. The structure of the week — Sunday to Sunday, crisis to crisis, meeting to meeting — creates a centrifugal force that spins the pastor outward until the interior goes unattended for months, then years. The congregation does not intend this. They are not taking anything that wasn't offered. The pastor who offers everything he has without ever returning to the source will eventually have nothing to offer.

Eugene Peterson spent decades arguing that the pastoral vocation requires a kind of countercultural stubbornness about the interior life [cite — confirm]. He was not describing a technique. He was describing a posture — the refusal to let the relentless demands of ministry crowd out the one thing that makes ministry possible: the pastor's own living relationship with the God he proclaims.

This is not about having more mystical experiences. It is not about journaling or therapy, though both may help. It is about the older, more demanding tradition of what Bernard of Clairvaux called *recessus* — withdrawal, return, the daily discipline of coming back to what is actually true about God and what is actually true about yourself [cite — confirm]. The pastor who practices this is not wasting time that should go to the congregation. He is doing the only work that makes everything else worth anything.

You can preach about the living God while living as a practical atheist in your interior life. The sermons will still be technically correct. The pastoral visits will still be professionally delivered. But there will be a weight missing. People will feel it before they can describe it.

The health of your soul is not a personal concern separate from your public calling. It is your calling. The congregation you serve deserves a pastor whose words come from somewhere real.

That requires going back there, regularly, whether or not you feel like it.

Especially when you don't.
