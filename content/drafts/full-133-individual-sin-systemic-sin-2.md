There is a confession I have to make before I make an argument.

For the better part of a decade in ministry, I preached sin as if it were always and only a personal transaction. A man lusts. A woman deceives. A person steals. The solution: conviction, repentance, grace, change. I was not wrong about any of that. I was incomplete. I was reading Amos as if Amos were simply a better version of a recovery group facilitator, addressing individual hearts when he was actually indicting structures — the grain merchants who shorted their measures, the judges who took bribes, the wealthy who built houses of hewn stone on the backs of the poor. Individual people were doing it. The system was designed to let them.

The church's current allergy to the language of systemic sin is not a theological conviction. It is a cultural reflex. In the decades since the civil rights movement was reframed as a political rather than a prophetic event, many white evangelicals came to treat any reference to systems or structures as a code word for a particular political program. That conflation is understandable as a historical accident. It is inexcusable as a permanent hermeneutic.

Scripture knows no such restriction.

Amos 5 does not only condemn the man who takes a bribe. It condemns the gate — the system of adjudication where bribery operated as standard procedure: "You hate the one who reproves in the gate, and you abhor the one who speaks the truth" (Amos 5:10). Isaiah 10 does not merely scold corrupt judges individually; it announces woe upon those who "make iniquitous decrees" — legislation, law, policy — "to turn aside the needy from justice and to rob the poor of my people of their right" (Isaiah 10:1–2). The prophetic tradition is not a collection of sermons about individual character improvement. It is a sustained indictment of the arrangement of power itself.

Paul understood this. His language about "principalities and powers" in Ephesians 6 has been domesticated into a vocabulary about personal spiritual warfare, but Walter Wink's work [cite — confirm] restores the social dimension: these powers are the spiritual reality behind institutional arrangements, the invisible architecture of systems that dehumanize and destroy. To fight them requires more than changed hearts, though changed hearts are essential. It requires confronting the structures that make certain sins profitable, certain injustices invisible, certain people disposable.

I have done what I am warning against. I have preached against the sin of materialism to congregants who worked in industries structured to produce and reward exactly that materialism, and I have called that prophetic. It was not. It was the comfortable half of the biblical witness, stripped of its most expensive demands.

The standard objection is worth taking seriously. Systems do not sin, people do. No institution repents; only persons can turn. This is true. And it does not defeat the argument. Individual repentance that produces no structural change is incomplete repentance. The slaveholder who has a genuine conversion experience but keeps his enslaved people in chains has not finished repenting. The banker who privately regrets predatory lending practices but continues them has not repented of them. The question "What must I do?" is always also the question "What must I stop participating in?" — and sometimes, "What must I work to dismantle?"

The Hebrew prophets understood something we have lost. They knew that sin calcifies into systems, that injustice writes itself into law, that what begins as individual wickedness eventually becomes the water everyone swims in, invisible, assumed, normal. The prophetic task is precisely to name what has become invisible. To call out the arrangement. To say what Amos said to Israel's comfortable religious culture: your festivals and assemblies are noise to God when the poor are ground beneath the heel of structures your worship never questions.

Individual sin is real. The broken will is real. Personal accountability is real, and I will not surrender it.

But so is the grain merchant's scale. So is the unjust gate. So is every system built on the theological error that some people matter more than others.

The church that addresses one without the other has chosen the easier half of the prophetic tradition and called it faithfulness.

That is its own kind of sin.
