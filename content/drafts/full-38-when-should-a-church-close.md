I have closed two churches in my pastoral career. One I knew should close; closing it was the most pastoral thing I did all year. The other I did not know should close; I tried to save it for three years, and the trying cost more than the closing would have. I want to talk about churches that should close. Most leadership writing will not. Most leadership writing is wrong.

This essay is for the pastor who is leading a declining congregation and has been wondering, quietly and without permission to say so out loud, whether the right move is to close. The question is real. The question deserves to be asked. The asking has been suppressed in our generation by leadership writing that assumes survival is always the goal, and the suppression has produced churches that should have closed years ago and are now being kept on institutional life support past the point of dignity.

## What the leadership culture refuses to say

The dominant evangelical leadership literature of the past forty years has, on the whole, assumed that every declining church should be revitalized rather than closed. The revitalization model has been the default frame for thinking about declining congregations. The model has produced a substantial industry of revitalization consultants, books, and denominational programs, all premised on the assumption that the right pastoral move is always to keep the doors open.

The model is right in some cases. There are declining churches that have, with appropriate leadership and adequate resources, been revitalized into healthy ministries that serve their communities well for additional decades. The literature documents these cases extensively, and the documentation is real.

The model is wrong in many other cases. There are declining churches whose decline is not the result of inadequate leadership or insufficient resources, but the result of structural realities that revitalization cannot address. There are declining churches whose continued existence consumes more pastoral and lay energy than the church produces in actual ministry. There are declining churches whose remaining members would be better served by joining other congregations. There are declining churches that have, in their decline, accumulated such substantial interpersonal toxicity that no incoming pastor can serve there sustainably. There are declining churches whose buildings have become the primary thing being maintained, with the congregation as the maintenance crew.

These churches should close. The closing is not failure. The closing is pastoral judgment.

The leadership culture's suppression of this option has produced a generation of pastors who feel obligated to keep churches open that should not be open, who burn out attempting revitalization in contexts where revitalization is structurally impossible, and who eventually leave the pastorate in numbers higher than they would have left if the closure option had been available to them as a legitimate pastoral choice.

## The four conditions that should produce closure

I want to name the conditions under which I believe closure is the faithful pastoral move. The conditions are not absolute, and reasonable pastors will disagree about specific applications. But the conditions are worth naming explicitly, because the naming begins to make the closure decision tractable in ways the current culture has prevented.

The first condition is theological corruption that cannot be repaired internally. There are churches whose theological drift has produced practices and teachings that, if continued, will cause substantial harm to congregants and to the broader Christian witness in their community. The harm is not always recognizable from inside the congregation; the people most embedded in the local culture often cannot see what the outside observer can see. When the theological drift has reached the point where the church's continued existence is producing harm faster than the church's ministry is producing fruit, the closing is the faithful move. The closing is a judgment that the gospel is better served by the absence of this particular institution than by its continued operation.

The second condition is institutional smallness that consumes all available energy without producing ministry. There are churches whose remaining numbers are so reduced that the entire energy of the congregation is consumed by institutional maintenance. The remaining members spend their time on building upkeep, on filling the necessary positions to keep the institutional forms going, on the various administrative tasks that the historic size of the church required and that the current size cannot sustainably support. The actual ministry to the surrounding community has stopped, because there is no energy left for it after the institutional maintenance is done. The institution has, in effect, become its own purpose. The closing in this case redirects the remaining members' energy toward congregations that can actually do ministry, and frees the resources currently consumed by maintenance to be redeployed in support of the broader work.

The third condition is interpersonal toxicity that has accumulated past the point of repair. There are churches whose history includes specific conflicts, betrayals, and unforgiveness that have, over years or decades, produced a relational environment in which no incoming pastor can serve sustainably. The toxicity is often invisible to the congregation, which has adjusted to it over time and treats the dysfunction as normal. The toxicity is visible to the incoming pastor in the first six months and is generally fatal to his ministry within two to three years. The pattern is recognizable across multiple congregations. When the toxicity has reached this point and the structural work to repair it has been refused, the closing is the faithful move. The closing acknowledges that the institutional inheritance is producing harm that the institutional structure cannot resolve.

The fourth condition is the building becoming the primary thing. There are churches whose remaining identity is bound up with their building — the building's history, its architectural particularity, its connection to a specific neighborhood or family lineage — in ways that have, over years, displaced the church's actual mission. The members of the church love the building. They are willing to do significant work to maintain the building. They are not, when the question is honestly asked, willing to do equivalent work to actually be the church in their community. The building has become the church. The church, in any meaningful sense, has stopped existing. The closing in this case allows the building to be sold, often to a different congregation or to a community organization, and frees the members to integrate into churches where the mission is still primary.

These are the conditions. They are not the only conditions, but they are the most common ones in the cases I have walked with through PCN and in my own pastoral experience. The naming of them is intended to make the closure conversation possible in particular congregations where it has been suppressed.

## What faithful closure looks like

The closing of a church is, if done well, one of the most pastoral things a congregation can do. The done-well piece is important, because most church closings I have observed have been done badly, and the badly-done closings produce relational damage that propagates outward into the broader community for years.

The done-well closing has several features.

It is deliberate rather than reactive. The decision is made by the congregation, with appropriate denominational consultation, over a period of months. The decision is not made in a panic at the moment of acute financial crisis. The deliberateness produces a closing that can be received by the congregation as a faithful pastoral choice rather than as a defeat.

It is honored rather than ashamed. The closing of the church is announced publicly. The congregation grieves together. The history of the church is celebrated. The faithful service of generations of members is acknowledged. The closing is treated as the completion of a particular work rather than as the failure of a project. The historic Christian funeral practices apply — the church is dying; the dying is treated with the dignity that any death in the Christian community deserves.

It includes the practical work of transition. The remaining members are commended to other congregations, often with specific introductions and accompanying pastoral conversations. The financial resources of the congregation are transferred to denominational or congregational uses that continue the work the closing congregation can no longer do. The building is sold or transferred in ways that honor the work that has been done in it. The records are preserved. The history is documented.

It includes the appropriate pastoral care of the members who are particularly affected. The closing is hardest on the longest-tenured members, who have known the church through several generations. These members deserve specific pastoral attention through and beyond the closing. The pastor who closes the church remains pastorally available to these members through the transition, even after the institutional form has dissolved.

The closing performed in this way is, in my pastoral experience, often the most theologically rich event a congregation experiences in its final years. The closing makes visible what the congregation has long forgotten — that the church is the body of Christ, that the building and the institution are means rather than ends, that the dying of any particular instance does not threaten the larger work of God in history. The closing is, in some real sense, the last sermon the church preaches. The sermon is about resurrection, in the only way the church can preach it credibly — by being willing to die.

## What gets in the way

I want to name what makes faithful closing hard, because the difficulties are part of why the option has been suppressed.

The denominational structures often resist closure. Denominations have institutional interests in maintaining the number of congregations under their oversight. Some denominations have explicit policies that make closure financially or politically costly. The denominational pressure to revitalize rather than close is, in many cases, a structural pressure that does not serve the actual congregation's interests.

The pastoral identity makes closure hard. Pastors are trained to see their work as the building up of the church. The willingness to close a church can feel, to many pastors, like the abandonment of their vocational identity. The willingness has to be cultivated, and the cultivation is not part of most seminary formation.

The financial dynamics make closure hard. Pastors are paid by the churches they serve. The pastor who recommends closure may be recommending the elimination of his own job. The conflict of interest is real and is rarely acknowledged. The denomination that requires the pastor to make the financial sacrifice without providing alternative work for him afterward is asking too much.

The relational dynamics make closure hard. The congregation includes people who have been members for decades, whose family weddings and funerals happened in the building, whose memories are bound up with the place. The closure asks them to grieve the loss of something that has been part of their identity. The grief is real and deserves pastoral care.

These difficulties are real. They are also not sufficient reasons to keep open a church that should close. The difficulties are part of the cost of doing the faithful thing. The cost can be borne by congregations and pastors who are supported through the process, and the supporting is part of what denominations and broader Christian networks should be doing.

## The verdict

Most churches should not close. Some should. Knowing which is the pastoral judgment of the season. Do not be afraid to make it.

The church that practices its own death is the church that has remembered its own theology. The willingness to die — and to die well — is the willingness to trust that the resurrection is not just doctrine. Some of the churches in your area should close. Some of them should have closed a decade ago. The faithfulness is in the discernment, the honesty, the willingness to perform the funeral the institution has earned.

The funeral is the last sermon the church preaches. Preach it well. The closing is not the failure of the church. The closing is, in the cases where it is the right move, the church being faithful to its own theology one last time. The being-faithful is the witness. The witness is what the church has been for the whole time, and the witness can include, in the appropriate cases, the closing of the doors.

I am writing this for the pastor who has been carrying the closure question without permission to ask it. The permission is here. The question is real. The asking is the beginning of the discernment. The discernment is yours to do, with appropriate consultation, in your particular congregation, under the particular conditions you are facing. Begin. The work is hard and is, in some cases, the work that ministry has actually been calling you to all along.
