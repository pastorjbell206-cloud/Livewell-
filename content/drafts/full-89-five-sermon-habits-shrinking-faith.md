The congregation learns what faith looks like from the pulpit. This is not an exaggeration — it is simply how communities form. What gets preached, repeatedly, shapes what the people believe is possible, what they think God is like, and what they understand Christianity to actually require of them. Which means that the patterns a preacher falls into — over months, over years — do more formative damage than any single bad sermon ever could.

I have preached these patterns. I am naming them from inside, not from above.

**The first is the clean application turn.** The text does its work — raises the question, names the tension, maybe even lets the darkness gather — and then the sermon pivots too quickly to the resolution. Three steps. A practical response. The road home clearly marked. What this communicates to the congregation, over time, is that the Bible is primarily a problem-solving document, that faith is primarily about technique, and that anyone still living in the unreceived part of the promise is probably doing something wrong. Paul wrote Philippians from a Roman prison cell (Philippians 1:13), and the letter does not resolve his situation. It holds it. Preaching that always resolves teaches people that unresolved lives are off the map.

**The second is therapeutic substitution.** The language of psychology is not wrong. But when the sermon consistently translates Scripture into psychological terms — your worth, your healing, your growth journey — without also holding the demands of the text, it has shifted the organizing category from the kingdom of God to the self. The congregation begins to read the Bible primarily as a mirror rather than as a window onto something larger than themselves. Dietrich Bonhoeffer warned, writing from the Germany of the 1930s, about a Christianity that had become so accommodated to the surrounding culture that it could no longer speak a word the culture had not already authorized. Therapeutic preaching is our version of that capitulation.

**The third is intellectual avoidance.** The text has a hard edge — a demand, a claim, a tension with the congregation's received politics or economics or family arrangements. And the sermon slides around it. It is not dishonest, exactly. It simply never arrives at the place where the text becomes genuinely costly. Over time, the congregation learns that the Bible is safe. That it will not ask anything they are not already prepared to give. A faith that never costs anything eventually means nothing.

**The fourth is the celebrity proof-text.** The sermon is built not from the biblical argument but from the accumulated authority of popular Christian voices — this well-known pastor said, that beloved author wrote. The congregation's faith becomes anchored to a personality rather than to the text itself. When that personality eventually falls — and some percentage of them always do — the faith built on their words falls with them. The Bereans in Acts 17:11 are commended specifically because they tested Paul's preaching against the Scriptures themselves. The congregation that has been trained to defer to authority rather than to examine the text has been given a weakened faith.

**The fifth is systematic avoidance of lament.** The psalms of lament — Psalm 88 gives us a full chapter of unanswered complaint, ending without resolution — represent a theological category that the American pulpit has almost entirely abandoned. The preacher who only celebrates, who frames every darkness as a setup for triumph, who cannot sit with genuine suffering and call it by name, creates a congregation ill-equipped to process their own lives when those lives refuse to resolve. Grief is not a phase. Doubt is not a failure. The preacher who cannot give language to those realities is leaving his people without a map for roughly half of what they will experience.

None of these habits are chosen deliberately. They accumulate under pressure, in the direction of least resistance, shaped by what the congregation appears to respond to and what keeps Sunday morning from feeling like a confrontation. I understand the gravity of that. The pulpit is not a laboratory for the preacher's intellectual interests. It is a place of care.

But care that shrinks the faith is not care. It is management.

The congregation is trying to live inside a world that is genuinely hard. They need a faith large enough to hold it. That requires a preacher who has been in the hard places himself — and who refuses to preach from anywhere safer than where the text actually goes.
