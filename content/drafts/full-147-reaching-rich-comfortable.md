The rich young ruler walked away sad (Mark 10:22). Jesus let him go.

This moment tends to make us uncomfortable, so we soften it. We note that Jesus loved him (Mark 10:21), which is true. We point to the broader context about things being possible with God, which is also true. What we do less readily is sit with the plain sequence of events: a wealthy, morally serious, spiritually seeking young man asked Jesus what was required, Jesus told him to sell what he had and give it to the poor, and the man left. And Jesus did not call after him with a more accommodating answer. He watched him go and then told his disciples: it is very hard for the wealthy to enter the kingdom of God.

Very hard. Not impossible, but the disciples' astonishment — "Then who can be saved?" — tells us they heard this as a radical narrowing of expectation. Jesus had just made a theological statement about the relationship between accumulated wealth and the kingdom, and the statement was not comfortable.

The American church has worked very hard to make it comfortable again.

Not explicitly. No one gives a sermon titled "the rich young ruler was wrong to leave." But the theology gets reconstructed through emphasis, through what gets said and what does not, through whose stories are told from the stage and whose names appear on the building and whose comfort level shapes the ministry culture. A congregation that measures its success by the quality of its facilities and the income level of its zip code has made choices about which demographic it is optimized to reach. Those choices are theological, even when they are not named as such.

There are articulate defenses of this orientation. Wealthy people have resources that multiply ministry impact. A congregation with financial stability can fund missionaries, support benevolence, sustain programs. The wealthy need the gospel too, and it would be reverse snobbery to treat their souls as less valuable. To deliberately avoid reaching successful people out of some romantic preference for the poor would be its own error. These arguments are not frivolous.

But they have a tendency to become cover for something less defensible: the decision to build ministry cultures that are comfortable for people with disposable income, located in areas accessible to the affluent, structured around programming sensibilities that reflect middle and upper-class assumptions, and staffed by leaders whose personal financial situation insulates them from the daily precarity that shapes the lives of the poor. None of this is consciously chosen as anti-poor policy. It is the accumulated effect of a thousand small decisions, each of which felt pragmatic rather than theological.

Jesus's ministry did not look like this. He was based in Galilee, not Jerusalem — a provincial region associated with economic marginality and limited cultural prestige. [cite — confirm] His primary audience was peasant farmers, fishermen, people living at subsistence level under both Roman taxation and the temple tax system. The twelve he chose were not drawn from the educated class. When he described the scope of his mission in Luke 4:18, borrowing from Isaiah 61, the list was specific: the poor, the captive, the blind, the oppressed. These are not metaphorical categories. They are descriptions of people at the bottom of every social hierarchy.

Paul makes an observation about the Corinthian church that reads as almost sociological: "Not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful, not many were of noble birth" (1 Corinthians 1:26). The church at Corinth was disproportionately poor, low-status, marginalized. Paul does not treat this as a problem to solve. He treats it as a revelation of God's logic — that God chose the foolish to shame the wise, the weak to shame the strong, the low and despised to bring to nothing the things that are (1 Corinthians 1:27–28).

I have made decisions about where to focus ministry energy that followed the logic of resource efficiency rather than the logic of the cross. I have considered which neighborhoods offered the best growth potential, which demographics were most likely to give, which partnerships would enhance the church's reputation. That is not entirely wrong — stewardship requires thinking about resources. But when it becomes the primary logic, it has displaced something essential.

The cross is not efficient. It reaches down rather than up. It spends toward the lost rather than the found, the marginal rather than the central, the ones who cannot reciprocate rather than the ones who can write a check.

The rich young ruler walked away. Jesus let him go. And then he turned to his disciples and told them the truth about money and the kingdom.

We have been qualifying that truth ever since.
