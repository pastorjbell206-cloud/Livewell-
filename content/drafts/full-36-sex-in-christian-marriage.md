Purity culture taught a generation of evangelicals to view sex as a problem solved by marriage. It is not solved by marriage. It is opened by marriage. The opening is harder than purity culture admitted, and richer than the surrounding culture has any vocabulary for. I want to write about both.

I have spent twenty years pastoring couples through the actual realities of sex in marriage, in conversations that most pastors are not having and most couples have nowhere to take. The conversations are necessary. The substance of what most Christian couples need is not what purity culture or its progressive critics have generally provided. The substance is older, more demanding, and more honest. This essay is the introduction.

## What purity culture promised, and why it failed

The purity culture that dominated American evangelical youth ministry from roughly the early 1990s through the mid-2010s made specific promises. Save sex for marriage, and your marriage will be sexually fulfilling. The unspoken corollary was that the wait would be worth it because the eventual sexual life would compensate for the years of restraint. The promise was framed in different ways by different teachers, but the underlying structure was consistent. Restraint now, reward later. The reward was, in the standard purity-culture telling, frequent and satisfying sex within marriage.

The promise was false in both directions. Many of the couples who waited until marriage did not find the satisfaction they had been promised. The wedding night did not produce the resolution the books had suggested. The first year of marriage often produced more sexual difficulty than the dating relationship had. The expectations the couples brought into marriage created their own problems, because the expectations had been formed by a teaching that did not accurately describe what marital sex actually is.

And many of the couples who did not wait until marriage found, in their own time, satisfying marital sex anyway. The promise had been framed as causal — wait, and the wait will produce the satisfaction. The actual relationship between premarital chastity and marital sexual satisfaction is much more complex than the causal framing suggested. The factors that produce satisfying marital sex are mostly downstream of broader features of the marriage — emotional safety, conflict resolution, mutual care, the shared formation of two people across years. The chastity question is one factor among many, and its weight is smaller than purity culture suggested.

The damage of the false promise has been substantial. The couples who waited and were then disappointed felt betrayed by the teaching that had shaped their formation. The couples who did not wait and felt guilt about their history were carrying a weight that the gospel of grace was supposed to lift. The young women who were taught they were the gatekeepers of male sexuality grew up with a sense of being responsible for the sins of men around them in ways that were psychologically corrosive. The young men who were taught that lust was their primary spiritual problem grew up with a fragmented sense of their sexuality and often without the tools to integrate sexual desire into a coherent Christian life. The damage has been widespread. Linda Kay Klein's *Pure* (2018) is the most thorough recent documentation. The damage has not been adequately acknowledged in most evangelical institutions, and the inadequate acknowledgment has been part of the Gen Z exit story I have written about elsewhere.

## What scripture actually says

I want to recover what scripture actually says about sex in marriage, because the scriptural account is richer than purity culture's truncated version, and richer than the progressive revisionists' counter-version.

Scripture takes sex seriously. The Song of Solomon is a sustained celebration of erotic love between a married couple, and the book has been part of the canon for two thousand years despite repeated attempts to allegorize it away. The celebration is unembarrassed and explicit and is the marital framework that scripture endorses.

Scripture takes sex seriously enough to constrain it. The Genesis 2 framework — *a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh* — is the architectural statement. Sex is the embodied expression of a covenantal union. The union has a specific shape — a man and a woman in covenant — and the sexual expression is the bodily participation in that covenant.

Scripture treats sex within marriage as mutual obligation rather than as one spouse's privilege. 1 Corinthians 7:3–4 is the clearest passage: *the husband should give to his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband. For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does.* The mutuality is more striking in the first-century context than it sounds in modern reading. Paul is naming sexual obligation as reciprocal in a culture that overwhelmingly assumed it was unilateral. The instruction has implications for how couples actually handle sexual frequency and consent within marriage that most contemporary Christian writing has not engaged carefully enough.

Scripture treats sex as theological, not just biological. The marriage in Ephesians 5 is read as a sign of Christ's relationship to the church. The sexual union is not just an exchange of pleasure or even just a covenantal practice; it is a participation in something larger about the nature of God's relationship with God's people. Christopher West's *Theology of the Body Made Simple* (2007) — even if you are not Catholic, the framework is rich — articulates this in ways that protestant evangelical writing has, on the whole, not matched.

These are the resources. They are larger than purity culture used. They are larger than the progressive revisionists have made room for. The recovery of the larger frame is part of the work of contemporary Christian marriage formation.

## What actually produces satisfying sex in marriage

The empirical research on marital sexual satisfaction has been consistent across multiple research traditions, and the findings are worth knowing if you are inside a Christian marriage and have been frustrated by the gap between what purity culture promised and what your marriage has been actually delivering.

Sexual satisfaction in long-term marriage is correlated with emotional safety more than with technique. The couples who report high sexual satisfaction across decades are not, on the whole, the couples who have read the most about sexual technique. They are the couples who have built marriages in which both spouses feel safe, known, honored, and chosen. The bedroom is downstream of the dinner table, as I have written elsewhere. The bedroom does not, in long-term marriage, run on parallel tracks from the rest of the marital relationship. The bedroom is an expression of the marriage's broader health.

Sexual satisfaction is correlated with conflict resolution more than with frequency. The couples who have worked through their conflicts, who have learned to repair after rupture, who have developed practices of return and reconciliation — these couples report higher sexual satisfaction than couples who have not. The avoidance of conflict produces, over time, the sexual distance that comes from emotional distance. The honest engagement with conflict, even when the engagement is hard, produces the emotional intimacy that makes sexual intimacy sustainable.

Sexual satisfaction is correlated with mutual care across all dimensions of the marriage. The husband who carries his share of household labor, who is engaged with the children, who is present for the emotional life of his spouse, is the husband whose marriage produces sustained sexual intimacy. The wife who honors her husband's interior life, who is present to his work and his struggles, who treats his vulnerability with care, is the wife whose marriage produces the same. The reciprocal honoring is what makes the long-term sexual relationship sustainable.

Sexual satisfaction is, surprisingly to many couples, not strongly correlated with frequency. The couples who have sex two or three times a week and the couples who have sex once a month can report similar satisfaction levels if the other factors are present. The frequency question is much more flexible than purity culture's emphasis on it suggested. The couples who have learned to receive what is available, to honor seasons of different frequency, and to keep the broader marital health intact across those seasons are the couples who report long-term satisfaction.

## What this means in practice

I want to offer concrete pastoral counsel for the couple who is inside a Christian marriage and has been frustrated by the gap between what they were taught and what they are experiencing.

First, name what you were taught and how it shaped your expectations. Most couples carry, into their marriages, expectations they have never explicitly articulated about what marital sex should be. The expectations came from purity culture, from the surrounding culture, from family-of-origin patterns, from media. The expectations shape disappointment. The naming of the expectations begins to make them less determinative.

Second, do the broader marital work. The bedroom is downstream of everything else. The couples I have pastored who have done sustained work on conflict resolution, emotional safety, household labor distribution, the family-of-origin patterns I have written about elsewhere — these couples have generally seen their sexual life become more sustainable over years. The work is not direct sexual work. The work is the work of building a marriage in which sex can flourish.

Third, get help if you need it. The couples who are struggling with persistent sexual difficulty have access to Christian therapists, sexual counselors, and pastoral counselors who can help. The willingness to seek help is part of the practice of marriage. The historic shame around sexual difficulty has been one of purity culture's costlier inheritances. The unbinding is available.

Fourth, treat sex as one language of the marriage rather than as the measurement of the marriage. The marriages that survive across decades are the marriages that have learned to speak in multiple languages of love and care and connection. Sex is one. Conversation is another. Shared work is another. Presence in suffering is another. Sustained ordinary kindness is another. The marriage in which sex is the only metric is fragile. The marriage in which sex is one part of a larger flourishing is more sustainable.

## The verdict

Sex is not the goal of marriage. Sex is one of marriage's languages of remaining. The marriage that learns this is the marriage that has stopped panicking about either too much or too little, and has started to do what the language was always for — be known, be honored, remain.

I am writing this for the Christian couple who has been carrying disappointment about their sexual life that no one in their church will help them name. The disappointment is real. The disappointment is also, in many cases, downstream of expectations that purity culture installed and that scripture does not require. The recovery of the older Christian frame is available. The recovery is part of how marriages grow rather than just persist.

Forget what purity culture said. Forget what the surrounding culture says. Read the older Christian tradition on sex in marriage. Get help. Do the broader marital work. Receive what is available. Honor what is sustainable. The marriage you want is built across years through the slow integration of all the marriage's languages, and the bedroom will participate in the integration if the other languages are being spoken honestly. The honest speaking is the work. The work is most of what makes the rest of it possible.
