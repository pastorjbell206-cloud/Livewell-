I have five sons. That fact is not incidental to anything I write. It is the weight that keeps my theology honest. Abstract arguments about intergenerational responsibility become very concrete when you can put five names to the generation that will inherit what you leave behind.

The Scripture's concern for those who come after is not a modern environmental sentiment grafted onto an ancient text. It is structural. The Hebrew word for covenant — berith — carries a temporal depth that extends past the parties present at its making. When God makes a covenant with Abraham in Genesis 15 and 17, the covenant is explicitly extended to his descendants for generations. The law given at Sinai is given to a generation who will die in the wilderness, for the sake of the generation that will enter the land. Deuteronomy is Moses's long sermon to the children of the people who heard the thunder at Horeb. The audience is already once-removed. The obligations are not diminished.

The Deuteronomic vision of faithfulness is explicitly generational: "These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). The covenant shapes parenting. It shapes pedagogy. It shapes the structure of daily life, because the stakes are multigenerational and the consequences of forgetting are borne not just by those who forgot but by those who follow.

## The category the church has avoided

The application of this to climate is uncomfortable for segments of the church, and I want to acknowledge why before making the argument.

The discomfort is partly political. Climate change has become a partisan category in the American context, and any church that touches it risks being heard as aligning with one political party rather than making a theological claim. I understand the wariness. I have felt it. I have also come to believe that the wariness has caused us to surrender the theological category to the political one, which is exactly backwards. The question of what we owe to those who come after us is not a Democrat question or a Republican question. It is a question that Moses asked, that the prophets asked, that every serious theology of covenant has always required an answer to.

The discomfort is also partly economic. The cost of addressing climate change falls, at least in the short term, on people and industries that are part of many congregations' social and economic world. Coal country churches, agricultural congregations, families whose livelihoods are tied to industries whose practices are implicated — these are real pastoral constituencies, and simplistic moralism that ignores their economic reality is not faithfulness. It is cruelty dressed up in prophetic language.

The steel-man case for the current approach is real: maintain political neutrality on contested policy questions, minister to all sides, focus on what the church knows. That is not without wisdom.

But the prophets did not maintain neutrality when the contested policy question was whether Israel would honor the covenant. They named what the generation in front of them was doing to the generations behind them. They named it specifically, with grief and without apology.

## What the prophets said about time

Isaiah's vision of redemption is consistently multigenerational. "You will be like a well-watered garden, like a spring whose waters never fail. Your people will rebuild the ancient ruins and will raise up the age-old foundations; you will be called Repairer of Broken Walls, Restorer of Streets with Dwellings" (Isaiah 58:11-12). The image is of a people who receive something broken, repair it, and pass it forward. The broken walls were left by the generation before. The restoration is for the generation after.

The language of stewardship in Genesis 1 and 2 — the command to tend and keep the garden — is language of responsibility extended through time. The gardener does not exhaust the soil. The gardener passes the soil forward.

Systemic racism is the same category of problem. The wealth gap between white and Black families in America is not the product of different choices made in the present tense by different people. It is the accumulated consequence of policy decisions made over generations — the exclusion from the GI Bill, the redlining of mortgage markets, the discriminatory application of New Deal programs — whose effects compound across time. [cite — confirm] The generation that did not make those decisions inherits their effects and the obligation to address them.

This is not guilt for what your grandfather did. It is responsibility for what you have received and what you will pass on.

## What I owe my sons

When I think about what I am leaving for my five sons, I do not think primarily about financial assets or career opportunities or even theological inheritance, though I care about all of those things. I think about whether they will inherit a world where the poor are still crushed by systems we refused to examine, where the earth is more depleted than it needed to be because we chose convenience, where the injustices that were visible in our generation are still unaddressed in theirs because we were too comfortable to speak.

I have done things that belong to that category. I have been silent when silence was easier. I have benefited from arrangements I did not examine. That is not a confession I make to feel better. It is a statement of where the obligation begins.

It begins with me. It ends with the ones who come after. The distance between those two points is the measure of what faithfulness requires.
