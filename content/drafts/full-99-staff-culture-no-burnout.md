The church staff that cannot keep good people has usually decided, usually without knowing it, that the mission matters more than the people serving it. Not in any document. Not in any stated value. In the daily accumulation of small decisions: the email sent at eleven on a Sunday night that expects a response, the week off that never quite materialized, the meeting scheduled over someone's standing personal commitment, the feedback that is always corrective and never affirming, the sense that the pastor is pleased when things go well and disappointed in the person when they do not.

This is how cultures are actually built. Not by the vision statement. By what happens between the meetings.

I have been that pastor. Not intentionally. But I have looked at the pattern of who left and why and seen in it a reflection of assumptions I carried without examining them — that commitment to ministry was demonstrated by availability, that the people who stayed late cared more than the people who went home, that excellence in the work was its own reward and the person providing it should find that sufficient. These assumptions are common among pastors who came to ministry with genuine calling and genuine zeal. They are also, quietly, brutal — because they treat the people on staff as instruments of the mission rather than as participants in it.

The people who build staff cultures where good people remain are not the people who have discovered a better benefits package. They are the people who have understood that sustainable ministry is only possible for people who are themselves sustainably cared for. Paul told the Thessalonians that he had been gentle among them "like a nursing mother caring for her own children" (1 Thessalonians 2:7) — that his ministry among them was not simply proclamation but the giving of his own life. That posture extends to the people a pastor leads, not only the congregation he serves.

The structural conditions that keep good people are not mysterious. Sustainable workloads — which means the pastor himself has to be willing to say that there is too much and something has to come off. Clear expectations — which means people know what they are responsible for and are not perpetually anxious about whether they are meeting an invisible standard. Genuine affirmation — which is not flattery but specific acknowledgment of specific contributions, given often enough that it does not feel like an event. Genuine development — which means the pastor cares whether the people on his staff are growing, and invests time and attention in that growth rather than treating it as a luxury.

But underneath all of these structural conditions is a theological one: the pastor who believes that the people on his staff are made in the image of God, that their rest and their flourishing and their family life and their inner world are not obstacles to the mission but are themselves part of what the mission is trying to produce — that pastor builds a different kind of team. Not because he has better management instincts, but because he has a different account of what people are for.

Staff cultures that retain good people almost always trace back to a leader who has genuinely internalized this: that the person in front of him is not a resource to be managed toward the ministry's goals. He is a neighbor. One who is owed the same kind of care that the pastor asks his staff to provide to everyone else.

The Sabbath command exists, in part, for this reason. It places a hard stop on the logic of productivity. It insists that even within the people of God, no one gets to extract unlimited labor from another person. That boundary belongs in the staff meeting as much as in the theology classroom.

Build the culture you would want to work inside.

Then build it before you have to.
