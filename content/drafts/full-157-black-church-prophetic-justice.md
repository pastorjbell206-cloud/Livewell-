When white evangelicalism talks about rediscovering the prophetic tradition, about learning to connect justice and the gospel, about the church's responsibility to the poor and the marginalized — when it talks about these things as if they are new discoveries — the Black church in America has been practicing them for four hundred years.

This is not a minor omission. It is a form of historical amnesia with theological consequences.

The Black church emerged under slavery. Its first theologians were people who could not legally own a Bible, who worshiped in secret under penalty of violence, who developed a theology of liberation not as an academic exercise but as a survival technology. The spirituals were not simply religious music. They were a theology of protest and hope — a refusal to accept the slaveholder's gospel, which taught that God had ordained the hierarchy of race and that submission to the master was submission to God. The enslaved communities knew better. They sang about a God who hears the cry of the oppressed, who parts the waters for those who have no other escape, who brings down the powerful and exalts the humble.

They were reading the same Bible the slaveholders were reading. They were drawing very different conclusions — and their conclusions were correct.

Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838 [cite — confirm], wrote in his autobiography about the distinction between the slaveholding religion of the South and the Christianity of Christ. He knew these were not the same thing. He had experienced both: the Christianity that baptized the whip and the Christianity that sustained the faith of those being whipped. The Black church was built on that second Christianity, and it never confused the two.

By the twentieth century, the Black church had developed institutions — seminaries, universities, denominations — that were doing systematic theology and prophetic witness simultaneously. Howard Thurman's *Jesus and the Disinherited* [cite — confirm] articulated the connection between the gospel and liberation with a precision that most white evangelical theology has still not matched. Martin Luther King Jr.'s prophetic ministry was rooted in the Black church's liturgical and theological tradition — his speeches are not political speeches with occasional Bible quotes. They are sermons, structured like sermons, drawing on the full weight of the prophetic tradition that his church had preserved and practiced.

I came to faith from atheism. My early formation was in predominantly white evangelical contexts that said very little about the Black church's theological contribution. When I encountered this tradition later — through reading, through relationships, through the corrective of people who had grown up in it — I recognized that I had been given a partial Christianity. Not false. Partial. A Christianity that had inherited the prophetic language while losing, somewhere in the centuries of comfort and cultural power, the prophetic practice.

The white church is not discovering something new when it talks about justice and the gospel. It is recovering something it lost — or more precisely, something it never had in the same way, because the conditions that produced it were conditions of suffering the white church was never subject to.

There is something important for the white church to understand here. The credibility of prophetic witness is inseparable from the willingness to bear its cost. The Black church's voice on justice has weight because it was forged under pressure. The witness of the enslaved, the witness of those who marched in Birmingham and were beaten on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, the witness of communities that preached resurrection hope while burying their sons — that witness is not a program. It is the product of centuries of practiced faith under conditions that the comfortable church cannot replicate.

What the white church can do is listen. It can acknowledge that the prophetic tradition did not disappear between the writing of the prophets and the present moment. It survived. It was carried by communities the church mostly ignored. It is available now, not as a lesson plan but as an inheritance — if the white church is willing to receive it from the hands that actually held it.

The Black church has been doing this work for centuries. The question is not whether the prophetic tradition is available. The question is whether the white church is willing to learn it from those who kept it alive.
