We have treated creation care as a political category when it is a theological one. That confusion has cost us badly — and I mean the church, and I include myself.

The command arrives in Genesis 2:15 before anything else has gone wrong. Before the fall, before the flood, before any of the arrangements that might make the command conditional or complicated: *The Lord God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it.* The Hebrew word translated "keep" is *shamar* — the same word used for the watchful protection of a shepherd over his flock, the same word the Aaronic blessing employs: *the Lord bless you and keep you.* To keep the garden is not to exploit it. It is to guard it with the attentiveness of someone who knows it belongs to another.

That framing is the one we have most thoroughly abandoned. The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof (Psalm 24:1). Not ours. Not humanity's to dispose of as we choose. We are stewards, which is to say: accountable. Accountable to the one who made it, who declared it good, who will one day judge what we did with it.

The evangelical tradition has been suspicious of creation care for the wrong reasons. Some of that suspicion is understandable — environmentalism has sometimes been accompanied by a thoroughgoing rejection of Christian anthropology, treating humanity as a parasite rather than a creature made in the image of God. That is a real error and worth resisting. But the response to bad theology is not no theology. The response is better theology. And the better theology here is clear: dominion (Genesis 1:28) was never license for destruction. The Greek and Hebrew both carry the weight of responsibility, not only authority. A king who ruins his kingdom has failed as a king, regardless of the power he held.

The dimension the church has been slowest to reckon with is justice. Environmental damage is not evenly distributed. The communities most exposed to polluted air, contaminated water, and the worst effects of a warming climate are, with remarkable consistency, the communities that are already poor, already marginalized, already the ones the prophets named as God's particular concern — the widow, the orphan, the stranger. To be indifferent to environmental destruction while claiming a heart for justice is not a coherent position. You cannot love your neighbor in the abstract and ignore the poison in his water.

I am not positioning this as a partisan argument. I am positioning it as what it is: a biblical argument. Amos condemned those who sold the poor for a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6). The economic and environmental arrangements of our time have their own versions of that transaction — arrangements where the costs of consumption are exported to people who had no vote in the decision. That pattern is not invisible to God. It was not invisible to the prophets. It should not be invisible to the church.

The practical response is not primarily political, though political engagement is not excluded. It is personal and communal. The individual Christian who examines her consumption, who asks whether her ordinary choices are consistent with stewardship, who refuses to treat convenience as a theological trump card — that person is doing something prophetic, regardless of whether it shows up in any legislation. The congregation that reduces its building's environmental impact, that teaches its children to see creation as gift rather than resource, that advocates for clean air and clean water in its community as a justice matter rather than a partisan one — that congregation is bearing witness to the God who made the world and called it good.

We were placed in a garden to work it and keep it. The garden is larger now. The accountability is the same.

To keep it is to begin. We have been slow to begin.
