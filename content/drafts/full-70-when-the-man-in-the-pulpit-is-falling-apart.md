Sometimes the crisis is not in the congregation. It is in the man at the front. A marriage coming apart, a depression that will not lift, a grief too large to preach around, a failure that is about to become public. And the pastor faces a question no class prepared him for: what do you do when the person whose job is to hold everyone else together is the one falling apart?

The first instinct is almost always to hide it, and the instinct is understandable. We have trained pastors to be the strong ones, the steady center, the non-anxious presence. To let the congregation see the collapse feels like a dereliction — as though the man's whole authority depended on appearing intact. So he performs wellness over an open wound, preaches strength he does not have, and spends his last reserves maintaining an image while the real man bleeds out behind it.

But the hiding is usually the deeper mistake, and not only because it is exhausting. It is a mistake because it preaches a false gospel. A pastor who must appear unbroken to keep his standing is quietly teaching his people that the Christian life is the management of an image — that grace is for the congregation but the pastor must be self-sufficient, that the gospel of weakness made strong applies to everyone except the man explaining it. The congregation absorbs that lesson whether or not he ever says it.

There is, of course, wisdom in how and what you disclose. Not every detail belongs in front of the church; some things are not yours alone to tell, and a pulpit is not a confessional booth for the preacher's every wound. Discernment matters, and counsel matters, and there are crises a pastor must walk through privately, held by a few, while continuing to serve. This is not a call to turn the congregation into your therapist. It is a call to stop pretending — to let the people see that their pastor is a man under the same grace he preaches, even when prudence limits the specifics.

Because here is what the congregation actually needs to see, and what will form them more than a hundred polished sermons: a man being a Christian in the collapse. Not a man who never falls apart, but a man who, falling apart, does in public the very things he has told them to do — leans on the body of Christ, refuses to pretend, lets himself be carried, takes his ruin to God without performing his composure. That is not the failure of his witness. That may be the truest sermon he ever preaches, because it is the one where the gospel is finally tested in him and found to hold.

I have been closer to this than I like to admit, and my instinct was always concealment. The fear underneath it was simple: that I was loved for my function, and that if the function faltered the love would go. The healing, when it came, came partly through discovering the fear was a lie — that the people who actually loved me wanted the man, not the performance, and that letting them see the broken version did not cost me my ministry. It deepened it. They had heard me say that we are saved by grace and not by strength. Now they watched me need it.

So if you are the one falling apart, hear this. You do not have to perform your way through it, and you should not. Get help — real help, counsel and friends and possibly time away. Tell the truth at the level prudence allows. And trust that a congregation watching their pastor be honestly, humbly human in a hard season is not losing confidence in the gospel. They are seeing, maybe for the first time, that it is real enough to lean on.

The man in the pulpit will, someday, fall apart. The only question is whether he does it hiding or held. Choose held. It is the truer sermon, and it is the only one that will keep you.
