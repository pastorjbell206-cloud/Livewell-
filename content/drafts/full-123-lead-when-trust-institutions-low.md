The question I am hearing from pastors is not primarily theological. It is credibility. How do you lead people who don't trust you? Not you personally, in most cases — but the institution you represent, the collar or the title or the building that signals to the neighborhood: *this is a church.* That signal, for decades, carried a certain weight. It does not carry the same weight anymore.

Trust in institutions across American society has been declining for several decades. [cite — confirm] The church has not been exempt. Abuse scandals, financial failures, political entanglements, the gap between stated values and institutional behavior — these are not isolated incidents that cynical observers have magnified. They are patterns, documented and repeated, and the people who left because of them are not wrong to have done so. The pastor who leads today does so in the wake of institutional failures he did not personally create but nonetheless inherits. That is the honest starting point.

The wrong response to that inheritance is to deny it. I have watched pastors in this moment try to lead by asserting authority — by leaning harder on title and position and the language of spiritual oversight — and they almost always accelerate the problem. The congregation that is already skeptical of institutions does not become more trusting when the institution performs confidence. It becomes more suspicious.

The right response is slower and harder. It is earning trust in the particular rather than inheriting it from the category. It is the difference between a congregation that trusts the pastor because he is the pastor and a congregation that trusts the pastor because they have watched him be what he says he is over time, in circumstances where it cost him something.

This is not a new problem, even if the scale is. The early church operated in a society with no reason to trust it — a new community making extraordinary claims, meeting in homes, subject to intermittent persecution, without any of the social capital that establishment eventually bestows. What they had was the quality of their common life. Tertullian records, perhaps apocryphally, that outsiders said of the early Christians: *see how they love one another.* [cite — confirm] That was not a communications strategy. It was a community genuinely ordered around something different from the surrounding culture, visible in its daily texture. That visibility was their credibility.

The pastor who wants to lead effectively in a low-trust era will build a congregation that is visible in the same way. Not a congregation that talks about love from the stage, but one in which love is the organizing fact of how its members treat each other in the parking lot, in the hospital room, in the disagreement that could have broken the small group. A congregation that is actually present in its neighborhood before it asks the neighborhood to be present on Sunday. A congregation whose members are known by their neighbors as people who show up when things go wrong.

Transparency matters in this environment in a way it did not when institutional authority was more stable. People who do not trust institutions are watching for the gap between word and deed, because they have been burned by that gap before. The pastor who names the church's failures honestly — not as theater, but as actual reckoning — creates more credibility than the pastor who defends the institution reflexively. Honesty about the past is not a concession to critics. It is the only foundation for genuine trust.

And the content of the Gospel itself is an asset here that pastors sometimes underestimate. The Gospel's central claim is that God entered a low-trust world — a world of corruption, of religious establishment using its power to protect itself, of occupying empires demanding loyalty — and was crucified by it. The resurrection is the announcement that that world does not have the last word. This is not merely a theological position. It is a compelling account of the human situation, one that the person who has been failed by institutions can recognize as true. Preach it like you believe it is true, not like you are managing a brand.

The institution will not regain its former social standing by pretending the last several decades did not happen. What it can do is become, in each particular place, the kind of community that earns what it can no longer assume.

That work starts with one relationship, one kept promise, one honest word.
