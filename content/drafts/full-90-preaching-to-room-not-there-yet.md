Sunday morning holds more than one kind of person. There is the elder who has sat in the third row for twenty years, whose Bible is underlined in four colors, who will correct the Greek if you get it wrong. And three rows back sits someone who came because a coworker kept asking, who does not own a Bible, who is frankly skeptical that any of this is true but is trying to stay open. The question is whether you can preach a sermon that speaks honestly to both of them without lying to either.

Most preachers solve this problem by choosing one audience. Either the sermon is deeply exegetical and the skeptic gets lost by the second point, or the sermon is so accessible that the third-row elder is eating candy for the third week running. Neither of these is preaching. One is graduate seminar. The other is inspirational speaking with cross-shaped props.

The biblical model is more demanding than either. When Paul stood on the Areopagus in Athens (Acts 17:22–31), he was speaking to a room that had no Scripture background, no category for resurrection, no prior commitment to the God of Israel. He did not begin with Genesis. He began with the altar to an unknown god that they had already built — their own intuition that there was something they had not yet accounted for. He moved from what they already knew toward what they had not yet received. He quoted their own poets. He built the argument from inside their frame before he pressed beyond it.

This is not accommodation. Paul did not soften the resurrection — he named it plainly, and some of them mocked him for it (Acts 17:32). What he did was earn the right to be heard by demonstrating that he had actually listened to where they were before he began telling them where to go.

Preaching to the unconvinced does not require abandoning the theological substance that feeds the already-committed. It requires placing that substance inside a frame of genuine questions rather than assumed answers. The skeptic is not opposed to depth. The skeptic is often hungry for it — specifically, for evidence that Christianity can sustain serious intellectual engagement rather than demanding that you check your questions at the door. What drives the unconvinced away is not rigor. It is the sense that the room has already decided, that doubt is unwelcome, that the only acceptable posture is one they cannot honestly hold.

The preacher who says, out loud, "I understand why this is difficult to believe, and here is how I think about that" has done something the unconvinced person almost never expects from the pulpit. He has acknowledged that they are there. He has invited them into the argument rather than past it.

At the same time, preaching toward the skeptic must not become performance of uncertainty. The congregation needs a pastor who has genuine convictions and can say so plainly. What they do not need is a preacher who has resolved every tension in advance, who cannot name a doubt or sit with a hard question because it might undermine his authority. That kind of certainty does not inspire the unconvinced. It reads as insulation.

The room that is not there yet will not be reached by a different style or a better production value. It will be reached by a preacher who has done the intellectual and spiritual work of taking their questions seriously — who has sat with the hardest objections to Christian faith, not as rhetorical problems to be deflected but as genuine challenges that deserve genuine engagement. Keller [cite — confirm] used to say that a sermon should be able to be summarized in a form that a skeptic would recognize as a fair representation of what they actually think, before the argument is mounted. That discipline changes everything about how you prepare.

You cannot preach to the room that is not there yet if you have never spent time thinking inside their categories. That is not a method. It is a posture. And it begins before the sermon starts.
