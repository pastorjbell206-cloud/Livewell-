The Hebrew prophets did not speak primarily about preventing future wrongdoing. They spoke about reckoning with what had already been done. Amos does not say: let us create conditions where injustice is less likely. He says: let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream (Amos 5:24). The image is not a committee. It is a flood.

We have reduced justice to prevention when the tradition demands repair.

The word the prophets use most often for what God requires is not "reform." It is restoration — the return of what was taken, the healing of what was broken, the re-establishment of relationship where relationship was severed. The Jubilee laws in Leviticus 25 were not aspirational. They were calendrical. Every fifty years, the land returned to its original families. Not because the current owners were evil but because accumulated advantage runs against the grain of the covenant. The covenant insists that the damage of dispossession does not become permanent simply because it becomes old.

I have preached about justice for fifteen years and most of what I preached was about stopping harm. I preached against racism and I preached against poverty and I did not preach about what is owed to those who have already suffered under both for generations. I named the injustice. I did not name the debt. Those are not the same sermon.

The Christian tradition has resources here that it has largely failed to deploy. Augustine's doctrine of sin includes not just the acts we commit but the disorder those acts create in the world — a disorder that does not resolve on its own. Thomas Aquinas [cite — confirm] argued that restitution is a precondition of genuine justice, that having wronged someone creates an obligation to repair the wrong, not merely to stop doing it. Luther preached grace freely given, but he did not preach that the consequences of sin are automatically absorbed by it. The cross forgives the sinner. It does not automatically restore what the sinner took.

This matters because the American church has repeatedly confused forgiveness with repair. We have told the survivors of abuse: God forgives your abuser, and closed the door on the question of what the abuser owes. We have told communities fractured by decades of redlining and economic exclusion that God loves everyone equally, and then wondered why that message lands as an insult rather than a comfort. It lands as an insult because it is incomplete. Divine love without divine restitution is not the full gospel. It is half of it.

The parable of Zacchaeus is instructive here (Luke 19:1–10). When Jesus arrives at his house and the people grumble, Zacchaeus stands and makes a declaration: half of what he owns he will give to the poor, and anyone he has defrauded he will repay fourfold. Jesus does not generate this response by first delivering a lecture about structural injustice. He generates it by presence — by arriving at the door of the man everyone had agreed to exclude. And what emerges from that encounter is not simply a changed heart. It is a changed ledger. Salvation came to this house, Jesus says. And salvation looked like math. It looked like fourfold restitution.

The theology of repair is not primarily a political program. It is a theological claim about what justice requires. It says that the stopping of injustice, while necessary, is not sufficient. It says that where damage has been done — to people, to communities, to the conditions of human flourishing — the gospel demands that we address the damage, not merely the behavior that caused it. The truth-and-reconciliation process that South Africa attempted after apartheid [cite — confirm] was not perfect. But it was animated by a theological instinct that the American church has rarely found the courage to inhabit: that reconciliation without truth-telling and restitution is not reconciliation. It is amnesia.

I am not arguing for a single policy position. I am arguing that the church must have the theological vocabulary for this conversation — that when we arrive at the hard questions of what repair looks like, we arrive with the full tradition, not just the convenient half. The God who requires mishpat and tsedaqah is not satisfied with gestures. He is satisfied with justice, which is to say: with the world made more fully right than it was.

The damage is older than we are. That does not make it ours to ignore.

We are the generation standing in the rubble. The question is not whether we caused all of it. The question is what we do now that we are here.
