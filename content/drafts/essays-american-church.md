# Essays — The American Church

Four essays. Honest diagnosis of the institution James has spent his career
inside.

## 17. The Decline Is Real and Mostly Deserved

**Slug:** `the-decline-is-real-and-mostly-deserved`
**Track:** `american-church` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Church attendance is collapsing. The collapse is not the world's fault. The collapse is mostly the church's. We made ourselves unlovable, and the people who could not stand us anymore are the ones who used to fill our buildings.

**Argument beats:**
1. The numbers. Pew, Barna, ARDA. The decline is not a blip. The decline is a transition.
2. The standard church explanation: secularism, media, sexual revolution, etc. These factors are real but secondary.
3. The honest explanation: the church became, over fifty years, something difficult to admire. Sex-abuse cover-ups. Race blindness. Anti-intellectualism. The politicization of the pulpit. Prosperity theology. Discipleship-by-entertainment. The seeker-sensitive movement that gave up the depth to chase the numbers.
4. The receiving end: a generation that watched their parents' churches and could not see Christ in them. The exit was rational. The exit was, in some cases, the most Christian thing the leaver could do.
5. The defensive responses we will be tempted to offer. None of them is right. The first move is repentance, and the repentance has to be specific.
6. The verdict: the decline is judgment. Judgment is not always bad news. Judgment is sometimes the love that does what nothing else has been able to do.

**Opening sketch:** "Church attendance in America is collapsing. Most of my fellow pastors blame the culture. The blame is partial truth and full lie. The decline is mostly our fault. We made the church difficult to admire, and the people who could not admire us anymore are the ones who used to fill our buildings. The repentance starts here."

**Closing weight:** Judgment that comes through decline is still judgment. Judgment that ends in repentance is still mercy. The American church is being judged. Whether the judgment ends in mercy is up to the church, not to the culture. The culture has already left. The question is whether the church will become the kind of church the culture might have wanted to stay for, had we been it the first time.

**Sources:** Pew Research on the "Nones"; Robert Putnam (*American Grace*); David Kinnaman (*UnChristian*; *You Lost Me*); Mark Noll; James Davison Hunter (*Democracy and Solidarity*).

## 18. The Megachurch Was Always Going to End This Way

**Slug:** `the-megachurch-was-always-going-to-end-this-way`
**Track:** `american-church` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The American megachurch is collapsing publicly — scandal, decline, fragmentation. The collapse is not a surprise. The model contained the seeds of the collapse from the beginning.

**Argument beats:**
1. The model. Centralized celebrity teaching. Volunteer corps. Marketing-driven attraction. Programmatic depth replaced by experiential intensity. Discipleship through small groups that were small only relative to the auditorium.
2. The hidden costs that are now visible: the pastor-as-CEO who is accountable to no elder board he did not choose. The branding that becomes the church. The volunteer burnout. The leadership succession that is impossible because the model is built around the celebrity.
3. The scandals are not aberrations. The scandals are what the model produces. (Name names carefully or speak in generalities — your call, but the pattern is undeniable.)
4. The Catholic and mainline lessons: every institution that lacks accountability eventually produces what the megachurch has produced. The protestant evangelical assumption that we were immune was always wrong.
5. The constructive: smaller, older, more accountable. The historic protestant churches got something right that the megachurch tradition rejected and is now relearning at cost.
6. The verdict: the megachurch model was never sustainable. The future is small.

**Opening sketch:** "I have watched, over twenty years, three megachurches near me collapse in scandal and seven more shrink in slow decline. The collapses look surprising in the news cycle. They are not surprising. They were structural. The model produces what we are seeing. The model was always going to produce what we are seeing. The only surprise is how long the music kept playing."

**Closing weight:** Small churches with old liturgies and slow rhythms will outlive the megachurches. They have, every other time the empire's attention has moved on. The era we are entering is good news for the small faithful church. It is the end for the model that overtook us in the eighties. The end has been coming for a while. Most of us were just not willing to say so out loud.

**Sources:** Ronald Sider (*The Scandal of the Evangelical Conscience*); David Wells (*God in the Wasteland*; *Above All Earthly Pow'rs*); Glenn Packiam (*The Resilient Pastor*); Kate Bowler (*The Preacher's Wife*).

## 19. The Black Church Has Always Been the Faithful Remnant

**Slug:** `the-black-church-has-always-been-the-faithful-remnant`
**Track:** `american-church` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~12 min

**Positioning sentence:** While white American evangelicalism was busy building Christendom and writing theology to defend it, the Black church was holding the gospel against the worst the empire could do. The Black church is not a footnote in American Christianity. It is, by every honest measure, the faithful remnant.

**Argument beats:**
1. The history. Slavery-era hush harbors. The Black church under Jim Crow. The civil-rights movement as the most explicitly Christian political movement in American history. The careful theological work of Howard Thurman, James Cone, J. Deotis Roberts.
2. The contrast: the white Southern church that defended slavery from the pulpit; the white Northern church that mostly remained silent; the white American church that still has not fully reckoned with either.
3. What the Black church has held that the white church has not: an honest theology of suffering; a prophetic critique of empire; a worship tradition that took the embodied dignity of all people seriously; a politics that refused to baptize either Caesar.
4. The current moment: the Black church is the part of American Christianity that has most consistently refused Christian nationalism. The pattern is not coincidental. The Black church already knew what cultural Christianity costs when you are the people it costs against.
5. The constructive: white American Christians who want to know what faithful church looks like in a post-Christendom moment should not be inventing it. They should be learning it. The Black church has been doing it for four hundred years.
6. The verdict: the future of faithful Christianity in America has, in many ways, already been written by the Black church. The question is whether the rest of us will read.

**Opening sketch:** "I write this essay as a white pastor who has spent a long time learning what I should have learned earlier. The Black church in America is, by every honest historical measure, the more faithful church. While white American evangelicalism was building Christendom, the Black church was being the church under conditions that should have destroyed it and did not. I am writing this essay because the white American Christians I know need to read what the Black church has been writing all along."

**Closing weight:** The Black church has been doing what we are about to need to learn how to do — be the faithful church under an empire that is not Christian, and may have never been. The Black church figured this out under conditions worse than anything most of us will ever face. We do not need to invent the post-Christendom future. We need to read the people who have already been living it. The reading is overdue.

**Sources:** Howard Thurman (*Jesus and the Disinherited*); James Cone (*The Cross and the Lynching Tree*); Jemar Tisby (*The Color of Compromise*; *How to Fight Racism*); Esau McCaulley (*Reading While Black*); Kelly Brown Douglas; Vincent Harding.

## 20. Why Gen Z Left, and What Would Bring Them Back

**Slug:** `why-gen-z-left-and-what-would-bring-them-back`
**Track:** `american-church` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~12 min (long-read)

**Positioning sentence:** Gen Z is the most disaffiliated generation in American religious history. The reasons they left are mostly reasons the church should have already corrected. The path back is narrower than we want it to be, and it does not lead through the strategies the church has tried.

**Argument beats:**
1. The numbers. Gen Z disaffiliation rates. The trajectory. The fact that they are not "rebelling" — they are leaving without drama.
2. Listen to them. Cite the actual interview studies. The reasons cluster: the church's politicization; the church's mishandling of sexuality; the church's anti-intellectualism; the church's complicity in racism; the church's smallness in the face of suffering.
3. The strategies that will not work: louder worship music, social-media presence, hipper pastors, more "relevant" sermons. These are the strategies the church has been running. They have not worked. They will not work. They mistake what is being rejected.
4. What might work: honest acknowledgment of what we got wrong; intellectual seriousness about the hard questions; refusing the partisan trap; actual hospitality (which is the one thing the church can still offer that the surrounding culture is no longer providing).
5. The hardest truth: most of Gen Z is not coming back to the church we are. Many will come to a church we have not yet become. The becoming is the work.
6. Self-implication: I have failed Gen Z in specific ways from the pulpit. Naming them now.
7. The verdict: the church that draws Gen Z back will be a church most current pastors will not recognize. Whether we are willing to become it is the question.

**Opening sketch:** "Gen Z is the most religiously disaffiliated generation in American history. They did not leave the church the way I left the church — angrily, with the door slammed. They left quietly, the way you leave a party you were not enjoying. Most of them are not coming back. Some of them will come to a church we have not yet become. I want to describe what that church looks like."

**Closing weight:** Gen Z is not coming back to the church the church spent thirty years trying to build for them. The seeker-sensitive megachurch did not keep them. The hip urban church plant did not keep them. The reformed-young-restless movement did not keep them. The post-evangelical progressive church is not keeping them. They are leaving everything. They will return — some of them — to a church that has not yet been built, and has not yet existed, and that the rest of us are going to have to become. The becoming is a generation's work. We are not far enough into it. We are barely begun.

**Sources:** Jean Twenge (*iGen*); David Kinnaman (*You Lost Me*); Ryan Burge (*The Nones*); Christian Smith (*Souls in Transition*); the Springtide Research Institute reports; the work of Hannah Anderson and Jen Wilkin on the unique formation tasks of post-Christendom catechesis.
