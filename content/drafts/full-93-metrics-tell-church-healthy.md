Ask the average pastor how his church is doing, and he will tell you a number. Attendance, usually. Sometimes giving. Sometimes both, in a ratio that signals whether things are moving in the right direction. These are the metrics the denomination wants, the metrics that appear on the annual report, the metrics that determine whether a pastor is perceived as effective. They are also, largely, the wrong metrics.

Not because they do not matter. They do — an empty room is a real problem, and a church that cannot fund its operations is a church in genuine crisis. But attendance and giving are lag indicators. They tell you what happened, not what is happening. They measure the output of a system whose real activity is largely invisible to any spreadsheet.

The first question a pastor ought to be able to answer honestly is this: are people in this congregation becoming more like Jesus? Not more committed to the church's programs. Not more knowledgeable about the Bible's content — though that matters too. More like Christ — more generous with their material resources, more patient in conflict, more willing to extend mercy to people who have not earned it, more honest about their own failures. This is formation. And it is almost never measured because it is very difficult to measure, and the difficulty is not a reason to stop asking.

A second honest question is: do people in this congregation actually know each other? Not know of each other. Know each other — their names, their children, their anxieties, the particular weight they carry that is not visible on a Sunday morning. The early church in Jerusalem is described in Acts 2:44–45 as a community that held goods in common and distributed to anyone who had need. That kind of generosity requires a level of mutual knowledge that is not produced by sitting in the same room once a week. It requires community that has gone deeper than attendance.

A third is harder: are people being sent from this congregation into the world with a genuine sense of vocation? The church that produces spiritually formed, deeply connected members who stay inside the church's walls and serve only its programs has not completed its mission. The measure of a healthy church is not only what happens inside its gathering, but what its people carry into their work, their neighborhoods, their families, their civic lives. The New Testament does not describe the church as an end in itself. It is the gathering that prepares people for the scattering.

The steel-man of the numbers-driven approach is real and should be heard: without adequate attendance, no pastor long remains to form anyone. Without adequate giving, programs that serve the vulnerable cannot be sustained. Institutional viability is not nothing — it is a precondition for everything else. I am not dismissing the spreadsheet. I am insisting that the spreadsheet cannot tell you whether the gospel is doing what it promises to do.

The pastor who reports good numbers to his board while privately knowing that his congregation is unchanged, unformed, and barely acquainted with each other is reporting on the wrong things. He is also, likely, carrying a private weight that the numbers cannot address — because the numbers look right and something still feels wrong, and he cannot say what it is to people who are reading the spreadsheet.

What feels wrong is that the metrics he is using cannot see what he is actually responsible for.

Name what you are measuring. Then ask whether those measurements are capturing the right thing.

If they are not, change the measurements first.
