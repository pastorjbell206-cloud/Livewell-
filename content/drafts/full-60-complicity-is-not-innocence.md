The comfortable Christian position in a culture with real injustice is almost never endorsement. Very few believers actively defend the thing that crushes the vulnerable. The comfortable position is also not resistance — that costs too much. It is something quieter, something that feels like neutrality from the inside and looks like consent from the outside: the decision not to get involved, not to take sides, not to make the faith "political." We have a word for what we think that is. We call it innocence. Scripture calls it something else.

It calls it complicity. And complicity is not innocence.

We badly want there to be a safe third option between doing wrong and paying the cost of doing right — a place where we can keep our hands clean by keeping them in our pockets. The Bible keeps refusing to grant it. "If you faint in the day of adversity, your strength is small," says Proverbs, just before: "Rescue those who are being taken away to death; hold back those stumbling to the slaughter. If you say, 'Behold, we did not know this' — does not he who weighs the heart perceive it?" (Proverbs 24:10–12). The text anticipates our favorite excuse — *we didn't really know* — and dismisses it. The God who weighs hearts is not fooled by a silence we have arranged to keep ourselves comfortable.

This is the priest and the Levite on the Jericho road (Luke 10). They did not beat the man. They did not rob him. They did nothing at all — they passed by on the other side — and Jesus tells the story precisely so that their nothing is the sin. The Samaritan is the neighbor not because he avoided harm but because he absorbed cost. The parable was built to take away the third option. There is no clean way to walk past the man in the ditch.

I have walked past. I have told myself that staying out of it was wisdom, that not taking sides was maturity, that my silence was a careful neutrality rather than a quiet vote for things to stay as they are. The honest name for most of my neutrality was not prudence. It was self-protection. I did not want the cost, so I called my unwillingness *balance*. This is a confession before it is a critique.

Here is the mechanism worth understanding, because it is how good people end up complicit without ever deciding to be. Injustice does not usually require the cooperation of the cruel. It requires only the silence of the decent. The people running the slave ship needed the people on shore to look away, and looking away is easy, and it never feels like participation. It feels like minding your business. But in a world where someone is being taken away to death, minding your business is a position, and it is the position that lets the death proceed.

There is a real danger on the other side, and honesty requires naming it: the church can become so addicted to taking sides that it loses the gospel in the noise, treating every issue as a hill and burning its witness on a hundred lesser fights. Complicity is a sin; so is the reflexive outrage that mistakes a comment thread for the Jericho road. The call is not to have an opinion about everything. It is to refuse the lie that silence is automatically safe — to know that when the suffering is real and the cost of speaking is the only thing holding our tongue, the silence is not neutral.

The witness is what hangs on this. The world is not mainly persuaded or repelled by the church's words. It watches what the church will *risk*. A church that always finds a reason to stay out of it — that is reliably absent from the ditch — teaches the world that the gospel is a private comfort with no claim on public suffering. And the world is right to find that unconvincing.

There is no innocent bystander on the Jericho road. There is the one who stops and the one who passes by. The passing by always feels like nothing. It was never nothing.
