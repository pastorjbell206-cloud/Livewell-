The word the New Testament uses for the church — ekklesia — means "those called out." Not called out into withdrawal. Called out from a previous set of allegiances, a previous way of organizing your life, a previous set of answers to the question of what you ultimately serve.

That calling is not primarily about behavior modification, though it produces behavioral changes. It is about a reordering of loves — what Augustine called the ordo amoris, the right ordering of what you love most. When that ordering shifts, everything else shifts with it. You no longer make decisions from the same center. You no longer evaluate the same things as ultimate. The culture around you will notice.

And when it notices, you have a choice.

The pressure to conform is not new. The Christians in Rome in the first century lived in a culture saturated with imperial theology — the claim that Caesar was lord, that the empire's flourishing was the measure of all things, that participation in the civic religion was the price of belonging. The early church's refusal of that participation was not experienced as mere religious preference. It was experienced as subversion. "Christians to the lions" was not a cultural slogan against people with unusual private spiritual beliefs. It was a slogan against people whose private spiritual beliefs produced public non-conformity, and the public non-conformity was felt as a threat.

The threat was accurate. The gospel does threaten. Not with violence — the early church did not wield that. But with an alternative account of reality that, if believed, reorganizes everything the surrounding culture depends on remaining unquestioned.

This is where the church in post-Christian America faces a particular temptation. We inherited the social form of cultural Christianity — the assumptions, the language, the institutional presence — without always inheriting the content that made it distinctive. We kept the form. The form is now dissolving. And the question is whether, in the dissolution, we will rediscover the content that actually gives us something to say, or whether we will spend our remaining social capital trying to restore the form.

The content — the actual, costly, countercultural claims of the gospel — is what the world around us has no reason to expect and every reason to be surprised by. A community that holds its possessions loosely. A community that welcomes people the world discards. A community where rank is expressed as service rather than as privilege. A community where forgiveness is not naive but is offered precisely because we have received something we did not earn. These practices are strange. They are meant to be strange.

I have made peace with the strangeness, though it took longer than it should have. For years I wanted a Christianity that could be presented as continuous with the aspirations of mainstream American culture — therapeutic, affirming, life-improving, broadly agreeable. A Christianity that did not ask too much. What I discovered, slowly, was that a Christianity that does not ask too much also does not offer too much. It offers adjustment, not liberation. It offers improvement, not resurrection.

The courage the gospel requires is not primarily the courage to confront culture publicly, though it sometimes includes that. It is first the courage to be formed differently — to let the practices of the faith, the Sabbath and the table and the prayer and the community, actually shape you into someone whose instincts no longer match the default settings. That formation is slow. It is unglamorous. But it produces people who are genuinely different, not by effort but by nature, and the difference is its own kind of witness.

The church that looks exactly like the culture has nothing to offer the culture. It can only reflect back what was already there.

The church that has been formed differently, that carries the strangeness of the gospel in its actual life together, becomes a sign — an evidence that the world as it is does not have to be the world as it must be.

That sign costs something to bear. It always has.

The question is whether we love what the gospel promises enough to bear what the gospel requires.
