The cross of Christ is the center of the Christian faith. Every tradition affirms this. But ask what the cross actually *accomplished* — the mechanism, the inner logic of how a Roman execution two thousand years ago saves anyone — and the agreement breaks. The history of the church is full of theories, and the fights between their defenders have been fierce. The mistake in most of those fights is the assumption that there must be one model, and that to hold one is to reject the others. The New Testament does not hold one. It reaches for many, and means all of them.

Consider the images Scripture itself uses. The cross is a *ransom* — a price paid to free captives (Mark 10:45). It is a *victory* — Christ disarming the powers and triumphing over them, leading death itself in defeat (Colossians 2:15). It is a *sacrifice* — the Lamb of God, the once-for-all offering that the whole sacrificial system was straining toward (Hebrews 9–10). It is *substitution* — the righteous for the unrighteous, the punishment that brought us peace falling on him (Isaiah 53:5; 1 Peter 3:18). It is *reconciliation* — enemies made friends, the far brought near (Romans 5:10; Ephesians 2). It is even *example and revelation* — the love of God displayed so that it might break and remake the human heart (Romans 5:8).

The tradition gave these names and refined them. The early church often spoke of *Christus Victor*, the cross as God's defeat of sin, death, and the devil. Anselm, in the eleventh century, framed it as *satisfaction* — the restoration of an honor that human sin had violated. The Reformers sharpened the substitution language into *penal substitution* — Christ bearing the legal penalty in our place. Others have emphasized the *moral influence* of the cross, its power to transform by demonstrating love. Here is the error that has done the most damage: taking one of these images, declaring it *the* gospel, and treating the others as optional or suspect. Whole camps have done it. Some make penal substitution the whole story and grow suspicious of anyone who leads with victory or love. Others recoil from substitution entirely, embarrassed by its courtroom logic, and lose the thing Isaiah and Peter plainly say. Both have done the same thing: taken a true image and made it the only one, which turns a true thing into a distortion by amputation.

I have argued these corners myself, with more heat than light. I defended one model as though the cross would collapse without my defense, and treated brothers who emphasized another as though they were soft on the gospel. What I was actually defending, much of the time, was my tribe's vocabulary — the words I had been formed in — not the fullness of what God did. The cross did not need my narrowing. It needed me to grow large enough to hold what it actually contains.

Because the reality the images point to is bigger than any one of them. The cross really is a victory *and* a substitution *and* a sacrifice *and* a reconciliation *and* a revelation of love — not because we cannot decide, but because the thing that happened there is too large for a single human metaphor to carry. Each image is a true window onto the same event. To stand at only one window is to see something real and to miss the rest of the view.

This is not a plea for vagueness. Precision matters; some accounts of the atonement genuinely distort it, and not every theory is equally faithful to the text. The plea is for amplitude — for letting the New Testament's own range be ours, for holding the courtroom and the battlefield and the altar and the open arms together, because Scripture does.

The cross is the center. It is also bigger than the center you were taught to stand on. Stand there still — but turn around, and see how much more of it there is.
