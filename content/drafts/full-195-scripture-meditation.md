At some point in pastoral ministry — and I cannot tell you precisely when it happened to me — the Bible became a resource rather than a word addressed to me. I read it professionally. I exegeted it carefully. I tracked the Hebrew roots, consulted the commentaries, traced the canonical echoes. I became, in some technical sense, more competent with the text. And something essential was lost.

The Psalms describe a different kind of engagement: "I will meditate on your precepts and fix my eyes on your ways" (Psalm 119:15). The Hebrew word there, *hagah*, carries the image of a low murmuring — the sound of a person turning a text over in the mouth, letting it slow in the mind, returning to it not for information but for encounter. This is not the posture of preparation. It is the posture of reception. The pastor who never shifts from one to the other is reading with his hands but not with his chest.

The practical consequence of this collapse is subtle and severe. You can prepare an exegetically responsible sermon on the Good Shepherd while having spent no time letting the text speak to you as a sheep. You can preach the wilderness wanderings with historical precision while your own soul is dry and directionless in ways you have not named. The congregation receives the information and never receives what only comes from the pastor who has been somewhere with God in the text. They can feel the difference. Most cannot articulate it.

Scripture meditation — the old tradition, the one practiced by the desert fathers, by the monastic communities of the Middle Ages, by the Puritans who spoke of *meditatio* and *ruminatio* — is not a technique for sermon improvement. It is a way of allowing the Word of God to do to you what it is designed to do: expose the condition of your heart, reorient your loves, and lodge itself deeply enough to shape what comes out of you without your engineering it. It is slow. It is uncomfortable. It does not produce anything you can put in a sermon outline. That is exactly why it is irreplaceable.

I have had the experience of sitting with a single verse for twenty minutes — not studying it, just sitting with it, reading it aloud slowly, letting questions arise without rushing to answer them — and finding that something shifted in me. Not dramatic. Not what anyone would call a vision. Something quieter: the beginning of a truthful self-assessment I had been avoiding, or a recognition of grace in a corner of my life I had been treating as a problem to manage. This is what the text does when you let it. When you stop approaching it only as a preacher and allow it to approach you as a person.

The practical shape of this is not complicated, but it requires the one thing most pastors feel they cannot give: time that has no measurable output. Not preparation time. Not study time. Time when you sit with a text and do not write anything down because the work being done is not on paper. The medieval church called this *lectio divina* — sacred reading — and structured the monastic day around it. [cite — confirm] The Reformers retained the discipline even as they reshaped its form. The tradition is not marginal or exotic. It is ancient and central and the contemporary church has largely forgotten it in favor of Bible studies optimized for applicational clarity.

For the pastor especially, the stakes are high. Ministry creates a relentless pressure to consume the Scripture instrumentally — for the sermon, for the counseling session, for the answer to the theological question someone asked after service. All of that is legitimate. None of it is sufficient. The pastor who meets the text only as a professional eventually runs out of things to say that cost him anything. The sermon becomes competent. The competence becomes empty. The congregation senses something is missing and wonders if it is them.

It is not them.

The Word does not need better exposition. It needs a pastor who has been sitting in it long enough that it has begun to expose him. Return there. Not for the sermon. For yourself.

That is where the ministry actually starts.
