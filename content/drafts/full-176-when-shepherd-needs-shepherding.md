You have sat in the hospital room at midnight. You have driven across town to sit with a family that just got the worst phone call of their lives. You have stood at graves in February weather and said the words that hold people together when nothing else will. You know how to shepherd. You have been doing it for years.

So let me ask you a question, and I want you to sit with it before you answer: Who shepherds you?

Most pastors I know go quiet when I ask that. Some give an answer that names a spouse or a mentor or a close friend, and when I probe it, what I find is that the relationship they're describing is one where they process the work of ministry — the logistical difficulties, the difficult people, the institutional problems — but not the interior of their own souls. There is a difference. The person who listens to you talk about your elders is not necessarily the person who is watching your soul.

The loneliness of pastoral leadership is real and it is structurally produced. The congregation needs you to be the shepherd. The role itself creates the expectation that you are the strong one, the one with answers, the one who holds the weight for others rather than laying it down. The role makes it very difficult to be known in the way that pastoral care requires being known — with your failures and fears and doubts visible rather than managed.

## What the Gospel you preach actually says

You were never designed to carry the weight of ministry alone. The Gospel you preach says so. It says that the human self in isolation is not the design — it is the fracture. "It is not good for man to be alone" is not a comment about marriage, at least not exclusively. It is a description of the structure of human existence. We are made for dependence on God and for interdependence with one another. The pastor who has constructed a ministry life in which he is the one who gives care and never the one who receives it has not achieved spiritual maturity. He has reproduced in himself the very isolation that the Gospel came to address.

Paul asked for prayer. Repeatedly. The man who wrote "I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13) also wrote "pray for us" (1 Thessalonians 5:25) and "I appeal to you, brothers, by our Lord Jesus Christ and by the love of the Spirit, to strive together with me in your prayers to God on my behalf" (Romans 15:30). He was not performing humility. He was naming a real dependence. The apostle needed the church's prayer in the same way the church needed his teaching. The relationship was mutual.

The pastor who cannot ask for prayer — who cannot name his own need to the people he leads — has confused professional distance with spiritual strength. They are not the same.

## What isolation produces

Pastoral isolation rarely announces itself. It develops slowly, across years, as the weight of holding confidences separates you from the community, as the role creates distance, as the fear of appearing weak makes you progressively less willing to name what is actually happening inside you.

And then one day you look up and you realize that you have no one. No one who knows the actual state of your marriage, not just the pastoral-care version of it. No one who knows that you have been having the same doubt for three years. No one who would notice if you stopped praying, because no one is close enough to see. The isolation is comfortable in the way that numbness is comfortable — you stop feeling the lack because you've adapted to it.

I have been in that place. I have sat in my study after a Sunday morning where everything went well — the service was strong, the people were encouraged, the feedback was warm — and felt something very close to despair, because the performance of pastoral health and actual pastoral health are two entirely different things, and I had been performing for long enough that I had lost track of which one I was.

## What shepherding the shepherd requires

It requires someone who will not treat you primarily as a pastor. A peer, a spiritual director, a counselor who has no stake in the church's success, a friend whose relationship to you predates and exists outside the ministry — someone who will ask the real question and wait for the real answer. Not "how's the church going" but "how are you, actually."

It requires that you stop protecting the image long enough to answer that question honestly. Not for the sake of emotional processing, but for the sake of your soul. The pastor whose interior life has not been named and witnessed by another human being in years is a pastor whose interior life is likely deteriorating in ways that will eventually show up in the pulpit, in the marriage, in the slow erosion of the capacity for genuine pastoral care.

The Psalms gave David a space to name what was happening inside him, in all its violence and darkness and grief. The fact that those prayers are now in the canon means that God received them. But David also had Nathan. Nathan was the one who walked into the room after David's worst decision and said: "You are the man" (2 Samuel 12:7). The prophet named what the king could not see about himself.

You need a Nathan. Not an enabler. Not a flatterer. Not someone who needs you to be strong. Someone who will tell you the truth.

Who is that person for you right now? If you can't name anyone, that is the most important pastoral problem you have. Not the difficult elder. Not the declining attendance. This.

The shepherd needs shepherding. The Gospel you preach has always known this.
