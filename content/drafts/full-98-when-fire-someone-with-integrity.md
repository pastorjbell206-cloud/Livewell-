No one who came to ministry out of a calling to care for people imagined that one of the most pastorally demanding acts they would ever perform would be firing a staff member. And yet it is. Because the person on the other side of that conversation is a person — someone with a mortgage and a family and an identity that is probably substantially wrapped up in the role you are about to remove them from — and how you carry out that conversation will shape what they believe about the church, and possibly about God, for a long time afterward.

This is why pastors avoid it too long. Not because they are cowardly, though sometimes that is part of it, but because the pastoral instinct is toward care — and care, misapplied, can become a refusal to tell the truth.

The first question is whether the situation actually requires termination, or whether it requires a different kind of intervention. Not every personnel failure is a firing offense. A staff member who is struggling with a specific area of performance, who has not received clear feedback and a defined opportunity to improve, who has not been held to an articulated standard — that person has not been given a fair chance to succeed. The pastoral leader who fires someone without having done the harder work of honest feedback and genuine support has not acted with integrity. He has protected himself from the discomfort of a development conversation and called it a leadership decision.

The situations that do require termination are clearer than pastors sometimes make them: when someone's behavior violates the ethical or theological standards of the community and the violation is ongoing rather than genuinely repented of; when a staff member is actively damaging the culture, staff relationships, or the congregation's trust, and every reasonable intervention has failed to produce change; when the role itself no longer exists or is being restructured in a way that the current person cannot fill. These are not ambiguous situations. They are painful ones.

When the decision is made, the conversation must be honest and direct. This is where pastoral instinct most often fails. The desire to soften the blow — to leave the person with hope that is not real, to imply that the door is not closed, to attribute the termination to circumstances rather than to the actual problem — is understandable and harmful. The person being released deserves to know the truth. Not every detail, not with cruelty, but clearly. They will construct the narrative of what happened either way. Giving them an accurate account allows them to grieve honestly and learn from the experience. Giving them a false one leaves them confused and likely to repeat the same patterns in the next role.

Paul's instruction to speak truth in love (Ephesians 4:15) does not permit a choice between truth and love — it insists on both simultaneously. The termination conversation that is all love and no truth leaves someone unable to understand what happened. The conversation that is all truth and no love causes unnecessary damage to someone who is already losing something significant. Both elements must be present.

The pastoral care does not end with the conversation. What happens to the person after they leave matters — whether the severance is generous, whether the public communication about the departure is honest without being exposing, whether the pastor makes himself available if the person needs to process what happened. The church is not simply an employer. How it treats the people it releases is a statement about what it believes about the worth of human beings.

I have released people from staff. It has never been easy. The ones I did with the most integrity were the ones where I told the truth, did it directly, provided what I could for the transition, and did not leave the person wondering what had actually happened. The ones I am least proud of are the ones where I delayed too long, softened the truth too much, and left the person without the clear accounting they deserved.

Do it clearly. Do it with care. Do not do it later than it should be done.

The ministry is not served by the compassion that cannot act.
