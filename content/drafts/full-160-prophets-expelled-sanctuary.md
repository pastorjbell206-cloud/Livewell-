Amos was told to go home. Amaziah the priest of Bethel sent him word directly: "Get out, you seer! Go back to the land of Judah. Earn your bread there and do your prophesying there. Don't prophesy anymore at Bethel, because this is the king's sanctuary and the temple of the kingdom" (Amos 7:12-13).

The phrase is worth sitting with. "The king's sanctuary and the temple of the kingdom." Not God's sanctuary. The king's. The institution of worship had become an institution of the political order, and the political order did not welcome a man who said that God's measuring line had found the wall crooked.

Amos was not expelled because he was wrong. He was expelled because he was right about something the institution had organized its life around not seeing.

The pattern repeats across the prophets with a consistency that ought to disturb us. Jeremiah was thrown into a cistern by the officials of the court (Jeremiah 38:6). He had preached in the temple courts that the temple itself would be destroyed if the people did not change their ways — and the priests and prophets who served the institution seized him and threatened him with death (Jeremiah 26:8-9). Isaiah's tradition remembers him as a man who spent his life in conflict with the official cult and the political establishment simultaneously. The writing prophets of the eighth and seventh centuries BC were not peripheral figures offering mild moral guidance. They were dangerous. Their message was dangerous because it told the truth about what the institutions were doing and who they were serving.

The institutions responded the way institutions respond: by treating the truth as a threat to order.

I want to be careful here, because the ease with which a pastor can identify with the expelled prophet while sitting safely in his own pulpit is itself a form of self-deception. I am not Amos. I am not Jeremiah. I preach in a building I did not build to a congregation that can leave if they dislike what they hear, and the worst consequence of a difficult sermon is a difficult conversation on Sunday afternoon. This is not the prophetic condition.

But the structural dynamic that Amos describes is not ancient history. The church in every generation has to ask: what has the sanctuary become the sanctuary of? What is it protecting? Whose interests is it serving when it falls silent?

Jeremiah's temple sermon in chapters 7 and 26 is one of the most unsettling passages in the Old Testament precisely because the target of his indictment is not the pagan nations but the people of God gathered at their most sacred site. "Has this house, which bears my Name, become a den of robbers to you?" (Jeremiah 7:11). Jesus would quote this line in the temple courts six centuries later. The point was the same: the religious institution had become a cover for injustice rather than a challenge to it. It had become useful to power rather than dangerous to it.

The prophet who is welcomed by the institutional religious establishment is not, as a rule, performing prophetic ministry. The prophet who makes the establishment comfortable has accommodated himself to its needs rather than the needs of the God who sent him. This does not mean every conflict is prophetic. Some conflicts are ego. Some are bad theology. Some are just personality. But the pattern across the biblical prophets is clear: the word of God, when it arrives at the sanctuary, is rarely comfortable for those who run the sanctuary.

I have not been expelled from my pulpit. I have expelled myself from certain truths that would have made me unwelcome in it. I have made calculated decisions about what the congregation was ready to hear, which is sometimes wisdom and is sometimes cowardice, and I have not always known which it was until later.

The temple that Jeremiah preached against was destroyed in 587 BC. Not because Jeremiah said it would be. Because the conditions he named were real and the consequences followed. The prophetic word does not create the judgment. It names what is already present.

The church that expels its prophets does not thereby silence the word. It silences its own hearing of it.
