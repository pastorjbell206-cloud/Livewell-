A newcomer to Christianity figures out the central claim quickly enough: there is one God, one Lord, one faith, one people gathered from every nation into a single body. It is repeated everywhere, sung in the hymns, printed on the websites. *One.* The church loves the word.

Then the same newcomer visits a few churches and runs into something that does not fit. At one, they are told gently that the bread and cup are only for members in good standing of that communion — that they may watch but not receive. At another, the table is open to anyone who loves Jesus. Down the road a third church believes something about that bread the first two consider an error serious enough to keep them apart. The newcomer does the obvious math. The one meal that this religion says makes its people *one* is, in practice, the single most visible place where they are divided. You can worship near each other. You frequently cannot eat together.

This should trouble Christians far more than it does. We have grown so used to it that we explain it away as a technicality, a matter of church order, the sort of thing that need not bother the spiritually mature. I do not think we get to be that comfortable. **The Lord's Supper was given as the sign and the engine of the church's unity — one loaf, one body — and we have turned it into the clearest evidence of our division. That is not a small irony. It is an indictment, and the table itself is the thing pronouncing it.** This essay is not an attack on any tradition's discipline of its table. It is an attempt to feel the weight of what our divisions have done to the very meal meant to heal them, and to refuse to call that weight normal.

## A meal that was already being abused on day one

Begin with an uncomfortable comfort: the division of the table is not a modern decline from a golden age. The very first church we have any detailed record of was wrecking the meal in its own way, and the apostle Paul tore into them for it.

In Corinth, the Lord's Supper was still a full meal eaten together, and the wealthier members — who could arrive early and bring plenty — were eating and drinking their fill while the poorer members, the slaves and laborers who came late from work, got nothing and went hungry. Paul is blistering about it: "your meetings do more harm than good... when you come together, it is not the Lord's Supper you eat, for when you are eating, some of you go ahead with your own private suppers. As a result, one person remains hungry and another gets drunk. Do you despise the church of God and humiliate those who have nothing?" (1 Corinthians 11:17–22). He says something staggering: that a meal eaten while despising your poorer brothers and sisters is *not the Lord's Supper at all,* whatever the words said over it.

Sit with that. The first recorded problem with Communion was not bad doctrine about the bread. It was a community whose social divisions — rich and poor, free and enslaved — had crawled right up onto the table and made the meal a lie. Paul's point is that the Supper is not a private transaction between an individual and God that happens to occur in a crowd. It is the act by which a divided humanity is supposed to become one body, and to eat it while keeping the divisions intact is to "eat and drink judgment" on yourself. The meal that was meant to make them one was being used to ratify their separation. We did not invent this sin. We inherited it and gave it new forms.

## What the meal was supposed to be

Set against that abuse is the staggering thing the meal was designed to do. Paul says it in a single line that is easy to read past: "Because there is one loaf, we, who are many, are one body, for we all share the one loaf" (1 Corinthians 10:17). The logic runs backward from how we usually think. We assume people who already feel unified might share a meal to express it. Paul says the sharing of the one loaf is what *makes* the many into one body in the first place. The meal is not the celebration of a unity achieved elsewhere. The meal is the place the unity is forged.

This is why Jesus, on the last night of his life, when he could have left his followers anything, left them a meal — and prayed, in the same hours, the prayer John records: "that all of them may be one, Father, just as you are in me and I am in you... so that the world may believe that you have sent me" (John 17:21). Read those last words carefully. Jesus stakes the world's *believing* on his people's *oneness.* The unity of the church was never meant to be an internal nicety. It was meant to be the evidence — the thing that would make a watching world conclude that something real had happened, that this God was who his people said he was. The divided table, then, is not only a wound the church suffers. It is a counter-witness. Every closed table the world notices is a small argument against the prayer Jesus prayed.

The theologian **Stanley Hauerwas** has spent a career insisting that the church does not so much *have* a social ethic as *be* one — that the most political thing the church does is gather as a community whose unity across every worldly division (race, class, nation, party) demonstrates that the powers which divide the world do not get the last word. For Hauerwas, the Eucharist is the heart of that demonstration. A people who can eat together across the lines that split everyone else are themselves the argument. Which means a people who *cannot* eat together have surrendered the argument before they open their mouths.

## How the divisions hardened, fairly told

It would be cheap to wave at the divided table and simply scold the church for it, as though the divisions were all pettiness and pride. Some of them were. But some of them are honest, and a fair telling has to hold both.

When churches practice what is called "closed Communion" — reserving the table for those who share their confession — it is usually not snobbery. It is, in their own understanding, integrity. To share the Supper is to declare a shared faith about what the Supper *is.* For a Catholic or Orthodox church, the Eucharist is the deepest possible expression of being one in the fullness of the faith, and to share it with those not in that communion would be to pretend a unity that does not yet exist — to lie with the most sacred thing they have. There is something in that I respect, even where I land elsewhere. They take the table so seriously that they will not cheapen it with a unity they have not actually reached. The discipline is, in its own logic, a form of honesty.

And yet. Even granting all of that, the result is a wound, and we are not permitted to stop feeling it just because each party can give honorable reasons for its part. The honest defenses do not cancel the scandal; they only explain how good people built it. Lesslie Newbigin, the missionary bishop who spent decades in India watching the Western church's divisions land like a curse on younger churches, saw this with terrible clarity. He had watched mission fields where the gospel was preached by people who could not share Communion with one another, and he understood what the watching not-yet-Christians concluded from it: that the unity these foreigners preached was not real, because they would not eat it. Newbigin spent the back half of his life laboring for church unity precisely because he had seen, up close, how the divided table undercuts the very message it accompanies. He could not unsee it. Neither should we.

## The table that judges, and the table that heals

Here is the deepest thing, and it is the one Scripture forces. The same meal that was meant to unite us also *judges* us — and it judges us precisely at the point of our divisions.

Paul says those who eat "without discerning the body" eat judgment on themselves (1 Corinthians 11:29), and "the body" there means at least two things at once: the body of the Lord given in the bread, and the body of the Lord that is the church. To fail to discern the *church* — to come to the table while despising or excluding or being indifferent to your brothers and sisters — is to profane the meal. The table is not neutral about our divisions. It stands over them in judgment.

Even the apostles felt this blade. There is a scene Paul recounts with raw honesty, where he had to confront Peter himself — the chief apostle — to his face. Peter, in Antioch, had been eating freely with Gentile believers until certain people arrived, and then he drew back and would no longer share the table with them, and others followed his lead. Paul names it for what it was: "I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned... they were not acting in line with the truth of the gospel" (Galatians 2:11–14). The issue, again, was who could eat with whom. Peter had let a worldly division — Jew and Gentile, the deepest social line of his world — climb back onto the table the gospel had cleared. And Paul understood that this was not a manners problem. It was a *gospel* problem. To divide the table along the lines the world draws is to deny in practice the very thing the meal proclaims.

Walter Brueggemann has taught a generation to hear the prophets as the voice that refuses to let God's people call their compromises peace — the voice that names the wound the community has agreed not to mention. The prophetic word about the table is not complicated. It is simply this: *the body of Christ is broken, and we keep breaking it further, and we have learned to do so without grief.* The prophetic task is to restore the grief. To make us feel again what we have trained ourselves not to feel — that the one meal meant to heal us has become a map of our wounds, and that this should not be able to happen without tears.

## The divisions that are not about the bread at all

It would be tidy if every division at the table were a serious disagreement about the nature of the meal, the kind honest people can hold with integrity. But a newcomer should know the harder truth: a great many of the lines that split the Lord's table have nothing to do with the bread and everything to do with us.

Sunday morning remains, in much of the world, the most segregated hour there is — and not because anyone believes the Eucharist means something different across a color line. The churches divided that way are divided by history, by comfort, by the gravitational pull of staying among people like ourselves, by sins the gospel was supposed to have ended at the table and we quietly carried back to it. The Corinthian problem never left. The rich still arrive early and eat their fill; the categories have simply changed. We sort ourselves by class and race and politics and musical taste, and then we hold separate Communions and tell ourselves the separations are about worship style. Paul would not have been fooled, and we should not fool ourselves.

This is the division that should trouble us most, precisely because it is the one with no honorable theological defense. The Catholic guarding a unified confession at the table is at least guarding something real. But two churches a mile apart, holding identical beliefs about the bread, who never share it because one is the church of the comfortable and the other the church of the poor, or one of one people and one of another — that division is not integrity. It is the Corinthian sin wearing Sunday clothes. And it is the kind the prophets reserved their sharpest words for, because it takes the meal meant to abolish exactly those divisions and uses it to bless them.

## What this asks of us

I am not going to end by pretending the divisions can be dissolved by an afternoon of good feeling, or by lowering the table to where it means nothing and so can offend no one. That would be its own betrayal. The traditions that guard their tables carefully are guarding something true. The answer to a divided table is not a meaningless one.

But I will say what the meal asks of every Christian who has stopped finding its division strange. It asks us to grieve. To refuse the comfortable explanation that lets us treat the broken body of Christ as an administrative footnote. It asks us to long, with something like the ache in Jesus' own prayer, for the day when the one loaf is shared by the one body across every line we have let harden — and to let that longing actually cost us something now: humility toward the tradition across the road, repentance for the divisions we have personally enjoyed, a refusal to despise any brother or sister Christ has died for and welcomed.

Because the meal is, finally, a promise as much as an indictment. It points back to a cross where the dividing wall was torn down, and it points forward to a feast the book of Revelation calls the marriage supper of the Lamb — a single table, every nation and tribe and tongue, no one turned away, the prayer of John 17 answered at last in full. We eat now in the gap between that cross and that feast, and the bread we share is a foretaste of a unity we do not yet fully have.

So when you see the divided table, do not look away, and do not shrug. Let it do to you what it is meant to do. Let it indict the church you belong to, the divisions you have found convenient, the grief you have stopped feeling. And then let it make you hungry — not for the bread alone, but for the day the one loaf is finally shared by the one body, and the world, watching us eat, believes.
