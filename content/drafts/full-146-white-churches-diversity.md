Revelation 7:9 is used often in this conversation. The great multitude that no one could count, from every nation, tribe, people, and language, standing before the throne — this is the final picture of the redeemed community, and it is explicitly, irreducibly diverse. The nations are not merged into one homogenous culture before they arrive. They arrive as themselves, in their particularity, and together constitute the fullness of worship that the throne receives.

Most predominantly white American churches use this passage as aspirational vision. It is the goal. We are working toward it. The diversity we see in heaven motivates our diversity efforts here. And then the diversity effort is launched: a gospel choir is invited to perform, a series on racial reconciliation is announced, a few families of color are recruited, the church's social media begins to feature photographs that communicate the desired direction of travel.

This is not diversity. It is aesthetics.

The distinction matters theologically. Aesthetics says: we want to look more like Revelation 7:9. Genuine plurality says: we are willing to have our culture, our decision-making, our power structures, our theological emphases, and our sense of what normal feels like actually changed by the presence and authority of people who are not like us. The first costs some discomfort. The second costs actual power. Most predominantly white churches are attempting the first while describing it in the language of the second.

I have been in predominantly white church leadership for years. I know the pattern from the inside. We celebrated when families of color joined. We were less clear about what it would mean to restructure leadership to reflect their presence, to adjust our liturgical comfort to accommodate different worship sensibilities, to hear prophetic voices that named our blind spots rather than affirmed our existing trajectory. What we wanted was their presence without requiring that we change. That is not integration. That is decoration.

The deeper problem is theological. The predominantly white church has often been formed in a tradition of Christianity that was itself shaped by whiteness in ways it does not recognize as such — assumptions about who produces authoritative theological knowledge, whose suffering the church's public voice addresses, which political concerns are properly "spiritual" and which are "too political," what acceptable worship sounds like, how authority is structured and communicated. These are not universal Christian patterns. They are cultural patterns that have been mistaken for universal ones, and that mistake is nearly invisible to the people inside them.

Willie James Jennings's work on the racial imagination embedded in Western Christianity [cite — confirm] makes the argument at depth: the church in the West developed its sense of identity in relation to the colonial encounter in ways that bound Christianity to whiteness at a formative level. Undoing that is not a program. It is a long, disorienting, expensive reckoning with assumptions so deep they have been treated as natural.

The steel-man of the diversity program: something has to start somewhere, and even imperfect efforts create openings. The gospel choir visit, the reconciliation series — these are not meaningless. They create occasions for encounter that did not exist before, and encounter is where change begins. True. And the problem is not the program. The problem is the program as a substitute for the more fundamental reckoning, used to signal a commitment while protecting the underlying culture from the cost of genuine change.

What genuine change requires in a predominantly white church is something closer to what Acts 15 describes: actual authority shared, actual conflict engaged honestly, actual theological reckoning with the perspectives of people whose experience of the faith has been different. The Jerusalem council in Acts 15 did not produce a nicely worded resolution about the value of Gentile inclusion. It required Jewish Christians to surrender something — the requirement of circumcision, the markers of Jewish identity as entrance criteria — in recognition that the Spirit had already acted outside their cultural frame. That cost something real. Real diversity always does.

The question for the predominantly white church is not "how do we add more diversity?" It is "are we willing to let our culture be changed by the people we are inviting in?" Those are different questions. The second one produces a different church. And the second one is the one the book of Revelation is describing.

The great multitude is not waiting to be recruited as guests into our cultural expression of faith.

They are the hosts of the worship the throne receives.
