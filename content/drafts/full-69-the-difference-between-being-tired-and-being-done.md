There is a difference between being tired and being done, and a pastor who cannot tell them apart will make one of two mistakes. He will quit a calling he was only tired in — walk away from the field during a hard season that rest would have healed. Or he will white-knuckle through a depletion that is telling him something true, mistaking *done* for mere tiredness, and grind himself into wreckage. Both errors come from the same failure: not knowing which one he is in.

Tired is normal. Tired is the honest cost of real work, and it is not a crisis. Ministry is demanding; love is depleting; a faithful pastor will be tired often, the way a farmer is tired at harvest. The mark of tired is that it responds to rest. Take the day, take the week, take the sleep and the Sabbath and the time away, and tired lifts. You come back wanting the work again. The fatigue was real and it was answerable, and the answer was rest.

Done is different, and rest does not touch it. Done is not in the body; it is deeper down. It is the quiet, settled sense that something has shifted at the level of calling or capacity — that the well is not low but dry, that you return from the sabbatical no more able to face Monday than when you left. Done does not lift with a vacation. That is precisely how you tell it from tired: you rested, genuinely, and nothing came back.

The two require opposite responses, which is why the diagnosis matters so much. Tired calls for rest and re-engagement — and to quit while merely tired is to abandon a calling over a passing weather front. Done calls for discernment, counsel, and sometimes change — a different role, a different field, occasionally a release from the work entirely. And to push through *done* as though it were tired is not faithfulness. It is a slow self-destruction that takes the pastor's health, his family, and often his faith down with it.

I have been in both, and I learned the hard way that I could not read myself accurately from inside. When I was merely tired, I sometimes felt the dramatic certainty that I was done — the lie that grows in exhaustion, that this time the dryness is permanent. And in at least one season when I was genuinely closer to done than I knew, I told myself I was just tired, and kept going long past the point where honesty would have stopped me. The view from inside the fatigue is not reliable. That is the first thing to admit.

Which is why this is not finally a diagnosis you can make alone. The line between tired and done is exactly the kind of thing a depleted man cannot see clearly about himself, and exactly what the people who know him can. A spouse, a mentor, a counselor, a few who have permission to tell you the truth — they can often see which one you are in long before you can, because they are not looking through the fog you are standing inside. A pastor without those people will guess, and he will tend to guess in whatever direction his fear or his pride is pushing.

So when the fatigue comes — and it will — resist the rush to label it. Rest first, really rest, the kind you have been refusing. If it lifts, it was tired, and you can return without shame. If you rest and nothing comes back, do not bully yourself forward as though more willpower were the answer. Bring it to the people who can see you, and ask the harder question with them.

Tired is a season. Done is a turning. Confuse them and you either leave a calling you were meant to keep or destroy yourself keeping one you were meant to lay down. Learn the difference — and do not try to learn it by yourself.
