# Essays — The Skeptic Track

Seven essays in argument order. Written for the version of James that was an
atheist at twenty-four. No conversion bait. No bad apologetics. The skeptic
on-ramp the site is built around.

These slugs match the seven stops on the `/skeptic-track` page. Writing all
seven is the highest-leverage move on the site — the page only works once
they exist.

---

## 21. The Questions That Actually Matter

**Slug:** `the-questions-that-actually-matter`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most defenses of Christianity answer questions skeptics aren't asking. The questions skeptics are actually asking are different, harder, more honest. Here they are.

**Argument beats:**
1. The bad apologetic move: addressing the strawman question. "Is there a god" debated as if Anselm and Aquinas and Plantinga had not happened.
2. The real questions: (1) If God exists, why does he stay hidden? (2) What kind of moral evidence is the universe? (3) What do I owe a story that requires my submission? (4) Why this religion and not the others? (5) Is the problem of evil a problem of *love*, not just of logic?
3. Walk each question once briefly. Show that you take it seriously, that you have the answer most apologetics offers and you do not find it sufficient, and that you are going to give a better answer over the course of the next six essays.
4. The honest acknowledgment: there are good arguments on the other side. There are not slam-dunk arguments on either side. The Christian who tells you otherwise is selling you something.
5. The verdict: the questions are real. The Christianity you reject is probably a bad Christianity. Stick with me for the next six essays, and I will offer you a Christianity that addresses these questions and not the questions our apologetics tends to answer.

**Opening sketch:** "Most defenses of Christianity I read at twenty-three did not answer the questions I was actually asking. They answered the questions Christians wished I were asking. The questions I was actually asking were harder, and more honest, and not addressed by the bad apologetics on my shelf. I came to faith despite the apologetics, not because of it. I want to write the apologetics I needed then."

**Closing weight:** I am not asking you to believe by the end of this essay. I am asking you to consider that the Christianity you have been offered is probably not the Christianity that has lasted two thousand years and outlived every regime that tried to kill it. The Christianity that outlasted the regimes is harder, stranger, more interesting, and more honest than what you have been pitched. Stay for the next six essays. The questions get a fair hearing.

**Sources:** Charles Taylor (*A Secular Age*); Alvin Plantinga (*Knowledge and Christian Belief*); Tim Keller (*Reason for God*); David Bentley Hart (*The Experience of God*).

## 22. What Secular Explanations Still Have to Explain

**Slug:** `what-secular-explanations-still-have-to-explain`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~11 min

**Positioning sentence:** The case for secular naturalism is stronger than the church admits. It also leaves real things uncovered. Both can be true.

**Argument beats:**
1. Concede generously what secular explanations get right. Evolutionary biology, neuroscience, the cosmological mainstream, the historical-critical reading of religious texts. The work has been honest. Pretending otherwise is the apologetics mistake.
2. What remains: (a) why is there something rather than nothing? — the cosmological question that is not closed; (b) the hard problem of consciousness; (c) the persistent intuition of moral realism; (d) the historical Jesus, who keeps refusing to be made un-historical; (e) the personal experience of the divine which is the data most secular accounts dismiss but cannot explain.
3. Walk each briefly. Cite the best secular response (Sean Carroll on cosmology, Dennett on consciousness, etc.) and explain why it is not, on its own terms, fully satisfying.
4. The honest acknowledgment: none of these is a proof of God. Each is an explanatory remainder. Christianity does not require proofs. Christianity requires that the cumulative case is reasonable enough to be worth a commitment.
5. The Pascal move: the bet is not what Pascal said it was, but the structure is right. Choosing has costs in both directions. The skeptic is also betting, just on a different proposition.
6. The verdict: secular naturalism is a serious worldview. So is Christianity. The serious skeptic and the serious Christian agree on more than the lazy versions of either admit, and they disagree on a few load-bearing questions that have not yet been settled.

**Opening sketch:** "I want to make the strongest case for the secular worldview I can. The strongest case is stronger than most Christians admit. It is also incomplete. I want to be honest about both, because the cumulative case for Christianity does not depend on the secular case being weak. The cumulative case depends on the secular case leaving certain things unexplained."

**Closing weight:** Both worldviews are bets. Both worldviews have explanatory remainders. The choice between them is not closed by either side's best arguments. It is closed by something the next essay takes up — the problem of suffering, which is the place every honest skeptic and every honest Christian has to meet each other, and the place I want to take you next.

**Sources:** Sean Carroll (*The Big Picture*); Daniel Dennett (*Consciousness Explained*); David Bentley Hart (*The Experience of God*); Alister McGrath (*A Theory of Everything (That Matters)*); Charles Taylor.

## 23. The Problem of Suffering, Honestly

**Slug:** `the-problem-of-suffering-honestly`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~12 min

**Positioning sentence:** The standard apologetic responses to suffering are too clean. The biblical responses are messier — and more livable.

**Argument beats:**
1. State the problem in its strongest form. The Holocaust. Cancer in children. Tsunami. Random meaningless suffering. Not as a debating tactic — as the actual condition we live in.
2. The standard apologetics: free will defense, soul-making theodicy, greater-good arguments. Each has something to say. Each is incomplete in the face of an actual sufferer.
3. The Christian thing the apologetics often skip: the Bible does not, on the whole, answer the problem of suffering by giving us reasons. It answers by giving us *a sufferer* — the God who became one. Job. Lamentations. The cross.
4. Why this is different from the apologetic answer: the apologetic answer says *here is why this is allowed.* The biblical answer says *here is who is with you in it.* The first is intellectually unsatisfying for good reasons. The second is what most sufferers actually need.
5. The verdict: the problem of suffering does not have a clean intellectual solution. The Christian story is the only major worldview that does not pretend to have one. Christianity says God himself entered the suffering. That is not an answer. It is a presence. The presence is the only thing that has ever been enough.
6. Self-implication: I have given the bad answers myself, from pulpits. I have also sat with sufferers and known that no answer would do, and that the presence — Christ, but also my presence as Christ's body — was the thing that mattered.

**Opening sketch:** "The problem of suffering is not a debating question. It is the condition we live inside. Cancer kills children. Tsunamis kill villages. The Holocaust happened, and the Christians of Germany mostly did not stop it, and that fact alone should disqualify the easy apologetic answers most of us have been taught to give. I want to give a different answer. It will not satisfy you intellectually. It is, I think, the only honest answer the Christian tradition has."

**Closing weight:** I will not give you a reason for the suffering you are inside of. The Bible does not. Job did not get one. He got God — who showed up, who answered him, who never told him *why.* God showed up. God is still showing up. The God who walks through the suffering with you is not a theodicy. He is a presence. He is the only theodicy that has ever been enough, and he is enough for the same reason he was enough for Job — not because he gave an answer, but because he was there.

**Sources:** Job; Lamentations; C.S. Lewis (*A Grief Observed*); David Bentley Hart (*The Doors of the Sea*); Eleonore Stump (*Wandering in Darkness*); Marilyn McCord Adams (*Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God*).

## 24. The Historical Jesus, Without the Shortcuts

**Slug:** `the-historical-jesus-without-the-shortcuts`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~13 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most popular Christian arguments for the historical Jesus rely on shortcuts mainstream scholarship would not endorse. Most popular secular dismissals rely on shortcuts mainstream scholarship would not endorse either. The actual scholarly conversation is more interesting than both.

**Argument beats:**
1. What can be defended historically: Jesus existed; he was a first-century Jewish itinerant teacher with a following; he was crucified under Pilate; his followers very quickly believed he had risen and were willing to die for that belief; the early Christian movement had a sociologically improbable explosive growth that requires explanation.
2. What is contested in the mainstream: the historicity of specific miracle accounts; the date and authorship of individual gospels; the precise relationship between the historical Jesus and the Christ of faith. Be honest about this.
3. The minimum that has to be explained for non-Christian accounts to work: the empty tomb (multiply attested, including by unfriendly sources); the resurrection appearances (multiple, varied, including to enemies of the movement like Paul); the rapid emergence of a Christian movement worshipping Jesus as God in a monotheistic Jewish context; the willingness of the first witnesses to die rather than recant.
4. Walk through the alternative explanations and what each leaves unexplained. Hallucination theories, stolen body theories, conspiracy theories. None of them, on close inspection, accounts for the data.
5. The honest conclusion: the resurrection is the best historical explanation of the data we have. It is also a wild explanation that most modern people are predisposed to reject. The question is whether the predisposition is doing more work than the evidence.
6. The verdict: if you reject the resurrection, do so with eyes open about what you are rejecting and what you have to explain instead. The case is stronger than secular pop apologetics admits. It is also not airtight. Christianity does not require it to be airtight.

**Opening sketch:** "I want to make the strongest historical case for the resurrection I can — and to be honest about what it can and cannot do. The pop apologetics has done the church a disservice by overpromising. The pop dismissals have done atheism a disservice by underdelivering. The actual historical conversation is more interesting than both. Let me walk you through it."

**Closing weight:** The resurrection is either the worst-attested wild claim in ancient history or the best-attested one. The honest historical work has not been able to dismiss it. It has also not been able to prove it in a way that compels every reasonable person. The decision the resurrection asks for is not a decision the historical evidence can finally close. It is a decision about what kind of universe you think you live in. That decision is yours, and it has consequences in both directions.

**Sources:** N.T. Wright (*The Resurrection of the Son of God*); Bart Ehrman (cite for the contested mainstream); Mike Licona (*The Resurrection of Jesus*); Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*); Larry Hurtado (*Lord Jesus Christ*).

## 25. The Bible Without the Marketing

**Slug:** `the-bible-without-the-marketing`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~11 min

**Positioning sentence:** Inerrancy is a fairly recent dogma. Inspiration is older. Read the Bible the way Christians actually have, for nineteen centuries, and the skeptical objections lose most of their force.

**Argument beats:**
1. The marketing pitch we are sold: the Bible is a single book, written by God, perfect in every detail, settling every question if you read it right.
2. The actual book we are reading: a library, composed of multiple genres over more than a millennium, by multiple authors, redacted and edited over time, transmitted with extraordinary care but with documented variations. None of this is news to careful Christians. All of it is news to most lay Christians, and the news is alarming when it arrives because the marketing pitch lied.
3. Inerrancy as a doctrine is largely a nineteenth- and twentieth-century American Protestant development. It is not, contra its defenders, the position of the historic church. Augustine, Calvin, Luther all held a high view of scripture without holding inerrancy in its strict modern form.
4. What inspiration is: God speaking through the human authors in their humanity. This is not a lower view of scripture. It is an older and more durable one. It does not require defending the indefensible.
5. The skeptical objections — apparent contradictions, scientific errors, troubling moral passages — lose most of their force when you stop reading the Bible as a uniform document and start reading it as the library it is.
6. The Bible as it is is more interesting, more rewarding, and harder than the Bible as the marketing pitched it. Most of my own confidence in scripture grew when I stopped defending the indefensible and started reading the library with care.
7. The verdict: the Bible is not the book the marketing said it was. It is a stranger, deeper, more demanding book. Read that book.

**Opening sketch:** "I have spent twenty years pastoring people who lost their faith in the Bible — not because they encountered a hard question, but because they encountered the Bible itself, and the Bible did not match the marketing they had been sold. The marketing was wrong. The marketing was always wrong. The Bible the church has actually read for two thousand years is a different book, and it is, in every way that matters, a better one."

**Closing weight:** The Bible the marketing sold you was thin. The Bible you have been refusing to read was thin. The Bible the church has actually read for nineteen centuries — a library, a chorus, a long argument with itself and with us — is the book worth reading. Read that book. Read it slowly. Read it with the church. Read it the way Augustine read it, and Calvin read it, and Karl Barth read it. The reading will change you. The marketing's version could not.

**Sources:** Karl Barth (*Church Dogmatics* I/2); Peter Enns (*The Bible Tells Me So*; *Inspiration and Incarnation*); Walter Brueggemann (*The Bible Makes Sense*); N.T. Wright (*Scripture and the Authority of God*); John Walton (*The Lost World of Genesis One*).

## 26. Morality Without God — And With Him

**Slug:** `morality-without-god-and-with-him`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~11 min

**Positioning sentence:** The argument that you cannot be moral without God is bad and embarrassing. The argument that morality, as we actually experience it, points to something beyond mere preference is better and worth taking seriously.

**Argument beats:**
1. Concede generously. Atheists are often very good people. The "you cannot be moral without God" trope is empirically false and theologically lazy.
2. The better argument is not about behavior. It is about the metaphysical status of morality itself. When we say "the Holocaust was wrong," we mean something stronger than "I personally dislike it" or "my culture does not approve." We mean it was actually, mind-independently, wrong.
3. Secular ethics has options for grounding this: evolutionary, contractarian, intuitionist. Each does some work. None, on close inspection, accounts for the full force of our moral intuitions.
4. The theistic alternative: morality is grounded in the character of God. This is not a heteronomous command-from-on-high. It is a participation in the divine life that the universe was made to reflect.
5. The honest move: the existence of objective morality is not a proof of God. It is a piece of evidence consistent with God and difficult to fully account for without him. Add it to the cumulative case.
6. Practical implication: if morality is grounded in something beyond us, then conscience is not just preference. The voice in your head telling you that your life is supposed to be different is data, not noise. Take it seriously.
7. The verdict: morality without God is possible. Morality without God is harder than the casual atheist admits, and the seriousness of conscience is harder to dismiss than secular ethics has made it look.

**Opening sketch:** "Some of the most moral people I know are atheists. The argument that you cannot be moral without God is empirically false, theologically lazy, and pastorally damaging. I will not make it here. I want to make a different argument — the one that has actually mattered to me — about what objective morality, if it exists, has to be grounded in. That argument is harder for both sides of this debate than the easy version."

**Closing weight:** Morality is the place secular and Christian worldviews come closest, and the place they diverge in ways that matter. If morality is grounded in something beyond us, the conscience that troubles you is not a malfunction. It is data. The data is pointing somewhere. The pointing is part of how God draws us. Or it is part of how evolution made us. The question of which is the question this essay does not finally close. The next essay takes up what is at stake in the answer.

**Sources:** C.S. Lewis (*Mere Christianity*, Book I); Charles Taylor (*Sources of the Self*); David Bentley Hart (*The Beauty of the Infinite*); Mark Linville (in *The Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology*); Iris Murdoch (*The Sovereignty of Good*).

## 27. What Following This Actually Costs

**Slug:** `what-following-this-actually-costs`
**Track:** `doubt` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~12 min

**Positioning sentence:** If you take Christianity seriously, here is what changes. Not your weekend. Your money, your marriage, your politics, your sex life, your career, your funeral. The cost is real. The cost is also what makes the thing worth doing.

**Argument beats:**
1. End the series honestly. If the previous six essays have made the case that Christianity is reasonable, the seventh has to make the case that it is costly. Otherwise the conversion is cheap, and a cheap conversion does not last.
2. The cost in money: tithing, generosity, the refusal to organize your finances around accumulation. The first-century Christian sold property to feed the church's poor. The modern Christian who keeps everything has not yet been converted in any economically meaningful sense.
3. The cost in marriage: not divorce-on-demand, not the romantic exit when feelings change, not the optimization of the spouse for personal happiness. Covenant.
4. The cost in politics: the refusal to belong fully to either team. The wilderness position. The willingness to lose friends in both directions.
5. The cost in sex: a vision of sexuality as covenantal and embodied and not infinitely customizable. This is the cost most contemporary skeptics most want to avoid. The honest pitch has to name it.
6. The cost in career: vocation as something other than self-actualization through paid work. The willingness to be poorer than your talents could make you for the sake of something larger.
7. The cost in death: the funeral that looks like resurrection because the person actually believed in one. The willingness to die well — which begins with the willingness to live well, knowing how it ends.
8. The verdict: this is what you are signing up for. It is not a self-help upgrade. It is a conscription. You join a body. The body has claims on you. The claims are absolute, and they are gentle, and they are the reason any of this is worth doing.

**Opening sketch:** "I have not asked you to believe yet, in any of the previous six essays. I will ask now. But before I ask, I want you to know what you are being asked into. Christianity is not a self-help program. It is a conscription. It will cost you money, marriage, sex, career, friendships, and finally your funeral. I want to be honest about the cost. The reason it is worth doing is the same reason it costs that much."

**Closing weight:** I converted at twenty-four, and I have spent twenty-two years finding out what the conversion cost. It cost more than I knew. It also gave me more than I knew was on offer. I would do it again. I would do it sooner. The decision is yours, and it is not a decision I can make for you, and the honest version of this faith requires me to tell you the cost before I ask you to take it. There is the cost. Now you decide.

**Sources:** Bonhoeffer (*Discipleship*, esp. "Costly Grace"); C.S. Lewis (*Mere Christianity*, Book IV); Eugene Peterson (*A Long Obedience in the Same Direction*); Tim Keller (*The Reason for God*; *Counterfeit Gods*); Dorothy Day; Stanley Hauerwas.
