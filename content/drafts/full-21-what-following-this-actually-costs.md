I have not asked you to believe yet, in any of the previous six essays. I am asking now. But before I ask, I want you to know what you are being asked into. Christianity is not a self-help program. It is a conscription. It will cost you money, marriage, sex, career, friendships, and finally your funeral. I want to be honest about the cost. The reason it is worth doing is the same reason it costs that much.

I am writing this last essay in the Skeptic Track for the reader who has been with me through the previous six and has not yet been finally convinced, and also for the reader who has been moved by the cumulative case and is now asking what acceptance would actually mean. Both readers deserve the honest answer. The pop apologetic that promises Christianity will improve your life without requiring much of you is a lie, and one of the reasons evangelicalism has lost two generations of younger Americans is that the children of the people sold this lie eventually discovered the lie and concluded that the whole project was dishonest. They were partly right. The project as marketed was dishonest. The project as actually held by the historic church is more demanding, and more honest, and worth considering on its real terms rather than the marketing terms.

## What money will cost

Christianity has things to say about money that most American Christians have not yet fully reckoned with.

Tithing — the ancient practice of giving the first ten percent of income — is, in the New Testament, a starting point rather than a ceiling. The early church practiced a much more thoroughgoing community of goods than tithing alone would describe. Acts 2 and 4 describe Christians selling possessions to support those in need. The continuing Christian tradition has, in various forms, held that the disciple is responsible for the use of his wealth in a way that the surrounding culture does not require. The wealth is not yours in the way the surrounding culture says it is. The wealth is held in trust, given for the formation of the church, given for the relief of the poor, given for the building of the institutions that serve the kingdom.

If you accept this framework, your financial life will change. You will give more than you currently give. You will hold your possessions more lightly than you currently hold them. You will be more generous in ways that are not tax-deductible, including to specific people whose need you can see and whose names you know. You will likely make less money over your career than you could have made, because the optimization pressure of the surrounding culture will weigh less heavily on you. You will be poorer than you would have been. Most American Christians have not yet faced this honestly. I have not faced it as fully as I should. I am still being conscripted in this area, in real time, in my forty-sixth year.

## What marriage and sex will cost

The Christian sexual ethic is more demanding than the surrounding culture's, and the difference is not a peripheral feature you can take or leave. It is structural.

If you are unmarried and you accept Christianity seriously, your sexual life will change. The sexual freedoms the surrounding culture treats as basic rights are not, on the historic Christian view, freedoms. They are the disordered expressions of a desire that has not yet been formed by the gospel. The Christian sexual ethic limits sexual expression to the covenant of marriage between a man and a woman. The historic Christian tradition is consistent on this. The contemporary progressive Christian attempts to revise this have not, in my reading, produced a coherent alternative that maintains continuity with the larger Christian tradition. You will either need to accept the historic ethic, with all the difficulty that involves, or you will need to develop a coherent revisionist position that you can defend in conversation with the historic tradition, and which will continue to constrain you in ways that the surrounding culture's sexual freedoms do not.

If you are married, your marriage will be reshaped. The covenantal frame for marriage is more demanding than the romantic-companionate frame the surrounding culture has trained us in. Divorce will not be an easy option even when you want it to be. The willingness to leave when feelings change will be foreclosed by the covenant you have undertaken. You will have to do the slow work of being formed by another person across decades, with all the painful self-revelation that involves. The marriage will not be optimized for your happiness. The marriage will be a place where your false self is dismantled, and the dismantling will be painful, and the dismantling will be most of what the marriage is for.

## What politics will cost

You will not be able to belong fully to either American political party. I wrote a separate essay on this earlier in the writing project; the substance of the argument is in the politics essays of this series. The third option — the gospel as the standard by which both parties are judged insufficient — is the only sustainable political position for a serious Christian. The position will cost you friendships in both directions. You will be too progressive for many conservatives and too conservative for many progressives, and the disagreements will be the kind that change the texture of relationships.

Your politics will be more demanding, not less. You will be called to actual concern for the poor, to actual concern for the unborn, to actual concern for racial justice, to actual concern for the family — all simultaneously, without the partisan filtering that has been telling American Christians which concerns are theirs and which can be left to the other side. The political life will be costlier, lonelier, and more theologically rigorous than the partisan life you were trained in.

## What career will cost

You will, in many cases, do work that is below your earning potential. You will turn down positions that the surrounding culture would expect you to take. You will refuse promotions that require choices your conscience cannot make. You will, in some industries and some positions, be excluded from advancement because of your stated convictions. You will be passed over. You will be tolerated. You will, in some cases, be pushed out.

The Christian vocation is not the same thing as the American career. The Christian vocation is the slow alignment of your work with the kingdom — work done with integrity, work done in service of your neighbors, work done in a way that participates in God's care for the world. The career optimization that the surrounding culture rewards will, in many cases, run counter to your vocational commitments. You will have to choose. The choosing is part of the cost.

## What friendships will cost

Some of the friendships you currently have will not survive your taking this seriously. The friends whose worldviews are seriously different from the Christian one will sometimes adapt, sometimes withdraw, sometimes feel betrayed by what they perceive as your change. The friendships built on shared assumptions you can no longer share will become harder to maintain.

In their place, you will be given new friendships. The church, when it is functioning, is a community of strange people who do not necessarily share much beyond the gospel. You will find yourself in conversation with people of different political views, different class backgrounds, different ethnic backgrounds, different educational levels, and the conversation will be more honest than most of the friendships you had before. The historic community of Christians has been the most religiously, ethnically, and economically diverse community in human history. You will be conscripted into it. The conscription will reshape your sense of who your people are.

## What your death will cost

This is the strangest cost. The Christian dies differently from the surrounding culture.

The Christian funeral is, in its historic form, an act of worship, not primarily an act of remembrance. The body of the deceased is treated as the body of someone who will rise, not as an object to be disposed of with dignity. The funeral liturgy proclaims the resurrection, not just the memory. The dying person, the family of the dying person, and the community that gathers all participate in something that the surrounding culture's funeral practices do not provide — the recognition that death is not the final word, that the body matters because the resurrection is bodily, that the dead are still part of the body of Christ.

You will, if you take Christianity seriously, prepare for your own death differently. You will be more present to the dying. You will treat the dead more reverently. You will refuse some of the medical interventions the surrounding culture defaults to, and you will accept others. Your funeral will be designed to point to the resurrection, not to celebrate your achievements. The whole shape of your last weeks and your last day will be different, in ways that may be invisible to your non-Christian friends but will be obvious to those who share the faith.

## Why it is worth it

I want to close this series the way I have been closing the others, with honesty.

The cost of following Christ seriously is large. I have laid out the dimensions — money, sex, marriage, politics, career, friendships, death. Each is real. Each is what the historic church has, in its better moments, said the disciple has to accept.

The question is whether the thing is worth doing despite the cost. My answer, after twenty-two years inside the faith, is yes. The reason it is worth doing is not that the costs are small — they are not. The reason it is worth doing is that the alternative is to live without the relationship to the one who made you, the redemption that delivers you from what you cannot deliver yourself from, and the presence that walks with you through suffering. The Christian framework is not a happiness optimization. The Christian framework is the recognition that you have been made for something that requires the surrender of your autonomy, the discipline of your desires, and the participation in a community that has claims on your life that the surrounding culture does not understand.

The Christian I have become is more demanding than the atheist I was. The Christian I have become is also, in every way that finally matters, more alive. The marriage I have is harder than the marriage I might have had if I had not taken the vow as a covenant. The marriage I have is also deeper, more real, more able to hold weight than the alternative would have been. The money I have given is more than I would have given otherwise. The money I have left is also more meaningful, more directed, more able to do good than it would have been. The friendships I have built across class and political lines through the church are more interesting and harder than the friendships I would have had within my own demographic. The faith I am still working out is more demanding than my atheism ever was. The faith is also, in my honest experience, the thing that makes my life intelligible.

## The verdict, and the asking

I have spent seven essays giving you the cumulative case for Christianity. I have tried to do it without the shortcuts the pop apologetics has often relied on. I have tried to grant the secular alternatives where they are strong. I have tried to be honest about the cost of what I am asking.

The asking is this. Consider Christianity as the historic church has actually held it, not as the marketing version sold it. Consider it as a serious option for the way you might live the rest of your life. Consider the costs honestly. Consider the goods honestly. Decide.

I am not asking you to decide today. I am asking you to take the question seriously, to do the work of reading and conversation and thinking that the question deserves, and to remain open to the possibility that the cumulative case is more adequate than the secular alternative you are currently holding. I came to faith from atheism over four years of slow consideration. I did not have a single conversion moment. I had a long process of weighing, in which the cumulative case gradually became more persuasive than the alternative, and at the end of the four years I found myself willing to make the commitment the case had been asking for. The commitment cost me. The commitment also gave me a life I would not have had otherwise. I do not regret it. I would do it again. I would do it sooner.

The Skeptic Track closes here. The work I owe to my younger self has been, partly, paid. The work I owe to the next generation of skeptics is the work of continued writing, continued pastoring, continued patient conversation with people whose questions are the questions I once had. The work is what the rest of my pastoral life will be about.

If you have been moved by any of this, I want to encourage you to do two things. First, find a serious church — one that takes the historic Christian tradition seriously, does its catechesis well, and is not running the pop apologetics I have been pressing against in this series. Second, find a Christian friend who is willing to walk with you slowly through the next year or two of your wrestling. The decision is not one to make alone. The decision is one to make in community with people who have been walking the same path. The path is hard. The path is real. The path leads, eventually, to a life I would not trade.

I am praying for you, whether you accept it or not. The praying is part of how I love my readers, including the ones who finally decide against. You are not alone, even in your skepticism. I have been there. Many readers of this series have been there. Some of us came out the other side. Some of you will, too. The asking is real. The answer is yours.
