Drive past your church building at eleven on a Tuesday morning and count the people within a mile radius who will have no meaningful contact with the gospel this week. Count the apartment complex where recent immigrants from Somalia live in quiet and considerable isolation. Count the truck stop twenty minutes outside town where men sleep in their cabs because they cannot afford a room. Count the nursing facility with eighty residents, three of whom have a visitor more than once a month. Count the county jail.

Then tell me where the unreached people groups are.

The missionary tradition in American Christianity has a specific geography: far away and difficult to get to. The maps with the red circles over the 10/40 Window [cite — confirm], the reports on people groups who have never had a Bible in their language, the photographs from unreached corners of Central Asia and the Horn of Africa — these form the visual imagination of global mission, and they are not wrong. Those populations deserve the church's attention and resources and prayer.

But the geography of mission in Scripture is more complicated than that. When Paul writes in Romans 15:20 that he makes it his ambition to preach the gospel where Christ has not already been named, he is not describing a continent. He is describing a posture. The ambition is to go where the good news has not landed, not to define that location from a conference in Colorado.

There are populations within your city — possibly within your zip code — where the gospel has not, in any meaningful sense, landed. Not because there are no church buildings. But because the church buildings are not inhabited by those populations, do not speak their languages, do not operate on their schedules, do not exist in their social world, and have never tried to.

The immigrant community is the most obvious and most neglected example. American cities have experienced significant immigration in the last three decades [cite — confirm], and the communities that formed in strip-mall mosques and apartment complexes and informal networks of mutual aid are not, by and large, communities in conversation with the local evangelical church. They are not in conversation with us partly because we have not sought them, partly because the cultural and linguistic distance requires significant investment to cross, and partly because we have been honest enough to notice that the crossing will cost us something and have quietly decided the cost is not worth bearing.

The incarcerated population is another. The United States incarcerates people at a rate that is extraordinary by global standards [cite — confirm]. The county jail in your community holds men and women who are, in many cases, more genuinely open to the gospel than anyone sitting in your Sunday morning congregation — because the conditions of their lives have stripped away the comfortable alternatives. The church that has a jail ministry carried by one or two volunteers who visit on Thursday evenings has done something. The church that has integrated the formerly incarcerated into its actual community, that has understood reentry as a mission field requiring the same sustained investment as a church plant in Indonesia — that church is rare.

The elderly isolated are not usually discussed in missionary terms at all. They are understood as a pastoral care challenge rather than a mission field. But the eighty-year-old in the nursing facility who has never been asked what she believes about God, who was raised in a nominally religious household and never made any kind of personal faith commitment, who has now been alone with her thoughts for three years — she is unreached. The pastoral imagination that sees her only as someone to visit, rather than someone to whom the gospel has not yet come, has missed what the text requires.

Jesus sent the seventy-two out in Luke 10 and told them to go to every town and place where he was about to go. Every town. Every place. Not the strategically significant ones. Not the ones with high potential for multiplication. Every place.

The unreached people nearest to you are not a consolation prize for the churches that cannot afford to go far. They are the mission. They have always been the mission.

The distance that matters is not geographic.
