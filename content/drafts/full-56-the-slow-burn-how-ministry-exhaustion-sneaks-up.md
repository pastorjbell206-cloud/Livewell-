Burnout almost never arrives as an event. We picture it that way — a collapse, a breakdown, a dramatic morning a man cannot get out of the car. But the collapse is only the last frame. What precedes it is a slow burn, months or years long, so gradual that the man living it cannot feel the temperature changing. He is the frog in the warming water, and the warming is the whole danger.

It does not look like exhaustion at first. It looks like productivity. The early stages of a slow burn are often a pastor's busiest and most admired season — more meetings, more output, more visible faithfulness. The decline hides inside the achievement, which is exactly why no one intervenes. You do not pull a man off the field while he is scoring. So the erosion proceeds underneath the success, unseen by the congregation and, for a long time, by the man himself.

I know the early frames because I have lived in them. The first to go was not my schedule but my interior life — the prayer that became preparation, the Scripture I now only read professionally, looking for sermons instead of being addressed by God. Then went the margin: the unhurried conversation, the walk with no agenda, the capacity to be interrupted without resentment. Then the small joys flattened. Things that used to move me did not. I called it maturity. It was numbness, and numbness is one of the body's last warnings before the system protects itself by shutting down.

Learn the signs while they are still quiet, because the loud sign is the one that ends ministries. Watch for the slow conversion of people into tasks — when the family in crisis becomes an item on the list rather than a soul to love. Watch for cynicism, which arrives disguised as discernment: the new, reflexive suspicion of everyone's motives, the eye-roll where compassion used to be. Watch for the disappearance of delight, because a man losing himself slowly does not usually feel sad first. He feels flat. Watch for the secret fantasies of escape — not dramatic ones, just the quiet Tuesday thought that it would be a relief if it all simply stopped.

None of these are moral failures. They are gauges, and a gauge is not an accusation; it is information. The tragedy is not that pastors run low — every faithful life runs low sometimes. The tragedy is the silence around the gauges, the long habit of ignoring the warning lights because the engine is still running and the schedule is still full.

The remedy is not a single dramatic rescue. A slow burn was built over months, and it is unwound the same way — by small, unglamorous reversals, repeated. A real day of rest, kept when the calendar protests. A return to prayer that has no sermon attached. A few people allowed close enough to notice the flatness and name it. Honest attention to the body, the sleep, the marriage, the ordinary creaturely things a depleted man is most tempted to neglect and most needs. None of it is heroic. The recovery of a soul rarely is.

And under all of it, the slow relearning of the truth the busyness buried: that you are a creature and not an engine, that the church is held up by Christ and not by your stamina, that the God who rested on the seventh day did not think less of his work for resting and will not think less of you. A pastor burns out fastest when he has quietly come to believe that the whole thing depends on him. It does not. It never did.

If you are reading this and recognizing yourself in the early frames — the prayer gone professional, the people gone flat, the quiet fantasy of escape — that recognition is not failure. It is the warning arriving while there is still time to heed it.

Most pastors get the warning. Fewer stop. Be one of the ones who stops — now, while it is still a slow burn, and not yet the morning the engine will not start.
