Patriotism is not a sin. Let that be said first, clearly, and without apology. The love of a particular place, a particular people, a particular inheritance — this is not idolatry. It is not wrong to weep at a flag, to feel the weight of what your nation's better moments have cost. But there is a point where patriotism stops being love of country and starts being faith in country, and that crossing is one of the defining spiritual catastrophes of American church history.

We have been crossing it for more than two centuries.

The Kingdom of God is the organizing center of Jesus's entire proclamation. Not America, not democracy, not Western civilization — the Kingdom. The Sermon on the Mount is not a speech about good governance. The Lord's Prayer is not a civic liturgy. When Jesus says "Thy kingdom come," he is not describing a project that requires congressional approval. He is announcing a sovereignty that stands over all other sovereignties, judges all other sovereignties, and will, in the end, outlast all other sovereignties. That is the thing we have quietly replaced with "God Bless America."

The replacement has been gradual, which is how most replacements work. It did not begin with malice. It began with gratitude — genuine, understandable, earned gratitude for a nation that provided unusual freedoms and unusual stability. But gratitude, when it is not held under something larger than itself, curdles into entitlement, and entitlement hardens into theology. By the early twentieth century, many American pulpits were preaching a gospel in which the blessings of democracy and the blessings of Christ were assumed to be the same blessings. By the mid-twentieth century, the confusion had become structural. By the time I came to faith at twenty-four, it was simply the water.

I swam in it. I preached it, with rather more confidence than I deserved. I told the story in which the American experiment was the fulfillment of God's purposes rather than one more chapter in the long, unfinished history of nations rising and falling under his gaze. I was wrong. The wrongness was comfortable enough that I had little incentive to notice it.

Here is what the replacement has cost.

When the Kingdom of God is the organizing principle of Christian life, you must take the Sermon on the Mount seriously as public ethics. The poor have a claim on you that your voting bloc does not. The stranger matters as much as the citizen. The enemy has a dignity that cannot be bombed away. But when patriotism becomes the practical savior — when the survival of American freedom becomes the thing we are actually fighting for — the Sermon on the Mount becomes optional. You spiritualize it. You schedule it for the afterlife. You give it a private domain that does not touch the public square where the real decisions are made.

The prophets of Israel never made that move. Amos did not tell the wealthy of Samaria that their economic predations were private matters between themselves and God. Isaiah did not suggest that the oppression of the poor was a question of personal morality that had nothing to do with the public order of the kingdom. Micah did not separate mishpat — the public establishment of right — from the daily life of the nation. The prophets assumed that God had opinions about how the community organized itself, and that those opinions were not requests.

The American church, when it chose nationalism as its organizing principle, lost access to the prophets. Not formally. We still read them. But we read them as poetry rather than as indictment. We have domesticated the fire.

The steel-man argument deserves a hearing. Patriotism, the argument goes, is simply ordered love — the love of the proximate, the love of the people placed near us by providence. And the liberal democratic order has produced conditions under which the church has flourished, under which the gospel has spread, under which human dignity has at least been argued for, even when it has been violated. These are real goods.

They are real goods. They are not ultimate goods. And the moment we treat proximate goods as ultimate goods, we commit the oldest error in the human story, which is idolatry. We have given to the flag what belongs to the cross. We have given to the nation-state what belongs to the Kingdom. We have let "God Bless America" replace "Thy Kingdom come," and in the replacement, we have made ourselves harder to save, because you cannot see the salvation you need when you already believe you have it.

The Kingdom of God does not need America. America, like every nation, needs the Kingdom.
