Half the church in America has, for most of my lifetime, told me that Jesus was a capitalist. The other half has told me that Jesus was a socialist. They cannot both be right. They are, in fact, both wrong. The Jesus of the gospels is neither, and the church that wants to enlist him for either project has misread him in the same way for opposite reasons.

I wrote the companion essay on the capitalist misreading already. This essay is the harder one to write in my own tradition, because the audience that most needs to hear it is the audience least likely to be reading my work. The progressive Christian reading of Jesus as proto-socialist has substantial traction in seminaries, in mainline denominations, and in the deconstructing-evangelical subculture that has been my pastoral concern for the past decade. The reading is wrong in ways I want to name carefully.

## Where the progressive reading comes from

The progressive reading is not invented from nothing. It picks up on real features of the biblical text that the conservative reading often suppresses.

Acts 2:44–45 and Acts 4:32–35 describe the early Jerusalem church as a community of shared property. *And all who believed were together and had all things in common. And they were selling their possessions and belongings and distributing the proceeds to all, as any had need.* The progressive reading takes this seriously and reads it as a model of explicit economic communalism that the church has, in most periods, failed to live up to.

The Old Testament prophetic tradition consistently denounces the accumulation of wealth at the expense of the poor. Isaiah 5 against those who *join house to house, who add field to field.* Amos 5 on the trampling of the poor and the lifting up of the wealthy by court systems that have been bought. Micah 2 against those who covet fields and seize them. The prophetic critique of economic injustice is sustained and severe.

The Jubilee tradition in Leviticus 25 mandates structural economic reset every fifty years. Land returns to its original family. Slaves are freed. Debts are cancelled. The Jubilee is a structural limit on the accumulation of wealth across generations.

The Lord's Prayer asks God to *forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors* — using the same word for both spiritual indebtedness and economic indebtedness, and refusing to let the spiritual reading be cleanly separated from the economic one. The Sermon on the Mount on storing up treasures, on the impossibility of serving both God and money, on the lilies of the field that neither toil nor spin — these are economic teachings as well as spiritual ones, and the progressive reading is right to take them seriously.

So the progressive reading has real biblical material to work with. The reading is not invented. The reading picks up on features of the text that the conservative reading has often suppressed, and the progressive reading deserves credit for taking these features seriously.

## Where the progressive reading goes wrong

The progressive reading goes wrong, in my reading, at four places.

The first is the state-coercion problem. The biblical economic practice, in every instance it is described as church practice, is voluntary. The Acts 2 and Acts 4 sharing was voluntary — Peter explicitly says to Ananias in Acts 5:4, *while it remained unsold, did it not remain your own? And after it was sold, was it not at your disposal?* The property was Ananias's; he chose to sell it; the proceeds were his to distribute. The sin was not that he kept some back. The sin was that he lied about what he had given. The early church was a community of voluntary economic sharing, not a community of compulsory redistribution. The progressive translation of this into modern socialist terms generally assumes the state as the redistributive agent. The state was not the redistributive agent in the biblical material. The church was. The two are not the same.

The second is the autonomy-paired-with-economics problem. The modern progressive Christian tradition has, on the whole, paired its economic critique of accumulation with a sexual and anthropological autonomy that scripture does not endorse. The pairing is not necessary, but it has been the historical reality. The result is that the progressive Christian economic critique often comes packaged with anthropological commitments — about the self, about the body, about sexuality, about identity — that have substantial theological costs. The packaging makes the economic critique easier for traditional Christians to dismiss. The packaging should not be necessary. The packaging is, in my reading, what has kept the progressive economic critique from being received as widely in evangelical churches as it could be.

The third is the historical-evidence problem. The progressive reading of Jesus as proto-socialist has to do interpretive work that overrides specific features of the gospels. Jesus had wealthy patrons. He accepted hospitality from Pharisees and from Romans. He praised the centurion's faith. He told parables in which faithful servants are entrusted with talents and rewarded for using them productively — the parable of the talents in Matthew 25 sits uncomfortably with any reading that sees Jesus as opposed to investment, accumulation in service of stewardship, or differential outcomes based on productive use of resources. The progressive reading has to suppress these features, in much the same way the conservative reading has to suppress the explicit warnings about wealth. Both suppressions are honest interpretive moves but both involve selective reading.

The fourth is the twentieth-century historical record. State-imposed atheism in the twentieth century — the Soviet, Chinese, Cambodian, North Korean, Albanian regimes that explicitly tried to abolish religion in the name of socialist redistribution — killed more Christians than every era of Christendom combined. The estimates vary; the most careful work puts the total in the tens of millions. The progressive Christian reading often elides this history or treats it as not really socialism, or as the result of particular pathologies that need not recur. The honest reading has to grant that state power organized around redistribution has been, in twentieth-century practice, one of the most dangerous things in the world for the church. The history matters. The history is part of what serious Christians should bring to the conversation about state-imposed economic systems.

## What the early church actually did

Let me describe the early church's economic practice with more care, because the description is what the progressive reading often gets right in part but wrong in framing.

The early church practiced extraordinary generosity. The historical record is consistent on this. Rodney Stark's analysis of early Christian economic practice in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) documents the disproportionate care for plague victims, the disproportionate care for abandoned infants, the disproportionate care for widows and orphans, the disproportionate care for the imprisoned, and the disproportionate freeing of slaves. The early church was, by every measurable standard, more economically generous than the surrounding pagan culture. The generosity was specific, embodied, and personally costly.

The early church practiced voluntary economic sharing. Acts 2 and 4 describe communities in which property was sold and proceeds were distributed according to need. The practice was voluntary. The practice was within the church. The practice was supplementary to other economic activities — the early Christians were not all selling everything and living in common; they were maintaining households, working their trades, and contributing to the community's care for the poor as part of their discipleship.

The early church did not, as far as the historical record shows, advocate for state-imposed redistribution. The early Christians lived under imperial Rome, which was not a structure they could have plausibly influenced toward redistributive policies. But the early Christians also did not, when Christianity gained political legitimacy under Constantine and his successors, advocate for state-imposed economic restructuring. The post-Constantinian church mostly tried to do the same kind of voluntary work the pre-Constantinian church had done, on a larger institutional scale. The state was not the agent.

The pattern is consistent. Christian economic practice has been voluntary, ecclesial, generous, and oriented toward specific people. The progressive translation of this into modern socialist categories — state-imposed, comprehensive, structural — is a translation that loses something. What it loses is the specifically Christian character of the practice. The state's redistribution is not the same act as the church's redistribution, even if the outcomes look superficially similar.

## What the constructive position looks like

The third position, between the capitalist misreading and the socialist misreading, is the recovery of Christian economic practice as the church's responsibility.

The church should do what the state cannot do. The state can fund social welfare programs. The state cannot love the poor. The state can redistribute resources. The state cannot disciple a wealthy person out of the love of money. The state can provide healthcare. The state cannot sit with the dying. The state has a role; the role is real but limited; the church's role is different and not transferable to the state.

The Christian household should be structured around generosity that costs the giver something real. The historic tithe is a starting point, not a ceiling. The Christian who is giving ten percent of income should be asking whether the next disciplinary step is twelve, fifteen, twenty percent. The giving should be specific — to particular poor people whose names the giver knows, to organizations whose work the giver can verify, to causes the giver has prayed over. The giving should be sacrificial — the giver should be poorer for it than the giver would otherwise be. The giving should be cross-shaped — modeled on Christ, who *though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor.*

The Christian congregation should be structured around mutual aid in concrete ways. The widows in your congregation should not be in poverty. The unemployed should not be unable to make rent. The medically catastrophic situations should not be financial disasters. The early church handled all of these things, in voluntary community, and the contemporary congregation can recover much of the practice if it is willing to.

The Christian's political engagement on economic questions should be informed by all of this and should not collapse into partisan identity. The Christian can vote for various policies on various grounds, including economic grounds, but the voting is not the discipleship. The discipleship is the household practice and the congregational practice, and the household and congregational practice are what scripture cares most about.

## The verdict

Jesus was not a socialist. The honest reading does not make him one. He was also not a capitalist. The honest reading does not make him that, either.

The Jesus of the Gospels is something more demanding than either modern category can accommodate. He calls his followers to a generosity that costs them real money, a use of wealth oriented toward specific neighbors, a refusal to organize life around accumulation, and an attention to the poor that is more sustained than either modern political coalition demands. The calling is harder than the capitalist version softens or the socialist version simplifies. The calling is the gospel.

I am writing this for the progressive Christian reader who has been told that the careful reading of Jesus produces a socialism that scripture endorses. The careful reading produces something different. The careful reading produces a discipleship that is more economically demanding than capitalism allows and is also more wary of state power than socialism allows. The discipleship is uncomfortable. The discipleship is right. The recovery of the third position is what the post-Christendom church will be working on, and the work is overdue.

The next essay takes up the Sermon on the Mount as the political document it actually is. The American church has read the Sermon as spiritualized devotion for two centuries. The honest reading is more political and more demanding, and it is the next thing this writing project will address.
