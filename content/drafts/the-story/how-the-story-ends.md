Ask almost anyone — churchgoer or not — how the Bible ends, and you will get some version of the same answer. The good people die and go to heaven. The bad people go somewhere else. The earth, presumably, is left behind, a used-up stage struck once the play is over. Heaven is pictured as a destination above and away from here — clouds, harps, disembodied souls, an eternity of floating somewhere that is emphatically not this world. That is the ending most people carry in their heads, including most Christians. It is on the sympathy cards. It is in the funeral homilies. It is what we tell the children.

It is also not how the Bible ends.

I want to be careful here, because this is not a technicality and it is not me being clever. The actual ending of the biblical story is stranger, far more physical, and immeasurably better than the one we have substituted for it — and getting it right changes what the whole story was ever about. **The Bible does not end with souls escaping the world to a place called heaven. It ends with heaven coming down to earth, with the dead raised in bodies, and with this creation — the very world that broke in Eden and groans in Romans — healed, renewed, and made the eternal home of God with his people.** The hope of the Christian story is not evacuation. It is resurrection. Not escape from the world, but the rescue of it.

## Read the last two chapters

The simplest way to see this is to read the end of the book, slowly, as if you had never had it pre-explained.

Revelation is a wild and difficult book, but its final two chapters are luminous and almost shockingly concrete. John sees "a new heaven and a new earth" — not the erasure of the old world but its renewal. And then watch the direction of travel, because it is the opposite of what we have been taught. He sees "the Holy City, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God." Coming down. Not us going up to heaven, but the city of God descending to earth. And a loud voice says the thing the whole Bible has been straining toward since the garden: "Behold, God's dwelling place is now among the people, and he will dwell with them. They will be his people, and God himself will be with them and be their God. He will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the old order of things has passed away" (Revelation 21:3–4). The story does not end with departed souls in the sky. It ends with God moving in — permanently, bodily, here — and personally wiping the tears off human faces.

And the final image, in chapter 22, is not a return to a heavenly nowhere. It is a garden-city, with the tree of life that was lost in Eden growing again, its leaves "for the healing of the nations," and a river running through it, and the curse — the curse pronounced on the ground in Genesis 3 — gone. Read the first chapters of the Bible and the last chapters side by side and you see what the storyteller is doing. The whole drama is a great arc from a garden to a garden-city, from a world made good and broken to a world remade and unbreakable, with the same God walking with the same kind of creatures in the cool of the day — except now there is no exit, no flaming sword, no way out, because there will be no more falling. The Bible ends where it began, only better, and it ends *here*, on a renewed earth, not somewhere else.

## Where the "heaven" idea came from, and what it cost us

If that is how the book ends, where did the floating-souls-in-the-sky version come from? Largely not from the Bible. It came from a centuries-long seepage of Greek philosophy into Christian imagination — the old Platonic instinct that the body is a prison and the soul's destiny is to escape matter altogether for a purer, immaterial realm. That instinct is deep and intuitive and it is not Christian. The Bible is relentlessly physical. It begins with God making matter and calling it very good. Its climax is not a soul ascending but a body rising — Jesus raised with a body that ate and could be touched. And its ending is not the abolition of the physical world but its renewal.

N. T. Wright has spent more energy than perhaps any living scholar trying to recover this for ordinary Christians, most accessibly in his 2008 book *Surprised by Hope.* Wright's argument, made patiently and at length, is that the church has badly garbled its own hope — that we have replaced the robust biblical expectation of resurrection and new creation with a thin, semi-pagan hope of going to heaven when we die, and that the swap has cost us dearly. He distinguishes carefully between what happens immediately after death — being with Christ, which the New Testament does affirm, a real but temporary rest — and the actual end of the story, which is the resurrection of the body into a renewed creation. Heaven, in Wright's careful phrasing, is not the final destination; it is the waystation. "Life after death" is followed by what he calls "life after life after death" — the resurrection, the new earth, the real ending. We have been telling the wrong ending, and telling it for so long that the right one sounds strange to the very people who should know it best.

And it has cost us more than precision. Wright argues that what you believe about the end shapes everything you do in the middle. If the world is a doomed stage to be abandoned, then the body does not finally matter, the earth does not finally matter, justice and beauty and the work of your hands do not finally matter — all of it burns, and only the rescued souls float free. But if the world is going to be *renewed* rather than discarded, if God intends to redeem this creation and not replace it, then everything done in love here has a future. It is not lost. It is taken up. The biblical ending makes the present world weightier, not lighter, because the present world is the one being saved.

You can see the difference in two kinds of Christians. One kind treats this life as a waiting room — the world a thing to be endured and escaped, the only real work the saving of souls out of a sinking ship. That instinct has a noble side and a terrible one. Its noble side rightly insists that people matter eternally. Its terrible side has, across the last two centuries, let Christians shrug at slavery, at poverty, at a poisoned creation, on the quiet assumption that none of it was going to last anyway. The other kind of Christian treats this life as the opening scene of the world's renewal — and so plants trees, makes things beautiful, pursues justice, heals bodies, builds, mends, and tends, not because any of it earns heaven but because all of it points to the new creation and, somehow Paul will not fully explain, gets taken up into it. The first works for the future. The second is "your labor in the Lord is not in vain," the line Paul places at the very end of his great resurrection chapter, right after he has finished describing the raising of the dead. The ending you believe in is not abstract. It is in your hands every ordinary Tuesday.

## The whole creation was always the point

This is not a fringe reading lifted from one obscure passage. It is the consistent hope of the New Testament, and Paul states it as plainly as he states anything.

In Romans, the same chapter that diagnosed the world's groaning announces its cure, and the cure is cosmic. The creation itself, Paul writes, "will be liberated from its bondage to decay and brought into the freedom and glory of the children of God." And he does not exempt us from the waiting; we too, "who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for our adoption to sonship, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:18–25). Read that last phrase again. The thing Christians are finally waiting for is not the escape of their souls but the *redemption of their bodies* — and not their bodies only, but the liberation of the whole creation alongside them. The future Paul describes is not less physical than the present. It is more. It is matter healed, bodies raised, the groaning world set free.

The British scholar Richard Bauckham, who has written deeply on both Revelation and the theology of creation, presses the same point from the other end of the New Testament: the biblical hope is for the renewal of all things, a creation set right and filled with God's presence, not a spiritualized exit from creation. Bauckham has spent particular care on Revelation, the very book we most often mine for fearful end-times speculation, and his reading reframes it entirely. Revelation is not, at heart, a coded forecast of disasters. It is a vision given to a small, persecuted church under the boot of an empire that looked like it would last forever — a vision meant to let them see, behind the apparent triumph of Rome, the actual government of the universe, and behind the apparent end of the world, its renewal. The point of the beasts and the bowls is not to frighten the curious. It is to steady the suffering by showing them how the story ends: with the empire fallen, the martyrs vindicated, the curse lifted, and God dwelling with his people on a healed earth. The God of the Bible does not make things in order to throw them away. He makes things in order to redeem them. The ending honors the beginning.

And Paul gives us the last note of the whole symphony, the destination of the entire arc, in a single line: that in the end God will be "all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). Every fracture mended, every estrangement healed, the whole renewed creation flooded with the presence of the God it was made for, with nothing left over and nothing left out. That is where the story is going. Not up and away. Into fullness. Into a world so saturated with God that there is no corner of it left untouched, no tear left unwiped, no death left undone.

## Why the right ending changes the grief

I have stood at more gravesides than I can count, and I have heard myself reach for the thin version — *she is in a better place now, looking down on us* — because it is what people expect and it is not entirely false. But it is not the thing the Bible actually promises, and the thing the Bible actually promises is so much stronger that I have come to think we cheat the grieving when we hand them the weaker hope.

Because the weaker hope leaves the body in the ground forever. It treats the casket as the end of that person's physical story, and the most it can offer is that some immaterial part has gone elsewhere. The biblical hope refuses to leave the body in the ground. It says that the same Jesus who walked out of his own tomb is the firstfruits, the first of a harvest, and that what happened to him is the preview of what God will do for everyone who belongs to him — not souls drifting in a disembodied heaven, but people raised, bodies and all, into a renewed and unbreakable world where the One who conquered death has wiped death out for good. When I stand at a graveside now, what I most want to say is not that the person has escaped the world. It is that the world is going to be put right, and they are going to be there for it, more solid and more alive than they ever were here.

There is one more thing the right ending does, and it is the hardest for a culture like ours to hear. It tells you that your hope does not finally rest on what you can build. We are a people exhausted by the demand to save the world ourselves — to fix the climate, the country, the family, the self, all of it on our own shoulders, with the clock running. The biblical ending lifts that crushing weight without excusing us from the work. The new creation is God's gift, descending from God, not the sum of human progress climbing up to meet him. We do not build the new Jerusalem. We receive it. And that means our labor here is freed from the terror of being the only thing standing between the world and ruin — freed to be what it was always meant to be, a sign of the ending rather than the desperate manufacture of it. You can plant the tree, love the person, do the good thing, and rest, because the outcome was never riding on you alone. It is riding on the One who has already promised to make all things new and walked out of his own grave to prove he means it.

That is the actual ending. It is not the one on the sympathy cards. It is the one the Bible has been telling from the first verse — a story that begins with God making a good world, runs through the breaking and the long rescue and the great turn at the cross, and ends not with the good world abandoned but with the good world healed, and God moving in to stay.

The story does not end with us leaving home. It ends with home being made new, and the door of the garden, this time, left open for good.
