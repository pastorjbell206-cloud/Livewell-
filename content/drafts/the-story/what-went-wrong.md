Read the news for ten minutes and you will not need convincing that something is wrong. The argument has never been about whether. The argument has always been about what — and that argument is the most important one a person ever has, because the diagnosis decides the cure. Tell me what you think is broken and I can predict almost everything else about how you will try to live.

The diagnoses on offer are familiar. The world is wrong because we have not yet been educated enough, says one — give us another generation of schooling and reason will do its work. The world is wrong because of bad systems, says another — fix the economics, the politics, the structures, and the people inside them will come right. The world is wrong because of *them*, says a third, always the most popular — the other tribe, the other party, the other people, and if we could just defeat or remove them, the trouble would lift. Each of these has a piece of the truth. None of them can account for the strangest fact about human wrongness, which is that it follows us across every reform. We educate ourselves and invent more sophisticated cruelties. We fix the system and corrupt the new one. We defeat *them* and discover, with a sinking heart, that the line ran through us all along.

The Bible opens with a diagnosis stranger and older than any of these, and it is the one this essay is about. **What went wrong with the world, according to Scripture, is not first a problem of ignorance, or systems, or other people. It is that a good world made for relationship with its Maker was fractured at the root, and the fracture runs through everything — every structure, every relationship, and the center of every human heart, including yours and certainly mine.** The Bible does not call the world broken the way a machine breaks. It calls it broken the way a marriage breaks. And that one shift in the metaphor changes the whole picture of what would have to happen to fix it.

## Start with how good it was meant to be

You cannot understand the breaking until you have felt the thing that broke. So the Bible does not open with the fall. It opens with a garden.

Genesis 1 is not, first of all, an argument about the age of rocks. It is a poem of order and gift — a God who speaks, and light comes; who separates and fills and names; who pauses after each act to call it good, and at the end calls the whole thing very good (Genesis 1:31). Read it slowly and you notice the tone. This is not a reluctant deity grinding out a creation. This is generosity. The world is made the way a host prepares a home for a guest he is glad to expect — land and sea sorted, lights hung, the table set with every green thing for food, and only then the guest brought in. Human beings arrive last, made in the image of God, given the world not to exploit but to tend, set in relationship with the Maker, with each other, and with the ground itself. Genesis 2 zooms in and shows it intimate: God forming the man from dust with his own hands, breathing into him, planting a garden for him, deciding it is not good for him to be alone and making another. The first thing the Bible tells you about reality is that it was meant to be a home, and you were meant to be wanted in it.

The Hebrew Bible has a word for the way that home was supposed to work, and it is one of the most important words you can learn: *shalom.* We translate it "peace," but that is far too thin. Shalom is not merely the absence of conflict. It is the presence of everything as it should be — wholeness, flourishing, every relationship intact, the human knit rightly to God, to neighbor, to self, to the world. The Calvin College theologian Cornelius Plantinga, in his 1995 book *Not the Way It's Supposed to Be*, defined shalom as "the webbing together of God, humans, and all creation in justice, fulfillment, and delight." That is the world as it left the Maker's hands. Webbed together. In delight.

Hold that picture, because everything that follows is the tearing of that web.

## The fracture

The third chapter of Genesis is the most consequential short story ever told, and it is told with terrible economy.

There is a command — one tree, one limit, the single mark that the humans are creatures and not the Creator. There is a question, hissed by the serpent, and notice that it is not first a temptation to do something but a temptation to doubt something: *Did God really say?* The whole catastrophe begins as a suspicion about the goodness of God — the suggestion that the Maker is holding out, that the limit is not love but envy, that the way to become fully human is to stop trusting and start seizing. And then the act. They take. They eat. They reach past the one boundary that defined them as creatures and grasp at being gods.

What happens next is not a thunderbolt. It is worse. It is a series of fractures, quiet and total. They hide — the relationship with God, severed, replaced by fear. They blame — the man accuses the woman, the woman accuses the serpent, the relationship between them already poisoned in its first crisis. The ground itself turns against the man, yielding thorns, making his work a struggle (Genesis 3:17–19). And finally they are sent out of the garden, east of Eden, and an angel with a flaming sword stands where the way back used to be. The web of shalom does not so much snap as unravel — every strand at once. God. Neighbor. Self. World. All of it pulling loose in a single afternoon.

This is the biblical diagnosis, and it is crucial to see what kind of diagnosis it is. The problem is not located in any one place where we could surgically remove it. It is not in the economy, though it corrupts the economy. It is not in the systems, though it deforms every system. It is not in the other tribe, though it animates the other tribe and ours. It is in the relationship — the broken trust between creatures and their Creator — and from that center it radiates into everything else. Plantinga's whole book is a recovery of the old word for this radiating fracture, a word we have nearly stopped being able to say without flinching: sin. Not a list of misdeeds. Sin as the *condition* — shalom violated, the web torn, the world bent away from the One who holds it together.

## Why this diagnosis explains what the others cannot

Here is where the biblical account earns its keep, because it explains the one thing the popular diagnoses keep tripping over.

If the problem were only ignorance, then knowledge would cure it — and the most educated century in human history produced the most efficient atrocities in human history. If the problem were only bad systems, then good systems would cure it — and we keep building good systems and watching them rot from the inside, because the rot was never only in the system. If the problem were only *them*, then removing them would cure it — and every revolution that has ever guillotined the oppressor has discovered the appetite for oppression intact in the new regime, sometimes by the following Tuesday. The reason these solutions keep failing is that they all locate the wrong somewhere outside the self. The Bible locates it, finally, inside — and then has the nerve to say it is inside everyone, no exceptions, the diagnosis that no one is exempt from and that is therefore the only one that ever describes the actual room.

This is the doctrine modern people most resist and most quietly confirm. We cannot stop grading the world. We are moral animals to the marrow — we rage at injustice, we are wounded by betrayal, we expect the world to be other than it is, and we expect it with a fierceness that makes no sense if the world is merely matter in motion with no should about it. Where did we get the ruler we keep measuring reality against and finding it short? The Bible's answer is that we remember the garden. We were made for shalom, and the ache we feel at every broken thing is the memory of a home we lost. The longing is not naïve. The longing is evidence.

Plantinga makes a further observation that has stayed with me for years, because it names something I see in myself. Sin, he argues, is a parasite. It has no power of its own to create; it can only corrupt good things. Pride is a corruption of proper self-respect. Lust is a corruption of desire, which is good. Greed is a corruption of the legitimate care a person owes his own household. Cruelty often masquerades as a corruption of justice. This is why sin is so hard to see and so hard to root out — it never arrives as something obviously alien. It arrives as a good thing slightly bent, a right desire aimed one degree off, a love that has curved in on itself. The man who wrecks his family for an affair did not set out to worship destruction; he set out chasing a real good, intimacy, by a ruinous road. The fracture works by spoiling, never by inventing. That is exactly why we keep mistaking it for something else, and exactly why no reform that only rearranges our goods will reach it. You cannot legislate the bend out of a heart.

And here is the part that should stop us. The same capacity that lets us recognize evil so sharply in others goes strangely quiet when it turns inward. We are prosecuting attorneys about the world's wrongness and defense attorneys about our own. I have watched it in myself — the speed with which I can diagnose a colleague's pride and the slowness with which I notice the identical thing in me. That asymmetry is not an accident of personality. It is the signature of the very condition the Bible is describing. A fracture that ran neatly outside us would be visible. The reason it is so hard to see is that it runs through the one instrument we are using to look.

## The whole creation, not just us

There is one more turn in the biblical diagnosis, and modern Christians often miss it because we have privatized sin into a personal matter of individual guilt. Scripture refuses that shrinking. The fracture, it insists, did not stay inside the human heart. It went out into the world.

Paul says it plainly, and it is one of the strangest sentences in the New Testament. The whole creation, he writes, "was subjected to frustration" and is "groaning as in the pains of childbirth," waiting to be liberated from its bondage to decay (Romans 8:20–22). The damage is cosmic. It is in the thorned ground and the failing body, in the predator and the famine and the slow running-down of everything. N. T. Wright, writing on this passage, points out that we have badly misread our problem if we think the trouble is merely that human souls have sinned and need rescuing *out* of a doomed world. The world itself is wounded and the world itself is groaning, and what it groans for is not evacuation but healing. The wrongness Paul describes is not a stain on an otherwise fine machine. It is a creation-wide grief, and creation knows it, the way a body running a fever knows something is wrong even when it cannot name it.

This is why the Christian account of wrongness is not, in the end, a counsel of despair, though it is more honest about ruin than any rival. To say the world is fractured the way a marriage is fractured is also to say that the world is the kind of thing that *can* be reconciled. You do not reconcile a machine. You repair it or you scrap it. But a broken relationship can be restored, by a costly act of love from the side that was wronged. And that — though it is the next chapters of the story, not this one — is precisely the shape of everything the Bible says God does next.

## Why naming it right is mercy

I want to say plainly why this diagnosis matters, especially for the reader who has only ever heard "sin" used as a club.

A wrong diagnosis is not neutral. It kills. The doctor who tells you your tumor is indigestion is not being kind; he is being lethal with a smile. And the cultural diagnoses we prefer — you are fine, the problem is out there, you only need more information or a better system or the defeat of your enemies — are comforting in exactly the way the cheerful doctor is comforting, and just as deadly. They send us to fight the wrong war, year after year, generation after generation, and they leave the actual fracture untouched at the center of us.

The Bible's diagnosis is harder to hear and is the beginning of mercy. It tells you the truth you already half-know in your most honest moments: that the line between the world as it is and the world as it should be does not run neatly between you and your enemies. It runs through your own chest. I know that line. I have crossed it in private in ways the people who admire me have never seen. The relief is not in pretending the line is not there. The relief is in finally being told the truth by Someone who knows exactly where it runs and has already moved, at unspeakable cost, to mend the thing it cut.

Something is wrong with the world. You knew that before you opened this. What the Bible adds is harder and, in the end, kinder than every other answer: the something that is wrong is not only out there, and the One who made the garden has not walked away from it groaning.

He went down into the wreckage himself. But that is the next part of the story.
