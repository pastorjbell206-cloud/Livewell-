The departure of young adults from the American church was so consistent and so well-documented over the first two decades of this century that it began to feel like a settled trajectory — that the demographic math was simply pointing in one direction and would continue to do so indefinitely. Recent data suggests the picture is more complicated than that. [cite — confirm] Some young adults are coming back. The question worth asking is why — and what that means for the churches they are entering.

The return is not driven by what the church was doing to attract them. That is the first important thing to understand. Most of the young adults returning to faith communities in significant numbers are not returning because a congregation's marketing got better or because the worship service became more accessible. They are returning because something in their lives — a birth, a death, a marriage, a crisis, a sustained encounter with mortality or meaninglessness — pressed them against questions that the secular alternatives in their life could not adequately answer. The return is, in many cases, a return to questions they left behind, not a return to the institution they remembered.

This creates both an opportunity and a danger. The opportunity is that these returning adults are not looking for the same thing they were offered before. They left partly because the version of Christianity they were handed could not sustain serious engagement with life's hardest realities. They are coming back, many of them, specifically because they have now lived enough of those realities to know they need something with more weight. They will not be satisfied with the consumer experience they once rejected.

The danger is that the church, encountering a return of young adults, will immediately try to reproduce what produced the previous engagement — more accessible services, stronger programming for their demographic, contemporary music, a pastor who speaks their cultural language. If these returning adults came back looking for depth and encounter instead what they encounter is a more polished version of the superficiality they left, they will leave again, faster than before, and this time with much less likelihood of return.

The parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15:11–32) is usually read as a story about an individual's restoration. But it also contains a portrait of the kind of reception that makes return possible. The father does not redesign the estate to look like the far country. He does not pretend the departure never happened. He runs. He embraces. He throws a party. He does not manage the returning son's expectations about what has changed. He welcomes what and who the son actually is now, not who he was when he left.

The church that will keep the returning young adult is the church that can receive a person who has changed — who has lived through the secular alternatives, who carries real questions and real doubts and real disappointment, who does not need to be convinced that life is hard but who needs to know whether the gospel has anything to say about life as it actually is. The congregation that can hold that conversation — honestly, without defensiveness, with theological substance rather than therapeutic platitude — is the congregation that someone will stay in.

I have watched people come back and leave again within six months because the community they found was warm but shallow. I have also watched people come back and stay, and what kept them was almost never the music or the building. It was the experience of being genuinely known, genuinely engaged, genuinely held by a community that had some idea what to do with the person they actually were.

The returning young adult is not a demographic opportunity. He is a neighbor who went to a far country and came back.

The question is what he finds when he arrives.

Make it worth the walk home.
