There is a particular kind of restlessness that arrives in December, and almost nobody names it correctly. We call it stress, or busyness, or the holidays, and we treat it as a logistics problem to be solved with better planning and more sleep. But underneath the errands there is something older and stranger moving — a low ache that the lights and the music are designed to cover and never quite do. A sense that we are waiting for something, and that whatever it is has not arrived, and that the wrapped boxes are not it.

The church has a name for that ache, and a season built to hold it. The season is called Advent, and it does the one thing the surrounding culture cannot bear to do in December.

It waits.

**Advent is the four weeks before Christmas in which the church deliberately refuses to celebrate yet — sitting instead in darkness and longing, rehearsing what it was to wait for a Savior who had not come, in order to learn how to wait for the one who has not come back.** That is the strange double motion of the season, and it is the thing newcomers find most confusing. The word *Advent* means *coming.* But we already know Christ came. So why spend a month waiting for an arrival the calendar has already filed under history?

The answer is the most useful thing the church year has to teach a restless age.

## The season the culture deleted

Notice first what has happened to December. The culture has collapsed Advent and Christmas into a single, frantic thirty-day sprint of celebration that begins the day after Thanksgiving and ends, abruptly, around two o'clock on Christmas afternoon, when the paper is in the trash and a strange flatness settles over the house. We have all felt that flatness. We rarely ask what it means.

It means we did the season backwards. We spent a month celebrating an arrival and then, at the precise moment the church year is just beginning to feast, we quit. The secular December is front-loaded with joy and ends in exhaustion. The Christian December is the reverse: it begins in the dark and builds, slowly, toward a light it will not let you have early. Advent is not the Christmas warm-up. It is a season with its own integrity, and the first gift it gives is permission to feel, in a culture that demands relentless cheer, the thing many people are actually feeling under the lights, which is some mixture of grief and hope and a longing they cannot place.

Fleming Rutledge, the Episcopal preacher whose collection of Advent sermons — published simply as *Advent* in 2018 — has become the season's modern handbook, insists that Advent is the most countercultural season in the church year precisely because it refuses to rush to the manger. It begins, she writes, in the dark. It takes the darkness seriously. It does not pretend the world is fine and only needs a little holiday sparkle to be reminded of it. Advent looks at the actual condition of things — the wars, the cruelty, the long silence of God that so many people feel and so few will admit in church — and it says: yes. This is what we are waiting to be rescued from. And it does not flinch.

That honesty is the first thing that makes Advent feel less like religion and more like the truth.

## Two comings, not one

Here is the piece that resolves the puzzle of waiting for what already happened. Advent has never been about only one arrival. It holds two, and it holds them at the same time, and the whole season vibrates with the tension between them.

The first coming is the one everybody knows. A child born in an occupied backwater of the Roman Empire, to a teenage mother, in conditions so ordinary they were nearly squalid. The prophet Isaiah had seen it coming centuries out: "The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light; those who dwelt in a land of deep darkness, on them has light shined" (Isaiah 9:2). Luke records the long human waiting that surrounded the birth — Mary's astonished song, Zechariah's recovered voice, old Simeon and Anna in the temple who had been waiting their whole lives to see the consolation of Israel and would not die until they had (Luke 1–2). The first coming is the answer to all that waiting. It is the arrival that Advent's first half remembers.

But the church discovered early that it was now living in a second waiting, stranger than the first. Christ had come. Christ had died and risen. And then Christ had gone, with a promise to return and finish what he started — to set right everything that is still, visibly, catastrophically wrong. The last words of the entire Bible are a prayer of waiting: "Come, Lord Jesus" (Revelation 22:20). So the Christian lives suspended between two advents. One has happened. One has not. And Advent is the season that names this exact position — the now-and-not-yet, the kingdom that has broken in but has not broken through, the deposit received and the inheritance still outstanding.

This is why waiting for something that already happened is not absurd. **In Advent the church rehearses the first coming in order to train itself for the second** — using the long-ago birth, which we know turned out, to learn how to keep faith through the long-still-coming return, which we do not yet see. We wait for Bethlehem so that we will know how to wait for the day God makes all things new. The first arrival is the church's evidence that the second one is not a fantasy. He came once. He keeps his appointments.

## Why waiting is not wasted time

We are, as a people, almost incapable of waiting. We have engineered our entire economy to abolish it — same-day delivery, streaming, the swipe that summons a car, the feed that refreshes the instant attention flags. Waiting now registers to us as a malfunction, a problem to be solved, a sign that the system is broken. And so we have lost an entire spiritual capacity, because the deepest things God does in a person, he does almost entirely in the waiting.

Paul knew this. In one of the most piercing passages in the New Testament he writes that the whole creation is groaning — straining like a woman in labor — and that we ourselves groan inwardly while we wait for our adoption to be completed, for our bodies to be redeemed. And then the line that Advent is built on: "But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience" (Romans 8:22–25). Hope, Paul says, is not the same as having. Hope is precisely the posture of the one who does not yet see and waits anyway. The groan and the hope are not opposites. They live in the same breath.

This is the discipline Advent restores, and it cuts against everything the surrounding age has trained into us. The world tells you that the gap between desire and fulfillment is a problem to be eliminated as fast as money allows. Advent tells you the gap is holy ground — that the waiting itself is where longing is purified, where you find out what you actually want underneath all the things you thought you wanted, where the soul learns to ache for God rather than for the next delivery. Bobby Gross, in his book *Living the Christian Year*, describes Advent as a season that teaches Christians to inhabit their longing rather than anesthetize it. Most of us spend enormous energy anesthetizing ours. Advent asks us to stop, and to feel the ache, and to let it point somewhere.

There is a deeper reason waiting matters, and it has to do with what hope actually is. We tend to use the word *hope* the way we use the word *wish* — a vague optimism that things will turn out, a pleasant expectation with no particular ground. That is not the biblical word. Hope, in Scripture, is a settled confidence in a promise from someone trustworthy, held onto precisely in the stretch of time before the promise comes true. Which means hope can only exist in the waiting. A hope that has already received what it hoped for is no longer hope; it is possession. The waiting is not the obstacle to hope. The waiting is the only place hope can live. Abolish the waiting and you do not get more hope. You get less, because you have removed the very condition that makes hope possible. This is why a culture that cannot wait is also, not coincidentally, a culture that has nearly lost the capacity for hope, and traded it for a thin, anxious optimism that collapses the moment things stop trending up.

Advent trains the older, sturdier thing. It puts you back in the position of the people of Israel, who waited centuries for a Messiah on the strength of a promise, who died without seeing him, who held the hope across generations because the One who made the promise was faithful. That kind of hope is a muscle, and like any muscle it atrophies without use. Most of us have let it waste away. We have not had to wait for anything in so long that we have forgotten how, and when the real waiting comes — for healing, for reconciliation, for a prodigal to come home — we find we have no strength for it. Advent is the annual rebuilding of that strength, four weeks of practiced waiting so that when the unchosen waiting arrives, we are not starting from nothing.

I have spent Decembers numbing the ache with activity — more events, more obligations, more noise, a calendar so full there was no quiet space in which any longing could speak. I called it the Christmas season. It was avoidance wearing tinsel. Advent is the season that takes the activity away and leaves you alone with the question the noise was covering: what are you really waiting for. For most of us, the honest answer is not a thing on a list. It is closer to a Person.

## Living in the dark, on purpose

So what does it actually look like to keep Advent if you have never done it?

It is quieter than you would expect, and slower. It means letting the four weeks before Christmas be different from Christmas itself — not jumping straight to the feast, but holding off, the way you hold off on a meal you are genuinely hungry for so that the eating, when it comes, means something. It often means a single candle, then two, then three, then four, a little more light each week of a darkening month, so that the room you sit in is teaching your body what the season is about before your mind has framed it. It means reading the parts of the prophets that ache, the parts about people walking in darkness and the parts that promise a light, and letting both halves stay true at once.

And it means resisting the enormous cultural pressure to skip it. This is harder than it sounds. Everything around you in December is already celebrating, has been since before December started, and to keep Advent is to stand slightly out of step with all of it — to be the household still waiting while the rest of the world has moved on to the party. That out-of-step quality is not a flaw in the practice. It is the point. Advent makes you a little strange in December, and the strangeness is itself a kind of witness, a quiet refusal to pretend the arrival has happened when, in the deepest sense, the world is still waiting for it to be completed.

I should be honest that the keeping of it is awkward at first, especially if you came up with no church calendar at all, as I did. The first year you try to hold off on Christmas while everyone around you has already started feels less like devotion and more like deprivation — as if you are the only one who did not get the memo that the celebration began weeks ago. The candles feel like a quaint gesture. The waiting feels like pretending. This is normal, and it passes, because what you are actually doing in that first awkward Advent is unlearning a December that the culture installed in you before you had any say. The strangeness fades as the season does its slow work, and one year, usually without warning, you notice that the early waiting has made the eventual feast taste like something — that Christmas, when it finally comes, lands with a weight it never had when you started celebrating it in November. The discipline of not-yet is what gives the at-last its force. You cannot feel the arrival of a light you were never sitting in the dark for.

There is also something the season does for grief, and this is the part newcomers least expect. December is, for an enormous number of people, the hardest month of the year — the season when the empty chair at the table is emptiest, when the forced cheer of the culture makes the private sorrow feel unspeakable, when everyone seems to be celebrating and you are quietly falling apart. The secular December has no room for these people. Its relentless brightness makes their darkness a kind of failure. Advent does the opposite. It begins in the dark, names the world's brokenness without flinching, and tells the grieving that their ache is not a malfunction but the very thing the season was built to hold. For the person carrying a loss into December, Advent is often the first time the church year stops feeling like decoration and starts feeling like the truth — a season honest enough to admit that we are not all fine, and faithful enough to insist that the light is coming anyway.

There is a reason the church put this season first, at the head of its whole year. It wanted to begin not with achievement but with longing. Not with the light but with the dark and the promise. It wanted the Christian year to open the way the gospel actually opens — with a people in the dark, waiting for a God who has promised to come, and who, against every probability, did.

He came once into a night no one expected. The whole of Advent is the church learning to trust that he will come again into a darkness we can barely name. Until he does, we light the candles one at a time, and we say the oldest prayer there is, and we wait.

Come, Lord Jesus.
