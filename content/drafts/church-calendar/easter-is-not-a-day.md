Picture a typical Easter. The lilies appear at the front of the church, the building is fuller than it has been since Christmas, there is a sunrise service for the devoted and a late service for everyone else, and by early afternoon the good clothes are off and the ham is on the table and the children are hunting plastic eggs in the yard. By Monday it is over. The lilies will be dead by Wednesday. The decorations come down. The world, and the church with it, moves on to whatever is next.

This is how nearly everyone experiences Easter — as a single, bright, slightly exhausting Sunday, the spring counterpart to Christmas morning. And it is, when you stand back and look at it, one of the strangest things the modern church has done to itself.

**Because Easter was never meant to be a day. It is a season — fifty days, called Eastertide, the longest celebration in the Christian year — and the church built it that long for a reason: what happened on that first Sunday is too enormous for the human heart to take in over a single morning.** We have shrunk the central event of our entire faith into an afternoon. We spend more time celebrating the end of a war or the start of a new year than the church historically spent celebrating the one event it stakes everything on. Something has gone wrong with our sense of scale.

## A celebration longer than the fast

Notice the imbalance in how most of us keep the year, because it reveals what we actually believe. Lent, the season of fasting and repentance that leads up to Easter, runs forty days, and serious Christians keep it with real discipline — giving things up, going without, walking into the wilderness. Then Easter arrives, the thing all that fasting was preparing for, and we celebrate it for one morning and quit.

The historic church did the opposite. It fasted for forty days and then feasted for fifty. Eastertide runs from Easter Sunday all the way to Pentecost, seven full weeks, and during those weeks the ancient church refused to fast at all, refused even to kneel in prayer, because kneeling was a posture of penitence and this was not a season for penitence. This was a season for standing up — for celebration so sustained it changed your body's posture for nearly two months. The feast was longer than the fast. The joy outlasted the sorrow. The proportion preached its own sermon: that the resurrection is bigger than everything it answered, that the celebration of life must run longer than the discipline of facing death.

We have reversed the proportion. We are rigorous about the fasting and careless about the feast. And I suspect that reversal is not an accident of scheduling. It is a confession. We find it easier to believe in our own sinfulness, which we experience daily, than in the resurrection, which we have never seen. The forty days of Lent feel true to us. The fifty days of Easter feel like a stretch. So we keep the one and abandon the other, and in doing so we quietly tell the truth about which we actually find more believable.

## Why one Sunday cannot hold it

To see why the resurrection needs fifty days, you have to grasp how large the New Testament thinks the event is. We have domesticated it into a happy ending — Jesus was dead, now he is alive, the story works out. But the first Christians did not experience it as a happy ending to a sad story. They experienced it as the beginning of a new creation breaking into the middle of the old one.

Paul, writing to the Corinthians in what is the earliest detailed account of the resurrection we possess, treats it not as a comforting belief but as the load-bearing wall of reality itself. "If Christ has not been raised," he writes, "your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). Everything hangs on it. And then he widens the lens until it takes in the whole cosmos: the risen Christ is the firstfruits, the first sheaf of an entire harvest of resurrection that will eventually include all who belong to him and the renewal of creation itself. Death, the last enemy, will be destroyed. This is not a man surviving his own funeral. This is the hinge of history turning.

N.T. Wright, the New Testament scholar whose book *Surprised by Hope*, published in 2008, has done more than any modern work to recover what the resurrection actually means, argues that the church lost the plot when it reduced Easter to a story about going to heaven when you die. That, Wright insists, is not the New Testament's hope at all. The resurrection of Jesus is the launch of God's project to renew the entire world — to raise bodies, restore creation, and bring heaven and earth together at last. Easter is not God's reassurance that we will escape this world. It is God's announcement that he is reclaiming it. A new creation has begun, in a garden, on a Sunday, in the body of a crucified man who walked out of his tomb and ate breakfast with his friends. You cannot fit that on a single morning. You can barely fit it in fifty days.

## What the fifty days are for

So what does the church do with the seven weeks after Easter Sunday? It does what the first witnesses did. It lives inside the astonishment, slowly, as the implications land one after another.

Read the resurrection accounts in John 20 and 21 and notice how gradual the believing is, even for the people who saw it with their own eyes. Mary mistakes him for the gardener. The disciples lock the doors in fear even after they have heard the reports. Thomas refuses to believe until he can put his hand in the wounds. And then, in the final chapter, the risen Jesus does the most ordinary thing imaginable — he builds a fire on a beach and cooks fish for breakfast and asks Peter, three times, whether he loves him. The resurrection does not arrive as a thunderclap that settles everything at once. It arrives in walks and meals and conversations, over weeks, as people who had watched their hope die learn, slowly, to trust that it is alive again. Eastertide is the church rehearsing that same slow believing. It takes time to absorb that death has been defeated. The season gives you the time.

And it builds toward something. The fifty days are not an aimless victory lap. They lead to Pentecost, the day the Spirit was poured out and the church was born. In Acts 2, Peter stands up in Jerusalem — the same Peter who had denied even knowing Jesus — and preaches the resurrection to a crowd, and three thousand people are added in a day. The resurrection does not stay private. It moves outward, from an empty tomb to a locked room to a public square to the ends of the earth. Eastertide traces that expansion. It begins with a few stunned witnesses in a garden and ends with the Spirit launching the church into the world. The season has a direction: from the resurrection of one man to the resurrection of everything.

This is why Eastertide cannot be a single Sunday. A single Sunday can hold the fact of the resurrection. It cannot hold the consequences, and the New Testament is almost entirely about the consequences.

It is worth pausing on how completely the resurrection reorganized the lives of the people closest to it, because that reorganization is the historical fingerprint of the thing. These were not men predisposed to belief. They had watched their leader executed in the most degrading way the Roman world could devise, a death that to every observer meant the movement was finished and the claims were false. They scattered. They hid. By every expectation of history, that should have been the end — one more failed messiah in a century full of them, his followers melting back into the obscurity they came from. Instead, within weeks, the same frightened men were standing in public proclaiming that the executed man was alive and was Lord, and they did not stop when it cost them everything, and most of them died for refusing to stop. Something happened in the gap. Whatever it was, it was strong enough to turn cowards into martyrs and to launch a movement that the most powerful empire on earth could not extinguish. The fifty days of Eastertide are the church's way of staying in that gap long enough to feel its force, rather than rushing past it to the next thing on the calendar.

## Living as resurrection people

Here is where the season stops being history and starts making claims on the ordinary Tuesday. Paul tells the Colossians: "If then you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above... Set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on earth. For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:1–4). Notice the tense. *You have been raised.* Not will be — have been. Paul speaks of the believer's resurrection as something that has, in some real sense, already begun. The same power that raised Jesus is already at work, and the Christian is meant to live now as a person on the far side of death, no longer ruled by the fear that organizes most human life.

That is what Eastertide is supposed to form: people who live as though the resurrection were true on a normal weekday, not just on a decorated Sunday. And this is harder than it sounds, because the world we wake up to every morning does not look like a world in which death has been defeated. It looks like a world where death is still winning — where people we love still get sick, still grieve, still die. The fifty days are the church's deliberate practice of insisting, against all the available evidence, that the deepest truth about reality is the empty tomb and not the full graveyard. Schmemann, again, saw the whole Christian life as fundamentally Paschal — shaped by Pascha, the resurrection — so that every Sunday is a little Easter and the believer learns to carry the joy of the empty tomb into the most unremarkable hours.

I have kept Easter as a single Sunday for most of my life, and I can tell you exactly what it produced. A spike of feeling on one morning, real but brief, and then a return to a Monday that looked indistinguishable from the Monday before — as if nothing cosmic had occurred, as if the most disruptive event in the history of the world had no bearing on my actual week. The fifty-day season is the church's correction to exactly that. It refuses to let the resurrection be a feeling you have once a year. It insists that it is a fact you live inside, all the way to Pentecost and beyond.

The difference this makes is hardest to feel and most important at a graveside. Every person who lives long enough stands eventually beside a coffin that holds someone they cannot imagine living without, and in that moment all the comfortable religion in the world is worth nothing, and the only question that matters is whether the resurrection is true. Wright is relentless on this point: the Christian hope is not that the soul of the departed has floated off to a better place while the body is discarded, but that the body itself — the actual person, raised and remade — will rise, because Christ's body rose, the firstfruits of all the rest. That is a far wilder and far more consoling hope than the vague spiritualism that passes for comfort at most funerals. It says the grave is not the end of the person but a waiting room for the person, that death is real and terrible and also, now, defeated, that the one we buried will be raised as surely as the One who walked out of the tomb in the garden. Eastertide is the season that trains us to hold that hope before we need it, so that when the graveside comes — and it always comes — we are not reaching for it for the first time, fumbling for a comfort we never practiced, but standing on a fact we have lived inside for fifty days a year, every year, for as long as we have kept the season.

## The day that refused to end

There is a reason the early church called Sunday the eighth day. The week has seven days; the resurrection happened on the first day of the week, the day after the Sabbath, which they began to call the eighth — a day outside the old count entirely, the first day of a new creation that the old calendar had no room for. Every Sunday since has been, in their understanding, a small Easter, a weekly return to the morning the tomb came up empty. This is the deepest reason Christians gather on Sunday rather than the Sabbath day their tradition had kept for centuries: they had been given a new day, an eighth day, because they were living in a new creation that began on a particular Sunday morning and has not ended. Every week the church returns to that morning. Every week is, in miniature, the fifty days compressed into one — a refusal to let the most important fact in the universe slip out of view between Easters.

And once you see Sunday as a weekly Easter, the whole rhythm of the Christian week changes shape. The day off stops being merely a rest from labor and becomes a celebration of resurrection, a small fifty days inside the seven, a recurring insistence against the gray evidence of every week that death has been beaten and the new creation has begun. The early Christians did not invent a new calendar because they were fond of tradition. They invented it because something had happened that the old way of counting time could not contain, and they needed a day that pointed past the closed circle of the week toward the open future the resurrection had cracked open. We inherited that eighth day. Most of us spend it like any other, never asking why the church set it apart, never feeling the strangeness of a people who reorganized the very week around an empty tomb.

That is the scale we have lost. We treat Easter as a single point on the calendar, a bright dot we touch once a year and leave behind. The church that knew what had happened treated it as a season too large for a day, a celebration too important to end on schedule, a fact that had cracked the old calendar open and started a new kind of time running underneath it.

The lilies die by Wednesday. The tomb is still empty. Easter was never one Sunday because the thing it celebrates was never finished — it was begun, that morning, and it is still unfolding, and it will not be complete until the firstfruits become the full harvest and death itself is the last thing to die.

Fifty days is not too long. Fifty days is barely a start.
