Ask someone what they know about the church year and you will hear about two things. Christmas, with its candles and its carols and its manger. And Easter, with its lilies and its empty tomb. The high seasons. The ones the whole culture half-remembers, the ones with the good music and the full pews. These are the parts of the Christian calendar everyone can name.

What almost no one can name is the part in between — the long, green, unspectacular stretch that fills most of the year, that has no famous music and no special decorations and no cultural footprint at all. The church calls it Ordinary Time, and it runs for something like half the year, the longest season by far. It is the part of the calendar nobody puts on a card. And I have come to believe it is the part that actually does the work.

**Ordinary Time is the church's deliberate decision to give its longest, least dramatic season to the ordinary — to insist that the unremarkable Tuesday, and not the mountaintop Sunday, is where most of a faithful life is actually lived and where most of it is actually formed.** The boring middle is not the church year's filler. It is its main event, disguised as nothing.

## The season with the honest name

First, a correction, because the name misleads almost everyone. Ordinary Time does not mean *boring time* or *ordinary-as-in-nothing-special.* The word comes from *ordinal* — as in the ordinal numbers, first, second, third. Ordinary Time is simply the counted time, the numbered weeks that run between the great festivals: the weeks after Epiphany before Lent begins, and then the long stretch after Pentecost that runs all the way to Advent. It is called ordinary because the weeks are counted in order, one after another, the way ordinary days actually pass.

But the accidental meaning turns out to be the true one. Ordinary Time really is the season of ordinary life, and the church was wise to make it the longest. Because if you are honest about your own life, you will admit that the mountaintop moments are rare. The dramatic conversions, the answered prayers you can point to, the Sundays the worship genuinely lifts you off the ground — these are real, but they are occasional. Most of life is not that. Most of life is the long middle: the same commute, the same dishes, the same prayers that feel like they hit the ceiling, the same faithfulness asked of you on a Wednesday that no one will ever notice. The church calendar tells the truth about this. It gives the most weeks to the kind of time you actually have the most of.

This is itself a quietly radical thing to do in a culture addicted to the spectacular. We are trained to measure our lives by peaks — the highlights, the achievements, the moments worth posting. We treat the ordinary stretches as dead time to be endured until the next significant thing. The church year refuses that. It dignifies the middle. It says the counted, uneventful weeks are not less holy than the festivals; they are simply where most of the following of Jesus gets done.

## Where Jesus spent most of his life

Here is the fact that should reorganize how we think about the unspectacular, and almost no one notices it. The Son of God, given roughly thirty-three years on earth, spent thirty of them doing nothing the Gospels found worth recording.

Think about that. We have four accounts of Jesus' life, and they are almost entirely about the final three years — the ministry, the miracles, the cross, the resurrection. The first thirty years are dispatched in a handful of verses. We get the birth, a single episode at age twelve in the temple, and then a sentence that should stop us cold: "And Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52). That is the entire record of three decades. Thirty years of growing up, learning a trade, working with his hands, living in an unremarkable town that produced a proverb about how nothing good comes out of it. Thirty years of Ordinary Time before there was ever a public ministry.

God, given a human life, chose to spend the overwhelming majority of it in obscurity, doing ordinary work no one would have called significant. The carpenter's shop in Nazareth was not a waiting room before the real life began. It was real life — thirty years of it — and it was where the wisdom and the stature and the favor were formed that the three famous years would draw on. The hidden decades were not wasted. They were the foundation. If the incarnate God gave thirty years to the ordinary and three to the spectacular, perhaps we have our proportions exactly backwards.

## The lie of the dramatic life

We are formed, most of us, by a story that says the meaningful life is the dramatic one — that significance lives in the big moments, the visible impact, the things that can be pointed to and measured and admired. This story is everywhere, and it is a quiet poison, because it teaches us to despise the very texture of the life we have actually been given, which is mostly small and mostly unwitnessed.

Eugene Peterson saw this clearly. His book *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction*, published in 1980, takes its title from a line about discipleship and aims it straight at the American hunger for the instant and the spectacular. We have, Peterson wrote, a religion of the dramatic conversion and the mountaintop experience, and almost no patience for the long, plodding, unremarkable faithfulness that is actually what discipleship consists of. The Christian life, he insisted, is not a series of peak experiences strung together. It is a long obedience in the same direction — the same small fidelities repeated across years until they become a life. There are no shortcuts to the things that matter. There is only the long way, walked faithfully, mostly in the dark, mostly unseen.

This is the spirituality of Ordinary Time, and it cuts against everything the surrounding culture trains into us. Paul, of all the apostles the most dramatic in his own conversion, told the Thessalonians the least dramatic thing imaginable: "Aspire to live quietly, and to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands" (1 Thessalonians 4:11). Aspire to live quietly. That is not advice we know what to do with. We aspire to live loudly, visibly, impactfully. Paul holds up the quiet, hand-working, attention-avoiding life as something to aim at — as itself a form of faithfulness. The ordinary, minded well, is not beneath the spiritual life. It is the spiritual life.

The technology of our moment has made this lesson harder to hear than it has ever been, because the screen in everyone's pocket is a machine for despising the day of small things. It serves a relentless feed of other people's peaks — the highlights, the achievements, the dramatic moments, edited and filtered and stripped of all the ordinary hours that surrounded them — and it sets that curated spectacle against the unremarkable reality of your own Tuesday. The comparison is rigged and constant, and its effect is to make the ordinary life, which is the only life any of us actually has, feel like a failure to launch. We scroll through everyone else's festival days and wonder why our own life feels so much like Ordinary Time, never grasping that everyone's life is mostly Ordinary Time, that the peaks we are comparing ourselves against are the rare exceptions other people also spend most of their hours waiting for. Ordinary Time is the church's quiet refusal of that whole rigged comparison. It says the unedited middle is not the part of life to be ashamed of and hidden. It is the part where you actually live, and where God actually works, and the sooner you stop despising it the sooner you can start being formed by it.

I have spent years measuring my own ministry by the dramatic — the big Sunday, the numbers, the visible result — and treating the ordinary weeks, the hospital visits no one saw, the long unspectacular discipling of one stubborn person, as the part that did not count. I had the proportions backwards. The ordinary weeks were the ministry. The dramatic moments, when they came, were built entirely out of the faithfulness no one had noticed.

## God in the dishes

Tish Harrison Warren, in her widely loved book *Liturgy of the Ordinary*, published in 2016, structures the whole thing around a single ordinary day — waking, brushing teeth, losing her keys, eating leftovers, fighting with her husband, going to bed — and finds in each unremarkable moment a place where God is forming her. Her argument is that we keep waiting for the spiritual life to happen in the extraordinary, and it keeps happening in the dishes and the commute and the small frustrations we treat as interruptions to the real thing. The ordinary is not the interruption. The ordinary is the real thing. The dishes are where the formation happens, precisely because that is where most of life happens.

Scripture says the same in its own way. Paul tells the Colossians, "Whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus" (Colossians 3:17). Whatever you do. Not just the religious acts, not just the dramatic obediences — the whatever, the ordinary, the work of your hands and the words of your mouth on a normal day. The entire ordinary life is gathered up and offered. There is no part of it too small to be holy.

This cuts against a division most of us carry without examining it — the assumption that life is split into the spiritual parts and the secular parts, the moments that count toward God and the long stretches that are just life. We treat prayer and church and the visibly religious as the real spiritual content, and we treat the commute, the email, the laundry, the meeting as neutral filler in between. But the New Testament knows no such division. The whole of a life is offered, or none of it is, and the ordinary hours are not the gaps between the holy moments. They are the holy moments, if they are done as unto God. A parent feeding a child at three in the morning, a worker doing an unseen task with care, a friend listening to the same complaint for the hundredth time — these are not lesser than the dramatic spiritual experiences we keep waiting for. They are very often more, because they cost more and ask for a faithfulness no one is applauding. Ordinary Time is the church's annual insistence on exactly this: that the secular-sacred divide is a fiction, and that God is just as present in the green months of nothing-much as in the festival weeks of everything.

And then there is the line from Zechariah that I have come to love more than almost any other in this connection. The people are rebuilding the temple after exile, and the new foundation looks pathetic compared to the glory of the one that was lost, and the older people who remember the first temple are weeping at how small the new beginning is. And God speaks through the prophet: "For whoever has despised the day of small things shall rejoice" (Zechariah 4:10). The day of small things. God's whole work in that moment looked small, unimpressive, hardly worth the effort — and God names the despising of small things as the actual error. The kingdom, it turns out, comes mostly in small things, in days that do not look like much, in faithfulness that no one would film.

## The season that finally tells the truth

So what does Ordinary Time teach the newcomer who has never thought about the church year at all? It teaches the one thing the rest of the culture will never tell you, because the culture has nothing to sell you in the ordinary.

It teaches that your real life — the one you are actually living, the unremarkable one, the one mostly made of repetition and small duties and unwitnessed fidelity — is not the waiting room for some more significant life that keeps failing to arrive. It is the life. It is where God is forming you, week by counted week, into the shape of Christ, and he is doing it not in spite of the ordinariness but precisely through it, the way he formed his own Son through thirty hidden years in a carpenter's shop.

There is one more thing Ordinary Time quietly insists on, and it is the hardest for a results-driven person to accept. It teaches that faithfulness is not the same as fruitfulness, and that you are responsible only for the first. We want to see the harvest. We measure ourselves by outcomes — the marriage that turned out, the child who kept the faith, the work that finally got noticed — and when the outcomes do not come, we conclude that the faithfulness was wasted. But the long green season says no. You sow in the ordinary weeks without seeing what grows. You teach the child, love the spouse, labor at the task, keep the prayer, and you may never see the result, because the result is God's province and not yours. The farmer in Jesus' parable scatters seed and then sleeps and rises, night and day, and the seed grows he knows not how. That is the posture Ordinary Time forms — the willingness to be faithful in the dark without demanding to see the crop, trusting that the One who counts the small things is also the One who, in his own time and not ours, brings the harvest in.

The festivals are real, and we need them. But you cannot live on festivals. You live in Ordinary Time, in the long green stretch where nothing seems to happen, and the church was telling the truth when it made that stretch the longest. Most of your faithfulness will be invisible. Most of your obedience will go unnoticed. Most of the following of Jesus is the same small turning, repeated, in the same direction, for years.

That is not the consolation prize. That is the thing itself. And the day you stop despising it is the day the boring middle of your life turns out to have been, all along, the place where God was actually at work.
