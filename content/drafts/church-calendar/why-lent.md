A coworker mentions she is giving up wine for Lent. Someone else is off social media until Easter. A friend has sworn off chocolate, again, the way he does every year, and will break by the second week, the way he does every year. From the outside it looks like a religious version of a New Year's resolution — a seasonal diet with a theological label, a quaint exercise in willpower that the modern world has mostly outgrown. Even many Christians treat it that way, picking a vice to suspend for six weeks and then forgetting why.

But the people who keep Lent seriously are not on a diet, and they are not testing their willpower. They are doing something far older and more deliberately uncomfortable, and once you understand what it is, the whole practice stops looking quaint and starts looking almost dangerous.

**Lent is forty days in which Christians deliberately remove the comforts that numb them — not to prove they can, but to expose what those comforts have been covering, and to walk, with Jesus, into the wilderness where a person finds out who he actually is when the easy things are gone.** Giving something up is not the point. It is the method. The point is what the absence reveals.

## What the fasting is actually for

Start with the misunderstanding, because nearly everyone holds it. We assume fasting is about deprivation — that the spiritual value lies in the suffering, as if God is pleased by our small miseries and keeps a ledger of foregone desserts. That is not the logic of Christian fasting, and it never was. Fasting is not about the thing you give up. It is about what becomes visible when you give it up.

Consider what actually happens when you remove a daily comfort. Take away the glass of wine at the end of the day and you discover, sometimes for the first time, exactly how much you had been depending on it to take the edge off — how the day's tension had nowhere to go but that glass. Take away the phone you reach for in every idle moment and you discover, in the sudden silences, just how restless you are, how unable to sit with your own thoughts, how relentlessly you have been managing your own discomfort with a screen. The fast does not create these things. It uncovers them. It pulls back the anesthetic and lets you feel what you have been medicating.

This is why Lent matters for people who think they have no particular problem. Most of us are not aware of our compulsions, our small idolatries, the dozen comforts we use to keep ourselves from ever sitting still long enough to face what is underneath. We are too well-supplied to notice. Lent is the deliberate removal of the supply. And what surfaces in the absence — the irritability, the craving, the strange grief when the comfort is gone — that is the real spiritual data. That is the season showing you, gently and without judgment, where your heart has actually been living.

Alexander Schmemann, in his slim and demanding classic *Great Lent*, published in 1969, argued that Lent is not a sad season but a season of return — a long act of remembering who we are and what we were made for, by stripping away the things that have made us forget. The fasting, he wrote, is meant to bring us back to a hunger deeper than food: the hunger for God that our endless small satisfactions have been quietly suppressing. We eat and drink and scroll and buy not because we are so hungry, Schmemann suggests, but because we are starving for something else and have mistaken the symptoms. Lent removes the substitutes so the real hunger can speak.

## Forty days, and why exactly that many

The number is not arbitrary, and the pattern goes deep into Scripture.

Before Jesus began his public ministry, the Spirit drove him into the wilderness for forty days, where he ate nothing and was tempted by the devil (Matthew 4:1–11). Read that account closely and notice what the wilderness does. It strips Jesus down to nothing — no food, no comfort, no crowd, no role to play — and in that stripped-down state the temptations arrive with full force. Turn the stones to bread. Throw yourself down and let the angels catch you. Bow to me and take the kingdoms without the cross. Every temptation is an offer of comfort, of shortcut, of glory without suffering. And Jesus, hungry and alone, refuses all three, answering each with the word of God rather than the relief his body was screaming for.

That is the template Lent follows. The forty days are not a random length of penance. They are a deliberate echo of those forty days in the wilderness — and behind them, of Israel's forty years in the desert, and Moses' forty days on the mountain, and Elijah's forty days of travel to the mountain of God. The wilderness, in Scripture, is the place where God's people are stripped of everything they were leaning on and learn, painfully, to lean on God instead. Lent is the church choosing to enter that wilderness on purpose, once a year, in the company of Jesus, who went there first.

This is the part that moves me most about the season. We do not go into the wilderness alone, and we do not go to earn anything. We go to walk where Jesus walked, to feel a fraction of what he felt, to let the hunger of forty days connect us to the One who was hungry so that we could be fed. The fasting is not a transaction. It is a kind of companionship.

And the timing of the church's forty days is itself instructive. Lent does not float somewhere convenient. It is anchored to the calendar so that it lands in the weeks leading to the cross, which means the wilderness it puts you in is not a detour away from Holy Week but the road directly into it. You fast through the gray end of winter, and the fasting is meant to mirror the season — the bare trees, the long nights not yet broken by spring, the sense of a world waiting for something it cannot yet see. The early church, in placing Lent here, was reading the natural year and the gospel year against each other, letting the dying-back of the world outside the window preach the same sermon as the dying that Holy Week remembers. None of this is sentimental. It is the church using everything available to it — the season, the body, the empty stomach, the bare branch — to make sure the truth gets all the way in, past the part of us that can affirm the cross intellectually while feeling nothing.

## Repentance without the shame

Now the word that scares people off, and the place where I have to be careful, because the church has often gotten this exactly wrong. Lent is a season of repentance. And many people, when they hear that word, hear shame — the grim, groveling, self-loathing religiosity that has driven so many away from the church and back into the very comforts Lent is trying to expose.

Biblical repentance is not that. The prophet Joel, calling a comfortable and complacent nation back to God, frames it like this: "Return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning; and rend your hearts and not your garments" (Joel 2:12–13). And then immediately, in the same breath, the reason: "for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love." The turning is serious. The God you are turning toward is not standing over you with a ledger. He is gracious and merciful and abounding in steadfast love, and the whole reason repentance is possible at all is that he is the kind of God who runs toward returning children rather than away from them.

Psalm 51, the great penitential prayer, written after a catastrophic moral failure, holds both truths in tension without ever collapsing into despair. It names the sin plainly — no minimizing, no excuse — and in the same prayer asks God to create a clean heart and restore the joy of salvation. The grief over sin and the confidence in mercy live together. That is the emotional register of Lent. Not shame. Honesty, in the presence of a God you trust enough to be honest with.

James puts it as bluntly as anyone: "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts. Be wretched and mourn and weep" (James 4:8–10). That sounds harsh until you read the promise wrapped inside it — *draw near to God, and he will draw near to you.* The mourning is not the destination. It is the door. You grieve what is broken precisely because you believe it can be healed, and you believe the One who heals it is already moving toward you.

I have avoided this honesty for long stretches of my own life, and I avoided it the way most of us do — not by denying my failures but by staying too comfortable and too busy to ever sit with them. Comfort is a wonderful place to hide. You can run from God for years without ever doing anything dramatic, simply by keeping yourself perpetually soothed. Lent is the season that takes away the soothing and lets you finally stop running, not into condemnation, but into the arms of a mercy you had been too distracted to receive.

This is the thing the church understood about repentance that the modern world has almost entirely lost: that it is a gift, not a punishment. We hear the word and brace, as if repentance were the bill we have to pay before grace will be extended. It is the reverse. Repentance is only possible because grace has already been extended — you can only turn back toward a God you already trust is waiting, and you can only name your failures honestly to a love you are already sure of. The penitent in Psalm 51 does not grovel in the hope of earning his way back. He pours out the truth precisely because he knows the character of the One he is pouring it out to. The honesty is the fruit of the confidence, not the price of it. And so Lent's repentance, kept rightly, does not make a person smaller. It makes a person freer, because there is nothing so exhausting as the management of a self you will not look at honestly, and nothing so unburdening as finally setting it down in front of a God who already knew and already loved and already paid.

## What it forms in you

So why would anyone do this? Why choose discomfort in a world engineered to eliminate it? Why give something up when the entire surrounding economy exists to make sure you never have to?

Because the alternative is to be formed entirely by comfort, and comfort makes weak people. This is not a moralism; it is an observation about how souls actually grow. Richard Foster, in *Celebration of Discipline*, the book that reintroduced a generation of Protestants to the practices they had abandoned, argued that fasting reveals the things that control us. We cover up what is inside us with food and other good things, he wrote, but in fasting these things surface. If pride controls us, it will surface. If anger, fear, bitterness — they surface. The fast does not make us holy. It shows us the truth, and only the truth can be healed.

What Lent forms, over the years, is a person who is no longer at the mercy of his own appetites — who has learned, through repeated practice, that he can be hungry and not collapse, can be uncomfortable and still choose God, can sit in silence without reaching for the screen, can want something badly and not need it. That freedom is rare and precious and almost impossible to acquire any other way. You cannot think your way into it. You have to fast your way into it, a little at a time, year after year.

Consider what the absence of that freedom looks like, because most of us are living inside it without noticing. A person who has never learned to be uncomfortable is a person who can be steered by anyone willing to offer him comfort or threaten him with its loss. He cannot tell the truth if the truth is costly, cannot keep a hard promise if keeping it hurts, cannot stand against a crowd if standing alone is painful, because every one of those things requires the capacity to choose something good over something easy, and that capacity is precisely what comfort, uninterrupted, never develops. We have raised entire generations — and I include myself and my own ministry in this — on the unexamined assumption that the goal of life is to reduce discomfort as far as possible, and we are now surprised to find ourselves fragile, easily manipulated, unable to bear much of anything. Lent is the ancient correction to exactly this fragility. It is strength training for the soul, and like all strength training, it works only through resistance, only by asking of you something slightly more than is comfortable, repeated until the muscle grows.

And the culture, for all its talk of wellness, has no equivalent. It will sell you a juice cleanse and a digital detox and a hundred forms of self-improvement, but every one of them is aimed back at the self — at making you healthier, more productive, more optimized, more you. Lent is aimed somewhere else entirely. Its fasting is not in service of a better you. It is in service of a deeper hunger for God, and that difference is the whole difference. A self-improvement fast leaves you more curved in on yourself than before, prouder of your discipline, more invested in your own betterment. A Lenten fast, kept rightly, turns you outward and upward, empties you of yourself just enough that God can fill the space. The disciplines look identical from the outside. They are pointed at opposite gods.

And the season has an end. This is the mercy built into its structure. Lent does not last forever. It walks toward something — toward the cross, and then through the cross to the empty tomb. The wilderness is real, but it is not the destination. After forty days of hunger comes a feast that the hunger was preparing you to taste. You give something up for a season precisely because there is a Sunday coming when everything will be given back, and more.

That is why anyone would do it. Not to suffer. To get free. And to arrive at Easter as someone who has walked the long road there, hungry enough, at last, to know what the bread is for.
