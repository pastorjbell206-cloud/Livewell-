The dishes are never the dishes. The money is never the money. The schedule is never the schedule. The fight is always about the deeper question — whether you are seen, whether you are honored, whether you matter in the way you needed to matter. Until you can name the deeper fight, the surface fights will keep coming back, and the marriage will spend years cycling through the same three or four arguments without ever moving forward.

I want to write this essay for the married couple who has been having the same fight for ten years and cannot understand why. The pattern is recognizable across most marriages I have pastored. The pattern has a name. The pattern can be interrupted. The interruption is most of what makes a marriage grow rather than just persist.

## Why the same fights keep coming back

If you have been married for more than five years, you can probably list the recurring fights in your marriage. They are usually three or four. They cycle. They are presented as content disputes — the dishes, the schedule, the in-laws, the money, the bedroom, the children's activities, the church involvement. The content shifts; the underlying fight does not.

The reason the underlying fight does not shift is that the content is not what you are actually fighting about. You are fighting about an older question. The older question is from before the marriage. The older question is about whether you will be seen, whether you will be honored, whether you matter in the way you needed to matter and did not quite get to matter in your family of origin. The unresolved question from before the marriage is now structuring the fights inside the marriage. The fights cannot resolve, because they are not about what they appear to be about.

This is what Bowen family-systems theory was named for, and what Edwin Friedman developed in *Generation to Generation* and later in *A Failure of Nerve*. The patterns of conflict in your current marriage are, in significant respects, the unresolved patterns from the families of origin you both came from. The unresolved patterns get re-enacted in the current relationship until they are explicitly named. The naming is what allows the patterns to change.

## What the deeper fight is actually about

Let me be concrete about what the deeper fight is, because the abstraction can be hard to apply.

The fight about the dishes is rarely about the dishes. It is about whether the labor of the household is being equitably distributed, which is about whether you are seen and honored as a partner rather than as a domestic resource. The fight about the dishes is the fight about whether your contribution is recognized.

The fight about money is rarely about money. It is about control, about security, about whose anxieties are being honored, about what each of you was taught about money in your family of origin and how those teachings are now colliding inside your shared economic life. The fight about money is the fight about whose voice gets to determine the shape of the household.

The fight about sex is rarely about sex. It is about emotional safety, about whether each of you feels desired and chosen and prioritized, about whether the broader patterns of the marriage have produced the conditions in which sexual intimacy is sustainable. The bedroom is downstream of the dinner table.

The fight about the in-laws is rarely about the in-laws. It is about whose family of origin gets the primary loyalty, about whether the marriage has actually separated from the prior generation in the way Genesis 2 describes, about whether each of you has fully chosen your spouse over the family you came from.

The fight about the schedule is rarely about the schedule. It is about whose preferences and priorities get to structure the shared life, which is about whose voice carries weight, which is about whether you are equal partners or whether one of you is, in practice, deferring to the other in ways that have become invisible to both of you.

The content of the fights cycles because the content is not what is at stake. What is at stake is the deeper relational reality, and the deeper relational reality is what most marriages never explicitly address. The deeper reality is what the content fights are a coded reference to. The decoding is the work.

## How to actually have the conversation

The strongest practical move in any marriage that is cycling through recurring fights is to interrupt the cycle by naming what is actually happening. The naming sounds something like this. *We are fighting about the dishes again. We have been fighting about the dishes for seven years. The dishes are not the actual issue. Let me try to name what the actual issue might be.* And then a serious attempt to name it.

The naming has to be specific. The naming has to be vulnerable. The naming has to come from inside, with the willingness to say something true about what you are actually carrying, rather than from outside, with the willingness to diagnose what your spouse is carrying. The naming has to be repeated, because the cycle has been running for years and one conversation will not interrupt it. The naming has to be done in calm rather than in conflict — the most productive conversations about the deeper fight are usually the ones that happen when neither of you is currently angry.

The naming has to include the family-of-origin work. *In my family, when this happened, X. I think part of what is happening for me when we fight about this is X. I have been bringing X into our marriage without explicitly naming it. I want to name it now.* This kind of self-disclosure is more vulnerable than most marriages have practiced. The vulnerability is the work. The marriages that figure this out, in my pastoral experience, are the marriages that grow. The marriages that do not figure this out are the marriages that, in many cases, end in resigned distance — both spouses still technically present, the household still functioning, the deeper relational reality slowly collapsing.

## What gets in the way

The naming is hard for several reasons that are worth being honest about.

The naming is hard because it requires both spouses to look at their own contribution to the pattern, rather than focusing on the spouse's contribution. The natural instinct in conflict is to identify what your spouse has done wrong. The productive instinct is to identify what you have been bringing that has been making the cycle continue. The second instinct is harder, requires more self-awareness, and is what almost every marriage counselor tries to coach couples toward. The coaching works in some cases and not in others, and the difference is largely the willingness of each spouse to do the looking-at-self work that the second instinct requires.

The naming is hard because it usually exposes wounds that have been unaddressed for decades. The family-of-origin work is not a five-minute conversation. The work, when it goes well, often opens up grief that has been deferred since adolescence. The opening up is part of the healing, but it is also painful, and most couples will avoid the pain unless they have a reason to engage it. The marriage being in crisis is sometimes the reason. The marriage being in slow decline can also be the reason, although the decline is harder to see while it is happening.

The naming is hard because it requires both spouses to have some basic emotional and psychological vocabulary that not every couple has been given. The work of naming what is actually happening in a recurring marital conflict requires categories — projection, attachment, family-of-origin patterns, the difference between what someone is saying and what they are actually communicating. Some couples have these categories from prior therapy, from reading, from one of their parents' marriages having been worked on visibly. Some couples do not. The couples without the categories often have to develop them in real time, and the developing is part of the work the marriage requires.

## What this looks like in my own marriage

I want to be honest. Susanna and I have cycled through versions of the same several fights for the entire twenty-two years of our marriage. We have made progress. We have not solved them. We have, over the past seven years particularly, gotten better at naming what is actually happening when we are fighting about the surface content. The naming has made the fights shorter, has made the recovery faster, and has made the underlying patterns less destructive than they were earlier.

The naming has also produced some of the hardest conversations of our marriage. The conversations about what each of us learned from our families of origin, what we have been bringing into the marriage without naming it, what each of us has been quietly resentful about for years — these conversations are not pleasant. The conversations are productive. The conversations are part of what has kept the marriage from drifting into resigned distance, which is the more common outcome among the couples I have pastored.

I am not writing this as someone who has figured it out. I am writing as someone who has been doing the work for two decades and has, in the past seven years, started to see what the work is actually about. The seeing is incomplete. The work continues.

## The verdict

The marriage that wins the argument loses the marriage. The marriage that names the pattern under the argument has begun the actual work. The work is not about being right. The work is about being seen — and seeing — at the level where the wound lives. Most of us flinch from that level. The marriage that does not flinch is the marriage that lasts.

Name the pattern. Name what you have been bringing. Name what your spouse has been bringing. Have the conversations in calm. Get a counselor if you cannot have them alone. Read Friedman or Bowen or Gottman. Do the work the marriage is asking you to do. The work is not optional. The work is what marriage is for.

The fights will not all go away. The marriages that work do not have no fights; they have fights that produce growth instead of cycles. The difference is the naming. The naming is yours to do. The naming is what the next twenty years of your marriage depend on. Begin.
