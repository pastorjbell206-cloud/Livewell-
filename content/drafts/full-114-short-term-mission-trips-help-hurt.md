The group flew fourteen hours, built a wall for three days, tore it down because it was not structurally sound, and then flew home with photographs and a renewed sense of purpose. The local construction workers who could have built the wall correctly in one day were not hired. The money spent on flights and lodging would have funded six months of local wages. The photographs were beautiful.

That story is not a caricature. It is a real pattern that has been documented extensively in the literature on short-term missions [cite — confirm], and the fact that it is still happening at scale inside the American church says something significant about the purposes the short-term trip actually serves — and for whom.

The honest question, the one that most pastors and youth pastors do not want to ask before they start the fundraising campaign, is: Who does this trip primarily benefit? The answer, more often than the church admits, is the participants.

That is not necessarily a damning answer. Formation of the trip participants is a legitimate purpose. The young person who returns from two weeks in another country having encountered poverty and faith at a depth that is not available in suburban America has been changed. That change has value. It may produce decades of giving and advocacy and genuine cross-cultural relationship. It may produce a missionary. The formation of the American church for global awareness and generosity is a real need, and short-term trips are genuinely one tool for meeting it.

The question is whether that formation can be accomplished without extracting a cost from the communities the trips are ostensibly serving.

The answer, developed by practitioners and researchers in international development over the past thirty years, is that it depends entirely on how the trip is designed and what relationship it exists within [cite — confirm]. The trip that serves the mission is embedded in a long-term partnership with a national church or ministry that identifies the need, directs the work, and retains authority over the outcome. The trip that serves the trip is designed from the sending church's end, around the availability and capacities of the participants, and arrives in the host community with a predetermined agenda that may or may not correspond to what is actually needed there.

The former requires relationship before the trip. It requires that the pastor or church planting leader in the host community have a genuine voice in the design — what tasks, what timeline, what preparation, what follow-up. It requires that the trip participants arrive with the posture of learners rather than rescuers. And it requires that the sending church be honest about the limitations of what a group of untrained volunteers can usefully accomplish in seven to ten days.

Those requirements are demanding. They are also the conditions under which short-term missions have historically produced durable good rather than durable dependency.

The trip that produces dependency — where the community learns to orient its development calendar around the annual arrival of American volunteers rather than around building its own capacities — is doing harm regardless of the good intentions involved. Sustained dependency is not a gift. It is a wound delivered with warmth and enthusiasm.

The pastoral question is not whether to do short-term trips. It is whether to design them in a way that serves the people in the host community rather than primarily serving the participants' need for a significant experience. That design question requires asking the host community — not the trip organizers — what they actually need, and being willing to hear that what they need is money rather than presence, local labor rather than foreign volunteers, sustained partnership rather than annual visits.

I have sent groups on trips I should not have sent them on. The budget, the enthusiasm, the formational opportunity for the participants — all of those were real, and none of them guaranteed that the people we visited were actually served.

That is a question worth sitting with before the next fundraising campaign begins.
