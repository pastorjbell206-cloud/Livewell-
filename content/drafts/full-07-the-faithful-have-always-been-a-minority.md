I want to make a case most of my pastoral colleagues have not yet been willing to make from their own pulpits. The case is simple. The faithful church has, for most of the church's history, been a minority. Christendom was the exception. Christendom was the historical anomaly, not the historical norm. The post-Christendom moment we are entering is not the end of the church. It is the church returning to the demographic and cultural condition it has been in for most of two thousand years.

We have been mourning the wrong thing. We have been mourning the loss of a position the church mostly did not have, and was probably better off not having. The mourning is sincere. The mourning is also misplaced. The mourning, by its misplacement, has prevented us from doing the actual work of recognizing what is happening and what to do about it.

## What scripture knew

The Bible is not a majoritarian document. It assumes minority status from the beginning to the end.

Abraham is called out of Ur. He is one family in a world of nations, and he is told he will become a great nation, but the becoming will take centuries, and he will spend his life as a stranger in a land that is not his. The patriarchs are minorities. The Israelites in Egypt are a slave minority. The wilderness generation are a wandering minority. The conquest under Joshua puts them in the land, but they are surrounded by Canaanites, and the conquest is partial and remains partial. The judges' era is the era of a partial people in a hostile land.

The monarchy under David and Solomon is the closest the Old Testament comes to majoritarian status, and even there, the prophets are constantly warning that the apparent strength is brittle, that the surrounding nations are stronger, and that the people's idolatry is hollowing them out from inside. The split kingdom diminishes both halves further. The exile reduces them to a minority again — first the northern kingdom under Assyria in 722 BC, then Judah under Babylon in 586. The return under Cyrus is a return of a minority, to a province inside the Persian Empire, and the post-exilic literature — Ezra, Nehemiah, the late prophets — assumes the minority condition throughout.

The intertestamental period is a minority period. Hasmonean independence under the Maccabees is a brief and partial exception, quickly overtaken by Roman conquest. The first-century Jewish situation is the situation of a minority inside an empire. Jesus' ministry is the ministry of a Jewish rabbi inside a colonial occupation. The disciples are a minority within a minority. The early church is a minority within a minority within an empire, persecuted in waves, scattered through the cities and the trade routes.

The remnant motif runs through the whole. Isaiah 10:20–22: *a remnant shall return.* Romans 11: *I have kept for myself seven thousand who have not bowed the knee to Baal.* The faithful are not the majority of those who bear the name. The faithful are the minority within the named community who have actually held the faith. The named community can be Israel under Ahab; the named community can be the church of the Constantinian era; the named community can be evangelical America in 1955. The faithful are the smaller community within that, the ones who have actually been formed by the gospel rather than absorbed by the surrounding culture.

This is the scriptural assumption. It is not a hopeful aside. It is the standing pattern. Most of the people who have called themselves God's people, throughout most of biblical history, have not been the faithful. The faithful have been the smaller community holding the gospel against the surrounding noise — including, often, the noise generated by the larger named community to which they nominally belonged.

## What history confirms

Move out of the canon and into the church's two thousand years, and the pattern repeats.

The early church, as I argued in the prior essay, was a minority for three centuries. They had no political power. They were periodically persecuted. They were the demographic small minority of the Roman world until Constantine, and even after Constantine, the actual Christianity-as-discipleship within the politically-Christianized population was probably a minority of the population that bore the name.

The medieval church is the period most American evangelicals point to as the era of Christian majoritarianism, but the medieval church's actual disciples were a minority within a much larger nominal Christianity. The monasteries existed because the monks understood themselves to be living the faith in a way most of their baptized neighbors were not. The medieval reformers — Bernard of Clairvaux, Francis of Assisi, the *Devotio Moderna* — were calling the named church back to a discipleship the named church mostly was not practicing. The reform movements were minority movements, and they understood themselves to be such.

The Reformation was a minority movement until it became a political event. Even after Luther's protections under the German princes, the actual Reformed and Lutheran churches inside their territories had to do enormous catechetical work to make the nominal Protestantism of the population into actual Protestantism — and they only ever partially succeeded. The Heidelberg Catechism (1563) and the Westminster Standards (1646) and the various smaller catechisms exist because the Reformers understood that the nominal church and the actual church were different, and the actual church had to be formed slowly out of the nominal one, one household at a time.

The Wesleyan revival in the eighteenth century, the First and Second Great Awakenings in America, the Welsh revival in 1904, the East African Revival in the 1930s — these are revival movements precisely because the named church had become formal, and the actual gospel had to be recovered against the formality. The revivalists were minorities calling the majoritarian named church back to discipleship. The named church mostly did not come. The revivalist minority became the seed of the next generation of the actual church.

The global church today, taken honestly, exhibits the same pattern. The named Christian populations in much of Latin America, in the European Christian heritage countries, in the historically Christian regions of the Middle East — these are populations whose nominal Christianity considerably exceeds their actual discipleship. The actual discipleship is found in subsets — the base communities in Latin America, the Pentecostal congregations in Brazil, the underground Catholics and Protestants of the Middle East, the Chinese house churches under both communist persecution and growing post-communist freedom. The actual church is a minority within the larger named population, and the actual church is, in many places, growing.

This is the standing pattern. Christendom was a brief exception, and even during Christendom, the actual disciples were a minority within the named population. The minority condition is not the disaster. The minority condition is the church being what the church mostly is.

## What we forgot

American evangelicalism forgot this for two reasons, both worth naming.

The first is that mid-twentieth-century America was an unusual period. The combination of postwar prosperity, the Cold War alignment of Christianity with anti-communism, the demographic dominance of white Protestant America, and the absorption of Catholic and Jewish minorities into a broadly Judeo-Christian civic religion produced a period in which Christianity appeared majoritarian in a way it had rarely been in any earlier American period. The 1955 evangelical church had a degree of cultural confidence and demographic majority that was historically anomalous, and we mistook the anomaly for the norm.

The second is that we lost contact with church history. American evangelical seminaries, over the twentieth century, increasingly trained pastors in systematic theology, biblical studies, and pastoral practice while shortening the history requirements. Most pastors I know graduated from seminary with a working knowledge of Calvin and Luther, a passing familiarity with Augustine, and a near-total ignorance of the patristic period, the medieval church, the Eastern tradition, the African and Asian Christianities, and the long history of minority Christianity through the centuries. We were trained to think of the church's history as a recent American phenomenon, and we did not have the historical perspective to see that the recent American phenomenon was the exception.

These two factors combined to produce a generation of pastors who genuinely believed that majoritarian cultural Christianity was the normal state of the church, and that its loss was a disaster of historic proportions. The genuine belief was sincere and was wrong. The loss is real and is not unprecedented. We have been here before, many times. We have generally come out the other side, smaller and more faithful.

## What it looks like to be a minority well

Minority status is not a defeat. Minority status is a condition. It can be inhabited badly or well, faithfully or unfaithfully, with bitterness or with practice. The question now is not whether we can avoid being a minority. We are going to be a minority. The question is whether we can be a minority well.

The minority church I want to be part of has a few characteristics worth naming.

It catechizes seriously. It cannot rely on the surrounding culture to teach the basics. It teaches them itself, slowly, intentionally, through liturgy and household and small group and sermon. The Heidelberg Catechism and the Westminster Standards were written for minority conditions, by people who understood that the actual church had to be formed out of the named population through patient teaching, and the catechetical work could not be skipped. We are in a similar position now.

It worships with weight. It does not chase the surrounding culture for relevance. It does not try to imitate the entertainment industry. It worships in a way that forms people who can stand against the noise — which means it draws deeply from the historic forms, the Psalms, the liturgical year, the eucharist celebrated with seriousness rather than as an afterthought. The thin worship of the late-Christendom megachurch will not form a minority church. The deeper worship of the historic church will.

It builds community across distance. The minority church cannot rely on geographic concentration; its members will be scattered through cities and suburbs that are mostly not Christian. The community has to be built intentionally, in households that welcome the unmarried and the elderly and the wanderer, in small groups that do real spiritual formation, in pastoral relationships that go deeper than the Sunday service can carry.

It is hospitable to the world without being captive to it. It loves its neighbors who do not share its faith. It refuses to retreat into a fortified Christian subculture. It does not, at the same time, soften its theology or its practices to make itself more palatable. It is what the early church was — a community of strange people who clearly cared about their neighbors and clearly believed something very different from their neighbors, and the strangeness and the care together were the witness.

It dies well. The minority church understands that some of its members will die for the faith, in various senses — some literally, most metaphorically, through the loss of careers and friendships and family standing. The minority church teaches its members to die well, because dying well in small ways is the daily practice, and dying well in large ways is the occasional necessity, and the willingness to die for the faith is the indispensable mark of the church Paul described and Polycarp embodied.

## The verdict

The faithful have always been a minority. The faithful are not less faithful for being a minority. The faithful are not failing because the cultural arrangement that gave them numerical majority for a few decades is dissolving. The faithful are returning to the standing condition of the church, the condition the Bible assumes throughout, the condition most of church history has occupied, the condition the global church is occupying right now.

I want to write this slowly enough for the grief to be honored and the recognition to land. The grief is real. The grief is also misplaced. We are grieving the loss of an anomaly. The actual church is fine. The actual church is smaller than the named church was, and is being shaken out of the named church, and will be smaller still in another generation. That is not the disaster. That is, in some ways, the mercy.

The remnant is the church when the church is not pretending. The remnant has always been smaller than the named church. The remnant has always been faithful. The remnant has always survived the collapse of the surrounding cultural arrangements, including the cultural arrangements that gave the named church its temporary majoritarian status. The remnant survives because the remnant is what God preserves. The named church survives only when and to the extent that it remembers that it is supposed to be the remnant.

We are about to find out who, in our time, is the remnant and who was the named church. The finding out will be painful. The finding out will also be merciful. The next essay in this series takes up a related question — the question of persecution, which is one of the lenses through which American evangelicalism has been reading the dissolution. The reading is mostly wrong. The next essay will say so plainly. The faithful have always been a minority. The actually-persecuted have always been a much smaller group still, and we have been calling our cultural defeat by a name that belongs to people who are paying a price we have not paid. That, too, needs to be addressed before this series can move on to the constructive.
