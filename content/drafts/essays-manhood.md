# Essays — Manhood

Five essays. The track: what it means to be a man in a culture that has
lost the language for either virtue or its opposite. Scholarly, not
nostalgic. Written with women, not over them. Honest about the failures of
both the muscular-Christianity tradition and the progressive
masculinity-is-a-problem tradition.

---

## 46. What Manhood Was, Is, and Is Going to Have to Become

**Slug:** `what-manhood-was-is-and-is-going-to-have-to-become`
**Track:** `manhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~12 min

**Positioning sentence:** The American conversation about manhood has been captured by two failed responses to the same crisis — the muscular-Christianity throwback and the progressive abandonment of the category. Both have failed. A theological account of manhood that is neither will have to be recovered.

**Argument beats:**
1. The crisis is real. Boys are falling behind girls in education, employment, mental health, and church participation. Richard Reeves' work (*Of Boys and Men*) is essential reading.
2. The two failed responses. (a) The throwback: muscular Christianity, the warrior model, the Mark Driscoll-era assertion that manhood is aggression and authority. (b) The abandonment: progressive Christianity's near-refusal to teach distinctive masculine virtue, on the grounds that all such teaching is patriarchal.
3. Both fail because both misread the crisis. The throwback prescribes the disease as cure. The abandonment leaves boys without formation and lets the manosphere fill the vacuum.
4. The recovery: a theological account of manhood that is older than both — drawing on the church's deeper tradition. Augustine on virtue, Aquinas on the four cardinal virtues, the medieval account of *fortitudo* and *temperantia*, the New Testament's letters to the men of the early church.
5. Specific virtues to teach boys: protectiveness without aggression; strength under discipline; responsibility without entitlement; the ability to be a non-anxious presence under pressure; the willingness to suffer for others without making the suffering visible.
6. The hard part. Distinctive masculine virtue does not mean *superior* masculine virtue. It means a particular shape of holiness that men have a particular access to. Women have their own shape. Both are honored. Neither is ranked.
7. The verdict: manhood needs a recovered theology. The vacuum is being filled by Andrew Tate. The church that does not offer a better account will lose the next generation of men to him.

**Opening sketch:** "I have spent five years watching the boys in my church drift toward the manosphere. They were not drifting because they wanted to be misogynists. They were drifting because the church had not offered them a vision of manhood that could compete. The manosphere offered them strength, agency, and identity. The church offered them moral correction. They chose the strength. We need to recover a theology of strength that is also theology."

**Closing weight:** The boys leaving our churches are not leaving because they want to be cruel. They are leaving because we have not offered them a theology of manhood that is as muscular as their hunger and as kind as the gospel. The recovery is not nostalgia. The recovery is older than the muscular-Christianity tradition. The recovery is the gospel's account of strength under discipline, of protection without aggression, of the man who can be present without dominating and absent when his absence is the strongest thing he can offer. That man is rare. We have to start raising more of them.

**Sources:** Richard Reeves (*Of Boys and Men*); Hannah Anderson (*All That's Good*); Beth Allison Barr (*The Making of Biblical Womanhood* — read it for the diagnosis even if you disagree with the prescription); Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae*, II-II on the cardinal virtues); Augustine.

## 47. The Father Wound and the Father God

**Slug:** `the-father-wound-and-the-father-god`
**Track:** `manhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most American men have a wounded relationship with their fathers. That wound is being projected onto God the Father by an entire generation. The pastoral work of helping men recover the doctrine of the Father is the most urgent psychological-theological work the church can do for men.

**Argument beats:**
1. The data. The fatherless household crisis. The under-fathered household crisis (present father, emotionally absent). Together they shape a generation's intuition of fatherhood.
2. The theological consequence: when scripture says God is Father, most American men hear "absent" or "harsh" or "disappointing." The doctrine cannot land because the analogy has been broken.
3. The Augustinian move. *Confessions* opens with Augustine addressing the God who is *more truly Father than my father was*. The doctrine corrects the analogy. The doctrine does not require the analogy to first be sound. The doctrine repairs what the analogy broke.
4. The pastoral work: helping men separate the projection from the doctrine. The God who is Father is not your father. The God who is Father is the Father your father failed to be. The healing comes from the doctrine, not from any human relationship.
5. The complementary work: as men become fathers themselves, the doctrine of the Father re-trains the intuition. My own essay on five sons (#36) is one entry into this; the essay applies broadly.
6. The verdict: most American men need pastoral help with the doctrine of the Father. Most pastors do not know they need to offer it. Most discipleship in men's ministries skips the doctrine entirely and substitutes activities. Recover the doctrine.

**Closing weight:** The Father who could not love you and the Father who can are two different beings. The first is the human father, with his failures and his finitude. The second is the eternal Father, who is what the human father was always supposed to point to and never could. Get to know the second. The first will be redeemed in the knowing. There is no other path.

**Sources:** Augustine (*Confessions*); J.I. Packer (*Knowing God*, the chapter on fatherhood); Larry Crabb (*The Silence of Adam*); Dan Allender (*The Healing Path*); the work of Curt Thompson on shame and the Father.

## 48. Strength Under Discipline

**Slug:** `strength-under-discipline`
**Track:** `manhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Strength is not the problem. Unregulated strength is the problem. The Christian tradition has always taught strength as a virtue — and always taught that it is dangerous without the disciplines that govern it. Recovering the disciplines is the work.

**Argument beats:**
1. The cultural moment. "Toxic masculinity" as a frame: useful for naming the pathology but inadequate as theology. The pathology is unregulated strength, not strength.
2. The Christian tradition. Aquinas on *fortitudo* (fortitude): the virtue that sits between cowardice and recklessness. The virtue cannot exist without the others — without prudence, without temperance, without justice.
3. The specific disciplines that govern strength: physical training that does not become vanity; courage that does not become aggression; speech that is restrained when restraint would serve; sexual desire that is governed by covenant; financial ambition that does not become greed; the protective instinct that does not become domination.
4. The training of these disciplines is slow. It used to be the work of the household, the rite of passage, the apprenticeship, the church. Most of those structures have collapsed. The disciplines now have to be self-imposed and community-supported.
5. The case study: a man whose strength is undisciplined breaks his marriage, his children, and his church. A man whose strength is disciplined builds them. Both men have the same raw strength. The difference is in the disciplines.
6. Self-implication: I have been the undisciplined version more often than I want to admit. The disciplines I have are mostly disciplines I learned late and from teachers I did not choose.
7. The verdict: do not be weak. Be strong. And let your strength be governed.

**Closing weight:** The man whose strength is not governed will break things he loved. The man whose strength is governed will build them. Both are the same man, separated only by the disciplines that took years to acquire and could be lost in a season of carelessness. The disciplines are the work. The strength was never the problem. The work is to make the strength worth having.

**Sources:** Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, qq. 123–140 on fortitude); Augustine; Eugene Peterson (*The Contemplative Pastor*); the recent work of John Mark Comer on apprenticeship; Hannah Anderson on the virtues.

## 49. The Man Who Carries Weight

**Slug:** `the-man-who-carries-weight`
**Track:** `manhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The mature man carries weight no one else has to know he is carrying. The carrying is not the absence of emotion. The carrying is the choice to bear what the household, the work, the friendships require of him without making the bearing visible.

**Argument beats:**
1. The premise. There is weight in a man's life — economic, relational, emotional, vocational — and the question is whether he carries it or makes others carry it.
2. The two failures. (a) The man who carries nothing and makes the household, the wife, the children carry his weight for him. (b) The man who carries weight visibly, performatively, making sure everyone knows what he is carrying. Both are immature in different ways.
3. The mature carrying. Invisible most of the time. Visible only when its visibility serves the people he is carrying for. The child who watches him carry without knowing it is being carried for.
4. The cost. Carrying weight invisibly is the work of a lifetime. It requires the disciplines named in essay #48. It requires the family-of-origin work of essay #47. It requires the formation of essay #46.
5. The constructive: how to begin. Start with one piece of weight you have been making visible — financial anxiety performed at the dinner table, work stress performed at home, marriage strain performed at the kids' bedtime. Begin to carry it differently. Take it to a therapist, a pastor, a friend. Carry it where the children do not have to see it.
6. The verdict: be the man who carries weight. The household will be lighter for the carrying. The children will become adults who can carry weight in their turn, because they watched it carried for them.

**Closing weight:** The mature man carries what the household cannot carry yet. He carries it quietly. He carries it in his body, in his prayers, in his confessions, in his choices about where to put the weight when he cannot carry it alone. The children watch. The wife notices. The work is invisible most of the time and is the gift the household received without knowing it. Be that man. Be him without performing it. The performance ruins the gift.

**Sources:** Henri Nouwen (*In the Name of Jesus*); Eugene Peterson; Wendell Berry (*Jayber Crow*); Marilynne Robinson (*Gilead*); the work of Curt Thompson on emotional regulation.

## 50. The Friendships Men Are Supposed to Have

**Slug:** `the-friendships-men-are-supposed-to-have`
**Track:** `manhood` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most American men over forty have no real friends. The data is consistent and devastating. The recovery is the most important relational work most men can do — and the church is one of the few institutions positioned to support it, if it will.

**Argument beats:**
1. The data. American men's friendship circles have collapsed since the 1990s. Men over forty who say they have no close friends: 15% in 1990; over 30% now. Robert Putnam, Robert Bellah, the Survey Center on American Life — the numbers are clear.
2. The structural reasons. Mobility, work hours, the privatization of leisure, the screens, the death of the third place. Most men do not see other men outside of work and family.
3. The theological problem. The Bible names friendship as a category of formation. David and Jonathan. The disciples. The early church's *koinonia*. The pattern assumed that men would have other men in their lives at a depth most contemporary men cannot fathom.
4. The cost. Men without friends are men whose entire emotional ecosystem depends on their spouse. The dependence is unfair to the spouse and unhealthy for the man. It also produces, predictably, the marriage problems and pastoral counseling cases I have seen most often.
5. The constructive: how friendships actually form. They form by *time and repetition*, not by intentionality. They form by being present at the same gym, the same hobby, the same church study group for years. The friendship at year three is not what it will be at year fifteen. Most men quit before year three.
6. The PCN piece. Pastors are the loneliest profession. The PCN exists in part to be the friendship structure pastors do not have access to elsewhere. The principle generalizes.
7. The verdict: find the men. Spend the time. Stay long enough for it to become real. Most of you will fail at this. Try anyway.

**Closing weight:** You were not made to do this alone. The cultural moment in which men are supposed to be lonely, self-reliant, and stoic is a cultural lie. The biblical pattern, the historical pattern, the human pattern is that men have other men in their lives at a depth that takes years to build. Build it. Build it now. Start by being present somewhere with men around, repeatedly, for years. The friendship at year fifteen is the friendship that saves men's lives. It cannot exist without the boring middle.

**Sources:** Robert Putnam (*Bowling Alone*); the Survey Center on American Life's reports on friendship; Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics*, Books VIII and IX on friendship); C.S. Lewis (*The Four Loves*, chapter on friendship); Henri Nouwen (*Out of Solitude*).
