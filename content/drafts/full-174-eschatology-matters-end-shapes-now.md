The word eschatology comes from the Greek eschaton — the last thing, the final moment, the end toward which everything is moving. It is the branch of theology that deals with what happens at the end of history: the return of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, the judgment, the new creation. In most American evangelical churches, it produces either charts or embarrassment. The charts belong to the prophecy conference circuit — elaborate dispensational timelines with color-coded dispensations and rapture sequences and tribulation chronologies. The embarrassment belongs to everyone else who has watched the charts be wrong again and again while the chart-makers adjust their timelines and proceed as though nothing has happened.

Both responses miss the point. The eschatology that actually matters is not a predictive timeline. It is a fundamental orientation toward reality — a set of convictions about the ultimate destiny of history that determines how the community of faith lives in the present.

## The eschatological community

The New Testament church was an eschatological community. This is not an observation about their theology. It is an observation about the lived structure of their common life. They believed that Jesus had been raised from the dead — not symbolically, not spiritually, not metaphorically, but bodily. They believed that his resurrection was the firstfruits of a general resurrection that was coming (1 Corinthians 15:20-23). They believed that history was moving toward a definitive moment in which God would complete what the resurrection had begun — the renewal of all things, the defeat of death, the restoration of the creation that had been subjected to futility.

That belief shaped what they did with their lives. If death is defeated, then no threat of death can ultimately control the community's behavior. If the resurrection is coming, then investment in the poor and the sick and the imprisoned is not lost labor — it is seed sown into a harvest that God will bring in. If God is working toward the renewal of all things, then the material world — bodies, meals, the care of the earth, the structures of human community — has ultimate weight rather than being a temporary stage that will soon be discarded.

This is why Paul, in 1 Corinthians 15, after his long argument for the resurrection, ends with a completely unexpected practical instruction: "Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain" (v. 58). The resurrection is not a comfort for the afterlife. It is the ground for present-tense action. The work you do now matters because it is being gathered into an ultimate story in which it will be honored.

## The gnostic alternative

The alternative to eschatological hope — the practical eschatology that operates below the surface in a lot of American Christianity — is something closer to ancient Gnosticism than to biblical Christianity. The Gnostics believed that the material world was not the good creation of the good God but the flawed product of a lesser deity, and that salvation consisted in the escape of the spiritual soul from its material prison. The material world was ultimately worthless. What mattered was the spiritual reality above it.

American Christianity has not embraced Gnosticism explicitly. It has absorbed a functional version of it. The body does not ultimately matter — what matters is the soul's eternal destiny. The earth does not ultimately matter — it will be burned up anyway. History does not ultimately matter — it is all moving toward the rapture, after which God starts fresh. The political and social structures of the present age do not ultimately matter — they are temporary arrangements of a fallen world that will soon be replaced.

This is not the eschatology of the New Testament. Revelation 21-22 does not describe the creation being destroyed and replaced by something entirely different. It describes the New Jerusalem coming down from heaven to earth — the heavenly reality descending into the material world, renewing and completing it. "Behold, I am making all things new," not "Behold, I am replacing all things with different things." The distinction is enormous. If creation is renewed rather than replaced, then the work done in creation — the justice sought, the relationships repaired, the earth tended — is not discarded. It is taken up and completed.

## What two eschatologies produce

A community that believes history will be burned up and replaced will have little motivation to work on its political and social structures. Why rearrange the deck chairs? The present order is not worth investing in because it has no ultimate future.

A community that believes God is working toward the renewal of all things will invest in the structures of the present order — not because the present order is not broken, but because the broken present is the raw material of the renewed future. The work of justice done now is seed sown into the ground of what God is bringing. It is not wasted. It will be gathered.

These are not identical communities. They will make different choices about where to spend their energy and their resources. They will answer differently the question of whether to build affordable housing or simply preach about the poor. They will answer differently the question of whether to advocate for just criminal sentencing or simply minister to people in prison. They will answer differently the question of whether the church has any stake in the political structures of the society it lives inside.

Eschatology is not a theoretical question about the end of history. It is a practical question about what we owe the present. The answer begins with what we believe about the end.

I have believed the wrong things about the end, and acted accordingly, for parts of my ministry. The consequences of getting this wrong are not merely theological. They are pastoral. They shape who we help, what we say, and what we are willing to leave behind.
