You know things about your congregation that no one else knows. You know whose marriage is barely hanging on while they hold hands in the third row. You know who is in recovery and who has relapsed. You know which respected family is coming apart behind the door, which volunteer is drowning in debt, which pillar of the church confessed something on a Tuesday that you will carry to your grave. You are the keeper of a city's worth of secrets, and you can tell no one. That is the job. It is also a wound almost no one names.

The weight of confidential knowledge is one of the least-discussed burdens of ministry, and one of the heaviest. It is not the weight of any single secret. It is the accumulation — years of them, layered, each one trustworthy and unspeakable, until the pastor is walking around as the local archive of everyone's worst hour. He smiles at the potluck knowing what three of the families there are actually enduring. He cannot unburden it to his friends, because they are also his congregants. Often he cannot even fully unburden it to his spouse, because it is not his to tell. So it stays in, and it compounds.

This is real, and it is not a complaint. The confidence is sacred; the ability to hold it is part of what makes a pastor safe to confess to. The privilege of being trusted with people's true lives is one of the great gifts of the calling. But a gift can also be a load, and pretending the load is weightless is how pastors end up carrying it alone until it bends them.

I have felt it. The dissonance of standing to preach hope to a room while holding the private knowledge of how much quiet despair is sitting in the chairs. The loneliness of being the one person who knows, and the one person who cannot say. The strange grief of a man who is intimately acquainted with his community's suffering and structurally cut off from processing it with anyone. It does something to you over time. It can make you cynical — the secret knowledge curdling into a low view of people you are also called to love. It can make you numb, the only defense a tired soul can manage against absorbing more than it can hold.

So a word about stewarding it, because the secrets are not going away and the cost is real.

First: you need at least one outlet that does not violate the trust. A confessor of your own — a spiritual director, a counselor bound by their own confidentiality, a peer in another city with no overlap to your flock — where you can lay down the weight of *carrying* without betraying the *content*. You do not have to name names to say *I am holding more sorrow than I can metabolize alone.* That sentence, spoken to the right person, is not gossip. It is survival, and it is permitted.

Second: take the secrets to God deliberately, as their actual keeper. The pastor is not finally the one who holds the congregation's sins and sorrows; he is the one who carries them to the one who can. There is a reason the priestly image in Scripture is a man bearing the names of the people into the presence of God (Exodus 28:29). You were never meant to be the terminus of all that weight. You were meant to be a bearer who hands it on. A great deal of pastoral exhaustion is the result of forgetting that — of becoming the container rather than the conduit, holding what was always meant to be passed up.

Third: let the weight make you tender rather than hard. The knowledge of how much people are suffering behind their composure is a terrible gift, but it can be a gift — it can keep you from ever again preaching to your congregation as though their lives were simple. You know they are not. Let that knowledge soften the sermon, not sour the soul.

You carry what others cannot see. That is the calling, and it is heavier than the people in the chairs will ever know. Do not carry it as though you were its final resting place. Hand it up. It was always his to hold.
