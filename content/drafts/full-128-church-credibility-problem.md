The church has not been silenced. It has plenty of platforms, enormous media presence, political influence that would have astonished the early martyrs. What it has lost is not access to the public square. What it has lost is the credibility to speak in it.

That is a harder problem than suppression. Suppression can be weathered. A community that has been silenced by force can keep the faith in private until the force relents, as the church under Roman persecution and the church under Soviet communism both demonstrate. But a community that has lost credibility among the people it claims to serve has lost something more difficult to recover — the right to be heard, which is earned not by power but by costly faithfulness.

The loss has been accumulating for decades. The abuse scandals — Catholic, then evangelical, then Baptist — revealed institutional patterns of protecting the institution at the expense of victims who came to the church for help. The political alignment of large sectors of American Christianity with specific partisan agendas produced a church whose witness was indistinguishable from its political preferences. The prosperity theology that promised blessing in exchange for obedience left congregations with nothing to say to suffering that didn't fit the formula. And then there were the ordinary, undramatic failures: the church that claimed to love its neighborhood but never appeared in it except to recruit; the pastor whose private life contradicted every public word; the congregation that had strong opinions about other people's moral failures and very little self-examination about its own.

These are not isolated incidents that hostile observers have aggregated to build a case. They are a pattern. And the people who walked away because of them are not unreasonable for having done so.

The steel-man version of the church's position: institutions are always going to have failures at the margins; the overall witness of Christians in history includes hospitals, universities, the abolition of slavery, the civil rights movement, the feeding of the hungry, the care of the dying. That witness is real and it matters. Any accounting of Christianity's role in history that omits it is as distorted as an accounting that omits the Crusades.

But the steel-man does not resolve the credibility problem. Because credibility is not assessed across centuries. It is assessed in the present moment, by the specific people who needed the church to speak and found it silent, or needed the church to protect them and found it protecting itself instead. Those people are not wrong about their own experience. And their experience has become the experience of enough people to constitute a crisis.

The witness that survives is the one willing to speak when speaking is costly. Bonhoeffer's credibility rests not on his theology, which was shared by others, but on the fact that he went back to Germany when he could have stayed safe in America — and eventually died for the position his theology required him to take. The German church of 1934 that signed the Barmen Declaration [cite — confirm] against the state church's accommodation to National Socialism was credible precisely because the Declaration cost its signers something real. Witness at no cost is not witness. It is brand management.

The path back toward credibility is not a communication strategy. It is not better messaging or a more winsome tone or a rebranding exercise. It is the slow, costly labor of becoming trustworthy again in specific communities, among specific people, through specific acts of fidelity that absorb risk on behalf of those who have no one else to absorb it for them.

That means naming what the church has done wrong without defensive qualification. It means standing with people whose standing is costly to share. It means preaching texts that indict the powerful when the powerful are in the room. It means the congregation being present — physically, materially — in the neighborhood long before it asks the neighborhood to be present in the sanctuary.

That kind of witness is slow to build. It takes a generation. And it is the only thing that will eventually be heard by people who have every reason to stop listening.

The institution does not recover credibility by explaining why it did not deserve to lose it. It recovers credibility by doing what it said it was going to do.

That is still possible. Begin there.
