The Bible was not written in English. This is obvious, and its consequences are not. The English Bible most Christians read is a translation — a good one, usually, made by careful and faithful scholars — but every translation is a thousand small decisions, and some of those decisions quietly shape what a doctrine feels like by the time it reaches the pew. You do not need a seminary degree to follow Christ. But it helps to know that there is a layer beneath the English, and that the layer occasionally matters more than you would guess.

A few examples, not to show off the languages but to show what they protect.

Take love. English has one word and makes it carry everything — God's love, a marriage, a preference for tacos. The Greek of the New Testament works with more than one, and the difference is not trivial. When the risen Jesus questions Peter by the fire (John 21), the text moves between *agapaō* and *phileō*, and readers have argued for centuries about whether the shift carries weight. Whether or not it does in that passage, the larger point stands: English flattens a distinction the original could make, and a reader who does not know the distinction exists cannot even ask the question. Take the famous "you." English lost its plural, and Scripture suffers for it. When Paul tells the Corinthians "you are God's temple, and God's Spirit dwells in you" (1 Corinthians 3:16), the "you" is plural — he is talking about the church, the community, not the individual believer's body. Generations of English readers have heard it as a verse about personal piety because our pronoun cannot tell us otherwise. The Greek can. The meaning was communal; the translation made it sound private; and a whole strand of individualistic Christianity has leaned on a reading the grammar does not support.

Take the Hebrew side, too, since the Old Testament has its own depths. *Hesed* — the covenant love of God — is notoriously hard to render; "lovingkindness," "steadfast love," "mercy" all reach for it and none quite holds it. *Shalom* is not merely "peace" as the absence of conflict, but wholeness, the way things are supposed to be. Read "peace" where the text says *shalom* and you have received a smaller promise than the one God made.

I am not saying the English Bible is unreliable. It is reliable. The gospel is not hiding behind the Greek, available only to specialists; the central things are clear in any decent translation, and God has seen to it that the unlearned can be saved. What the original languages offer is not the gospel's existence but its *texture* — the nuance, the connections, the weight a word carried that an English equivalent cannot quite bear. They are the difference between hearing a piece of music and seeing the score.

And there is a guardrail here worth naming, because the languages get misused. A little Greek is a dangerous thing, and the internet is full of people "correcting" their pastor with a half-understood word study, as though one lexicon entry overturns the consensus of every translator in the room. That is not learning; it is point-scoring dressed as scholarship. The original languages are meant to deepen the church's reading, not to arm individuals against it. Used rightly, they make you more humble, not less — more aware of how much careful labor stands between you and the text, more grateful for it.

So you do not need the languages to be a faithful Christian. But it is worth knowing the layer is there. It is worth supporting the long, patient scholarship that tends it. And it is worth holding your most confident readings a little more loosely, knowing that beneath the English you trust is a depth you have only seen the surface of.

The score is richer than the recording. The recording is still true. But there is more there than you have heard.
