# Essays — Politics & the Cross

Six essays. Run after the After Christendom arc finishes. These are the ones
that get you on podcasts and on the wrong side of partisans.

---

## 11. The Cross Is Not a Flag

**Slug:** `the-cross-is-not-a-flag`
**Track:** `politics` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The most photographed object in white evangelicalism is the cross-and-flag-on-the-same-stage. The image is not Christian. The image is idolatry.

**Argument beats:**
1. The image. Describe it specifically: the church platform, the cross, the American flag, sometimes a presidential candidate. Name the visual.
2. Iconography matters. What you put next to the cross in a worship space is a theological claim, whether you intended it or not.
3. The historical analog: the Reichskirche put swastikas on church walls. Slovak Catholic churches put crucifixes next to the Tiso regime's symbols. Imperial Russian Orthodoxy put double-headed eagles inside icons. Every time, the result was the same — the gospel was conscripted to bless a regime, and the gospel could not survive the blessing.
4. Why this is not merely political: it is sacramental. The cross sanctifies what is placed next to it. Place the cross next to a nation and you have made that nation sacred. Sacred nations have always been a heresy.
5. The constructive move: take the flag out of the sanctuary. This is not unpatriotic. It is theological.
6. Self-implication: I have preached under the image. I should have removed it sooner.

**Opening sketch:** "I have stood under that image. The image is a cross and a flag on the same stage, photographed from the right angle, sold as patriotism. The image is not patriotism. The image is idolatry, and the idolatry has been so normalized in the American sanctuary that most of us no longer see it. I want us to see it."

**Closing weight:** The cross does not need the flag. The cross has never needed any flag. The cross is the thing that judges every flag, and the church that places a flag beside it has misunderstood what is doing the judging. Take the flag out. Let the cross stand alone. See what happens to your church when the cross is the only object on the platform. You will discover what your worship was about.

**Sources:** Karl Barth (*Barmen*); William Cavanaugh (*Migrations of the Holy*); Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry (*Taking America Back for God*); James K.A. Smith (*Awaiting the King*).

## 12. Left and Right Are Both Mistakes

**Slug:** `left-and-right-are-both-mistakes`
**Track:** `politics` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** The Christian who picks a partisan team has misunderstood the gospel. Both teams are insufficient. Both teams contain real truth. Both teams contain heresy. The job is to be the third option.

**Argument beats:**
1. Where the left gets things right: justice, neighbor-love, the structural critique of poverty, attention to the marginalized. Walter Brueggemann is right.
2. Where the left gets things wrong: the autonomy axiom, the sexual ethic, the soft determinism, the assumption that the state is the primary agent of love.
3. Where the right gets things right: subsidiarity, family, religious liberty, distrust of utopian state projects. The conservative anthropology is closer to scripture than the progressive one in important ways.
4. Where the right gets things wrong: the willingness to subordinate justice to order, the racial blindness, the recent absorption of Christian nationalism, the prosperity-gospel theology baked into the policy preferences.
5. The third option is not centrism. Centrism is the average of two errors. The third option is the gospel — which judges left and right by a standard neither one accepts.
6. Self-implication: I have been right-coded for most of my pastoral life. I have failed the third option more often than I have held it.
7. The verdict: pick the gospel. Then watch what the gospel does to your politics. It will not let you stay on either team.

**Opening sketch:** "I have been wrong about politics for most of my adult life. Not wrong in the way each side thinks the other side is wrong. Wrong in the older way — wrong because I had picked a team, and the picking had cost me the ability to see what the team was costing me. Both teams cost something. The third option costs more, and is worth the cost, and is the only option a Christian can finally take."

**Key moves:**
- The Brueggemann line on the left getting some things right; the O'Donovan line on the right getting some things right.
- *Not X. Y.:* "The third option is not centrism. Centrism is the average of two errors."
- Concrete: the policy I changed my mind about and what changing my mind cost me.

**Closing weight:** I am no longer interested in being on either team. I am interested in being inside the gospel, and watching what the gospel does to the political instincts I was given. The gospel takes them apart. The taking-apart is uncomfortable. It is also the only way to stop being a useful idiot for one team or the other, and to become — at the end of a long process — a Christian who can speak to both teams from somewhere outside them.

**Sources:** Brueggemann (*The Prophetic Imagination*); O'Donovan (*The Desire of the Nations*); Hauerwas; David French (*Divided We Fall*); Tim Alberta.

## 13. When Pastors Become Pundits

**Slug:** `when-pastors-become-pundits`
**Track:** `pastoral-ministry` · **Audience:** `pastors` · **Reading time:** ~8 min

**Positioning sentence:** The pastor who becomes a pundit has stopped being a pastor. The transformation is gradual and almost always invisible to the pastor doing it.

**Argument beats:**
1. The seduction. Pastoring is hard, slow, often invisible. Punditry is fast, visible, rewarded. The pull is real.
2. The category error: a pulpit is not a microphone, and a sermon is not a column. The two require different authorities, different audiences, different accountabilities.
3. What is lost: the pastor's authority depends on knowing the people. The pundit's authority depends on having a take. When a pastor adopts pundit-mode, the pastor becomes the take, and the people stop being known.
4. The historical pattern. The mid-twentieth-century pastors who became civil-rights pundits — King, Heschel — did it without losing the pastoral ground under their feet. They were pastors first. The contemporary pattern is reversed. The pundit is performing the role of pastor without having actually pastored anyone.
5. Diagnostic questions: When was the last time you visited a member of your congregation in the hospital? When was the last time you preached a sermon on something that was not in the news cycle? When was the last time someone in your congregation told you something embarrassing about themselves?
6. The cost: the pastor-pundit eventually becomes the pundit, and the church becomes the platform, and the platform devours the church.
7. The verdict: stop. Pastor someone. The pastoring is the authority that makes anything else you say worth listening to.

**Opening sketch:** "I have watched, in twenty years of ministry, more pastors become pundits than I want to count. Some did it on purpose. Most did it gradually, the way a marriage falls apart gradually — small choices, small substitutions, the slow replacement of one role with another, until the original role was gone and no one had noticed when it left."

**Closing weight:** The pastor who has stopped being a pastor and become a pundit cannot return easily. The path back is the path of the original work — quiet visits, slow conversations, the funeral of someone whose family does not subscribe to your podcast, the wedding of two people who have no idea what your Twitter bio says. Most of the pastor-pundits I know are not coming back. The path is not closed. The path is just hard, and they have grown used to a different kind of reward.

**Sources:** Eugene Peterson (*The Pastor*); Andy Crouch (*Strong and Weak*); David Brooks (*The Second Mountain*); James Hunter (*To Change the World*, esp. the section on the elite-network model of culture-change).

## 14. Jesus Was Not a Capitalist (twin essay with #15)

**Slug:** `jesus-was-not-a-capitalist`
**Track:** `politics` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~7 min

**Positioning sentence:** The American church baptized capitalism in the twentieth century. The baptism took. Now we cannot tell the gospel from the market. We need to.

**Argument beats:**
1. The Sermon on the Mount on money. The story of the rich young ruler. The early church's communal economics in Acts 2 and 4. The repeated prophetic critique of accumulated wealth without responsibility to the poor.
2. The historical drift: how the American Protestant tradition, from the Puritans through the Gilded Age through the prosperity gospel, gradually identified Christianity with free-market capitalism.
3. The cost: when capitalism is the gospel, the poor become a problem to be solved rather than the recipients of Christ. When capitalism is the gospel, the church's witness on economic justice goes silent.
4. The clarification: scripture is not against private property. Scripture is against the worship of accumulation, and against any economic system that produces wealth by destroying neighbors.
5. The verdict: Jesus was not a capitalist. The honest reading of scripture does not make him one.
6. Pair with #15: this is half of a twin essay. Read them together.

**Opening sketch:** "The American evangelical tradition has, for most of the past century, identified Christianity with capitalism. The identification has been mostly unconscious and almost entirely unexamined. It is also wrong. The scripture does not endorse capitalism, and it does not endorse capitalism's twentieth-century American form, and the church that has spent a century pretending otherwise has done so at the cost of its witness to the poor."

**Closing weight:** Jesus was not a capitalist. The honest reading does not allow it. The honest reading also does not endorse the alternative the next essay names. The third option, again. Read on.

**Sources:** Walter Brueggemann (*Money and Possessions*); Ron Sider (*Rich Christians in an Age of Hunger*); William Cavanaugh (*Being Consumed*); Kelly Brown Douglas; David Bentley Hart's recent essays on Christian economics.

## 15. Jesus Was Not a Socialist (twin essay with #14)

**Slug:** `jesus-was-not-a-socialist`
**Track:** `politics` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~7 min

**Positioning sentence:** The progressive Christian tradition has tried, since the Social Gospel, to enlist Jesus as a socialist. The enlistment is just as wrong as the conservative one. Both miss what scripture actually says.

**Argument beats:**
1. The progressive reading of Acts 2: communal property, "from each according to his ability, to each according to his need." It looks socialist. It is voluntary, ecclesial, and not coerced.
2. The state-coercion problem: socialism in its modern forms assumes the state as the redistributive agent. Scripture does not. The agent of mercy in scripture is the *people of God*, not the *imperial bureaucracy*.
3. The autonomy problem: progressive Christianity has often paired its economic critique with a sexual and anthropological autonomy that scripture does not endorse. The pairing makes the economic critique easier to dismiss. It should not be paired.
4. The historical lesson: state-imposed atheism in the twentieth century — Soviet, Chinese, Cambodian — killed more Christians than every era of Christendom combined. State power is not the agent of redemption. State power has been, historically, one of the most dangerous things in the world for the church.
5. The constructive: the church should do what the state cannot do — voluntary, costly, neighborly economic life. Acts 2 is the model. The model is *not* socialism. The model is *also not* capitalism. The model is the church.
6. Pair with #14.

**Opening sketch:** "Half the church in America has, for most of my lifetime, told me that Jesus was a capitalist. The other half has told me that Jesus was a socialist. They cannot both be right. They are, in fact, both wrong. The Jesus of the gospels is neither, and the church that wants to enlist him for either project has misread him in the same way for opposite reasons."

**Closing weight:** Jesus was not a socialist. The honest reading does not make him one. He was also not a capitalist. The honest reading does not make him that, either. The honest reading puts him outside both projects, and judges both. The judgment is uncomfortable. The judgment is the gospel. Sit with it.

**Sources:** Walter Brueggemann; Lesslie Newbigin; Solzhenitsyn (*The Gulag Archipelago*); the work of Dorothy Day, treated honestly (she rejected the socialist label); David Bentley Hart (*The Beauty of the Infinite*).

## 16. The Sermon on the Mount as Politics

**Slug:** `the-sermon-on-the-mount-as-politics`
**Track:** `politics` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~14 min (long-read)

**Positioning sentence:** The Sermon on the Mount is not spiritual advice. It is a political constitution. The American church has spiritualized it to make it survivable. We do not need to survive it. We need to be governed by it.

**Argument beats:**
1. The text. Matthew 5–7. Walk slowly through the Beatitudes. They are political — naming who is honored in this Kingdom and who is not. They reverse the existing political honor order.
2. The salt-and-light passage. This is not an inspirational call to be nice. It is a description of what the polis-of-Christ does in the polis-of-the-world.
3. The anti-violence passages. Turn the other cheek, go the second mile, love your enemies. The American church has read these as personal ethics. They are also — explicitly — political ethics. They describe how the citizens of the Kingdom relate to the citizens of empires.
4. The economic passages. Treasures in heaven, the unforgiving servant, the lilies of the field, the Lord's Prayer's "forgive us our debts." This is a political economy.
5. The judgment passages. The Pharisees, the false prophets, the foundations of sand. The Sermon ends in apocalyptic political warning.
6. Why we have to read it as politics now. The depoliticized reading was a luxury Christendom afforded us. Post-Christendom, we need the Sermon as constitution, not devotional.
7. The verdict: the Sermon on the Mount is the most political document in the New Testament. We have read it as spirituality for two hundred years. We have run out of time for that reading.

**Opening sketch:** "Matthew 5 through 7 is the longest single political speech in the New Testament. The American church has not read it as politics for two centuries. The not-reading has cost us a politics. It has also cost us the Sermon. I want to read it back."

**Closing weight:** The Sermon on the Mount is the politics of the people of God. It is not negotiable. It is not aspirational. It is not the ideal we will attain in heaven. It is the constitution of the polis we already belong to, the citizenship we already hold, the alternative city we have been carrying around inside the empire for two thousand years. The empire reads the Sermon and calls it impractical. It is impractical *for an empire*. It is the only practical politics for the people who refuse to be one.

**Sources:** N.T. Wright (*Matthew for Everyone*); Dietrich Bonhoeffer (*Discipleship*, esp. the Sermon section); Stanley Hauerwas (*Matthew*); John Howard Yoder (*The Politics of Jesus*); Glen Stassen (*Living the Sermon on the Mount*).
