Every century has its heretics. The remarkable thing is not that they existed. The remarkable thing is that most of them believed, with complete sincerity, that they were the ones finally reading Scripture correctly. That all the previous interpreters had gotten it wrong, and they had gotten it right. The medieval theologians defending the Crusades believed they had the Bible on their side. The eighteenth-century divines who wrote elaborate defenses of chattel slavery believed they had the Bible on their side. The congregations that maintained racial segregation through the 1950s and 60s believed they had the Bible on their side. They were wrong. They were not lying. That is the harder fact.

This is not a comfortable history to sit with, and most of us do not sit with it long enough to feel its weight. We note it, acknowledge it, and then proceed to make exactly the same error we are acknowledging — the error of assuming that we, unlike all the previous generations, have finally gotten the interpretive problem under control.

The problem is not fundamentally about intelligence. The generations I named produced brilliant people, scholars with real linguistic competence and serious devotional commitments. The problem is structural. Every interpreter reads Scripture from somewhere. From a body, a century, a class, a nation, a set of economic arrangements, a family of origin that has shaped what seems obvious and what seems impossible. These are not obstacles to interpretation that can be removed by trying harder. They are the conditions of all interpretation. The question is not whether you read from somewhere. You do. The question is whether you know where you are reading from and what that location is likely to make you miss.

I have done this. I came to faith from atheism and brought with me a certain mode of argument, a certain confidence in the clarity of propositions, a certain impatience with ambiguity — all of which shaped how I read the text long after I stopped being an atheist. I have preached from those assumptions as if they were simply the Bible, and the gap between the two is wider than I would have admitted at the time. The log I needed to remove from my eye was not the obvious one.

The philosopher Hans-Georg Gadamer [cite — confirm] argued that all understanding involves a "fusion of horizons" — the horizon of the text meeting the horizon of the reader, neither one simply overriding the other. What this means for Bible readers is that our cultural moment is always in the room when we read, whether or not we invited it. The interpreters who thought slavery was defensible were not reading Scripture without presuppositions. They were reading it with the presuppositions of a slave-holding economy, and those presuppositions had become so natural to them that they could not distinguish them from the text itself.

The question this forces on every serious Christian is this: what are the equivalent presuppositions in my own reading, right now, that I cannot yet see because they are too close to my eyes?

There are candidates. The individualism of modern Western culture shapes how we read texts that were written to and for communities, not isolated persons. Our therapeutic assumptions about the self shape how we read sin. Our consumer instincts shape how we read discipleship. Our nation's particular relationship to wealth and power shapes how we read the prophets and the Sermon on the Mount. These are not accusations aimed at anyone in particular. They are descriptions of water that everyone in this cultural moment is swimming in, whether or not they know it.

The answer is not to pretend these influences do not exist. The answer is not to throw up our hands and conclude that the Bible means nothing stable. The church's interpretive tradition, tested across centuries and cultures, has produced a center that holds: the canonical witness to the death and resurrection of Jesus, the love of God and neighbor, the dignity of all human beings made in the image of God. These are not contested at the center. What is contested is almost always at the edges, and the edges are often where our cultural location exerts its strongest pressure.

What does this require? It requires reading the Bible with people who are different from you. It requires taking the interpretive tradition seriously, including the parts that make you uncomfortable. It requires the specific humility of sitting under a text that is measuring you rather than confirming you. That last one is the hardest. The Bible read merely for confirmation is not really being read. It is being used.

The monster in the mirror is never someone else. I have looked long enough now to know that the interpretive failures of previous generations are not evidence of their unusual moral weakness. They are evidence of a condition I share. My reading will be wrong in ways I cannot see from here. The only question is whether I have enough people around me who can see what I cannot, and whether I am humble enough to hear them.

That humility does not come naturally. It has to be chosen. Repeatedly.
