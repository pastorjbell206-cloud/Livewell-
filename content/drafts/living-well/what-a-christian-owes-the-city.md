The sermon had gone the way those sermons go. I had said, as carefully as fifteen years of practice allows, that the cross is not a party asset — that a church which cannot tell the difference between the kingdom of God and a November outcome has already lost something no election can return. People nodded. Some of them meant it. And then in the foyer a man I have known for years — a deacon's steadiness in him, a man who reads — shook my hand and asked the question that has been working on me ever since. All right, he said. I hear you. The flag comes off the altar. Then what? Do I still vote? Do I still care who wins? What do you actually want from me on a Tuesday in November?

I gave him something warm and inconclusive, and he was gracious about it, and I drove home knowing what had happened. I had handed him a whole theology of what not to worship and half a page on what to do. A diagnosis with no prescription leaves the patient two doors, and both are wrong. The first goes back into the tribe — the sermon fades, the feed resumes, and by the next election the idolatry has been forgiven for the excellent reason that it never left. The second looks like repentance and is really resignation: politics is dirty, both sides are compromised, I am done — withdrawal from the city, renamed purity. I have watched members of my own congregation walk through each door, sometimes the same member through both, one election apart.

**So this is the essay the diagnosis has been pointing toward, and its claim is easy to say and hard to hold. After the idolatry is named, the question is not whether a Christian loves the city. It is how. And the answer Scripture gives is a both/and that neither tribe can carry: genuine, costly care for the city's welfare, and a flat refusal to seek salvation from it. Engagement without worship. Love without trust. The tribes split that sentence and take half apiece — one keeps the engagement and loses the refusal, the other keeps the refusal and loses the love — and each half, alone, becomes a lie.**

## A letter to people who lost

Begin where God begins the constructive work, because the address matters as much as the instruction. In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar's army took Jerusalem, and the first great deportation carried the young king, the elders, the craftsmen, and the smiths — the whole functioning class of the city — east to Babylon. The temple still stood; it had a decade left. And among the deportees a market in hope sprang up at once, stocked by prophets. One of them, Hananiah, put a date on it: within two years the temple vessels would come home and the yoke of Babylon would snap (Jeremiah 28 records the scene, and records that Hananiah was dead within the year). Notice what the false prophecy in Babylon was not. It was not paganism. It was optimism about power — the announcement that the political situation would shortly be corrected. The exiles' strongest religious temptation was a rescue on a political timetable. Some things do not change.

Into that market Jeremiah sent a letter, and it said the unforgivable thing — seventy years — and then it said this:

> "Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, to all the exiles whom I have sent into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon: Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce. Take wives and have sons and daughters; take wives for your sons, and give your daughters in marriage, that they may bear sons and daughters; multiply there, and do not decrease. But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare." (Jeremiah 29:4–7, ESV)

Read the address line first. This is written to people who did not hold power and were not going to — no vote in Babylon, no seat at court, no path to the throne, no return ticket inside their own lifetimes. Whatever this command is, it cannot be a strategy for taking the city, because the people it was given to could not take a city block. And read the strange claim buried in the greeting — *whom I have sent*. Not whom Nebuchadnezzar dragged. God puts his own name on the catastrophe, which means the exile is an assignment before it is a punishment, and the address they did not choose is the address where obedience now lives.

Then the instruction, and its shape is almost insultingly ordinary. Houses. Gardens. Weddings. Grandchildren. No underground, no counter-court, no siege engines of the military or the cultural kind — just the patient machinery of settled life, planted in enemy soil. And then the sentence that breaks the categories: seek the welfare of the city. The word is *shalom* — not the city's mere order but its flourishing, the knitting-together of its common life. Pray to the LORD *on its behalf*. This is Babylon. These streets run with the wealth stripped out of Jerusalem. Seek its shalom, says the God of Israel, for in its shalom you will find your own — your welfare and its welfare have been tied together, on purpose, by me.

Hold the two edges of that text and you can feel it cutting both ways. It cannot be conscripted for conquest, because it was issued to the powerless: seeking a city's welfare is not seeking its throne, and the letter pointedly offers no throne. And it cannot be conscripted for withdrawal, because *build, plant, marry, invest, pray* is a command to entangle yourself with a pagan city's flourishing until its good becomes your good. The exiles were forbidden both the fantasy of rescue-by-regime-change and the purity of the sealed enclave. What remained was harder than either. Love the city. Expect nothing from it that only God can give.

## Two cities in one ruin

Eight centuries of Roman confidence ended on August 24, 410, when Alaric's Goths sacked the city the whole Mediterranean world had assumed was permanent. The refugees who washed up in North Africa carried an accusation with them: Rome fell because it abandoned the old gods for Christ. So in 413 the bishop of Hippo began the book that took him thirteen years to finish, *The City of God* — among a dozen other things, the church's deepest sustained answer to what Christians are doing in a political order at all.

Augustine's answer runs on a distinction. There are two cities, he argued, and they are not church and state, not Rome and Jerusalem, not any pair you can find on a map, because their citizens live on the same streets and their memberships are invisible until the end. What distinguishes them is what they love. Two loves built two cities — his famous formulation, near the close of Book XIV — the earthly city by love of self swollen to contempt for God, the heavenly city by love of God carried to contempt for self. Everything else hangs off that hinge, including his account of virtue as rightly ordered love — *ordo amoris* — loving each thing as much as it deserves and nothing more. Sin, on this reckoning, is rarely loving a bad thing. It is loving a good thing out of order.

Now set that beside the political air we breathe, because the diagnosis translates without remainder. There are two ways a Christian's loves and politics can relate, and only one of them is faith. Politics can order your loves: the coalition tells you whom to love and whom to fear, which sufferings count and which are propaganda, which sins are emergencies and which are quirks — until the party has done the precise work Augustine assigned to God, arranging the heart's priorities, and has thereby become, in the only sense that matters, your god. Or love can order your politics: the city becomes one of the goods you care for — a real good, worth real labor — but a created good, loved in its place, beneath the God who will outlast it. Augustine grieved Rome; the book is not a shrug. But he had watched the thing every political theology must reckon with: the city that called itself eternal, wasn't. He wrote from inside the rubble that the earthly city's peace is genuinely valuable and genuinely temporary, that the heavenly city makes grateful use of it while refusing to call it home. He was not lowering the reader's care for the city. He was moving its foundation off the city's permanence — an illusion — and onto the command of the God who assigns his people to cities the way he assigned exiles to Babylon.

## The servant and the beast

Whoever wants a one-verse political theology has to suppress a canonical fact: the New Testament looks at the same empire twice and reports two different things, and both reports are Scripture. Writing to the Christians of Rome around the year 57, Paul is unambiguous: "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God" (Romans 13:1, ESV). The ruler, he adds, "is God's servant for your good" (Romans 13:4, ESV). The emperor Paul calls God's servant was Nero. A generation later — most scholars place it in the mid-90s, under Domitian — John, exiled on Patmos, sees the same empire as a beast rising from the sea, wearing blasphemous names, demanding the world's worship: "Also it was allowed to make war on the saints and to conquer them. And authority was given it over every tribe and people and language and nation" (Revelation 13:7, ESV).

Romans 13 and Revelation 13. The same Rome. The canon is not confused; it is teaching. Government is God's servant for your good — really, even when its ministers never heard of him — *and* government can swell into a beast demanding what belongs to God alone, and the same government can travel from the one to the other within a Christian's lifetime. A political theology that can only read Romans 13 will baptize whatever holds the sword. One that can only read Revelation 13 will find the beast under every statute and never honor anything. The apostles held both, and Peter compressed the both/and into four clauses: "Honor everyone. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the emperor" (1 Peter 2:17, ESV). Honor for the emperor; fear reserved for God. The same sentence bows and refuses to kneel.

And here is where the tribes give themselves away, because each of them owns both chapters and switches between them on schedule. Romans 13 when our man holds the sword; Revelation 13 the moment the other man takes it. Submission preached as godliness in one administration becomes resistance preached as godliness in the next, and the exegesis tracks the election returns to the decimal. Both sides run the same play; only the years change. I am not describing it from a safe distance — I know which verse rises in me unbidden depending on who won. A man whose canon rotates with the White House has not submitted his politics to Scripture. He has submitted Scripture to his politics and kept the leather cover.

## What faithful presence actually asks

The best-known modern name for the constructive posture is James Davison Hunter's, and it is worth stating what he actually argued, because "faithful presence" has been worn as a brand by people recommending nearly the opposite. In *To Change the World* (2010), Hunter — a sociologist at the University of Virginia, not a culture-war chaplain for either side — dismantled the theory of change both Christian tribes run on: that culture is transformed by changing individual hearts, or by winning political power. Cultures do not mainly change that way, he argued; they change slowly, through networks of institutions and elites near the center, which is why a century of Christian mobilization produced so little of what it promised. Worse, he showed what the mobilization did produce. When the church made politics its master frame — and he indicts the Christian right, the Christian left, and the neo-Anabaptist purists together, which is why partisans of all three dislike the book — it absorbed the politics of *ressentiment*, a settled grievance that became the church's public face regardless of its stated creed. His prescription scandalized activists: the church might need to be silent for a season about politics, not because the public square does not matter but because the church's speech there had become toxic to its own witness.

What he offered instead is the phrase everyone quotes and fewer have read: faithful presence. Not withdrawal — Hunter rejects the sealed enclave. Not conquest — the book exists to bury that theory. Faithful presence means Christians fully committed to the people, places, and vocations where they already are — the school, the shop, the block, the county — working for the flourishing of everyone within reach, believer and unbeliever alike, without needing to run the world or win the era. It is Jeremiah's letter translated into sociology: build, plant, seek the shalom of the city, and let go of the timetable.

Lesslie Newbigin, home in England after nearly four decades as a missionary bishop in India, reached the same place by a different road. In *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) he asked how the gospel could be found credible in a culture that no longer grants its premises, and his answer was not an argument but an address: the only hermeneutic of the gospel, he wrote, is a congregation of men and women who believe it and live by it. Which means the church's first political act is not a statement, an endorsement, or a march. It is being the church — a visible community where the gospel is legible in how enemies are treated. A congregation that cannot hold two yard signs at one table is not merely failing at fellowship. It is failing at evangelism, because the community was the argument, and the argument just collapsed.

## One table, two yard signs

Which brings me to the room I actually serve, because everything above this line I learned the hard way, standing in it. My congregation is small-town Michigan, politically mixed the way small towns are: not a debating society, just neighbors — the retired shop teacher and the nurse, the farmer and the schoolteacher, people who cancel each other's votes every cycle and know it. Every election year the country's argument arrives at our door dressed as a member. And every communion Sunday I watch something the country would not believe: people whose yard signs face each other across the street kneeling at the same rail, served from the same loaf, by each other. I can count on one hand the rooms left in my town where that happens. The church is one of the last places in America where the two tribes still touch the same bread, and I have stopped treating that as a scheduling accident. It is a political fact — maybe the most political thing we do.

Now the confession, because this essay does not get to indict from the gallery. Over fifteen years I have preached bravely against the idolatry my room already distrusted and carefully about the one it brought in the door. I knew which applause was cheap and I banked it anyway. I called the caution pastoral wisdom — and some of it was, and some of it was cowardice wearing wisdom's jacket, and on the honest days I can tell which was which. A guardrail that only fires in one political direction is not orthodoxy; it is a tribe in robes, and for stretches of my ministry I wore them. My pews deserved a shepherd who grieved both captivities at the same volume. Getting there cost me approval I had grown used to. It was owed.

## The scale you actually live at

So what should the man in the foyer do? Here the essay has to refuse two temptations at once: the voter guide, which would betray everything above, and the shrug, which would betray him. What Scripture and the tradition hand us is not a platform. It is a posture, and it takes flesh at the scale where you actually live — which is, for nearly everyone reading this, not the national scale. The national argument is the one you can do least about and the one built to occupy you most; the county you live in is the reverse. Faithful presence begins by trading the politics you can only watch for the politics you can actually touch.

That means the school board meeting, attended — or the seat itself, held the way a deacon holds an office rather than the way a soldier holds a hill: read the budget, learn the names, pray for the superintendent whether or not you voted for the levy, and when the vote goes six to one against you, lose like a Christian — without contempt and without leaving. It means the food pantry, quietly the most political square footage in town in the old sense of the word — the *polis*, the shared life — because the line outside it does not sort by party. It means the neighbor whose sign opposes yours: his name learned, his driveway shoveled, his kids known, so that whatever the feed says his kind of people are like, you are disqualified from believing it, having eaten at his table. None of this will trend. That is much of its value. The future does not belong to stronger tribes; it belongs to deeper disciples — and the school board, the pantry line, and the fence line are where the depth is formed, one unspectacular Tuesday at a time.

And the vote. Cast it. Abstention is also a political act whose costs land on neighbors, and the exiles were not told to keep their hands clean of Babylon's common life. But cast it the way you would use any tool that cannot love you back — soberly, prayerfully, with a loose grip — and walk out of the booth exactly as free as you walked in, belonging to no one you just voted for. The lever is a stewardship. It is not a sacrament, and it was never a confession of faith.

What I will not tell you is where the lever should land, and the refusal is not cowardice this time; it is doctrine. Christians have always distinguished what the faith settles from what it leaves to judgment, and almost every policy mechanism — the tax rate, the zoning variance, the immigration number, the healthcare design, the school funding formula — lives in the second category, where serious believers reading the same Bible reach different prudential conclusions and remain brothers. What the faith settles is smaller and far more demanding: the person on the other side bears the image of God and is therefore permanently off-limits to your contempt. James would not let his churches split the tongue's allegiance: "With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse people who are made in the likeness of God" (James 3:9, ESV). Fight your opponent's proposal with everything lawful and honest. You may not despise the man. Contempt is the one truly bipartisan sin of this era, the habit both tribes teach as virtue and reward as loyalty — and the one thing on the whole political menu a Christian is flatly forbidden to order.

## The welfare of a city that is not home

The exiles who received Jeremiah's letter mostly died in Babylon. They built the houses, planted the gardens, raised the grandchildren, prayed for the city that had burned their city — and were buried in its soil, seventy years short of the return their obedience made possible for someone else. That is the address the constructive answer was mailed to, and it has not changed. Seek the welfare of a city that is not your home, will not thank you, and cannot save you. Expect the work to outlast your lifetime and the credit to skip your generation.

It would be easier to be told to conquer the city. It would be easier to be told to abandon it. We were told to love it, and love is the costlier instruction — it always is. I owe the man in the foyer a better answer than the one he got, and this is its beginning, not its end; he will have to work out the school board and the shovel and the lever in the county where he actually lives. But the two truths he carries into November are not in tension after all. The city cannot save you. You are commanded to seek its welfare anyway. The space between those sentences is not a contradiction to resolve. It is the address, and we were sent to it.
