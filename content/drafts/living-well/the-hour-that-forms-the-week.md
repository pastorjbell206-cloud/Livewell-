The most common sentence I hear at the door after a Sunday service is a review. The sermon was strong this week, or long. The music was too loud, or finally right. And at least once a month, from someone I love and have pastored for years, the sentence underneath all the others: "I just didn't get much out of it today." Fifteen years of Sundays, and the grammar never changes. Church is a thing you get something out of. We talk about the service the way we talk about a restaurant, and nobody finds this strange, because nobody remembers deciding to talk this way.

I am not above the grammar. I helped teach it. For years I built services exactly as my tradition handed them to me — and my tradition had a name for everything before the sermon. The old Baptist word was "the preliminaries." Songs to warm the room. Announcements to inform it. An offering to sustain it. Then the sermon — the point, the reason we had come, the product the rest of the hour existed to package. I never said any of that out loud. I did not have to. The order said it every week, and the congregation learned it the way children learn a language — not from the lessons, but from the house.

**So this essay stands on a claim that took me most of two decades to see: worship is not the warm-up for the sermon, and liturgy is not a Catholic word. A liturgy is simply what a congregation repeats, in the order it repeats it — which means every church has one, including the church that says it does not. The order of the service is a curriculum. It is teaching whether or not anyone has examined the syllabus. And what we repeat is what we become.**

## The animals that worship

The philosopher James K.A. Smith opened *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) with an argument the church has been slow to absorb. Human beings, he says, are not primarily thinking things, brains on a stick that need better information — nor even believing things that need better doctrine. We are lovers, aimed at some vision of the good life, pulled through the world by what we want long before we can say what we think. And our wants are not installed by arguments. They are trained by practices — repeated, embodied routines that rehearse a picture of flourishing until we desire it without deciding to. Smith calls these practices liturgies, and he calls the potent ones pedagogies of desire: they do not teach you facts; they teach you loves. His most quoted move is to walk through a shopping mall as if it were a temple — the atrium, the icons in the shop windows, the sacrament at the register — not as a joke but as an exegesis, because the mall really is aiming your heart, and it never once asks you to believe anything. We are liturgical animals. The only question is whose liturgy is training us.

I have written elsewhere that the feed in your pocket is exactly such a liturgy — a daily office with its own catechism, run by a machine that needs only your presence — and this essay is the other half of that argument. Because if Smith is right, the rival liturgies have the week — the commute, the checkout line, the last minutes before sleep. The church has an hour. An hour against a week is not a fair fight on any arithmetic — unless that hour is doing something different in kind, not just in content: not another stream of information about God but a rehearsal, in order, of a story strong enough to reorder the loves the week has been bending. That is what the church's hour was for. Which makes it strange that so many of us who lead it have never asked what our hour actually rehearses.

## The church that says it has no liturgy

My tradition is the free-church tradition. Baptist, low, plain. And for generations we have said, usually with some pride, that we don't do liturgy — no robes, no written prayers, no book telling us what to say in October. I have said it myself, from the front. It is not true. It has never been true. What we have is forty minutes of songs and announcements, an offering, and a sermon, in an order so fixed that a member could call the sequence from the parking lot — and because we told ourselves it was not a liturgy, it became the one kind of liturgy that is genuinely dangerous: an unexamined one.

Because the order teaches, whether or not it was designed to. When a church uses the word "worship" to mean only the singing — "worship, then the message" — it is teaching, week by week, that worship is a musical experience and the mind takes over when the music stops. When everything before the sermon functions as preliminaries, it teaches that God's people gather chiefly to receive content, and that the sermon has an audience rather than a congregation. When the only confession in the building is the soft instrumental moment before the offering, it teaches that sin is a mood to be soothed rather than a debt that was cancelled. And when the service has no shape but build-up and delivery, it borrows the only other shape our people know — the concert, the show, the feed — and the borrowed shape brings its catechism: you are the audience; your attention is currency; evaluate accordingly. Then we stand at the door and wonder why they review us like a restaurant. They are not being shallow. They are being formed. By us.

The spontaneity is its own tell. Free-church people distrust written prayers because a prayer read off a page might not be sincere — and then the pastor prays, unscripted, the same prayer he prayed last week and the week before, the same phrases in the same order, the word "just" holding it together the way "thee" and "thou" once did. I know, because the prayer is mine. We did not escape liturgy. We wrote one by accident, standardized it by habit, and exempted it from review by calling it freedom.

## The oldest Sunday we can see

It helps to look at the oldest surviving description of a Christian service, written by a man who was explaining it to a pagan. Around the year 155, a philosopher named Justin — converted to Christ, still wearing the philosopher's cloak, a decade from the execution that history would attach to his name — addressed his *First Apology* to the emperor Antoninus Pius, defending Christians against the rumors swirling around them. Near the end he simply describes what they do. On the day called Sunday, he says, all who live in city or countryside gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits. The presider exhorts everyone to imitate these good things. All stand together and pray. Bread and wine are brought; the presider gives thanks as best he is able; the people answer with their Amen; the gifts are distributed, and deacons carry them to those who could not come. Then a collection — and Justin is specific about where it goes: to orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners, strangers passing through.

That is his description, not a divine blueprint — one man's account of the churches he knew, offered to an emperor, and it should be handled as exactly that. But look at what it shows, barely a century after the apostles: Word, prayer, table, mercy. A spine. The reading is long — "as long as time permits" — and the preaching is its servant, an exhortation to live what was just read, not a headline act with warm-up bands. The table is where the hour lands. And the offering is not an awkward pause in the worship while the plates go by. It is worship — the gospel completing its own circuit, the fed church feeding the widow. Luke describes the first congregation in the same four strokes: "And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42, ESV). Devoted themselves — the Greek behind it is a word for stubborn persistence. Not a service they attended. A shape they kept.

The church did not begin with an unstructured gathering that later hardened into ritual. The shape was there before Christians had buildings, before they were legal, before anyone could confuse the liturgy with the empire's pageantry — because the empire was still feeding them to it.

## What the Reformers actually did

The other honesty my tradition owes itself concerns the Reformation, because we tell that story as liberation from liturgy, and that is not what happened. What happened was an argument about which liturgy tells the truth.

When Luther finally turned to the worship question — the *Formula Missae* in 1523, the German Mass in 1526 — he did not abolish the historic shape of the service. He kept it. Kept the readings, the sermon, the creed, the table at the center, and cut what he believed preached a false gospel: above all the canon's language of the priest offering sacrifice, as if Calvary needed repeating. Then he translated the whole thing into the language of the people and put the singing in their mouths instead of the choir's. Reform, for Luther, meant a corrected liturgy, not a deleted one.

Zurich went the other way. Under Zwingli, the city removed the images from its churches in 1524 and abolished the mass in April 1525. The Supper shrank to four times a year, taken seated, from wooden vessels. The service became sermon and prayer, plain as the newly whitewashed walls. And notice — this is the point my people miss — Zurich's stripped service was every inch as much a liturgy as Rome's full one. Its bareness taught. It taught that God is heard rather than seen, that the ear is the organ of faith, that the Word needs no ornament. One can argue with that curriculum or embrace it. One cannot call it the absence of a curriculum. The free-church service I inherited descends, through the centuries after 1517, from that reordering — from a set of deliberate liturgical decisions made by men who knew exactly what they were doing and why. When we say "we don't do liturgy," what we actually mean is that we inherited Zurich and forgot.

## The two ways to miss the same thing

Here the honesty has to cut in both directions, with the same instrument, because there are two ways to mistake the form for the thing, and each tradition is blind to its own.

The liturgical churches face the ancient one: beauty mistaken for faith. A man can love the candlelight, the chant, the weight of the old words, the sheer relief of walking out of a world made of screens into a room made of stone — and love all of it the way one loves any excellent aesthetic experience — without repentance. There is a kind of convert the high traditions know well, converted less to the crucified Lord than to the atmosphere around him; and a kind of lifelong member who has genuflected through five decades with a heart the liturgy never once got permission to touch. The form was built to carry faith. Kept polished and empty, it can also carry its absence undetected — which is why the prophets spent so much ink on Israel's beautiful, orthodox, God-nauseating worship.

My tradition faces the mirror image: spontaneity mistaken for sincerity. We assume the unplanned prayer is the honest one and the prepared prayer is the performance — as if the Spirit worked only in improvisation, as if he could not inhabit a sentence written on Tuesday for Sunday. But the informal service performs too; it performs casualness instead of ceremony. And its improvisations ossify into repetition anyway, as all human speech does — only without the safeguards, because the accidental liturgy was never tested against Scripture by anyone, at any point, on purpose. Both failures are the same failure. Both grieve the same Lord, who told a woman at a well that the Father seeks worshippers in spirit *and* in truth — not one tradition's half.

And it matters to say what order of question this is. How a church structures its worship — vestments or shirtsleeves, weekly table or quarterly, written prayers or free — is second-order ground; faithful churches have divided over it without dividing the faith, and the reader who lands elsewhere than I do remains my brother. What is not up for a vote is the fact underneath the question: the worship forms the worshipper. That claim is not high-church or low-church. It is what repetition does to human beings, and Scripture assumes it on nearly every page.

## The hour with a shape in it

Because Scripture does not only command worship. Once, in the year a king died, it shows us the inside of the hour.

> In the year that King Uzziah died I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up; and the train of his robe filled the temple. Above him stood the seraphim. Each had six wings: with two he covered his face, and with two he covered his feet, and with two he flew. And one called to another and said: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory!" And the foundations of the thresholds shook at the voice of him who called, and the house was filled with smoke. And I said: "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!" Then one of the seraphim flew to me, having in his hand a burning coal that he had taken with tongs from the altar. And he touched my mouth and said: "Behold, this has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away, and your sin atoned for." And I heard the voice of the Lord saying, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" Then I said, "Here I am! Send me." (Isaiah 6:1–8, ESV)

Read it slowly and a shape appears. Not a mood. A sequence. First the vision: God high and lifted up, holiness filling the room, and Isaiah does not open his mouth. Then, and only then, confession — and not because a worship leader suggested a reflective posture, but because that is what the sight of holiness does to an honest man; "woe is me" is not liturgical language he selected, it is what was left of him. Then God acts. The coal comes from the altar — the place of sacrifice — carried to lips that could never have cleansed themselves, and the words over the burning are pure gift: guilt taken away, sin atoned for. And only after all of that does the Word come with its question, and only a forgiven man can answer it. Approach. Confession. Cleansing. Commission.

The order is not decoration. It is the gospel, walked through in sequence, and it cannot be rearranged without preaching a different gospel. Put commission before cleansing and you have works. Put confession without cleansing and you have despair. Skip confession altogether and you have the religious pep rally — which is, if I am honest about the services I have built, the rearrangement my own tradition drifted into: adoration straight to commission, glory to application, with nothing in between about the unclean lips, because the middle movements slow the show down. The historic shape of Christian worship — the call, the praise, the confession, the assurance of pardon, the Word, the table, the sending — is not Catholic furniture that Bible churches are free to leave on the curb. It is Isaiah's hour, formalized by people who noticed that God had shown us one. And notice the deepest thing about it: from the coal onward, Isaiah is mostly receiving. Worship in this chapter is not something the man performs for God's review. It is something God does to the man, who leaves the room commissioned, cleansed, and not the same. An hour like that is not the warm-up for anything. It is the event.

## What surfaces at the end

Fifteen years of shaping one congregation's worship has taught me things no book did, and the plainest is this: I have learned what my people actually retained, because I have sat with them while they died. At a hospital bed, no one has ever recited one of my sermon outlines to me. I say that with no bitterness; I could not recite most of them myself. What surfaces at the end is what was repeated. The Lord's Prayer. The twenty-third Psalm in the old words. The second verse of a hymn sung four hundred times across a lifetime, rising intact through a mind that dementia had otherwise emptied. The repeated things go down into a person the way rain goes into ground, and they are what remains when everything performed and everything explained has washed away. I spent my early ministry curating novelty — the fresh series, the new song, the element nobody expected — because I thought repetition was the enemy of life. My people's deaths have corrected me. Repetition is not the enemy of sincerity. Repetition is the soil sincerity grows deep in.

Scripture knew. When Moses set out how the love of God would survive in Israel, he did not commission an experience. He prescribed a rhythm: "And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:6–7, ESV). Sitting, walking, lying down, rising — the whole turning day, claimed by recitation. And when Paul told a young church what its singing was for, he did not mention atmosphere: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God" (Colossians 3:16, ESV). Teaching and admonishing. The songs are the curriculum too — often the most effective course in the building, because nobody hums the sermon on Tuesday. A congregation will believe what it sings long after it has forgotten what it heard, which means the person who chooses the songs is one of the most consequential theologians in the church, whether or not anyone told them so.

## Reading your own service

The counsel here is not a template, and it is certainly not a migration. There is a well-worn traffic pattern among the liturgically restless — the low-church man who discovers candles and cannot stop talking about them, the high-church man who discovers guitars and calls it freedom — and much of that traffic is style shopping wearing theology's clothes. The grass is not greener in the other tradition. It is greener where the liturgy is examined.

What I can hand you is the way of seeing — the question I have learned to carry into my own sanctuary, counterpart to the one I carry toward the machine: not *did I get anything out of it*, but *what is this hour rehearsing, and who is it making us?* A service can be read like any curriculum. What gets the most minutes, because the minutes are the emphasis. What is never said — whether a person could attend for a year and never once confess sin, never hear that it was atoned for, never be reminded that the poor exist. Where the offering sits, and whether it is worship or interruption. Who acts first in the room's implied story — a God who comes for his people, or an audience that came for a product. A church can keep its guitars and recover the spine.

I no longer stand at the door hoping to hear that people got something out of it. I stand there knowing they did — everyone did, every week, in every church, because the hour always delivers its curriculum, examined or not. The feed will resume its own liturgy in the parking lot; it has the week, and it knows exactly what it is rehearsing. The church has the hour. We keep asking whether the service was good. The older question — the one Isaiah's chapter and Justin's letter and my congregation's deathbeds keep asking back — is what the service is doing. Because it is doing it now, to you, to me, to the child in the third row learning what worship is from the order nobody chose on purpose.

What we repeat is what we become. The service this Sunday will keep that promise, one way or the other. It always has.
