A question has been quietly circulating among researchers who study these things, and the answers have grown alarming enough to make headlines. The question is simple: how many close friends do you have — people you could call at two in the morning, people who know the unflattering version of your life and stay anyway? Across the last few decades the number has been falling, and not gently. A growing share of adults now report having no close friends at all. Zero. Not few. None. We have more ways to reach each other than any humans who ever lived, and we are lonelier than our grandparents were on farms ten miles from the nearest neighbor.

Ask a roomful of adults — especially men — when they last made a genuinely new close friend, and watch the room get uncomfortable. For many it was college. For some it was high school. The honest ones will tell you they are running on friendships formed twenty years ago and slowly thinning, that the calendar fills with work and family and the maintenance of a life, and that friendship is the thing that gets fitted into the gaps, when there are gaps, which there increasingly are not. We do not decide to abandon our friends. We just stop having time, and then we stop having friends, and then one day we notice the silence.

Part of what makes this so hard to fix is that we have quietly demoted friendship in our hierarchy of loves. Romantic love is serious — we build holidays, industries, and entire identities around it. Family love is serious — obligatory, foundational, the thing you are supposed to prioritize. But friendship? Friendship is the optional one. The nice-to-have. The love you are allowed to let slide when life gets full, because nobody promised anybody anything and no vow gets broken when a friendship simply fades.

**I want to argue that this demotion is a historical accident and a spiritual catastrophe — that friendship was once understood, by Christians especially, to be one of the highest and most God-revealing loves available to a human being, and that recovering it is not a lifestyle upgrade but something close to a recovery of our humanity.** We did not lose friendship because friendship stopped mattering. We lost it because we stopped believing it mattered, and then arranged our lives to prove ourselves right.

## The strangest of the loves

C.S. Lewis saw the demotion coming and named it in *The Four Loves* (1960). He took the old practice of distinguishing the loves — affection, friendship, romantic love, and charity — and noticed something about the modern world's treatment of friendship in particular: we no longer take it seriously, and the ancients would have found that incomprehensible. To the ancient world, Lewis observed, friendship was the crown of the loves, the one the great thinkers wrote treatises about, the love that was thought to be the most freely chosen and therefore the most fully human. To us it has become "the least natural of loves," the one our biology least requires and our culture least honors.

Lewis put his finger on why friendship feels so optional. The other loves, he said, are about the people themselves — lovers face each other, absorbed in one another. Friends, by contrast, stand side by side, looking together at something beyond them both — a shared truth, a common quest, an interest, a faith. Friendship is born, Lewis said, in the moment one person says to another, "What? You too? I thought I was the only one." It is two people who have discovered they are walking toward the same thing, and find the walking better for the company. And precisely because it is about something outside the pair, it asks for no vows, produces no offspring, secures no household. It is gloriously useless — and that uselessness, Lewis argued, is the whole point. Friendship is the love least entangled with survival, which is exactly why it most resembles the way the angels love, and the way God's people will love in the end. It is a foretaste, not a leftover.

But that same uselessness is its vulnerability. Because friendship secures nothing and demands nothing, it is the first love to be sacrificed to a busy life. Nobody schedules a custody battle over a lost friendship. Nobody files papers. It just goes quiet. And a culture that organizes everything around productivity and family and romance will, almost without deciding to, starve the one love that produces nothing and binds no household together.

## The monk who called it the highest thing

To feel how far we have fallen, you have to meet a man who would have found our whole arrangement bewildering. Aelred of Rievaulx was a twelfth-century English abbot, and somewhere around 1160 he wrote *Spiritual Friendship*, a book that takes friendship more seriously than nearly anything written since. Aelred was a monk. He had given up romantic love and family entirely. And he did not experience this as an absence of intimacy, because for Aelred friendship *was* intimacy of the highest order, and the monastery was a school of it.

Aelred made a claim that should startle us. He took the line from the first letter of John — "God is love" — and dared to rephrase it. One might almost say, he wrote, that God is friendship. He believed that the bond of true friendship, two souls knit together in the love of God and the pursuit of the good, was not a distraction from the spiritual life but a road straight into it. Christ himself, Aelred taught, comes to dwell in the love between friends. When two friends pray together, encourage each other toward holiness, and love each other in God, Christ makes a third between them. Friendship, for Aelred, was not below the spiritual life. It was a primary form of it.

We have lost the capacity to even hear this. A monk telling us that friendship is a path to God, that Christ lives in the bond between friends, that to love a friend well is to do something holy — it sounds to our ears like an exaggeration, a sweetness, perhaps a little embarrassing. That reaction is the measure of our impoverishment. Aelred would say we have simply forgotten what friendship is, mistaking the thin networking of contacts for the thing he meant. He meant a love that wills the friend's good at cost to yourself, that knows the friend deeply and is known, that walks beside the friend toward God for a lifetime. He thought there was almost nothing higher a human being could do.

Aelred drew much of this from Augustine, who had felt the weight of friendship more sharply than almost anyone. In the *Confessions*, written around 397, Augustine describes the death of a close friend from his youth and writes some of the most devastating lines about grief in all of literature — that his eyes searched for his friend everywhere and could not find him, that he had become a great riddle to himself, that he wondered how he could go on living when half his soul had been torn away. Augustine was not exaggerating for effect. He had loved a friend that completely, and the loss nearly undid him. But Augustine also saw the danger in his own grief: he had loved the friend as though the friend were eternal, had rested in a mortal man the weight that belongs only to God, and when the man died the whole structure collapsed. His mature counsel was not to love friends less but to love them in God — to love them so that their mortality does not destroy you, because the friendship is held inside a love that does not die. This is exactly what Aelred meant by Christ being the third between friends. He is the one who keeps the friendship from becoming an idol and keeps the friend from becoming a god, which is the only thing that finally makes it safe to love a fellow mortal with your whole heart.

## What Scripture thinks of it

Aelred was not inventing. He was reading his Bible, and the Bible takes friendship far more seriously than we tend to notice, because we read past it. Consider the most famous friendship in Scripture: David and Jonathan, in 1 Samuel 18. The text says Jonathan's soul "was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul." Jonathan was the king's son, the heir to the throne, and David was the man who would take that throne from him — and Jonathan loved him anyway, protected him, warned him, gave him his own royal robe and sword. Jonathan loved his friend more than he loved his own crown. When Jonathan died, David's lament called their friendship a love surpassing even the love of women. The Bible records this without embarrassment, as one of the great loves of the story.

The book of Proverbs returns to it again and again. "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity" (Proverbs 17:17). The friend is the one who stays in the season when staying costs something — the at-all-times being the whole weight of the verse, the love that does not thin out when you stop being useful or fun. And Ecclesiastes, that bracingly honest book, makes the practical case with no romance at all: "Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil. For if they fall, one will lift up his fellow. But woe to him who is alone when he falls and has not another to lift him up. And though a man might prevail against one who is alone, two will withstand him. A threefold cord is not quickly broken" (Ecclesiastes 4:9–12). The Preacher is not being sentimental. He is being realistic about a fragile life. You will fall. The question is whether anyone will be there to lift you, and the answer to that question is decided years before you fall, by whether you built the friendships while you still had the time.

## The friendship that defines the word

And then Jesus does the thing that should settle the matter forever. On the last night, hours from the cross, he turns to the men who have walked with him for three years and reaches for the highest word he can find to describe what he is about to do for them — and the word he reaches for is friend. "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends. You are my friends. No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you" (John 15:13–15).

Sit with the promotion in that. They could have remained servants — and given who he was, servant would have been a more than generous standing. He refuses it. He calls them friends, and he defines the word in the same breath by the cross he is walking toward. The greatest love, he says, is to lay down your life for your friends, and he is about to prove it is not a figure of speech. Christianity does not treat friendship as the lesser love. It puts friendship at the very center, makes it the name Jesus gives his own disciples, and defines its highest form as the laying down of a life. The God who could have kept us at the distance of servants drew us in and called us friends. To take friendship lightly, after that, is to misunderstand the gospel.

## What it costs to get it back

So why don't we have it? Not because we don't want it. Because friendship of this kind cannot be had cheaply, and we have built lives engineered for cheapness. Aelred's friendship and Jonathan's friendship and the friendship Jesus names all require the one thing our culture has the least of: unhurried, repeated, undefended time. You cannot become someone's two-in-the-morning friend over coffee twice a year. The knitting of soul to soul that Scripture describes happens slowly, through ordinary presence — through being there, again and again, when nothing dramatic is happening, until the day something dramatic does and you are already in the room.

That is the cost, and it is why we keep declining to pay it. Deep friendship is inefficient. It does not advance your career, build your household, or fit neatly into a calendar already full. It asks for time you have allocated elsewhere, and it offers no contract guaranteeing a return. Every incentive in a productive, mobile, screen-saturated life pushes against it. Which means recovering friendship is not a matter of feeling differently. It is a matter of arranging your life differently — of deciding, against the grain of everything, that this useless and glorious love is worth real hours, repeated for years, with no promised payoff except the friend.

The lonely number is climbing because we keep choosing the things that crowd friendship out, and each choice is reasonable, and the sum of the reasonable choices is a person at midlife with a full calendar and no one to call at two in the morning. The God who calls us friends built us for the love we keep deferring. We were never meant to fall with no one to lift us up.

A threefold cord is not quickly broken. But someone has to take the time to braid it, and that someone is you, and the time is now, while you still have it.
