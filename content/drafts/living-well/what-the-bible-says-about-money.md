> [PERSONAL STORY — James to supply: a concrete moment with money — a time you gave when it cost you, or a time you caught your own grip tightening around what you had. Drop it in after the third paragraph, before "Start with the scale of it."]

Open your banking app right now and look at the number. You feel something. You don't have to name it, but it moves through you — relief, or anxiety, or a small flush of pride, or that low hum of not-enough that follows so many of us around like a draft under a door. Notice that the number produced a feeling at all. That is the first piece of evidence in this essay. Money is never just a number. It is a place where the heart is exposed, and we know it, which is exactly why we would rather not talk about it.

We will talk about almost anything else first. Surveys of churchgoers turn up the same finding over and over: people would sooner have their pastor preach about sex, politics, or their own dying than about what they earn and spend. We treat a person's finances as the last truly private thing, more private than their marriage, more private than their faith. And the church has largely agreed to the arrangement. We tithe sermons on prayer and grace and miss the subject our own Lord could not stop returning to.

Because here is the fact that should reorganize the whole conversation. Jesus spoke about money and possessions more than he spoke about heaven and hell combined. Roughly one out of every ten verses in the Gospels touches it. Of the parables he told, a striking share turn on coins, debts, wages, harvests, treasure, and stewards. The subject we find most awkward is the subject he found most urgent. That gap is worth sitting in.

**Here is what I want to argue across this essay: the Bible does not condemn money and it does not bless the love of money — it treats wealth as a genuine good, a serious danger, and a thing you cannot finally serve alongside God, all at once, and the whole of the Christian life with money is learning to hold those three truths together without collapsing into either the guilt of the ascetic or the ease of the comfortable.** The reason we get money wrong is almost always that we have grabbed one of those three and dropped the other two.

## Start with the scale of it

Craig Blomberg, the New Testament scholar, wrote the book I wish every Christian would read on this, *Neither Poverty Nor Riches* (1999). The title is its thesis. Blomberg walked the entire Bible — Genesis to Revelation, law and prophets, Gospels and letters — looking for what Scripture as a whole says about material possessions, and he found something more textured than either the prosperity preacher or the guilt-ridden ascetic wants to hear.

The prosperity gospel says wealth is a sign of God's favor; get your faith right and the money will follow. Blomberg shows this is a distortion of a real biblical thread — the Old Testament does often connect covenant faithfulness with material blessing — torn loose from everything that qualifies it. The opposite error, the one common among earnest younger Christians, says money is dirty, ownership is suspect, and the holy thing is to have as little as possible. Blomberg shows this too crashes against the text. Abraham was rich. Job was rich, lost it, and was given more. The early church included people of means whose homes and resources made the whole movement possible. Scripture never says, simply, that wealth is evil.

What it says is harder. Wealth is a good gift and a spiritual danger in the same breath, and the danger rises in direct proportion to the gift. The richer you are, the more carefully you must watch your soul, because the thing that can fund a hundred generosities can also quietly become the thing you trust instead of God. Blomberg's conclusion, drawn from the whole canon, is that the biblical ideal is neither poverty nor riches but a life of enough — sufficiency held with open hands, surplus moving outward toward need. "Give me neither poverty nor riches," prayed the writer of Proverbs, "lest I be full and deny you, or lest I be poor and steal." The middle is not lukewarm. The middle is the dangerous, faithful place where you can actually see God clearly.

## Why Jesus called it a rival god

Now the sharpest thing Jesus said, and he said it without softening. "No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money" (Matthew 6:24). Read that slowly. He does not say you should not serve both. He says you cannot. It is not permitted because it is not possible. The two make total claims, and a heart cannot bow to two thrones.

Notice the word he chose. Not money as a tool, money as a master. The Greek word behind it, mammon, names wealth treated as a power — something with a will, something that wants you. Jesus personifies it deliberately, and the twentieth-century French thinker Jacques Ellul built much of his writing on money around that choice. Ellul, in *Money and Power*, argued that Scripture treats money not as a neutral instrument but as a spiritual force with a tendency, a gravity, a pull toward becoming an object of devotion. Money is never just sitting there. It is always recruiting. It promises security, status, freedom, the ability to insulate yourself from the chances and changes of a fragile life — and those promises are precisely the things only God can finally make good on. That is why it functions as a rival god and not merely a temptation. It offers salvation.

Ellul's answer to a power is not management but desecration. You break the spell of money, he said, by giving it away — specifically by giving in a way the world finds irrational, giving without return, giving where no advantage comes back to you. The act of generosity is not mainly about helping the poor, though it does. It is about profaning the false god, proving with your bank account that the thing does not own you. You demonstrate money is not your master by treating it the way you would treat a defeated power: with a freedom that shocks it.

This is why the New Testament's instructions about money so often sound less like financial advice and more like spiritual warfare. Jesus tells a rich young ruler to sell everything — not because possessions are evil, but because he can see the man cannot let go, and what cannot be let go has become a god. He warns that it is harder for a rich man to enter the kingdom than for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle, and the disciples are rightly stunned, because they had assumed, as we assume, that wealth was a sign of being on God's good side. He keeps returning to the subject not because he is preoccupied with our wallets but because he can see, with terrible clarity, that the wallet is where the heart goes to hide. Show me what you do with your money in the dark, he is effectively saying, and I will show you what you actually worship.

## The man who had a great year

Jesus told a story about exactly this, and it is the most uncomfortable parable for a comfortable people. A rich man's land produced an abundant harvest — note that nothing in the story says he cheated or oppressed anyone; he simply had a very good year. He thought to himself: I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones, and store up all my grain, and say to my soul, soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry. And God said to him: "Fool. This night your soul is required of you, and the things you have prepared, whose will they be?" (Luke 12:13–21).

Sit with what is and is not condemned. The man is not called a fool for being rich, or for working hard, or even for saving. He is called a fool for one thing: he built his entire security around what he could store, and he forgot he was mortal and forgot he was not alone in the world. Every verb in his plan is singular and reflexive — I, my, myself, my soul. There is no neighbor in his sentence and no God in his future. He had enough to bless a county and he could imagine nothing to do with it but cushion himself. The terror of the parable is how reasonable he sounds. His financial plan would pass any advisor's review. It failed the only audit that counts, and he never saw it coming because nobody around him would have called it failure either. We would have called it a retirement plan.

This is the danger Blomberg keeps naming. Not that wealth is sin, but that wealth anesthetizes — it makes the soul drowsy, it lets you mistake insulation for security, it quietly removes God and neighbor from your imagined future until you wake up rich and alone and surprised.

## The clearest verse, and the way we misquote it

Now the verse everybody knows and almost everybody gets wrong. People say money is the root of all evil. Paul did not write that. He wrote: "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (1 Timothy 6:10). The difference is the entire doctrine. Not money — the love of it. Not the thing — the grip. And read the verses around it, because they hold the cure inside the warning. "Godliness with contentment is great gain," Paul says. "We brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of it. But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content" (1 Timothy 6:6–8).

Contentment. That is the word the love of money cannot survive, and the word our entire economy is engineered to prevent you from ever feeling. The whole machinery of modern advertising is a sustained campaign against the verse "with these we will be content." It must be, because contentment is bad for sales. A person who has decided they have enough has stepped off the wheel, and the wheel needs you on it. Paul's point is that the way out of money's grip is not earning more but wanting differently — a settledness in God that simply stops the engine of acquisition, because the hunger it ran on has been fed somewhere else.

## What generosity actually does

So what do you do with money, practically, if all this is true? The New Testament's answer is consistent and a little startling: you give, and you give in a way that feels like worship rather than tax. "Whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and whoever sows bountifully will also reap bountifully. Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:6–8).

Hear what Paul does not say. He does not say give until it hurts and grit your teeth. He says God loves a cheerful giver — not a grim one, not a guilted one, a cheerful one. And the cheer is not a duty you fake. It is the natural fruit of believing what we have been saying all along: that money is a defeated power, that God is your real security, that you brought nothing in and will take nothing out, and so the open hand is simply the honest hand. The miser is not bad so much as confused; he is clutching something he cannot keep as though it could save him from something it cannot touch. The cheerful giver has seen through the spell. His generosity is not heroism. It is sanity.

This is why the early church gave the way it did, holding possessions loosely, meeting need without bookkeeping, treating what they owned as on loan rather than owned outright. They were not better people than we are. They had simply caught sight of what money is, and what it is not, and acted accordingly. They had stopped serving the rival god, and the first symptom of that freedom, every time, is an open hand.

There is a practical wisdom buried in all of this that the giving Christian discovers and the hoarding one never does. Generosity, practiced regularly, is the most reliable cure for the anxiety money produces — not because giving makes you richer, but because the act itself proves to your own frightened heart that you are not finally dependent on the number in the account. Every gift is a small rehearsal of trust. Every time you let real money go toward a real need without calculating the return, you teach yourself again that the rival god is not your master and that the true God can be relied upon. People who tithe will tell you this, and they are not pretending: the giving did not make them poorer in any way they can feel, and it loosened something in them that hoarding had clenched. The grip relaxes from the giving side. You cannot reason your way out of the love of money. You can only give your way out, one open-handed act at a time, until the fear that drove the clutching has quietly lost its hold.

## Holding the three together

Come back to the three truths I promised at the start, because the whole essay has been an argument that you must hold all three at once. Wealth is a genuine good — Scripture never apologizes for it, and you need not pretend that having is sin. Wealth is a serious danger — the more of it you hold, the more vigilantly you must watch your heart, because the gift and the trap are the same object. And money is a power you cannot serve alongside God — it wants the throne, and there is only one throne, and you will have to decide, not once but daily, who sits on it.

Drop any one of the three and you fall into a recognizable error. Drop the goodness and you get the guilt-ridden Christian who treats every dollar as contamination. Drop the danger and you get the prosperity gospel, baptizing greed and calling the bigger barn a blessing. Drop the rivalry and you get the comfortable believer who has quietly seated money beside God on the throne and tells himself there is room for two. There is not. There never was.

So open the app again and look at the number, and let it expose you the way it always does, but this time ask the better question. Not how much do I have, and not how much do I need, but who am I serving — because the number was never the point. The number was only ever a mirror.

You cannot serve both. He told us that plainly, more often than he told us almost anything. The mercy is that the master we keep choosing was always going to fail us, and the one we keep refusing was always going to be enough.
