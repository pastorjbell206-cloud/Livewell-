Some years ago I went back through my old sermon notes looking for something I no longer remember, and I found something I had never once noticed while writing them. The illustrations. Year after year of them — my wife, my sons, the dinner table, the minivan, the argument at bedtime about brushing teeth. If you reconstructed my congregation from my sermons alone, you would conclude that every adult in the room went home to a full house. On any given Sunday, a third of them went home to an empty one. I had been preaching to my own kitchen for fifteen years and calling it application. And no one ever complained — which is the detail that should have alarmed me most, because it means the single members of my church had learned, long before I stood up, that church is a place where other people's lives are the examples.

They are not a niche. In 1960, roughly seven in ten American adults were married; by 2010, Pew Research Center found the share had fallen to barely half, and it has not recovered. Add together the never married, the divorced, and the widowed, and the unmarried are the fastest-growing presence in the American pew — and the American church, especially my wing of it, has spent those same decades building as if the household were the unit of membership. The nursery wing, the youth wing, the marriage retreat, the family camp, the sermon series every February. We called ourselves a family of families and never heard what the phrase said to the person who walked in alone.

**So this essay stands on a claim I have no natural right to make and every scriptural obligation to: the New Testament does not treat singleness as a waiting room, a consolation prize, or a problem God has been asked to fix. It calls the unmarried life good — Paul's own word — a calling with its own content, kept to the end by the Lord of the church and by the apostle who planted half of it. A church that cannot say this plainly has not overvalued marriage. It has misread its own book. And I write this as a man with a wedding ring and five sons — which is exactly why it needs writing, and exactly why you should watch my hands while I write it. The people who built the family-shaped church were people shaped like me.**

## The chapter married preachers skip

Paul takes up the question directly, in a letter to a church that had asked him about marriage. His answer has been read aloud at almost no weddings, for reasons that will become obvious.

> I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another. To the unmarried and the widows I say that it is good for them to remain single, as I am. (1 Corinthians 7:7–8, ESV)

Sit with the first sentence before the qualifications arrive. *I wish that all were as I myself am.* Paul is unmarried, and he wishes everyone were. Not tolerates it. Not makes pastoral peace with it. Wishes it — for the whole church. If a preacher stood up on a Sunday and said those words as his own, his elders would meet with him by Thursday. Paul says them as Scripture. And the word he chooses for the unmarried state is the word the family-centered church has never quite managed to say without a comma after it: *good*. Not good-for-now. Not good-until. Good — the same verdict God pronounced over creation.

Later in the chapter he explains why, and this is the passage that gets skipped hardest, because it does not merely permit singleness. It ranks it.

> I want you to be free from anxieties. The unmarried man is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But the married man is anxious about worldly things, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided. And the unmarried or betrothed woman is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to be holy in body and spirit. But the married woman is anxious about worldly things, how to please her husband. I say this for your own benefit, not to lay any restraint upon you, but to promote good order and to secure your undivided devotion to the Lord. (1 Corinthians 7:32–35, ESV)

Read it without flinching. The unmarried person is anxious about the things of the Lord; the married person's interests are *divided* — and Paul does not mean that as a rebuke of the married, because the division is right and owed. A husband who was not anxious to please his wife would be sinning against her. That is precisely the point. Marriage is a good that divides you, lawfully and necessarily, and singleness is a good that leaves the whole self available for undivided devotion. On the one axis Paul actually measures — freedom for the things of the Lord — the advantage runs toward the single. Fifteen centuries of family-pew Christianity has read this chapter upside down: marriage as the norm requiring no defense, singleness as the exception requiring an explanation. The chapter itself argues the reverse. If either estate has to justify itself before this text, it is not the unmarried one.

Paul is careful, and honesty requires tracking his care. He commands no one; "each has his own gift from God" — marriage one gift, singleness another, neither the default issue of being human. He is blunt that celibacy without self-control is a fire hazard: "For it is better to marry than to burn with passion" (1 Corinthians 7:9, ESV). And he says he intends "not to lay any restraint upon you." The chapter is not anti-marriage; the man who wrote Ephesians 5 cannot be. But the direction of its argument is unmistakable, and we have spent a very long time preaching it as if it ran the other way.

## The strongest case against me

The family-centered reading of Scripture is not stupid, and it deserves its best form before any verdict. It runs like this. The first command God gives humanity is "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, ESV), and the first problem God names in creation is solitude: "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him" (Genesis 2:18, ESV). Marriage is a creation ordinance, older than Israel, older than the fall. The household is the Bible's ordinary school of faith — Deuteronomy 6 assigns the teaching of the children to the home, not the temple. The Psalms call children a heritage from the Lord and picture blessing as a table ringed with olive shoots. And when Paul commends singleness, he flags his own context: "in view of the present distress" (1 Corinthians 7:26, ESV) — counsel for a persecuted minority in a hostile empire, the argument goes, not a permanent ranking of estates. Scripture even warns, in 1 Timothy 4, against those who forbid marriage as a mark of false teaching. A defender of the family-shaped church would sign every sentence of that paragraph, and every sentence of it is true.

Two things it cannot do. It cannot make "not good that the man should be alone" mean "not good that the man should be unmarried" — because the Bible answers Adam's solitude twice, once with Eve and once with the church, and the second answer is the one the New Testament spends its pages building. And it cannot quarantine 1 Corinthians 7 inside a first-century emergency, because the grounds Paul actually gives in verses 32 through 35 are not crisis-grounds. They are devotion-grounds — anxieties, interests, the undivided heart — and they hold in a suburb as firmly as under Nero. When Paul says "the appointed time has grown very short" (1 Corinthians 7:29, ESV), he is not misjudging the calendar. He is naming the church's permanent address: between the resurrection and the return, every generation lives in the shortened time, and the unmarried life is one of the two faithful ways to keep house there.

And beneath both answers stands a fact the family-centered reading has never had a comfortable place for. Jesus of Nazareth — the one human being in whom our nature exists without defect, the man Chalcedon confesses as fully God and fully man — never married. If the unmarried life were a lesser or incomplete humanity, the incarnation would be its refutation. He was not waiting for his real life to begin. He was not half a person hoping to be completed at an altar. He is the standard of the completed human, and he lived and died single. The church can say marriage is good with its whole chest. What it cannot say, while looking at its own Lord, is that marriage is the norm from which singleness deviates.

## The single Savior and the dry tree

Jesus said so himself, in a passage nearly as unpreached as Paul's. When his teaching on divorce landed so hard that the disciples blurted out that maybe it is better not to marry at all, he did not correct them. He said something stranger:

> For there are eunuchs who have been so from birth, and there are eunuchs who have been made eunuchs by men, and there are eunuchs who have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven. Let the one who is able to receive this receive it. (Matthew 19:12, ESV)

Eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom — people who set marriage aside not because they failed to find it but because the kingdom claimed the space it would have filled. Jesus names a category of person the modern church barely has vocabulary for: not single-because-unlucky but single-because-called. And he had already redefined the family in a scene that scandalized his own. Told that his mother and brothers were outside asking for him, "looking about at those who sat around him, he said, 'Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother'" (Mark 3:34–35, ESV). The exclamation is his, not mine. Blood built the old family; the will of God builds the new one, and it is the only family Jesus ever calls his own in that scene. Later, pressed with a riddle about a much-married widow, he says plainly that marriage belongs to this age and not to the resurrection — the children of the age to come "neither marry nor are given in marriage" (Luke 20:34–36, paraphrased with the reference so you can weigh it yourself). Every marriage in your congregation is temporary. Every single believer in your congregation is living, now, in the estate the whole church will share forever. They are not behind the times. They are ahead of them.

None of this fell out of the sky. Seven centuries before, Isaiah had already heard God make a promise to the person the covenant community treated as its most obvious dead end — the eunuch, cut off from the one thing the ancient world counted as a future:

> Let not the foreigner who has joined himself to the LORD say, "The LORD will surely separate me from his people"; and let not the eunuch say, "Behold, I am a dry tree." For thus says the LORD: "To the eunuchs who keep my Sabbaths, who choose the things that please me and hold fast my covenant, I will give in my house and within my walls a monument and a name better than sons and daughters; I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off." (Isaiah 56:3–5, ESV)

A name *better* than sons and daughters. Not a consolation matching what the childless man lost — an exceeding of it, spoken to the man whose family tree ends with him. I have heard a thousand sermons on family legacy. I have preached my share. I cannot remember hearing this text preached once, and I know I never preached it, though I might have noticed that when Luke narrates the gospel's first great leap outward in Acts 8, the convert in the chariot is a eunuch, reading the scroll of Isaiah, a few columns from the promise written to him. The Bible kept honoring the people my sermon file forgot.

## Fifteen centuries, two ditches

The early church took all this literally enough to build institutions on it. Paul's instruction that widows over sixty be "enrolled" (1 Timothy 5:9) hardened into a recognized order — the unmarried not as a pastoral problem but as a roster, with duties and honor. By the year 251, a letter from Cornelius, bishop of Rome, preserved in Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History*, could report more than fifteen hundred widows and distressed persons supported by that one church's rolls. And the celibates were not the church's leftovers; in a Roman world where the family line was the empire's pension plan and childbearing a civic duty, the Christian who renounced marriage for the kingdom was a public scandal — a living claim that a person's future did not depend on producing heirs, because God had promised the dry tree a name.

Then the church drove into the first ditch. Honor for celibacy curdled into a caste system: the celibate as spiritual athlete, the married as the crowd in the cheap seats. When a monk named Jovinian argued, around 390, that marriage and virginity stood equal in merit before God, synods at Rome and Milan condemned him in 393 — and Jerome's treatise against him disparaged marriage so ferociously that Jerome's own friends tried to pull the book out of circulation. The medieval settlement that followed made celibacy mandatory for the clergy and marriage a concession to weakness, and it took the Reformation to break it. When Luther married Katharina von Bora in 1525 — an ex-monk marrying an ex-nun, to the delight of every pamphleteer in Europe — he was not lowering the church's standards. He was restoring Paul's actual sentence: each has his own gift.

But Protestants did not stop at the correction. Over four centuries we drove, slowly and with the best intentions, into the opposite ditch. Marriage went from despised to permitted to celebrated to assumed — until the family was no longer one calling among two but the shape of the church itself. The postwar suburban congregation finished the job: programming by household, discipleship routed through the nursery and the youth group, "family church" as a selling point, and the unmarried adult filed in a ministry named like a junk drawer. Singles ministry — as if the widows Paul enrolled and the eunuchs Jesus blessed were a demographic to be matched off. Two ditches, fifteen centuries apart, and the same error at the bottom of both: one estate crowned the norm and the other made to explain itself. The medieval church demanded a reason you would marry. We demand a reason you have not. Paul refused both demands in a single verse, and neither age could hear him.

I did not inherit this from a distance. I built it. My church calendar, my programming instincts, my sermon file full of minivans — a man with five sons constructing, in perfect sincerity, a church where his own life was the template. The father of five is exactly the wrong man to write this essay comfortably, which is why he has to write it uncomfortably or not at all.

## The culture's version of the same mistake

It would flatter the church to say the surrounding culture got this right, but the culture has simply dug the same hole from the other side. The secular script for singleness is not a waiting room; it is a showroom — the optimized self, the curated feed, the options kept permanently open, the app that renders other persons as inventory to be swiped through. It sounds like freedom and functions like weightlessness: a single life with no calling in it, answerable to nothing, pointed at nothing but its own refinement. And notice what this script shares with the church's script, because underneath they agree. Both assume the single life is about the self. The church says: the self on hold, waiting to be chosen. The culture says: the self as the project, endlessly under renovation. Paul says neither. His single person is not waiting and not self-curating; she is *anxious about the things of the Lord* — seized, occupied, spent on something. The Christian answer to the church that pities singles and the culture that flatters them is the same answer, delivered with the same instrument: the unmarried life is not an absence. It is a vocation with content.

## A calling with content

Name the content, because a calling with no substance is just a compliment. Devotion is the first — the undivided heart Paul describes, the whole self available to God without the lawful, honorable division a family requires. Availability is the second, and church history is largely a record of it: the missionaries, the scholars, the pastors of impossible parishes, the ones who could go because no one at home would be abandoned by their going, who could stay late in the crisis, give away the savings, take the assignment. My own divided interests are not a failure; they are my vows working. But I know what my vows cost the church in my availability, and the single believer's freedom is not what is left over from a marriage that failed to happen. It is a resource the kingdom has spent lavishly in every generation.

And the third is friendship — not as consolation, but as covenant. Around the 1160s, Aelred, the Cistercian abbot of Rievaulx, wrote *Spiritual Friendship*, a book by a celibate man that takes friendship more seriously than nearly anything written since. Aelred had renounced marriage and family entirely and did not experience this as exile from intimacy, because for him friendship was intimacy of the highest order — two souls knit in the love of God, with Christ himself making a third between them. He dared to say one might almost rephrase Scripture: God is friendship. Our age hears that as sentimentality. It is actually an indictment — of a church that has made marriage the only covenant it knows how to honor, so that every other love in the building floats, unpromised and unprotected. The single life Scripture commends was never meant to be a solitary life. It was meant to be lived inside a family that does not run on blood — the one Jesus pointed to when he looked around the room at those seated near him.

That family carries a claim the modern church has almost forgotten it makes. Stanley Hauerwas argued, in *A Community of Character* (1981), that Christian singleness is the church's boldest wager: a congregation that honors the unmarried is declaring that its future comes by witness and conversion, not by birthrate — that God, not biology, secures the church's tomorrow. Every honored single believer in a pew is that doctrine, standing up. And Sam Allberry — a pastor for whom singleness is not a season but the whole road, and who has written about it from inside a lifelong celibacy — puts the complementary claim in *7 Myths About Singleness* (2019): if marriage bears witness to the shape of the gospel, Christ's covenant love for his church, then singleness bears witness to its sufficiency — that Christ is actually enough for a whole human life. Both estates preach. A church with only married members has lost a sermon it cannot get from anyone else.

Hear what is not being said. Marriage is not lowered an inch by any of this; the house rule of this essay is that no calling gets softened to sharpen another. Scripture opens with a wedding in a garden and closes with one at the end of the age, and marriage is a mystery that refers to Christ and the church — I believe that, I preach it, I live inside it with gratitude. But the wedding at the end is the only one that lasts, and both estates are testimonies to it: marriage a portrait of that covenant, singleness a proof it can fill a life. Two gifts, Paul said. One of one kind and one of another. Neither is the default human.

## The people in the room

I have been writing about singleness as a doctrine, and no one lives inside a doctrine. In the rooms I pastor, the unmarried life has many faces, and a faithful church learns to meet each without diagnosing any. There are the contentedly single, who mostly need the church to stop treating their contentment as denial — to stop auditing their peace for the sadness it assumes must be underneath. There are the painfully lonely, and their ache is real and owed no minimizing: the empty side of the bed, the long Sunday afternoon, the absence of one person whose whole job is to know you. Genesis is still true — it is not good for a human being to be alone — but the New Testament's answer to that verse is bigger than matchmaking, and if a single believer is alone inside a congregation, the church is failing its own doctrine of the household of God; the single person is not failing theirs. There are the divorced, carrying a grief with a verdict stapled to it, who have learned that some churches treat a broken marriage as a contagious condition. There are the widowed — Paul's first addressees, the original enrolled order, too often thanked for their casseroles and forgotten by the calendar. And there are believers whose same-sex attraction has led them, at real and continuing cost, into a celibacy most married Christians have never priced for a single day — often inside churches that praise the sacrifice in the abstract while building no household in which a person could actually live it. Isaiah 56 was written to the one whose obedience costs the future everyone else takes for granted. A name better than sons and daughters. The church that will not make that promise audible has no business quoting the rest of the chapter.

And one sentence in this whole subject needs to be retired from married mouths, or earned before it is spoken. *Singleness is a gift.* Paul said it, and Paul could — he lived it to the end. When a man with a wedding ring says it from a stage and drives home to a loud table, the single person in the third row hears exactly what he means: a gift he is glad someone else received. I have said the sentence that way. I am the man in that sentence. The words are true, and in my mouth they were cheap, because a church earns the right to call singleness a gift only by becoming the kind of family in which it functions as one — where the unmarried are hosts and not perpetual guests, godparents and elders and not a demographic, woven into households and wills and Tuesday dinners, honored the way the church of Cornelius's Rome honored its fifteen hundred.

I will go home tonight to the full house. Five boys, a loud table, the life my sermons kept mistaking for everyone's. Somewhere in my congregation a woman my age will let herself into a quiet apartment, and the question this essay finally asks is not whether her life is whole. Scripture settled that; her Lord lived her estate, and his apostle called it good, and Isaiah promised her a name that outlasts my genealogy. The question is whether her church believes its own book — because he was thirty-three, unmarried, and no one's father, and if he walked into most of our lobbies we would welcome him warmly, hand him a card, and wonder quietly when he was going to settle down. The church that has no honored place for the unmarried has not overvalued marriage. It has undervalued Christ.

Two gifts, one of one kind and one of another. He kept the one we pity.
