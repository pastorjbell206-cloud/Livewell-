Listen to how we talk about our bodies now and you will hear a strange thing, repeated so often we no longer notice it. We say the soul is *trapped in* a body. We say we feel *like ourselves* on a good day, as if there were a self underneath the tired flesh waiting to get out. We say a person *has* a body the way they have a car, and we speak of upgrading it, hacking it, optimizing it, overriding its signals, and one day, the more ambitious among us hope, uploading the self off of it entirely and into something that does not age. The body, in all this talk, is equipment. The real you is the thing inside, looking out through the eyes as through a windshield.

We are the first people in history rich and medicated enough to half-believe we could pull it off — to live mostly in our heads, treat our bodies as inconvenient hardware, and feel faintly betrayed every time the hardware gets sick or tired or old. We have screens that let the mind roam anywhere while the body sits forgotten in a chair. We have a wellness industry built on the premise that the body is a problem to be managed by the superior, governing self. And underneath it all runs an assumption so deep we mistake it for common sense: that you are essentially a mind, a will, a spirit — and the body is the cage you happen to be doing your sentence in.

**Christianity says something the modern world finds almost impossible to believe, and I want to argue for it without flinching: your body is not a cage your real self is trapped inside. Your body is good, it was made on purpose, and it is not something you have — in some real sense, it is something you are.** This is not a side issue. It is one of the places where the Christian account of being human collides most directly with the air we breathe, and getting it wrong distorts nearly everything downstream — how we treat sex, suffering, aging, death, and one another.

## The old heresy wearing new clothes

What we are dealing with is not new. The early church faced it in its first centuries and named it for what it was. The Gnostics taught that the material world was a mistake or a prison, that flesh was low and spirit was high, and that salvation meant the enlightened spirit escaping the contamination of the body. Matter bad, spirit good. The point of religion was to get out.

The church fought this with everything it had, and the man who fought hardest was Irenaeus of Lyon in the second century. Irenaeus saw that Gnosticism was not a minor error but an attack on the whole gospel, because the whole gospel rests on God taking flesh and rising in flesh. If the body is a prison, then the Incarnation is a degradation and the resurrection is a step backward. If matter is evil, then the God who made it and called it good is either fooled or malicious. Irenaeus answered with a line that should be carved over the door of the modern wellness clinic: the glory of God is a human being fully alive — not a spirit liberated from a body, but a whole creature, flesh and breath together, functioning as made. He insisted that salvation includes the flesh, that God intends to redeem the body and not merely rescue a soul from it. The Gnostics wanted to be saved from creation. Irenaeus said God was busy saving creation itself.

We tend to file Gnosticism under ancient curiosities. It is the most contemporary heresy alive. Every time we speak of the body as something to escape, override, or upgrade past, we are Gnostics with better technology. Nancy Pearcey made exactly this case in *Love Thy Body* (2018), tracing how a quietly Gnostic assumption — that the real person is the inner self, and the body is raw material the self may use or alter or disregard at will — now underwrites huge swaths of how our culture thinks about sex, identity, and the beginning and end of life. The body, in this picture, carries no meaning of its own. It is just there, neutral stuff, awaiting the verdict of the sovereign will inside. Pearcey's point is that this is not the Christian view of the body. It is the ancient enemy of the Christian view, returned in modern dress, and the church has often absorbed it without noticing.

And the church absorbs it from both directions, which is what makes it so hard to see. On one side stands the secular version: the body is malleable equipment, and the inner self is free to use it, alter it, or discard its given signals however it wishes. On the other side stands a religious version that imagines itself the opposite but shares the same root — the suspicion that the body is the lowly part, that real spirituality is a matter of the soul, that the goal of faith is to get our spirits ready for a disembodied heaven while the flesh is just the temporary container we are glad to be rid of. Both treat the body as separable from the true self. Both, at bottom, are embarrassed by the flesh. And Scripture is embarrassed by neither, because Scripture knows where the flesh came from.

## Genesis says you were sculpted

So go back, as we keep having to, to the beginning. "Then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature" (Genesis 2:7). Read what that verse actually does. God does not create a soul and then issue it a body. God forms a body — sculpts it from the dirt, an image of intimate, hands-on craft — and then breathes life into it, and the result, the text says, is that the man *became* a living creature. Not the man *received* a soul that now lives in a creature. The man became one. Body and breath together make the living being. You are not a ghost piloting meat. You are dust that God decided to love and animate.

Notice also where the body comes from. The ground. The same stuff as the trees and the rivers and the animals. The Hebrew makes a pun of it — *adam* from *adamah*, the human from the humus, the earthling from the earth. Scripture is not embarrassed by this. It is the point. You are kin to the soil. Your body roots you into the good creation God made and called good six times before he ever made you. To despise the body is to despise the dirt God chose, the dust he stooped to shape, the creature he breathed into and pronounced very good. The cage you are so eager to escape is the masterpiece he is least embarrassed by.

## The scandal at the center

And then comes the claim that made Irenaeus's enemies recoil and ought to still stop us cold. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). Not the Word *appeared* to be flesh. Not the Word *used* a body as a disguise and discarded it later. The Word *became* flesh — took on a body with a digestive system and tear ducts and tired feet, a body that hungered and slept and bled and could be touched. The eternal God did not rescue us from embodiment. He entered it. He has a body now and will forever.

If you want to know what God thinks of the human body, this is the answer, and it is not subtle. He did not regard it as beneath him. He took one up and never set it down. The God who could have saved us as pure spirit, who could have beamed truth into our minds and left the flesh behind, instead chose a womb, a manger, a workbench, a cross, and a tomb that could not hold the body it was given. Every Gnostic instinct in us — that the spiritual is the real and the physical is the shabby — breaks against the manger. God thought a body was a fit place to be.

Notice, too, how much of Jesus's ministry happened through the body and to the body. He touched lepers when he could have healed them with a word, because the touch was part of the healing — the restoration of a man to the human contact disease had stolen from him. He spat in the dirt and made mud and put it on a blind man's eyes. He fed crowds with bread and fish, real food for real stomachs. He let a woman pour expensive oil over his feet and wipe them with her hair, and rebuked the man who called it a waste. After the resurrection, the first thing he did with the disciples was eat breakfast on a beach. None of this is incidental. A faith that thought the body was a problem would have a Savior who kept his distance from flesh. Ours could not stop touching it, feeding it, healing it, and finally sharing it.

## The body you will have forever

Here is where the modern Christian usually expects a comforting turn about the soul going to heaven when we die, the spirit at last set free from the flesh. But that is not the Christian hope. That is the Gnostic hope, smuggled into the church, and N.T. Wright has spent much of his career trying to pry it back out. The biblical hope is not the escape of the soul from the body. It is resurrection — the raising and remaking of the body itself.

Paul lays it out plainly. "It is sown a perishable body; it is raised an imperishable. It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:42–44). And mark the word "spiritual" there, because we read it wrong every time. Wright points out that "spiritual body" does not mean a body made of spirit, a ghost, a wisp. It means a body animated and ruled by the Spirit of God — a physical body, raised and transformed, more solid and more real than the one you have now, not less. The risen Jesus is the prototype: he could be touched, he ate fish, he bore the scars, and he was unmistakably, gloriously physical. That is your future, if you are his. Not liberation from your body. The redemption of it.

This changes how you hold your aging, your sickness, your scars, your fatigue. The body that fails you is not a doomed prison you are waiting to shed. It is the seed of the body you will have forever, sown now in weakness, to be raised in power. The wrinkles are not the end of the story. The wearing-out is real and grievous, and the gospel does not ask you to pretend otherwise — but it is the wearing-out of a thing destined for resurrection, not the slow release of a soul from a cage it was always too good for.

## Why this is not optional

So treat your body well, and I mean that in the full weight of the word treat. "Do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you, whom you have from God? You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:19–20). Paul is writing to people in a port city who thought, exactly as we do, that what you do with your body is spiritually irrelevant — that the body is a lower thing, neutral, your own business, separate from the real you. He answers that the body is a temple, that the Spirit of God lives in it, that it was bought at the cost of another body broken on a cross, and that therefore there is no such thing as a private, spiritually neutral use of the flesh. Glorify God in your body, he says. Not in spite of it. In it.

This is the hardest sell to a culture that has decided the body is just hardware. But notice what it gives back. If the body is a cage, then aging is pure loss, sex is just appetite, suffering is meaningless, and death is either a release or an outrage with nothing in between. If the body is good, made on purpose, indwelt, and destined for resurrection, then every one of those things changes. Your aching joints are part of you, and part of the you God is going to raise. Your sexuality is not a function to be managed but a part of an embodied self that means something. Your suffering happens to the real you and is gathered into the suffering of a God who also had a body and used it to save you. And your death is not the soul finally getting loose. It is the seed going into the ground.

It even changes the small things, the ordinary pleasures we have been half-taught to feel guilty about. The taste of good food, the warmth of sun on skin, the ache of muscles after honest labor, the comfort of a hand held, the deep animal relief of sleep — these are not distractions from the spiritual life or concessions to a lower nature. They are gifts given to a creature God made to receive them through a body he called good. The Gnostic feels a flicker of shame at bodily delight, as though the higher self should be above such things. The Christian, rightly taught, gives thanks. The same God who will raise your body is the one who gave it the capacity for pleasure in the first place, and to receive those pleasures with gratitude, in their proper place and order, is not a fall from spirituality. It is part of what it means to honor the Maker by loving what he made.

You have spent your life being told, in a hundred quiet ways, that you are a ghost in a machine, a self that happens to be saddled with a body, a mind that would be freer without the flesh. It is a lie, and it is an old one, and it has made us strangely miserable in our own skin. The truth is humbler and far better. You are dust that God loved enough to shape with his hands and breathe into with his own breath. You are a body he thought was worth becoming. You are a creature he intends to raise.

Your body is not a cage. It is the part of you he stooped down to touch — and the part of you he refuses to leave in the ground.
