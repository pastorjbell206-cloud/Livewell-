The first Christian I remember trying to convert me had a diagram. Two cliffs, a chasm between them, a cross laid across the gap like a footbridge — he drew it on a napkin with the speed of a man who had drawn it many times, and when he finished he looked up and asked whether I could think of any reason not to pray the prayer right now. I was an atheist. I could think of several. But what stayed with me was not the argument, because there had not really been one. What stayed was the feeling of being processed — the sense that somewhere behind his eyes a procedure was running, that I had stopped being a person to him around the second cliff, and that whatever he wanted from me had the shape of a quota. He was not a bad man. He was almost certainly braver than the hundreds of Christians who had moved through my life saying nothing at all. But I left that table further from God than I sat down at it, and it took me years to say why.

What eventually reached me never once felt like that table. It was a man who took my contempt without returning it, who conceded my strongest objections so plainly that I began to trust him with the weaker ones, who stayed in the friendship through years when I gave him no reason to hope it was going anywhere — and whose ordinary life, watched at close range for a long time, finally could not be accounted for by anything I believed. Nobody closed me. I was not a sale. I was a witness to something before I was ever a believer in it, and the witnessing did what the diagram could not.

So here is the confession this essay stands on. I have been a pastor for fifteen years, and I have taught the napkin. Early in ministry I stood in fellowship halls and trained volunteers in the conversational pivots, the diagnostic questions, the urgency applied on schedule — techniques I would have despised, and did despise, when they were aimed at me. I taught good people to do to their neighbors what had been done to me in that booth, because the notebook said it worked. I never asked the only question that mattered: worked on whom? Because the *whom* has changed. The country the notebook was written for is gone, and most of our evangelism has not noticed, and I helped not notice.

**The claim this essay makes is simple to state and long to absorb: for roughly fifteen centuries the church evangelized with home-field advantage — a surrounding culture that pre-taught the vocabulary, pre-aimed the guilt, and assumed the God, so that the evangelist's real work was calling half-believers home. That arrangement is over. Its end is not only loss, because much of what died was assent rather than faith. And the New Testament was written, every book of it, to churches that never had the advantage in the first place — which means the oldest playbook the church owns has suddenly come back into print.**

## Fifteen centuries at home

Put a date on the arrangement. In 313 the Edict of Milan made it legal to be a Christian in the Roman Empire; in 380 the Edict of Thessalonica made it official, and from Theodosius forward, across most of the West, to be born was to be handed the story along with your name. Christendom never meant that everyone believed. It meant that everyone knew what there was to believe — that the words *sin*, *grace*, *heaven*, and *judgment* arrived pre-loaded, that guilt came from the factory already aimed at a God whose existence was the one premise nobody had to argue. The culture did the church's catechesis. The parish register and the birth register were, for long stretches, the same book.

Evangelism under those conditions took a particular shape, and it was a reasonable shape for the conditions: revival. When George Whitefield preached to the miners at Kingswood in 1739, when Charles Finney set up the anxious bench in Rochester in 1830, when Billy Graham raised his tent in Los Angeles in 1949, the crowd arrived already knowing the story. They believed in the God they were ignoring. Their guilt had an address. The preacher's task was not to introduce anything; it was to close a sale the culture had spent centuries opening — and I do not use *close* with a sneer, because real grace moved through those tents and benches, and people I have buried were converted in them. But listen to what the era sang. The great invitation hymn of 1880 put the whole missiology in one word, repeated: *come home, come home*. That is evangelism with home-field advantage. It assumes the hearer has a home in this faith to come back to.

Nearly all of our inherited technique descends from that assumption. The tract, the crusade, the altar call, the four-step diagram on the napkin — each is a tool for finishing something the culture started. None of them was built for a hearer in whom the culture started nothing.

## The end of the arrangement

The numbers first, because they are concrete. When the General Social Survey first asked Americans about religion in 1972, about one in twenty claimed none. By 2021, Pew Research put the religiously unaffiliated at 29 percent of American adults — from a rounding error to nearly a third of the country inside my parents' lifetime. But the numbers understate the change, because the change is not chiefly in what people answer. It is in what the question assumes. Charles Taylor opened *A Secular Age* (2007) by asking why belief in God was nearly impossible to escape in 1500 and has become, by 2000, one option among many — and often the hardest one to hold. That is the ground shifting, not the crowd.

Which means the person across the fence from you is not who the notebook says. The notebook assumes a lapsed believer — someone who knows the story, feels its pull, and needs urgency. Your actual neighbor, increasingly, never had the story at all. It was not rejected in her. It was never told. What she has instead is a file: the scandals, the cover-ups, the televangelist, the political operation wearing a cross, the church that hurt someone she loves. She has heard everything about Christians and nothing of Christ, and evangelism built to press on guilt she does not carry, in a vocabulary she was never taught, toward a God she does not assume, reads to her as exactly what it felt like to me at that table — a procedure, running.

Now say the harder thing, the thing the panic keeps us from saying. Much of what died was not faith. It was assent. Christendom could fill a room; it never could fill a heart, and it produced, alongside its genuine saints, whole generations for whom the faith was a citizenship rather than a conversion. Søren Kierkegaard spent the last two years of his life, 1854 and 1855, savaging the Danish state church with precisely this arithmetic: when everyone is a Christian by census, Christianity has quietly ceased to exist. He was buried before the returns came in, but the returns came in. The collapse we grieve is real, and I grieve it — the shared moral grammar, the open doors, the funerals where everyone could still say the words. But a good portion of what collapsed was the pretense, and the pretense was never ours to keep. We did not lose a Christian nation. We lost the assumption that we had one, and those are different losses, and only one of them is worth the mourning we have spent.

## Two reflexes, one grief

A church stripped of its advantage reaches, in my experience, for one of two consolations, and I have preached both, so what follows is diagnosis from inside the ward.

The first reflex is volume. If the culture no longer delivers the crowd, manufacture the draw — better production, better marketing, a service engineered like a show and an outreach engineered like a funnel, the gospel repackaged as a product and the neighbor recast as a market. I sat in the conferences. I bought the campaign kits. I once measured a season of my ministry in response cards, and it took me an embarrassing number of years to hear what every unbeliever hears in all of it instantly, because I had heard it myself and forgotten: being marketed to does not feel like being loved. It feels like being wanted *for* something — my attendance, my yes, my slot in the report. The unbeliever's radar for this is not cynicism. It is discernment, and it is usually correct, and the technique confirms the file it was deployed to overcome.

The second reflex is quieter and wears better clothes: silence dressed as humility. Preach the gospel always, use words when necessary — a line Francis of Assisi almost certainly never said, doing more work in the modern church than most verses of Scripture. We tell ourselves the silence is respect. Some of it is; manipulation is a real sin and the fear of committing it is not dishonorable. But I can testify from the far side of the fence that the silent Christians in my unbelieving years did not feel respectful to me. They felt absent. The loud ones treated me as a target; the quiet ones left me alone with my conclusions, and between the salesman and the mute, an unbeliever can pass an entire American life without once being loved in the direction of God.

Name both reflexes with the same instrument, because they are the same failure. The church that answers lost advantage with technique does not believe the gospel can draw without help; the church that answers with silence does not believe it can survive being spoken. Both are unbelief wearing a strategy. And both deserve the same grief rather than separate contempts, because I did not choose one from a menu of errors — I cycled through the pair, volume when I was confident and silence when I was tired, and neither season bore the kind of fruit that lasted.

## The playbook written for away games

Here is what the panic misses: the church's founding documents do not know anything about home-field advantage. Not one book of the New Testament was written inside a Christian culture. Every letter is addressed to a minority — small congregations in Corinth, Philippi, Ephesus, Rome, surrounded by neighbors who owed the gospel nothing, pre-taught no vocabulary, carrying no aimed guilt, assuming very different gods. When Paul stood before the Areopagus in Athens, he had no shared Scripture to cite, so he quoted their own poets back to them and started from an altar they had built to a god they could not name. The apostles never once evangelized a Christian country. They did not lose the advantage we lost. They built the church without it — which is why their letters read less like our notebooks every year and more like field manuals for exactly the ground we are standing on.

And we can watch the method working, one generation past the apostles, in a short letter almost nobody reads. The Epistle to Diognetus was written sometime in the second century by an author whose name is lost, to a pagan inquirer who wanted to know what this new people actually was. Read what the writer reaches for as his best evidence. Not miracles. Not numbers, which were still embarrassing. Not power, of which they had none. He describes how they live: indistinguishable from their neighbors in country, language, and dress, he says, yet living in their own cities as resident foreigners — every foreign land a homeland to them, every homeland a foreign land. They marry like everyone, he says, and bear children like everyone, but they do not discard their infants — in an empire where exposing a newborn was legal and unremarkable. They share their tables with anyone, but not their beds. They obey the laws and then exceed them. They are poor and make many rich. What the soul is in the body, he concludes, Christians are in the world. Honesty requires the caveat: this is an apology, a portrait polished for a skeptical reader, and the actual congregations surely fell short of it — congregations always do. But the polish tells you the strategy. When a second-century Christian wanted to commend the faith to an unbeliever, his exhibit was the church's manner of life, because it was the only exhibit he had. Tertullian, writing his *Apology* around 197, records what the watching pagans actually said about them — see how they love one another — and notes that the remark was made in astonishment, not admiration of doctrine. The early church did not argue its way into a hostile empire and then live the argument. The life came first, and the life was the argument, and the words explained the life to the people the life had unsettled.

## The bishop who came home

The man who saw all of this most clearly in our own time learned it by a strange route: he left Christendom for the mission field and came home to find the mission field had followed him. Lesslie Newbigin sailed for India in 1936 and spent nearly four decades there, a missionary and then a bishop in the Church of South India, planting the gospel in ground where it had never grown. In 1974 he retired and traveled home overland to England — and was stunned by what he found. The country that had sent him out was harder ground than the one he had worked. India had never been Christian; England had been Christian and was now something else — not ignorant of the gospel but inoculated against it, carrying just enough of the story to be immune to the telling of it. He spent his last two decades saying, to a church that mostly did not want to hear it, that the West was now a mission field — the hardest one on earth — and he did not say it from a lectern only. In his seventies he took the pastorate of a small, struggling congregation in inner-city Birmingham, because a man who believes the West is a mission field goes to the field.

His deepest answer came in *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989), and it arrives as a question first: how could anyone come to believe that the last word in human affairs belongs to a man hanging on a cross? What could make a claim like that credible in a culture that has filed it under private opinion? His answer: "the only hermeneutic of the gospel is a congregation of men and women who believe it and live by it." A hermeneutic is what makes a text legible — the frame that lets you read it as what it is. For fifteen centuries, the culture was the gospel's hermeneutic; the world around the text taught you how to read it before you ever opened it. That interpreter has retired. What remains — the only interpreter left, Newbigin insisted — is a community living as though the story were true, close enough to be watched. This is Diognetus again, sixty generations later. And notice what Newbigin was not saying, because both of our reflexes will try to claim him. He was not saying the church should perform better; the congregation he means is not an attraction but an argument, and arguments made of lives cannot be staged. And he was not counseling silence — the man preached, debated, and wrote to the end of his life. He was saying the words have lost their translator, and the church's shared life is the only translation the unbeliever can still read. The congregation does not replace the message. It makes the message legible.

## The letter to exiles

If the New Testament has an after-Christendom book, it is 1 Peter. The address alone tells you the situation: "To those who are elect exiles of the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1 Peter 1:1, ESV) — scattered households of believers across Asia Minor, socially marginal, politically powerless, already being slandered as enemies of the human race. Peter writes to people with no advantage of any kind, and his strategy for them is stated early: "Keep your conduct among the Gentiles honorable, so that when they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:12, ESV). Then, in the third chapter, he compresses the whole missiology of the powerless church into two verses that the modern church quotes at half strength:

> but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy, always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect, having a good conscience, so that, when you are slandered, those who revile your good behavior in Christ may be put to shame. (1 Peter 3:15–16, ESV)

Unfold it slowly, in order, because the order is the instruction. It begins in the heart, not the mouth: *honor Christ the Lord as holy*. Witness is downstream of worship, and the first audience of the sentence is the believer's own interior — before Peter says a word about the neighbor, he addresses the throne room of the person speaking, because an evangelist whose heart is not settled will reach for pressure to make up the deficit, and pressure is what pressure produces.

Then the verse makes an assumption so quiet we read past it: *to anyone who asks you*. Someone asks. Peter takes it for granted that the exile's life — hope held under slander, good rendered for evil, a household that does not run on the empire's fuel — will be strange enough to generate the question. The witness he describes is not an opening move. It is an answer. And this is the hinge on which everything I have written here turns, because I can tell you from the unbelieving side exactly what the difference feels like. An answer to a question I never asked arrived as an intrusion, however true it was; it taught me only that this person needed something from me. But the friend who finally told me why his life held together was answering a question I had asked — it had taken me years to ask it — and the same sentences that would have bounced off me in a parking lot landed like news, because by then I wanted to know. You cannot rush the question. You can only live the kind of life that raises it, and be unashamed when it comes.

Then the content: *a reason for the hope that is in you*. Not a worldview, not a rebuttal, not the napkin — hope, and hope located somewhere: *in you*. The thing to be explained is not an argument the Christian carries but a life the Christian is visibly living, which is why no technique can substitute for it; there is no method for explaining a hope you do not have.

And then the clause we trained ourselves to forget: *yet do it with gentleness and respect*. Peter puts the manner inside the command, not beside it. The word behind *respect* is the old word for fear — reverence — and whether Peter means reverence before God or before the conscience of the questioner, the effect is the same: no contempt survives it, no procedure survives it, nothing that treats the person as terrain survives it. This is not packaging. A gospel of unearned grace delivered as a conquest contradicts itself in the delivery; the manner is part of the message, and the hearer — I promise you, having been the hearer — reads the manner first. Verse 16 finishes the thought: the good conscience, the good behavior, the slander answered not by a counterattack but by a life the accusation cannot be made to fit. The exiles' whole apologetic, like Diognetus's, like Newbigin's, stands or falls on how they live between the questions.

## What remains to be asked

I owe a word to the reader who does not believe any of this, because I know you are here — I read essays like this one for years, the way a man reads letters not addressed to him, checking whether the writer would tell the truth about people like me. So: you were right to resent the diagram. The feeling of being processed was not oversensitivity; something really was treating you as terrain, and it was not the gospel, and the parts of the church that learned evangelism from the sales floor owe you the apology I am attempting here. Nothing in this essay is a subtler technique aimed at you. It is, if anything, the dismantling of the techniques — an argument to my own people that you are not a market, not a quota, not a project with a closing date, and that any Christian who befriends you with a conversion deadline in mind has already misrepresented the faith he came to commend. What I hope you find among us is what I finally found: people whose kindness is not a strategy, who can hear your strongest objection without flinching or needing to win, and whose lives raise a question you are free never to ask. If you never ask it, the kindness was still real. That is the whole test of whether it was ever kindness at all.

And to the church: nothing here is a case for silence. The news must still be told — it is news; a watched life can raise the question, but no casserole ever explained an empty tomb, and the generation that mistakes its quietness for witness will comfort itself into extinction. What has changed is not whether we speak but where the speaking sits. It sits after love, inside trust, in answer to questions our common life has raised — which is to say it sits where it sat for the church's first three centuries, before the empire made it easy, back when the whole enterprise ran on nothing but the Spirit of God and the strangeness of the saints. The exiles Peter wrote to had no tents, no campaigns, no cultural memory to press on, and they buried the empire that slandered them. They did not have the advantage. They had a hope that required explanation.

That is the sentence I cannot get out from under, and I will not resolve it for you, because it does not resolve. Peter's instruction has a prerequisite no technique can fake: it only works on a church that is asked. The old arrangement asked on our behalf for fifteen centuries, and the old arrangement is dead, and no amount of volume will resurrect it, and no depth of silence will excuse us from the question it used to answer. I think of the man with the napkin more charitably now than I did then. He believed the chasm was real and could not bear to watch me walk toward it politely. What he lacked was not courage but a country that still did his catechesis for him — and that country is gone for good, which leaves the matter where it was in the year 155, where it was in Pontus and Bithynia, where it is on my street and yours tonight. The advantage is over. The question is whether anything about us is still strange enough — hopeful enough, gentle enough, unaccountable enough — to be asked about. My neighbors are not asking me the reason for my hope this week. Before I teach one more class on what to say, I have to sit with why.
