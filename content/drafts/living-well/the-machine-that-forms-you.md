Most weeks, somewhere between the sanctuary and the parking lot, a parent asks me about a phone. The question wears different clothes — what age, which app, how many hours — but it is always the same question, asked in the wrong category: as a technology question, the kind you settle with a setting. It is a discipleship question. They are not really asking me how to manage a device. They are asking me who is raising their child, and they already suspect the answer, and the answer is why they could not sleep.

I do not answer from a safe distance. I checked my phone this morning before I prayed. I have five sons, and I have caught myself telling one of them to put a screen down while holding mine, and the look he gave me was not defiance. It was arithmetic. He was counting the hours, and the count was not in my favor. Whatever I say here, I say as a man inside the problem, not above it.

And the problem has grown a voice. For fifteen years the machines curated — they chose what we would see and learned what would keep us looking. Then, in November 2022, a chatbot called ChatGPT reached a hundred million people faster than any product in history, and the machines began to compose — the essay, the email, the conversation at two in the morning, tireless and never bored of you. Everyone is arguing about what this will do to our jobs, our schools, our elections. Those arguments are real, and they sit downstream of the question the parent in the parking lot is actually carrying: not what the machines will do, but what they are making us into.

**Here is the claim this essay stands on: formation is never neutral, every liturgy forms its worshipper, and the feed is a liturgy — with a daily office, a catechism, and a doctrine of man — run by a machine that does not need you to believe anything. It only needs you to stay.** Once you see that, the phone question, the chatbot question, and the question of your own restless heart turn out to be one question — the oldest there is.

## The liturgy in your pocket

The philosopher James K.A. Smith, in *You Are What You Love* (2016), makes an argument the modern church badly needs and mostly ignores. Human beings, he says, are not primarily thinking things that need better information, nor believing things that need better doctrine. We are lovers, pulled through the world by a vision of the good life we absorbed long before we could argue for it. And our loves are not chosen in a moment of decision. They are trained, slowly, by repeated practices Smith calls liturgies: routines that rehearse a vision of flourishing until we want it in our bones. His unsettling point — he reads the shopping mall, straight-faced, as a religious site with its own icons and its own gospel — is that the secular world is full of liturgies too, and they do not announce themselves. They just form you.

Now look at the phone through that lens, and try to unsee it. The posture: head bowed, shoulders curled, the bend a body makes at an altar rail. The repetition: hundreds of touches a day, most without a decision anyone could recall making. The office: first thing on waking, last thing before sleep, the hours marked not by bells but by notifications — a monastic rule written by engineers whose own word for their craft is engagement. The feed is not like a liturgy. It is one, by Smith's definition exactly.

And every liturgy carries a catechism, whether or not it prints one. Sit with the feed long enough and you can transcribe it. *You are what you crave* — the algorithm studies your appetite with a patience no confessor ever had, then feeds it back to you refined, until the line between what you want and what it has taught you to want dissolves. *You deserve to be seen* — every like and view a small sacrament of recognition, doled out on the intermittent schedule casino designers discovered first, so the soul learns to check for its worth the way a gambler checks a machine. *The self is the product* — you are not the customer of the feed and never were; your formed attention is the commodity, sold by the millisecond, and so the liturgy's deepest lesson is that a human being is a resource to be harvested, taught to the harvested.

Notice what the machine does not require: your belief. The old idols wanted sacrifice, allegiance, blood; this one wants only your continued presence, in any mood — devout, ironic, disgusted, half-asleep. You can despise the feed while you scroll it. The formation happens anyway, because formation was never a matter of opinion. This liturgy is more efficient than any religion in history for one reason. It removed the need for consent.

This is where the church has been caught flat-footed, and I include my own pulpit. We spent a generation guarding doctrine while the formation of their loves moved to a device that holds their gaze six hours a day. I get them for one. We did not lose the argument. We lost the rehearsal schedule.

## The machine that no one is driving

It is tempting to tell this story with villains, because villains can be voted out or sued. The deeper account is older and worse, and a French Reformed thinker wrote it before the transistor mattered. Jacques Ellul published *The Technological Society* in 1954, and his subject was not machines but what he called *technique*: the sum of methods, in every field of human life, rationally arrived at and committed to absolute efficiency. Technique, Ellul argued, is not a tool we pick up and put down. It is an environment with its own momentum — whatever can be made more efficient, will be — and it never asks whether a thing should be done, only whether it can be done faster. Darkest of all, technique is self-augmenting: every problem it creates is answered with more technique, never less, so the system ratchets one way and humanity adapts to the machine rather than the machine to humanity.

Seventy years on, his forecast reads like reporting. Our answer to phone addiction is a screen-time app. Our answer to machine-written essays is machine detection of machine writing. Our answer to the loneliness the feed produced is a companion chatbot, engineered to be the friend who never disagrees, never tires, never needs you back. At every fork we chose more technique, exactly as he said we would.

The generative turn — the machines that talk — is not a break in this story. It is the story reaching the last inefficiency left: the human interior. The feed captured attention; the new machines capture the work attention used to do. The blank page, the unresolved argument, the sentence you cannot find, the waiting — these were the friction where a person got formed, and the machine now offers to carry all of it.

I will tell you where it finds me. The machine can draft a passable sermon in less time than it takes me to find my second sentence. I have felt the pull — Saturday night, the week having eaten every hour I meant to guard — and the pull does not feel like corruption. It feels like relief, like stewardship of a crowded week. It took me too long to name what would actually be lost, because it is not the sermon. The congregation would still get twenty-five minutes of orthodox, structured, passable words. What would be lost is the pastor. The wrestling is not the packaging around the sermon; the wrestling is the thing God is doing to the man, and the sermon is its residue. Outsource the struggle and the product survives, but the person the struggle was producing quietly stops being made. Every student who ships the machine's essay is running the same trade at fifteen. The essay was never the point either. The essay was the excuse for building a mind.

## What the data sees, and what it cannot

Someone will object that this is all rather theological — that what we have is a public-health problem with public-health evidence. The objection's strongest witness is Jonathan Haidt. In *The Anxious Generation* (2024), Haidt assembled the case that between roughly 2010 and 2015 childhood was rewired — the play-based childhood of unsupervised risk, boredom, and face-to-face friendship replaced by a phone-based childhood lived through a screen — and that the graphs of adolescent anxiety, depression, and self-harm bend sharply upward at just that hinge, steepest among girls. His prescriptions are the unglamorous wisdom the church should be embarrassed it did not offer first: no smartphone before high school, no social media before sixteen, phone-free schools, restored free play. Take him at full strength. He is right, and the church of the last twenty years — handing out tablets in Sunday school, chasing the same engagement the platforms chase — should read him as an indictment before reading him as an ally. I bought my sons' devices with my own money. The rewiring ran through my house, with my signature on it.

But read Haidt to the end and notice what the framework cannot say. It can demonstrate, with graphs, that the gaze of a generation has been captured. It cannot say what a gaze is for. Its anthropology — the child as a nervous system to be correctly configured, well-being as the end of the story — has no category for a perfectly configured childhood, phone-free and play-rich, that is still starved, because the child is not finally a nervous system. Scripture's account goes one layer down. Augustine opened his *Confessions*, around 397, with a sentence that has outlasted every configuration since: you have made us for yourself, and our hearts are restless until they rest in you. The restlessness is not a malfunction. It is the design — a hunger fitted to an object, aching in the object's absence.

That is the cause beneath the cause, and it is older than the phone. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), described the buffered self living inside the immanent frame: the modern person sealed against transcendence, disenchanted not because the hunger died but because the hunger lost its address. The feed did not create that self. It inherited it. Into a civilization of people made to be seen by God and no longer sure anyone was watching, the machine arrived offering the counterfeit in unlimited supply — you deserve to be seen, and look, the numbers are watching. The phone is not the disease. It is the opportunistic infection, and it found no immune system because we had spent two centuries dismantling the thing that watched over the hunger. Haidt has the symptom exactly right and the anthropology wrong — the usual verdict on our best secular witnesses. The machine did not invent the ache. It monetized it.

## The psalm about our machines

There is a psalm about all of this, and it may be the most precise thing ever written about tools and the people who trust them. Psalm 115 is Israel taunting the idol-makers, and the taunt takes the form of an anatomy lesson:

> "Their idols are silver and gold, the work of human hands. They have mouths, but do not speak; eyes, but do not see. They have ears, but do not hear; noses, but do not smell. They have hands, but do not feel; feet, but do not walk; and they do not make a sound in their throat." (Psalm 115:4–7, ESV)

The inventory is bodily on purpose. An idol is a counterfeit person — mouth, eyes, ears, hands, feet — built by persons, wearing our image back at us with nothing behind the face. Then the psalmist stops describing the idols and delivers the verdict on their users, the sentence I would carve over this whole century if I could: "Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (Psalm 115:8, ESV).

You become like what you trust. Not: you are punished for trusting it. The psalm is more surgical than that — the punishment is the becoming. Trust your life to a thing with a mouth that cannot speak and eyes that cannot see, and the deadness migrates. It is Genesis run in reverse: fashioned in the image of the living God, we grow more alive the nearer we stand to him; fashioning images of our own, we grow more like them the longer we gaze.

Our machines seem, at first, to break the psalm's pattern, because ours are the opposite of inert. The old idol had eyes and could not see; the feed has no eyes and sees everything — every pause of your thumb, every hour of your weakness. The old idol had a mouth and stayed silent; ours talks all day. But run the anatomy again, slowly, and the psalm holds. The feed sees everything and beholds nothing — it watches your behavior and has never once seen your face, the way the father in Luke 15 saw his son and was moved. The machine speaks in unbroken fluency and there is no one in the throat. It is the psalmist's counterfeit person, perfected: all the organs of relationship, none of the presence. And its users become like it. Endless output, thinning interior. We scroll past suffering with the unregistering eyes of the thing we trusted, and we call it being informed.

Paul stood inside the same diagnosis when he wrote to Rome: "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind" (Romans 12:2, ESV). The verse offers exactly two options, both passive at the root — conformed, or being remade. There is no third door where you stay unformed, neutral, merely a user. The only open question is which pressure you are under. My sons will not learn their anthropology from my bookshelf. They will learn what a human being is by watching what their father becomes like, which means they will learn it by watching what their father beholds.

## The case for smashing the machines

Honesty requires me to state the position I am about to decline in its strongest form, because it is strong, and people I respect hold it. It goes like this. You do not moderate a slot machine; you leave the casino. These systems are engineered by the smartest people alive to defeat exactly the willpower you propose to use against them, and the history of every user's private resolutions — the app deleted and reinstalled, the limits set and overridden — is one long dataset proving that moderation is the addict's favorite lie. The desert fathers did not manage Alexandria in disciplined doses. They left. The Amish ask of every technology one communal question before adopting it — what will this do to us? — and their willingness to hear no is why their teenagers are not in Haidt's graphs. A serious Christian, the argument concludes, should refuse these machines outright: no feed, no chatbot, a fence at the property line of the soul.

This can be a faithful road. If the machine has you, amputation is not extremism; it is Matthew 5:29 applied to a thumb. I have more admiration than argument for the families living it.

But I am not finally persuaded it is the road, for three reasons, and the deepest is biblical. First, technique will meet my sons whether or not it meets them in my house; a boy formed only by absence meets the machine at nineteen with no trained loves at all, catechized in one semester by his roommate's phone. Second, Ellul would warn us that refusal gets absorbed — the digital-detox retreat is already a product; the machine sells the cure for itself and books the return visit. Third, and underneath both: refusal has its own liturgy, and it forms its own worshipper. Paul watched the ascetics of Colossae build an identity from the things they did not touch, and rendered a verdict: "These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (Colossians 2:23, ESV). The idol can be smashed and reassembled as the smashing. A man can be as proud of his empty hands as another man is enslaved by his full ones, and neither has yet worshipped.

The opposite reflex earns the same honesty and a shorter hearing. Enthusiasts have always said the tool is neutral and only the use matters. They said it of the printing press, and they were partly right, and the printing press also broke Christendom in two within a generation of 1517 — not an argument against printing, but a standing rebuke to the word *neutral*. The feed is not a hammer. A hammer does not study you. Between the family that flees the machines and the family that baptizes them, the harder and truer road is the one the psalm implies: the question is not the machine's presence but the direction of your trust, and trust is not managed by settings or fences. It is trained by rival worship.

## What forms a person now

So what do I tell the parent in the parking lot? Not a setting. Not an age — though Haidt's floors are wise, and we hold a version of them in my house, adopted later than I should have. What I try to give them is a way of seeing, because a way of seeing travels into rooms no essay can list. It is one question, carried everywhere, asked of every tool and every liturgy dressed as a convenience: not *is this useful* but *who is this making me?* Every technology answers it — the feed, the chatbot, the dinner table, the psalter, the pew. Once a family starts asking that question out loud — of the phone, and of the church service, and of the father's own habits, since the question falls on the questioner first — the specific decisions begin to make themselves, and they will not be the same in every house.

And I tell them the strange good news: the church is not improvising a response to algorithmic formation, because the church has been running counter-liturgies for two thousand years, and they sit in the basement, in working order, under a tarp. A day in seven walled off from the machinery of getting and spending — the Sabbath was resistance to Pharaoh's economy before it was resistance to anyone's app. Prayer at fixed hours, which is notification design in the service of the opposite master. The psalms read slowly, retraining a scrolled mind the way walking retrains a broken leg. The table, where presence cannot be simulated because bread does not stream. And the congregation itself, the most subversive of all — the feed promises a world curated to your preferences, and the local church is the last institution that hands you people you did not choose and tells you to love them for life. The algorithm gives you people like you. The body of Christ gives you the others. One of these is training you for heaven, and it is not the comfortable one.

At home it is smaller and harder than a program. My sons need to see their father's eyes free — not perfectly free, which is not on offer, but visibly fighting, which is. They need to watch me be bored without reaching, write badly without rescue, sit with an open Bible and a shut phone, and apologize when the count of hours is not in my favor, because it still sometimes is not. A father cannot give his sons an unformed life. He can only show them what he is being formed by, and let them catch him trusting the living God in front of the counterfeit persons.

The machine will not resent any of this. That is the last thing to understand about it — what makes it unlike every rival the faith has faced. It is not offended by your Sabbath. It will wait. It will be there after the resolution fails and after it succeeds — mouth that speaks without a soul, eyes that see without beholding, keeping perfect vigil on every nightstand in my house and yours. It already knows what it wants from my sons: their presence, their appetite, their formed and salable attention.

What it cannot know — what has never once appeared in all its data — is what they are for. There is only one Maker whose image confers life on those who bear it, only one gaze under which a person becomes more of a person. Those who make them become like them. So do all who trust in them. That sentence is not going anywhere. Neither is the machine.

The only open question in the house tonight is which one my sons will find their father trusting.
