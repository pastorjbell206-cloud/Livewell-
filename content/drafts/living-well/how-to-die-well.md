Picture a bedroom in the fifteenth century. A man is dying — slowly, over days, surrounded by family, his children in the room, the priest nearby, neighbors coming and going. There is a small book by the bed, or its lessons are known by heart, instructing everyone present in what to do: the questions to ask, the prayers to pray, the temptations the dying man will face in his final hours and how to help him resist them. Death is happening in the middle of the house, in full view of the children, attended like a birth. The dying man has been preparing for this hour for years. He considers it the most important thing he will ever do.

Now picture how most of us will actually die. In a hospital, or a facility, behind a curtain, attended by professionals who are strangers, our children kept at a careful distance, often unconscious or sedated past the point of being present to it. Most people in the developed world now die in institutions rather than at home. The dying have been moved out of the house and out of sight. We have, with the best of intentions, professionalized and hidden death so thoroughly that the average person reaches middle age having never once watched someone die. The thing every previous generation considered the central, defining event of a life has become the one event we arrange never to see.

We have done something no previous generation managed: we have made death unmentionable. Not by denying it happens — we know it happens — but by refusing to prepare for it, look at it, or speak of it until it is upon us and there is no time left to learn. We will spend a year preparing for a wedding and not one hour preparing for the thing that is more certain than the wedding and that every single one of us will do.

**Here is what I want to recover in this essay: that dying well is an art, that the Christian tradition developed it deliberately and considered it essential, that it has to be learned in advance because the hour itself is no time to start, and that this is not morbid but among the most life-giving things a person can do — because you cannot live well until you have made peace with the fact that you will die.** The recovery of the art of dying is, strangely, a recovery of the art of living.

## An art the church used to teach

There was once a literature for this, and it had a name. In the fifteenth century, in the aftermath of the plagues that had killed a third of Europe and left a continent staring at mortality, the church produced a body of writing called the *ars moriendi* — the art of dying. These were practical manuals, widely copied and eventually printed, instructing ordinary Christians in how to die faithfully. They were not gloomy. They were among the most circulated books of their age, because everyone understood they would need them.

The *ars moriendi* assumed several things we have forgotten. It assumed that dying is something you do, an act requiring preparation and skill, not merely something that happens to you. It assumed that the dying person faces real spiritual struggle in those final hours — temptations toward despair, toward false confidence, toward clinging to the things of this life, toward bitterness — and that these can be anticipated and resisted if you know they are coming. And it assumed that the community has a job: to be present, to pray, to read Scripture aloud, to help the dying person fix their eyes where they need to be fixed. Dying was a corporate act, done in the company of the church, rehearsed long before the hour arrived.

The genius of the tradition was its insistence that you must learn this in advance. You do not improvise your death any more than you improvise a wedding. The *ars moriendi* taught the living how to die precisely because it knew the dying would be in no condition to learn. The whole structure rested on a conviction we have inverted: that a good death is the fruit of a prepared life, and an unprepared death is a tragedy that a little foresight could have prevented.

## What we lost when we hid it

Rob Moll wrote the modern book on this, *The Art of Dying* (2010), and his argument is essentially that the church abandoned a craft it had practiced for fifteen centuries, and that we are all poorer for it. Moll, a journalist who sat with the dying and with hospice workers, observed that the church has largely outsourced death to medicine — and that medicine, for all its mercies, is institutionally committed to fighting death rather than helping people die. The hospital is built to defeat death. It is not built to accompany it. And so the Christian who is dying often finds the most important hours of life managed by a system whose whole orientation is to treat death as the enemy to be resisted to the last possible intervention.

Moll's point is not against medicine, which is a gift. His point is that we have let medicine fill a space that used to belong to the church, and in doing so we have lost the practices that let people die as Christians — the deathbed prayers, the gathered community, the unhurried preparation, the willingness to name what is happening and to stop fighting when fighting has become its own form of denial. We have so thoroughly committed to keeping death at bay that we no longer know how to walk through the door when it finally must be walked through. And the dying often die badly for it — frightened, alone, surprised, unrehearsed.

He notes something else, gently: that the Christians who die best are almost always the ones who prepared, who had spent their lives soaked in the hope that makes death survivable, who had practiced surrender in a thousand small things and so could practice it in the last great thing. The good death is not a stroke of luck. It is a harvest. You reap in the dying what you planted in the living.

## Why a Christian can look at it

But none of these practices make sense, and none of them can be recovered, unless the thing underneath them is true — that for the Christian, death has been fundamentally altered. This is where the tradition's confidence comes from, and it is worth being precise about it, because vague comfort will not hold up at a deathbed.

Athanasius made the case in the fourth century, in *On the Incarnation*, the book C.S. Lewis loved enough to write an introduction for. Athanasius argued that the Son of God became human and died precisely in order to do something to death itself. By dying, the deathless One entered death, and being deathless, could not be held by it — and in rising, he broke death from the inside, like a man breaking out of a prison and leaving the door standing open behind him. Death, Athanasius wrote, has become like a tyrant who has been completely conquered: it still rages and threatens, but it has lost its real authority, the way a defeated power keeps its uniform after losing its throne. Christ has so emptied death of its terror, he argued, that Christians now trample it underfoot, and even children and ordinary believers face it without fear, because they know what it now is. It is no longer the end. It is a defeated thing they pass through.

This is what makes the art of dying possible rather than merely brave. Paul says it directly, quoting the prophets in triumph: "Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting? The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law. But thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:54–57). And Jesus says it at the grave of his friend, in the line every Christian funeral leans on: "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die" (John 11:25–26). The Christian does not die well by pretending death is not frightening. The Christian dies well because death, while still an enemy, is a defeated enemy, and the dying person is passing through a door Christ has already broken open and walked through ahead of them.

## What dying well actually looks like

So what is it, concretely, to die well? It is not a stoic performance, and it is not denial dressed up as faith. The man who dies well in the Christian tradition does several things, and they can be learned.

He numbers his days. "Teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom," prays the ninetieth Psalm — the oldest psalm, attributed to Moses, the one that looks human mortality full in the face: our years are seventy, or eighty if we have the strength, and they are soon gone, and we fly away. To number your days is to stop living as though you had unlimited time, to let the fact of your death press wisdom into your living. The man who has numbered his days does not arrive at his deathbed shocked. He has been expecting this appointment his whole life and has lived accordingly.

There is a discipline hidden in that psalm that the ancients took seriously and we have abandoned. They called it the remembrance of death, and they did not mean morbid brooding. They meant a regular, deliberate looking at the fact of the end, so that it could do its work on the priorities of the present. A man who genuinely remembers that he will die does not spend his short years on the things our culture spends its years on. He does not nurse grudges that will outlive their occasion, or hoard against a future he will not see, or postpone forgiveness and affection to a someday that mortality will not honor. The skull on the medieval scholar's desk was not a gesture of despair. It was a clock — a reminder that the hours were finite and therefore precious, that this conversation, this meal, this ordinary Tuesday, was a portion of a life that would not be repeated. To remember death is to be made unable to waste life. That is its gift, and we have thrown it away in the name of staying cheerful.

He holds his life loosely, even his life with the people he loves. Paul writes from prison, genuinely unsure whether he will live or die, and lands in the most extraordinary place: "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain. I am hard pressed between the two. My desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better. But to remain in the flesh is more necessary on your account" (Philippians 1:21–23). Notice the freedom in that. Paul is not suicidal and not death-obsessed; he loves his life and his people. But he has so located his treasure in Christ that death cannot rob him — to live is more of Christ in service, to die is more of Christ in presence, and either way he wins. That is the freedom the art of dying aims at. Not a man clutching his life so hard that death must tear it from his fingers, but a man holding it open, ready to be either kept here or called home, undefeated by the outcome.

And he dies in company, having let the people he loves come close. This is the practice we have most thoroughly lost, and it is the one most worth fighting to recover. The dying person who is surrounded, prayed over, read to, held, and accompanied dies differently than the one hidden behind a curtain. And the watching changes the living too. A child who has seen a grandparent die well in faith has been given something a sermon cannot give — has seen with their own eyes that the thing we say we believe holds up at the only moment that finally tests it. The hidden death protects the living from a fear they then carry, unrehearsed, into their own dying. The witnessed death hands down courage.

## Begin now

The reason to recover this now, while you are well, is the whole point of the *ars moriendi*: because the hour itself is too late to begin. You cannot learn surrender for the first time when everything is being taken. You cannot suddenly trust a Savior at the deathbed whom you spent your life keeping at arm's length. The peace that lets a person die well is not issued at the door. It is the accumulated fruit of a life of small surrenders, a thousand daily letting-gos that rehearse the final one, a long friendship with the God you are about to see.

So here is the strange gift hidden in this grim-sounding subject. To learn to die well is to be set free to live well, because the great unspoken anxiety underneath so much of our striving is the death we refuse to look at. The man who has made his peace with dying has nothing left to be afraid of, and a man with nothing left to be afraid of can love recklessly, give freely, and live without the low constant hum of self-protection that mortality drives in those who will not face it. The ancients were not morbid for keeping a skull on the desk. They were free, because they had looked at the thing and seen, behind it, an empty tomb.

You are going to die. It is the most certain fact about your future, more certain than anything you are currently anxious about. The world has trained you to look away from it, to hide it behind curtains and professionals and the firm refusal to mention it at dinner. The church once knew better, and called the looking an art, and taught it to children.

Number your days. Hold your life open. Let the people you love come close at the end. And fix your eyes where the dying have always been told to fix them — on the One who walked through the broken door ahead of you, and left it standing open, and is waiting on the other side.
