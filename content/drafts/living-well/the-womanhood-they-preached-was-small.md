You will not get details from me. Not because I cannot supply them, and not because anyone is owed my silence — but because details become spectacle, and spectacle is a way of not listening. The pattern is the point. And the pattern is common enough that some of the women reading this will not need the details filled in. You have your own set.

I was raised inside a fundamentalism that preached its rules as though the rules were the gospel. Fear was the engine of everything it called formation. And womanhood, in that world, was a discipline of shrinkage. Modesty was taught not as dignity but as fear of the body — a girl's body presented to her as a hazard to be managed, other people's sins pre-assigned to her hemline. Submission was taught not as anything the New Testament describes but as erasure: the will treated as a defect awaiting surrender. Silence was a virtue, the crowning one. A woman's godliness was measured by how little room she took up — how little she asked, wanted, said, weighed upon the world. And every inch of it came stapled to Bible verses, because Scripture, in that world, was not the bread of the household. It was the leash.

These days I hear preaching from a different kind of pulpit — my husband's — sitting in a pew with our five sons beside me, which is its own quiet astonishment for a woman with my beginnings, because the pulpit was where the leash used to be held. It took me years to hear a sermon without bracing. This essay is about what happened between that world and this pew, and about the strange road I took to get here: not away from the book they weaponized, but further into it.

## The small god of the small womanhood

The first thing to say about that world is not what it did to women. It is what it did to God, because the two are a single error. The god of my girlhood was small. He needed managing. He watched for infractions the way an anxious man watches a till, and his favor was a thin surface you could fall through at any hour. Whatever the doctrinal statement said, the gospel as actually preached was *behave*. A small god makes everyone underneath him small, and the people most underneath him smallest of all. Women were most underneath him.

I want to be exact here, because exactness is owed in both directions. The problem with the world I grew up in was not that it took the Bible too seriously. That was its claim about itself, and the claim was false. It took fear seriously and called the fear Scripture. I know churches that hold convictions about men and women that some of my readers do not share — I worship in one — and hold them with warmth and open books, where the women are large-souled and nobody's silence is enforced. Faithful churches genuinely disagree about women and ordained office; that is an old argument among people who love the same Bible, second-order by any honest reckoning, and nothing in this essay depends on where you land in it. What I am naming lives beneath that argument. Whatever a church concludes about office, a womanhood of fear, managed smallness, and Scripture-as-leash is not among the options the text leaves open. That is not one side of a debate. That is the abuse of the book, carried out in the book's own name, and I watched it done.

## Two ways out

Nearly every woman I have known from a world like mine left it by one of two roads.

The first is the obvious one: burn the book. When a book has been the handle of the whip, throwing the book away looks less like rebellion than like sanity — the first sane act, maybe, in a life that had been arranged to prevent it. I will not stand at a safe distance and scold the women who took that road. They are not weak, and about what was done to them they are not even wrong; they are witnesses, and a church that wonders aloud why they left should first sit down and hear what was preached to them as the word of the Lord. If tearing down means taking apart a thing that was built wrong, their demolition was overdue. My only quarrel with that road is where it ends. It ends in the rubble. You can spend a whole life proving the old house deserved condemning — it did — and still have nowhere to live.

One thing before I go further, because I know who may be reading this. Some of you are not out. If you are inside a home or a church where obedience is kept in place by fear — or by anything worse than fear — then the first faithful thing is not to win the theological argument. It is to be safe. Safety comes before theology; it is not a detour from faithfulness but the ground of it, and getting safe from an abusive place usually takes help from outside its walls — a person you trust, a professional, someone the people who control you do not control. Ask for that help. God is not on the side of what is being done to you. The rest of this essay will keep.

The second road is harder to see from inside, and it is the one I took — not out of courage, I want that on the record, but out of something closer to stubbornness. They had always told me the Bible was on their side. Every rule came with a verse; every verse arrived pre-read. And it occurred to me, later than I like to admit, that I had never actually checked. I had heard the book quoted my whole life and had never once read it as though it might be a witness for the other side. So I read it — not devotionally at first; forensically, the way you read a contract someone has been enforcing against you. And this is what I found, and what the rest of this essay is about: the book did not say what they said it said. Page after page, the book was worse news for them than it ever was for me.

## The first page

They should never have let me read Genesis. On the first page of the book they used to shrink me, before a single command about marriage, before any curse, before the fall itself, there is this:

> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them. (Genesis 1:27, ESV)

Male and female, in the same verse, in the same breath, at the same instant. The image of God is not conferred on the man and then extended to the woman as a courtesy. It is spoken over both, first, before anything else is true of them — and the commission that follows lands on both as well: "And God blessed them. And God said to them, 'Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion'" (Genesis 1:28, ESV). *Them.* The world I was raised in taught, without ever quite writing it down, that the image of God reached a woman secondhand — routed through a father, then a husband, the way electricity reaches a lamp. The first page of the Bible refuses it. Whatever else Scripture goes on to say about men and women — and it says things Christians argue over in good faith — it says this first, and it never takes it back.

## A word they read small

In my girlhood, Proverbs 31 was a measuring stick. The "excellent wife" was read at women — her rising while it is yet night, her distaff and her merchant ships — as a to-do list nobody could finish, which was, I came to think, the point of reading it that way. A woman kept permanently behind an impossible standard is a woman easy to govern.

Then I looked at the word. "An excellent wife who can find? She is far more precious than jewels" (Proverbs 31:10, ESV). The word translated *excellent* is the Hebrew *chayil*, and it is not a domestic word. It is the Old Testament's word for might — the word for armies, for wealth, for warriors. When the angel of the LORD appears to Gideon, the greeting is "The LORD is with you, O mighty man of valor" (Judges 6:12, ESV) — the same word. The poem in Proverbs 31 is an acrostic, a victory hymn built on the alphabet, and it crowns not a chore chart but a campaign: "Strength and dignity are her clothing, and she laughs at the time to come" (Proverbs 31:25, ESV). She laughs at the future. That is not the posture of a woman being kept small. And in observant Jewish homes to this day, the poem is sung at the Sabbath table — sung *to* the woman, in her hearing, as praise. It was composed to be sung to a woman, not read at her.

Ruth settles what the word cannot mean. Boaz calls Ruth "a worthy woman" — *eshet chayil*, the exact phrase of Proverbs 31:10 — "for all my fellow townsmen know that you are a worthy woman" (Ruth 3:11, ESV). At that moment Ruth is a destitute foreign widow gleaning other people's fields. No husband, no household, no output the old checklist could score. Whatever valor is, it is not domestic production. They translated a war-word into a chore list, and the mistranslation was not linguistic. It was convenient.

## The names nobody read to me

Then there were the women in the text itself — there the whole time, in the plain reading of the plain Bible that world claimed to live by, and somehow never once read to me.

Deborah. "Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel at that time" (Judges 4:4, ESV). Judging Israel — and the next verse says the people of Israel "came up to her for judgment." The men of a nation climbing a hill to hear a woman render verdicts, in the era my girlhood held up as the Bible's own world. The text records it without apology and without a flicker of embarrassment. In the victory song that follows, she is given the title in her own voice: "I, Deborah, arose as a mother in Israel" (Judges 5:7, ESV). The Scripture I was handed had somehow misplaced her.

Huldah. When the Book of the Law was rediscovered in the temple in the reign of Josiah, and the young king tore his robes and demanded to know what the LORD's word meant for his people, he sent the highest delegation his kingdom could assemble — Hilkiah the high priest among them — to inquire of the LORD. They went to "Huldah the prophetess" (2 Kings 22:14, ESV). This while Jeremiah and Zephaniah were both alive and prophesying in those same years. The high priest of Israel carried the newly found Scripture to a woman to hear the LORD's verdict on it, and she gave it — "Thus says the LORD" — to the king's own officials. I grew up under men who would not let a woman read announcements. Nobody ever mentioned Huldah.

The women of Luke 8. Jesus's traveling ministry ran on money, and Luke tells you plainly whose it was: "and also some women who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities: Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out, and Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod's household manager, and Susanna, and many others, who provided for them out of their means" (Luke 8:2–3, ESV). The itinerant preaching of the Son of God was funded by women — named, particular women, one of them ranking gentry from Herod's own court. And one of the three names is Susanna. I have carried that name my whole life, and no one in the world that raised me ever once showed me hers.

Junia. Near the end of Romans, Paul greets two coworkers: "Greet Andronicus and Junia, my kinsmen and my fellow prisoners. They are well known to the apostles, and they were with Christ before me" (Romans 16:7, ESV). A woman who went to prison with Paul and was in Christ before him. Honesty requires the footnote, so here it is: the Greek phrase can be read "well known to the apostles," as my translation has it, or "outstanding among the apostles," as others do, and scholars argue the grammar in good faith. But John Chrysostom — a fourth-century preacher no one has ever accused of flattering women — read the verse, took Junia for a woman, and marveled that she should be counted worthy of the title of apostle. What happened afterward is its own small parable: for centuries, editors and translators quietly gave her a man's name, Junias — a name otherwise unattested in the ancient world. A woman stood in the text, and generations of readers corrected her. I know how she must have felt.

## The witness they would not have called

And then there is Jesus, who is the argument underneath all the other arguments.

Watch him with the sisters at Bethany. Martha is doing what my girlhood would have blessed — the kitchen, the serving, the being useful — and Mary "sat at the Lord's feet and listened to his teaching" (Luke 10:39, ESV), which is not a posture of sentiment; sitting at a rabbi's feet is the posture of a disciple, the seat Paul says he took at the feet of Gamaliel. When Martha objects, Jesus defends the woman in the student's chair: "Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her" (Luke 10:42, ESV). Will not be taken away. He said that knowing exactly how many hands, over how many centuries, would try.

But the morning that ends the argument is the one at the tomb. Some context first, because the text deserves it: in the first-century world, a woman's testimony carried almost no legal weight — the historian Josephus records the assumption of the age plainly, that evidence from women was not to be admitted. Every early Christian knew this. Which is why no one inventing a resurrection story in that world would have written the story we have.

> But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb, and as she wept she stooped to look into the tomb. (John 20:11, ESV)

She had come to tend a body. She stayed because grief does not follow instructions. And the risen Christ, with all of history to choose from — Peter was available; John had already been to the tomb and gone home — appeared first to her, and she mistook him for the gardener until he said one word:

> Jesus said to her, "Mary." She turned and said to him in Aramaic, "Rabboni!" (which means Teacher). (John 20:16, ESV)

Her name. The small god of my girlhood knew girls only by their infractions. The risen Lord of the actual Scriptures knows this woman by name, in the dark of the greatest morning there has ever been — and then he does the thing that should have ended womanhood-as-silence forever. He gives her the news, and he gives her the sending: "go to my brothers and say to them, 'I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God'" (John 20:17, ESV). And she goes: "Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, 'I have seen the Lord'" (John 20:18, ESV).

The first human being entrusted with the message on which everything since has rested was a woman whose testimony a court of her own day would not have taken. The church's older memory gave her a title for it — the apostle to the apostles. If the story were fabricated, she would never have been in it; her presence in that garden is there for the reason unflattering details are always there, which is that it happened. The first sermon of the new creation was five words long, and a woman preached it to preachers, because the risen Christ told her to. Whatever your church concludes about offices and order — and mine has its convictions — no honest reading of that morning can ever again call a woman's silence her glory. He did not ask Mary to be small. He asked her to be a witness, which is the opposite thing.

## The other smallness

Now the harder honesty, the one the house rules of this platform require and my own history makes me slow to say: the culture that would rescue me from all this carries a leash of its own.

The world I fled taught me to need permission for everything. The world I fled into teaches women to need nothing and no one — the self as fortress, every attachment a liability, every need a failure of the project, rescue defined as requiring no rescue ever again. It sounds like the antidote. It is the same instrument held at the other end. Fundamentalism shrank a woman to a role; this shrinks her to a résumé of self-sufficiency, and both hand her an identity smaller than the image of God. One forbade her voice; the other forbids her need. Neither can imagine what Genesis says at the start and the garden tomb says at the end — a woman whose dignity is conferred, not performed; who can be strong precisely because she does not have to be invulnerable.

I say this from inside it. When I first got out, I built the fortress. A woman leaving a world that punished her needs learns to stop having any, and calls the amputation strength — I confused being hard to hurt with being healed, and they are not the same thing, and the people who lived with me in those years paid the difference. The old fear had not left. It had straightened its posture. Erasing yourself for God's supposed sake and armoring yourself against everyone made in his image are two failures of the same confession: that you were made an image-bearer, which means made for communion — seen, needed, needing — from the first page.

## What was under the house

So no, I did not keep the faith I was handed. It could not be kept; it was not load-bearing. But I did not burn it down either, in the end, and the difference between those two sentences is the whole road. I dug. Under the small womanhood, under the rules preached as gospel, there was an older house — the one the counterfeiters had been imitating badly all along. In it I found the God who wrote women into every hinge of the story: naming himself to a slave woman in the desert, raising up a mother in Israel to judge a nation, sending his high priest to a prophetess, funding his Son's ministry out of women's purses, seating Mary in the disciple's chair, choosing the witness no court would call. When Matthew opens the New Testament with a genealogy, he breaks the conventions of the form to name the women in the Messiah's line — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, "the wife of Uriah," and Mary (Matthew 1:3–16) — and not one of them is the kind of name polite religion puts on the wall. Nothing about this book was ever small except some of the hands it got read through.

I think of the women still inside — the ones this essay has been quietly addressed to since the first paragraph. I cannot hand you the years it took me. I can hand you the one thing I wish someone had said over the fence: the book they are holding is not on their side. I checked. Read it yourself, all of it, in the light, and see whose witness it turns out to be.

And I think of my five sons, who are learning what a woman is by living with one, and who will someday sit in pews and read texts and vote in churches I will never see. The womanhood preached over my girlhood was small, and it did not survive contact with the Scriptures it claimed. The woman in the text is not small. On the first morning of the new creation, the risen Christ had one sentence to deliver and all the world to choose a herald from, and he said a woman's name, and told her to go and say it.

He did not tell her to be small. There is no verse where he tells her to be small. He told her: go and say. He has not changed the instruction.
