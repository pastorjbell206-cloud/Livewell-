The American Christian gives, on average, about 2.5 percent of his income to all charitable causes combined. That figure has been roughly stable for several decades and is significantly lower than the 10 percent tithe most American Christians say they affirm as a biblical baseline. The gap between what we say about giving and what we actually do is one of the visible signs of how thoroughly the surrounding consumer economy has formed the contemporary Christian's relationship with money. The gap is also one of the most addressable conditions of contemporary Christian discipleship. This essay is about what to do.

I want to write this for the Christian reader who has been giving 2.5 percent and feels mildly guilty about it, and for the Christian reader who has been giving 10 percent and feels the discipline is sufficient, and for the Christian reader who has not been giving in any consistent way and has been telling himself the absence is theological rather than practical. Each of these readers has the same essay to engage with, because the essay is about what the biblical witness actually says about Christian giving and what the witness requires.

## What the tithe was

The Old Testament tithe was a specific practice with a specific function in the economy of ancient Israel. The tithe was ten percent of agricultural produce, given to support the Levitical priesthood, the temple system, and the care of widows, orphans, and resident aliens in the land. The tithe was not a voluntary contribution. The tithe was a structural feature of the Israelite economic system, comparable in some respects to a religious tax. The tithe was the baseline, and the additional contributions on top of the tithe — freewill offerings, the various sacrifices, the alms — were the expressions of devotion that went beyond what was required.

The contemporary American evangelical use of the tithe is structurally different. The contemporary tithe is a voluntary practice within a market economy in which the giver retains the freedom to give or not to give. The contemporary tithe goes primarily to the local congregation rather than to a comprehensive religious-political-welfare system. The contemporary tithe operates in a context where the giver is also paying significant taxes that fund secular versions of some of the functions the Old Testament tithe supported. The structural transposition has produced confusion about what the tithe is for and what level of giving is appropriate.

The New Testament does not, as far as I have been able to determine, command the tithe explicitly for Christians. The New Testament repeatedly commends giving — generous, sacrificial, cheerful, oriented toward the poor and toward the support of the broader Christian mission — but the specific ten-percent figure is not a New Testament command in the way the Old Testament structure made it for Israelites. The American evangelical tradition has, on the whole, taken the tithe as the starting point for Christian giving, treating it as a continuity from Old Testament practice. The continuity is defensible. The continuity does not, however, address the deeper question of whether the tithe is sufficient as a baseline for Christian giving under New Testament economic discipleship.

## What the New Testament actually expects

The New Testament economic vision is, in significant respects, more demanding than the tithe rather than less. I want to lay out the witness, because the witness has been suppressed in much of contemporary evangelical preaching on money.

Jesus told the rich young ruler to sell everything and give to the poor. The instruction was specific to that young man, in his specific circumstances, with his specific spiritual condition. But the structure of the instruction — that wealth itself can be the obstacle to Christian discipleship, and that the disciple's relationship to wealth has to be subordinated to discipleship — is not specific to that young man. The structure runs throughout Jesus' teaching on money.

Jesus told a parable about the widow's mite in Mark 12 and Luke 21. The widow put in two small copper coins. The wealthy were putting in much more in absolute terms. Jesus praised the widow specifically because she gave *all she had to live on*. The contrast is striking. The wealthy gave from their abundance; their giving did not cost them anything meaningful. The widow gave from her poverty; her giving cost her everything. Jesus measured the giving by what it cost the giver, not by what it added to the institutional total. The measurement is uncomfortable.

The early church in Acts 2 and Acts 4 practiced voluntary economic sharing at a level that substantially exceeded tithing. Members sold property and contributed proceeds to the community for distribution to those in need. The practice was voluntary, not compulsory; Acts 5 makes this explicit. But the practice was extensive, and the description in Acts treats it as a normal feature of early Christian life.

Paul's economic theology in 2 Corinthians 8 and 9 holds up the example of the Macedonian churches, who *in a severe test of affliction, their abundance of joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part.* The Macedonians were poor. They gave generously anyway. Paul commends the generosity not because it produced large total contributions in absolute terms — they were poor; the totals were small — but because the giving was sacrificial. Paul's measurement matches Jesus' measurement of the widow's mite.

James 5 denounces the wealthy who have accumulated possessions without using them for the care of laborers and the poor: *the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you.* The denunciation is structural. James is not condemning all wealth. James is condemning wealth that has been accumulated at the expense of the poor and that has not been used for the care of the poor.

The cumulative New Testament witness is that Christian economic life is supposed to be marked by sacrificial generosity, not merely by the technical fulfillment of a tithe. The tithe may be a starting point. The tithe is not the destination. The destination is a relationship with money in which the disciple holds his wealth lightly, gives sacrificially, cares specifically for the poor he knows, and lets his economic life be shaped by the kingdom rather than by the optimization pressures of the surrounding consumer economy.

## What sacrificial giving actually looks like

I want to be concrete, because the abstract conversation about generosity often produces nothing in practice.

Sacrificial giving is giving that costs the giver something he could otherwise have bought. The tithe from disposable income is not, in this sense, fully sacrificial. The tithe from disposable income leaves the giver's purchasing power for everything that mattered to him personally largely intact. He still has the lifestyle, the car, the house, the vacations he would have had without the tithe. The tithe affects the margin without affecting the substance. Sacrificial giving, in the sense Jesus and Paul commended, affects the substance. It requires the giver to not buy something he could have bought. It requires the lifestyle to be smaller than the income would have permitted.

This is the harder version. This is the version most American Christians have not, in my pastoral experience, fully embraced. The version requires real choices about what to spend on and what to forgo. The version requires the household to live below its earning potential. The version requires the disciple to choose a smaller life so that a larger giving is possible.

I am not commanding this. I am describing what the biblical witness actually points toward. The disciple who reads the witness honestly has to make his own decisions about what level of sacrificial giving is appropriate for his household and his stage of life. The decisions are different for the young single Christian, the married couple with young children, the empty-nest household, the elderly Christian with limited resources. The decisions should be made deliberately rather than by default. The default in American Christian life has been the 2.5 percent figure. The default does not match what the biblical witness points toward.

## Specific moves that have helped

I want to share specific practices that have helped the Christians I have walked with through deeper economic discipleship.

The deliberate annual review. The household sits down once a year, usually in the new year or before the start of the fiscal year for the church, and explicitly reviews its giving level. The review asks two questions. What did we give last year as a percentage of income? What do we want to give this year, and why? The review makes the giving conscious rather than automatic. The conscious giving is more likely to grow over time than the automatic giving.

The graduated tithe. The tithe is calculated not as ten percent of all income but as a higher percentage of income above a baseline necessary for the household. The household decides what level of income is necessary for its sustainable function — recognizing that the level should be set by need rather than by surrounding cultural standards of comfort — and gives a higher percentage of income above that level. The graduated approach makes the giving more sacrificial without requiring the household to abandon sustainable function.

The specific-person giving. In addition to giving to the local congregation, the household gives directly to specific poor people whose names they know. The widow in the next pew whose Social Security does not cover her medical bills. The young family in the church whose income has not yet caught up with their children's expenses. The neighbor whose marriage just dissolved and whose finances are in crisis. The specific-person giving is harder than institutional giving, because it requires the giver to know the recipients and to work through the relational complexity. The harder giving is, in my pastoral experience, often the more spiritually formative.

The withholding of certain purchases. The household decides what categories of purchases it will not make, and gives the money it would have spent. The new car becomes the older car that is still reliable. The larger house becomes the smaller house that is sufficient. The expensive vacation becomes the modest one. The decisions are not about asceticism; the decisions are about freeing resources for the giving the disciple wants to do.

The slow increase. The household that has been giving 2.5 percent does not jump to 25 percent in one year; the jump is rarely sustainable. The household that has been giving 2.5 percent can move to 5 percent in the next year, to 7 percent in the year after, to 10 percent, and beyond, over a sustained period of years. The slow increase is sustainable. The slow increase produces, over a decade, substantially different giving patterns than the household started with.

## The verdict

The widow had two coins. She put both in. The act was foolish by any financial metric and was praised by the only metric that finally matters. I am not asking you to put in both coins this week. I am asking you to find the act of giving this year that costs you something you could otherwise have bought. The cost is the offering. The cost is what makes it worship and not philanthropy.

I am writing this for the Christian who has been giving 2.5 percent and has been telling himself it is enough. The 2.5 percent is not enough by any reading of the biblical witness I find honest. The tithe is the floor. The disciple gives above the floor as he is able, with the goal of sacrificial giving that costs him something real. The discipline is not a legalistic requirement. The discipline is the participation in the kingdom that has been the practice of Christian disciples for two thousand years.

The American Christian household that recovers sacrificial giving will, over time, be a different household than the household that did not. The difference will be visible in the giving, in the lifestyle, in the household's relationship with money, in the children's formation about what wealth is for. The difference is worth the cost. The cost is the offering. Begin.
