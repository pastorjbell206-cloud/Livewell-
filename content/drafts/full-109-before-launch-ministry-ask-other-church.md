Before you build the food pantry, ask who already runs one.

Before you launch the recovery program, ask which church in your city has been running one for a decade with trained staff and the community relationships to sustain it.

Before you hire a counselor and hang a shingle, ask whether the counseling center two miles away is already understaffed and under-funded and could do more with your partnership than your competition.

These are not romantic questions. They are stewardship questions. And they are questions that most pastors do not ask, because the church has absorbed the logic of the marketplace, where differentiation is survival and duplication is acceptable as long as your version is better than the other person's.

That logic is not wrong in a marketplace. It is destructive in the body of Christ.

Paul's language in Ephesians 4:11-16 does not describe a collection of independent ministries competing for the same population. It describes a body that builds itself up in love, with every part working properly, each contributing to the growth of the whole. The language is of coordination, not competition. The knee does not say to the ankle, "I have developed a better walking program." The knee bends when the ankle requires it, because they are part of the same body and the body needs both of them doing what they are designed to do.

I have launched ministries I should not have launched. I am certain of this. There were moments when the vision felt urgent and the need felt clear and the call felt genuine, and the call was genuine — but the honest question I did not ask was whether I was being called to start something new or to strengthen something that already existed. Those are not the same calling. The second one is harder, because it requires giving up the satisfactions of ownership and credit. But it may produce more fruit.

The waste in American ministry from duplicated programs serving the same population is enormous. Two recovery programs in the same city, each running on shoestring budgets, each turning away volunteers they cannot train, each reinventing the curriculum the other program already developed — neither of them as effective as the single, well-resourced program they could have built together. That is not faithfulness. It is, at its root, a failure of pastoral humility dressed as pastoral vision.

The more difficult question is what to do when the program that already exists in your city is being run by a church you disagree with theologically, or by a secular nonprofit with no gospel component, or by a denomination that operates differently than yours. That is where the stewardship question gets genuinely hard. And it is exactly where the answer matters most.

A food pantry run by a Catholic parish and a Baptist church together, with their theological differences fully acknowledged and their practical cooperation fully real, is a more honest picture of the kingdom than either of them running a separate pantry. A recovery program run in partnership with a secular counseling center, where the church provides community and spiritual formation and the center provides clinical expertise, may serve the person in front of you better than the church's attempt to do both alone.

This requires conversations that are awkward. It requires phone calls to pastors you do not know well. It requires walking into meetings where you are not in charge. It requires being the person who said, "Tell me what you are already doing and what you need, before I tell you what I am thinking of launching."

Most pastors never make that call. It feels like weakness. It is not weakness. It is the posture of someone who actually believes the kingdom belongs to God rather than to his church's portfolio.

Start there. See what already exists. Ask what is needed.

Then decide whether the call to build is actually a call to join.
