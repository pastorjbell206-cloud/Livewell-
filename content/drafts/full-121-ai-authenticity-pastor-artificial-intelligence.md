A pastor asked me recently whether it was wrong to use artificial intelligence to write his sermons. I did not answer immediately. Not because the question was complicated technically, but because I was trying to locate what it was actually asking underneath the surface — and what I found there was older than any algorithm.

The question was really this: what does it mean to be called to preach?

The ministry of the word has always rested on a double claim. First, that the Scripture is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, able to divide and discern in ways that no technique can replicate (Hebrews 4:12). Second, that the preacher who stands before a congregation to open that Scripture has been formed — by study, by suffering, by encounter with the text over years — in ways that make his voice particular and irreplaceable. The congregation does not gather on Sunday to receive a competent communication of correct theological content. They gather to hear a human being, wounded and restored like them, who has wrestled with this text in the dark and come back to tell them what he found.

Artificial intelligence can produce content that is theologically accurate. It can organize an argument, identify relevant texts, and construct prose that reads smoothly. What it cannot do is spend Wednesday afternoon with a dying deacon and then bring the weight of that room into Sunday's exposition of John 11. What it cannot do is carry the specific grief and specific hope of a specific community into the particular encounter with Scripture that preaching, at its best, is. The machine has no Wednesday afternoon. It has no dying deacon. It has never stood at a graveside in February in Michigan and tried to say something true.

I am not arguing that pastors should not use these tools at all. I have used them. I have asked an AI to summarize a commentary, to suggest parallel passages, to help me structure a difficult argument. Those are research tasks. They are not preaching.

The distinction matters because what erodes is not immediately obvious. The pastor who begins outsourcing the generative labor of sermon preparation — the sustained wrestle with the text, the search for the right word, the willingness to sit with what the passage demands before rushing to what it offers — does not suddenly become a fraud. He becomes, over time, something subtler and more dangerous: a communicator who no longer has anything of his own to say. The congregation eventually feels this, even if they cannot name it. They receive information. They do not receive witness.

This is not new technology producing a new problem. The problem is as old as the difference between a scribe and a prophet. The scribe copies what has been written. The prophet has stood in the council of God and speaks from that encounter. Jeremiah did not dictate from a manual. He wept in chapter 20 because the word was in him like a fire he could not hold back, and the speaking cost him everything. That fire is not generated. It is given — given through the kind of slow, costly, personal formation that cannot be automated.

The pastoral question AI raises is not: how do we use this tool responsibly? That is a legitimate question with practical answers. The deeper question is: am I still doing the things that form a preacher, or have I begun substituting efficient output for the irreplaceable labor of actual encounter with God and with the people I serve?

That is a question worth sitting with long before it becomes easy to answer.

The congregation that receives a sermon a human being actually wrestled through — even if it is rougher, even if the transitions are less polished — receives something the most sophisticated language model cannot produce: a witness. A person who went somewhere hard and came back changed.

The tools are new. The calling is not.
