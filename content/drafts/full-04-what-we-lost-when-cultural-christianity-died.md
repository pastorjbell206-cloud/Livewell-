I want to write about grief before I write about anything else. The grief is real. The grief is also misnamed.

We are losing Christendom. We are not losing the faith. The distinction is the load-bearing one in everything I will write in this series, and most American Christians I know have not yet sat with it long enough to feel the difference in their bodies.

Cultural Christianity was a long agreement, two and a half centuries old in this country, that Christian moral vocabulary would be the public moral vocabulary. The wedding was a Christian wedding by default. The funeral was Christian. The Sabbath was Sunday, and the question was whether you observed it, not whether the calendar said the word. The school-board opening invocation was Christian. The president closed his speeches with *God bless America* and meant the God of the King James Bible. The Christmas tree was on the courthouse lawn, and no one had to argue for it being there.

That agreement is over. The data is everywhere. The Pew "religious nones" graph that every pastor has seen too many times. The decline in Christian self-identification by a full thirty points in two generations. The collapse of evangelical influence in the universities, in the corporations, in the surrounding culture. Cultural Christianity is not under attack. Cultural Christianity is dying of natural causes, and the natural causes are mostly its own.

## The two stories we tell ourselves

American evangelicalism has, on the whole, told itself two stories about the dying.

The first story is the persecution story. The world hates us. The institutions are coming for us. Secular humanism has captured the universities and the media and the courts and will not stop until they have captured the church. This story is comforting because it makes us the protagonist. It is also mostly false. We were never quite as central as we thought we were, and the world is not quite as hostile as we have decided it is. We have confused the loss of cultural privilege with persecution. The confusion has cost us.

The second story is the secularization story. The world has gotten too sophisticated, too educated, too modern for the faith. This is the story most academics tell themselves, and many of our own thoughtful pastors have absorbed it. It is also mostly false. The places where Christianity is growing fastest are the parts of the world that are getting more modern, not less — Africa, parts of Asia, the Hispanic world in the Americas. Secularization is the story of a particular slice of white North Atlantic civilization, and we have mistaken our slice for the whole.

Neither story is honest. The honest third story is the harder one.

## What we actually had

James Davison Hunter saw this clearly in 1991, in *Culture Wars*, and even more clearly in his later work. What we called cultural Christianity in the twentieth century was, on close inspection, a thin baptism of mid-century Anglo-American middle-class respectability. The respectability was Christian-adjacent. It used Christian vocabulary. It honored Christian institutions in ways that paid social dividends. It also had very little to do with discipleship, very little theological depth, very little capacity to form people whose lives looked different from their non-Christian neighbors in any way you could measure.

We mistook the thinness for the body. The thinness was the costume. The body has always been the church — the actual community of disciples, gathered around scripture and sacrament, scattered through the surrounding culture, never coextensive with it.

The costume is coming off. We are reading the off-coming as the end of the body, and the misreading is the source of most of the panic in our institutions.

I have spent twenty years pastoring people who confused the two. I have done it myself. The grief I have grieved most of my pastoral career was the grief for the costume. The grief I should have grieved was the grief that the body, even at the height of cultural Christianity, was smaller and more fragile than the costume had let us believe.

## What we are actually losing

It is worth naming what we are losing precisely. The honest list goes like this.

We are losing the assumption that Christian moral vocabulary will be understood by our neighbors. We can no longer say *sin* and assume the word lands. We can no longer say *grace* and assume the word means what we mean. The basic theological grammar is now a foreign language to people who used to share the alphabet with us.

We are losing the ambient civic Christianity. The public prayer at the football game. The Christian holiday as default. The Sunday-as-Sabbath assumption that ordered the rhythm of a community. None of this is coming back, and most of us are not going to be able to recover it through political maneuver, no matter how many strongmen we elect.

We are losing the cultural legitimacy that let the church grow without explicit catechesis. For two centuries, evangelical churches in America could assume the surrounding culture would catechize the basic Christian categories — the existence of God, the seriousness of sin, the dignity of human persons — and the church only had to add the gospel on top. The surrounding culture is not doing this anymore. The church will now have to teach everything from the foundation up. Most of us are not yet ready to.

We are losing, finally, the social respectability that made being Christian an easy thing to be. It is becoming costly again. Not the way it is costly in Pakistan or Nigeria; I will write a whole essay against that comparison. But costly in the social sense — being known as a serious Christian is, in many American contexts, a social liability now. It was an asset for two centuries. It is becoming a liability again, the way it was for the first three centuries of the church.

This is the actual list. Most of it is loss. Some of it is mercy.

## What we are not losing

The actual faith is fine.

The doctrines are fine. The scriptures are fine. The sacraments are fine. The historic creeds are fine. The two thousand years of accumulated theological wisdom is fine. The remnant — the actual disciples in your church, the saints who pray when no one is watching, the elderly women whose lives have been formed by decades of faithful obedience — is fine.

The faith has survived the collapse of every cultural arrangement it has been embedded in. Egyptian. Roman. Frankish. Byzantine. Holy Roman. British imperial. Russian Orthodox under the tsars and then under the Soviets. South Korean under the Japanese occupation. Chinese under Mao. The faith has buried every empire it has lived inside and walked out of the grave. It will bury American Christendom too. The burying does not require our help. The burying is just the next chapter in a very long book.

## What Augustine knew

Augustine wrote *City of God* between 413 and 426, after the sack of Rome. He wrote it because Roman Christians were panicking. The empire they had thought was eternal had been violated by barbarians. The Christianity they had thought was permanently established was being blamed for the empire's weakness. Augustine sat down to write his response, and the response took him twelve years and twenty-two books.

What Augustine said, slowly and patiently and over many pages, was that the Roman Christians had confused two cities. They had assumed that Rome and the church were the same project, that Rome's strength was the church's strength, that Rome's fall would be the church's fall. They had assumed this because they had not been paying attention to what the church actually was. The church is the city of God — a community gathered out of every nation, scattered through every nation, with citizenship in a polis whose government is not yet finished being formed. The other city, the city of man, is the city of empire and ambition and power and self-love. The two cities have lived intermingled in human history since the fall, and will live intermingled until the end. The fall of any particular instance of the city of man is not the fall of the church. It is the fall of an arrangement the church had been borrowing.

I want this to land slowly. The American church is, on the whole, in the position of Augustine's Romans. We have confused the city of man with the city of God. We are reading the fall of our particular arrangement of cultural Christianity as the fall of Christianity itself. The reading is wrong. The fall is real. The fall is also not what we think it is.

## The grief, honored

The grief is real. I have grieved it. I have grieved it next to my elders' wives, in funerals where the next generation did not know how to handle the dying. I have grieved it in conversations with younger pastors who do not know how to do this work without the cultural scaffolding their teachers had. I have grieved it for my own sons, who will be raising their children in a country whose civic Christianity is not coming back.

The grief deserves to be honored. The grief deserves not to be papered over with hope. The grief is the cost of having loved a thing that was, in some real ways, lovable. Cultural Christianity gave us hospitals and schools and a public moral vocabulary and a thousand small dignities that the surrounding culture is not, on the whole, replacing with anything as humane. We can be honest about its failures and still grieve its loss. Both are appropriate. Both are part of the work.

## The verdict

We are not losing the faith. We are losing the costume. The costume was a thin baptism of cultural arrangements that did, for a long time, do real good — and did, for a long time, conceal what the church actually was. The concealment was part of the gift and part of the cost. The cost is coming due. The gift is going to have to take a different shape.

The church on the other side of this will be smaller, costlier, and more honest. It will be the church the early centuries knew. It will be the church the Black church in America has been all along. It will be the church the global south is teaching us how to be. It will, in many ways, be a better church than the one we had — though most of us, who lived inside the costume, will not be able to say so until we have actually grieved it.

The grieving is the work. The work has not been done. Most of the writing I have to do in this series is the work of grieving the right thing, in the right way, with the right hope at the end of it. The hope is real. The hope is also not what we expected.

We are not losing the faith. We never had it as completely as we thought. We are about to find out what we have actually had all along, and the finding out is going to take the rest of our generation.

I am writing for the church on the other side. Most of us will not make it. Some of us will. The ones who do will not recognize themselves at the end, and that, too, will be part of the mercy.
