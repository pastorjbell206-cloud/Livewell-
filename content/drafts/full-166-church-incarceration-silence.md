The United States incarcerates more people per capita than any nation on earth. [cite — confirm current figures] The people held in its prisons and jails are disproportionately poor, disproportionately Black and Brown, and disproportionately drawn from communities the church claims to care about. Many of them are fathers. Many of them are sons. Some of them are members of congregations that have not spoken their names from the pulpit since they were arrested.

The church has been mostly quiet about this. That silence is not neutrality. It is a statement about whose suffering qualifies for prophetic attention.

## What the text says

Jesus, in Matthew 25, names the incarcerated explicitly among those with whom he identifies: "I was in prison and you came to me." Not you visited the prison as a ministry. Not you donated to the prison chaplaincy budget. You came to me. The incarcerated person is, in Jesus's own account, the site of his presence. To visit is to encounter Christ. To ignore is to pass him.

The early church took this seriously. Prison epistles — Paul writing from Roman custody to the Philippians, to Philemon, to Timothy — form a significant portion of the New Testament. The early Christian community maintained contact with imprisoned members at considerable personal risk. By the third century, care for the imprisoned was considered a standard obligation of the Christian community, not a specialized ministry for the particularly zealous.

The medieval church had prison ministry. The Reformers wrote from prison. John Bunyan wrote Pilgrim's Progress in a Bedford jail. The Quakers, in the eighteenth century, pioneered prison reform as a theological conviction. This history is not incidental. It is the record of what the church believed it was obligated to do.

## What changed

The carceral expansion in the United States began in earnest in the 1970s and accelerated through the 1980s and 1990s. Policies that produced mandatory minimum sentences, three-strikes provisions, and the militarized prosecution of drug offenses produced an incarcerated population that grew from roughly 300,000 in 1975 to over two million by the early 2000s. [cite — confirm] These were policy choices, not the inevitable response to rising crime. In many cases, crime fell while incarceration rose. The expansion was political.

The church was not absent from the politics of this era. White evangelical Christianity was, in many places, an enthusiastic constituency for the policies that produced the expansion. The language of law and order, the equation of toughness on crime with moral seriousness, the cultural positioning of the incarcerated as the deserving victims of their own choices — these were not theologically neutral positions. They were positions the church, in many cases, baptized.

I say this as a person who holds conservative convictions on many things. I am not making a partisan argument. I am making a prophetic one. What the prophets consistently said about the powerful and the powerless is not a Democratic or Republican position. It is the position of the God who, in Isaiah 61:1, sends his anointed to proclaim liberty to the captives and the opening of the prison to those who are bound. That text is the text Jesus stands up and reads in the Nazareth synagogue in Luke 4 when he announces his own ministry. If that is where Jesus starts, the church that claims to follow him has to account for its relative indifference to the people still in the prisons.

## The specific forms of silence

The silence takes several shapes. There is the silence of the congregation that has no prison ministry, no reentry support, no relationship with the county jail, no advocacy for the people cycling through the system in its neighborhood — and has never named this as a gap. There is the silence of the preacher who addresses addiction, sexual sin, anger, and pride from the pulpit, and has never once named the racial disparities in drug prosecution that land a young Black man in a federal cell for a crime that earns a young white man a treatment referral. There is the silence of the denomination that has position papers on marriage and sexuality and medical ethics and has nothing systematic to say about an incarceration system that costs families generations.

These silences are choices. They are shaped by who is in the pews and who is not. The families of the incarcerated, the people who carry the stigma, the ones who can't get housing or employment because of a conviction from twenty years ago — they are less likely to be in the room when the elders decide what the church cares about. Absence from the room produces absence from the agenda.

## What faithfulness requires

Not silence. Not the silence of confusion or the silence of overextension or the silence of "we don't want to be political." The silence of the prophets was not a posture they adopted when the situation was complex. They named what the system was doing to the vulnerable, and they named it specifically.

Matthew 25 does not ask the church to have a carefully calibrated position on sentencing reform. It asks whether we visited. Whether we came. Whether the man in the cell knew that there were people outside it who had not forgotten him.

That is where it starts. The policy work is downstream from the visiting. The advocacy is downstream from the relationship. The reformation of an unjust system is downstream from the church deciding that the people inside it are not invisible.

They were never invisible to Jesus. We chose not to see them.
