Fifteen years of pastoring keeps sending me the same two people.

The first sits down in my office and apologizes before she says anything else. A doctor diagnosed her with depression years ago — her doctor, her word, not mine — and the medication helps, and that is what she has come to confess. A church she loved before this one taught her an arithmetic she cannot stop running: joy is a fruit of the Spirit, she cannot feel joy, therefore the branch must be bad. Someone in a Bible study told her the pills were a way of not trusting God. Someone else asked, gently and wrongly, what sin she might be harboring. So she has managed her illness the way other people manage an affair — in secret, off the family ledger, confessed to no one — and what she wants from me is permission. She wants to know whether she can take the medication and the Lord's Supper in the same week.

The second has no shame at all; he has vocabulary instead. He can name his anhedonia, his attachment style, the family system that produced him. He has a therapist he likes, and I am glad he does — that gladness is real, and it will matter later. But he is not sleeping either, and when we finally get underneath the language he tells me what the language was built over: a thing he did two years ago that he has never once called wrong out loud. Every frame he owns keeps translating the ache into symptoms — stress, rumination, an inner critic to be quieted. It has not worked, because some guilt is not a malfunction. Some guilt is working exactly as designed, like the pain that gets your hand off the stove, and there is no one left in his life with a category for that.

The church has failed them both, and failed them with the same confidence. To the first we said: this darkness is spiritual, so pray harder. To the second the culture said — and the church, tired of being embarrassed by its own record here, increasingly says along with it: every darkness is chemical or circumstantial, so there is nothing in you a professional cannot reach. Two opposite errors. One result. A person gets erased either way — reduced to a soul that happens to be haunting a body, or to a body that has somehow talked itself into believing it has a soul.

**So this essay stands on a claim I have spent fifteen years earning the right to make: depression is real, and the dark night of the soul is real, and they are not the same thing, and they can occupy the same person in the same season. A broken serotonin system is no more a verdict on your faith than a broken leg. And a soul under conviction, or in grief, or in the silence John of the Cross mapped, is not a disorder to be managed out of the way. The line between them is genuinely hard to find — which is exactly why no pastor should hunt for it alone, and no Christian should be ashamed of needing both a doctor and a church.**

## The sermon I preached wrong

I know the first error from the inside, because I committed it from a pulpit.

Early in my ministry a man in my congregation stopped being able to feel his own life. He said it almost that plainly — the color had gone out of things, prayer included, work included, his kids included — and I gave him what I had. Philippians 4. A reading plan. The assurance that the joy would return when the disciplines did. He was more faithful to my prescription than I have ever been to anyone's. He prayed more, read more, served more, and sank further, and now he carried the extra stone I had handed him: the certainty that his continued darkness was evidence against his faith, since he had done everything the pastor said and the lights had not come on. What finally helped him was a physician. What I had been treating as a doctrine problem had a body in it the whole time — sleep gone, appetite gone, concentration gone, everything grey at once — and I had walked past every one of those signals because my theology had no shelf to put them on. He forgave me before I asked. I have been slower, because I did not merely fail to help him. I baptized his suffering as his fault.

The error is not stupid, and it did not come from nowhere. Say the strongest version of it, because its defenders deserve that much: the pray-harder church is holding a truth the clinic cannot say out loud — that the soul is real, that some darkness does lift when sin is confessed and forgiven, that a human being is more than chemistry, and that handing every sorrow straight to the pharmacy is its own kind of despair. The people who say pray harder have usually watched prayer work. Their mistake is not believing too much in the soul. It is declining, in practice, to believe in the body — quietly refusing the doctrine of creation they would defend to the death on paper.

And the suspicion behind it was not invented from nothing. When the modern clinic introduced itself to the church, it introduced itself as a rival: Freud, in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927), classed religion as a wish-projection humanity would outgrow, a kind of inherited neurosis. The church took him at his word and returned fire for two generations, and our people bled in the crossfire — told, from pulpits like mine, that the enemy's medicine could not be God's provision. Add the newer teaching that equates belonging to God with feeling well, prosperity in its emotional key, and the arithmetic my first visitor runs was complete: if you are his, you should be happy; you are not happy; draw the conclusion. That arithmetic is not the theology of the psalms, half of which are written from the bottom of a well. It is a theology with no cross in it, and it breaks the people who believe it.

## The frame that swallowed the soul

The second error is younger and it is winning.

Philip Rieff saw it coming in *The Triumph of the Therapeutic* (1966): the culture's center of gravity shifting from religious man to psychological man — from the person who asks *am I forgiven* to the person who asks *am I well*, with well-being installed as the final measure and the clinic inheriting the authority the church used to hold. A person in crisis once went somewhere to be saved. Now he goes somewhere to feel better, and the difference between those two errands has quietly become invisible.

Be honest about why the therapeutic won: in large part, the church deserved to lose. The clinic sat with sufferers we had shamed. It did not ask the weeping woman what sin she was harboring. It kept people alive whom sermons like my early ones were actively harming, and its medicine works — measurably, repeatably, mercifully. None of that is the enemy of the faith. When I say I am glad the man has a therapist, it is not diplomacy.

But a frame with no category for God cannot see everything, and what it cannot see, it renames. Conviction becomes toxic guilt — and since the frame can only treat the feeling of guilt, never the fact of it, the man in my office got two years of increasingly sophisticated language for an ache that wanted one old word. Grief becomes a disorder: in 2022 the DSM's revisers added prolonged grief to the manual, so that mourning which outstays roughly a year can now be coded and billed. The clinicians who added it were not villains; they were reaching for mourners whose grief had genuinely trapped them. But notice what kind of culture needs the category — one with no practices left for carrying sorrow, so the only place to put a widow who still weeps at fourteen months is a diagnostic bin. And the dark night — the deliberate, purifying silence of God that the tradition spent centuries learning to recognize — reads, in this frame, as low-grade depression to be medicated at the first sign of dryness. The verdict on the therapeutic is the one this platform keeps reaching about every secular account worth taking seriously: it got the symptom right and the anthropology wrong. The first error erases the body. This one erases the soul, and it does it politely, with excellent intentions, one renamed reality at a time.

## The body that groans

I did not come to faith believing in souls. I was an atheist first, and the atheist I was would have sided cheerfully with the second error: the mind is the brain, the brain is matter, and everything called spiritual is chemistry that has not been explained yet. Becoming a Christian did not require me to renounce that respect for the body. It required me to stop believing the body was the whole story.

Because Scripture's picture of a human being is stranger than either error. God forms dust and breathes into it, "and the man became a living creature" (Genesis 2:7, ESV) — not a soul issued a container, not meat that learned to dream, but one creature, dust and breath in a unity so tight the seam never shows. And that unity is fallen all the way through. Paul is explicit that the wreckage runs wider than the will: "For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now. And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:22–23, ESV). The brain is creation. Three pounds of it, electric and bruisable, and it groans with everything else — which is why a failing thyroid can flatten a saint, why the weeks after childbirth can turn a faithful woman's mind against her, why a man can inherit darkness down the family line the way he inherits his father's hands. Calling that inheritance a faith problem is not rigor. It is cruelty with a verse attached. And notice where Paul's sentence lands: not on escape from the body but on "the redemption of our bodies." Christianity is, in this sense, the least spiritualized of faiths. It does not promise to rescue you out of your nervous system. It promises to raise it.

## What John of the Cross actually wrote

The soul's side of the line has a map, and we owe it to a prisoner.

In December 1577 John of the Cross was seized by his own Carmelite brothers — the reform he served had enemies inside the order — and locked in a cell in Toledo scarcely larger than his body. He escaped the next summer, and out of that darkness came a poem, and out of the poem, in the years that followed (c. 1578–1585), the commentary the world knows as *The Dark Night of the Soul*. We have worn his phrase down to a synonym for any bad season, and the wear has cost us his precision. John was not describing sadness. He was describing a specific work of God: the deliberate withdrawal of felt sweetness from a believer who had, without noticing, begun to love the sweetness more than the God it pointed to — consolation removed the way a surgeon removes what the patient cannot heal around, so the soul learns to love God in the dark, for himself, with nothing in it for her. Not abandonment. Weaning.

But the detail we skip is that John wrote a differential. In the first book of the commentary he sets down signs for telling his dark night apart from its counterfeits, and he names *melancholy* — his century's word for what a clinician would now assess as depression — as a condition that can mimic the night or tangle with it. The marks he gives are worth restating in plain words. In the true night, the dryness empties prayer of all its old pleasure, yet the ache stays aimed at God: the soul is anxious *about him*, afraid of having failed him, longing without a landing place. The person still cares, painfully, in one particular direction. Mere melancholy, John says, carries no such Godward solicitude — the dryness is just dryness, weather without a compass. The founding text of the dark night refuses, on its own pages, to treat every darkness as the dark night. We kept his phrase and lost his caution.

Held alongside what medicine has learned since, John's marks still work, roughly, and I hand them to you as a pastor and not as a screening tool. The dark night tends to be strangely local: prayer goes dead while dinner still tastes like something, the work still holds some color, and the grief is *about God* — his silence is the wound. Depression tends to flatten the whole field at once — God, food, work, sleep, the faces of your own children going grey together, the body sinking with the mind. If everything has gone grey, your body is telling you something, and the faithful response is a doctor's appointment this week, not a longer quiet time. If the world still has color and only God has gone silent while you ache toward him, the tradition has a name and a map for where you are, and I have written about that country elsewhere. And often — this is the maddening part, and the true one — it is both, in the same chest, in the same month. The two travel together, each darkening the other. Which is why the sorting was never meant to be homework you finish alone.

## The preacher who kept fainting

If the pray-harder equation were true, church history would falsify it on one man alone.

On the evening of 19 October 1856, Charles Spurgeon — twenty-two years old, already the most famous preacher in England — stood before thousands at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall when someone shouted fire. There was no fire. There was a stampede, and seven people died in it, and Spurgeon was carried from the pulpit a different man than the one who had mounted it. By the testimony of those closest to him, and by his own, the darkness that opened that night revisited him for the rest of his life.

He did not hide it. He built a lecture for his pastoral students — it sits in *Lectures to My Students* under the title "The Minister's Fainting Fits" — and told them, in substance, that seasons of causeless despondency had fallen on him so heavily he would not wish the acquaintance on any of them; that the mind can sink to depths the body never reaches; that such fits visit most who carry the weight of souls, and that the men who comfort best are the ones who have been in the dark themselves. He refused to call the darkness sin, in himself or anyone. He prescribed what his century had — rest, time away, the company of friends — and he kept preaching, heavy, for decades, to the largest congregation in the world. His theology did not cure him. His theology carried him, and the difference between those two verbs is nearly the whole argument of this essay. No one audits Spurgeon's orthodoxy. If the medicine of his century had been better, he would have been right to take it, and we would have lost nothing of him but some of the agony.

## Bread before the whisper

Scripture got to all of this first, and it got there with a story the church keeps preaching around the edges of.

First Kings 18 is the triumph: fire from heaven on Carmel, the prophets of Baal routed, the long drought broken, Elijah vindicated in front of the whole nation. Then one queen sends one threat, and the man who called down fire runs for his life — south past Beersheba, out into the wilderness, alone.

> But he himself went a day's journey into the wilderness and came and sat down under a broom tree. And he asked that he might die, saying, "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life, for I am no better than my fathers." And he lay down and slept under a broom tree. And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, "Arise and eat." And he looked, and behold, there was at his head a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water. And he ate and drank and lay down again. And the angel of the LORD came again a second time and touched him and said, "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you." And he arose and ate and drank, and went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mount of God. (1 Kings 19:4–8, ESV)

The text says it as plainly as it can be said: he asked that he might die. Scripture does not flinch from the sentence, does not dress it up, and does not linger over it, and I will follow the text on all three. If that sentence has taken up residence in you, notice that God does not argue with Elijah about it. What he does instead is stranger and better than an argument.

Count what is missing from God's response. No rebuke. No sermon. Not one word, yet, about the theology of despair — and there was plenty to correct; Elijah's claim to be the last faithful man in Israel was factually wrong. Instead: sleep. A touch. Bread, warm, and water. More sleep. Bread again. The first thing the LORD of hosts does for a prophet who wants to die is feed him and let him rest, twice, and the only sentence spoken under that tree may be the most pastoral in the Old Testament — "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you." Not: your faith is too small. The road is too long, the strength is gone, and God addresses the body first because the man is a creature and his Maker has never once been embarrassed by that.

Only later, at Horeb, comes the question — "What are you doing here, Elijah?" — and Elijah's answer, given twice, word for word, is the isolation talking: I alone am left, and they want my life. Then the wind that tore the mountains, "but the LORD was not in the wind"; the earthquake and the fire, and the LORD was not in them either; "and after the fire the sound of a low whisper" (1 Kings 19:11–12, ESV). And even then God never explains the darkness. He re-gives the man his work — kings to anoint, a successor to call. He gives him a companion; Elisha will not leave his side. And he corrects despair's arithmetic with a number: seven thousand in Israel who have never bowed to Baal. The despair claimed *only I am left*. God did not debate the claim. He outnumbered it.

The order of the text is the theology. Body, presence, word, work, company — in that sequence, none skipped. One of our errors starts at the word and shames the body; the other stops at the bread and starves the soul. God ran the whole sequence, in order, for a man at the bottom, and any care that calls itself biblical has to keep his order.

And if the broom tree is where you are tonight — if the darkness has begun to tell you that you should not be here, that the people you love would be lighter without you — then hear one thing said with no art at all: that thought is a symptom, not a verdict, and it lies with tremendous fluency, and it is lying now. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour, and a human being will answer. Do that, and then tell one person who loves you, out loud, today. God's first answer to a prophet who asked to die was to keep him alive and feed him. Let the people near you be the angel with the bread.

## Both a doctor and a church

Some of what this essay claims is doctrine, and most of it is not, and the difference matters. That bodies are real, created, fallen, and destined for resurrection — that is first-order ground, creed-level, not up for a vote. That souls are real, that guilt can be true, that God sometimes withdraws sweetness on purpose — doctrine as well, old as the psalms. But where the line falls in any particular life — whether this Tuesday's darkness is a body failing, a soul in surgery, or both at once — is not doctrine at all. It is wisdom, and Scripture is blunt about how wisdom arrives: "in an abundance of counselors there is safety" (Proverbs 11:14, ESV). Plural on purpose. A pastor alone is not an abundance. Neither is a prescription pad.

So let me say the plain thing in my own voice rather than in fine print: I am a pastor, not a clinician, and nothing in this essay is medical advice. It is one shepherd's account of the territory, written so that you will be quicker, not slower, to put real help around you. What fifteen years has actually built in me is a shorter road to the phone — the names of counselors and a psychiatrist I trust, handed out the way we hand out casseroles, as ordinary church. And two questions I did not know to ask the man I failed: Has everything gone grey, or is the ache aimed at God? And — before any of that — when did you last sleep through a night? The people I have pastored through both darknesses tell me the same two things from opposite directions. The medication did not medicate God away; it cleared enough fog for them to find him in the dark, still there, un-offended by the pill. And the therapy did not absolve them; a session cannot forgive sins, and the honest therapists never claimed it could — but it taught them to sleep, and forgiveness is easier to believe at seven in the morning than at three.

I told the woman in my office what I will tell you: take the medication and take the Supper, in the same week, with the same clean conscience, because both come from the hand of the same Lord — one through the common grace he gives to medicine, one through the covenant grace he gives to his church, and he is not divided against himself. I told the man the old word for what he did, and then the older word, forgiveness, and what it cost, and we needed no scare quotes for either. And I keep the memory of my wrong sermon where I can see it, because the man I preached it to deserves to be more than my regret. He is my instruction.

The line between the body's darkness and the soul's is hard to find. That is not a flaw in the map; it is a fact about creatures — we are dust and breath in one piece, and the seam does not show. So the darkness, when it comes, will rarely announce its department. Go to the doctor. Stay in the church. Say the psalm you cannot feel and take the pill you wish you did not need, and let no one — not the deacon with the verse, not the culture with the diagnosis — talk you out of half of what you are. Elijah ate the bread before he heard the whisper. Both came from the same God, tending the same man, who was one creature the whole way down. So are you.
