The question does not knock. It is already in the room.

You are driving, or you are awake at two in the morning, or you are halfway through a hymn you have sung a thousand times, and it surfaces with no warning and no mercy. What if we are wrong. What if the whole thing is a beautiful, two-thousand-year-old story that frightened people told themselves so they could bear the dark. What if the building you have given your life to was built on nothing, and everyone inside it is too invested to say so. The question does not feel like temptation. It feels like clarity. That is what makes it terrifying.

I want to say something that most pastors will not say from a stage. I have asked it. I came to faith from atheism, so the question is not a foreign army to me; it is my own first language, and it never fully leaves. There are mornings I wake up and for a few seconds the whole edifice looks like wishful thinking, and I have learned not to panic at those seconds. A faith that has never met the question has not been tested. It has only been sheltered. And sheltered faith breaks the first time the weather turns.

So let us actually ask it, without flinching. What if we are wrong.

Start by noticing what the question assumes. It assumes that doubt is the neutral position and belief is the leap, that the skeptic stands on solid ground and the believer floats. But this is not neutral at all. It is a particular faith with its own unexamined creed. To say the universe is finally meaningless, that consciousness is an accident, that love is a chemical event dressed up by evolution to keep us breeding, is to make claims as large as any in the Apostles' Creed, and to make them without evidence the skeptic would accept from anyone else. Everyone is believing something they cannot prove. The honest question is not whether you have faith. The honest question is which faith carries the weight of an actual life.

Charles Taylor gave this its name. The doubt is contagious.

In *A Secular Age* he describes the modern condition as one where every belief is haunted by its alternatives. The believer feels the pull of unbelief; the unbeliever feels the pull of belief in the cathedral, at the deathbed, under the stars. None of us holds a position the others cannot reach. Five hundred years ago atheism was nearly unthinkable. Now belief is the contested thing, and we feel the contesting in our own chests. Taylor's point is not that this proves anyone right. His point is that the cross-pressure is the air we all breathe, and the man who thinks his doubt is pure reason is as naive as the man who thinks his belief has never wavered. We are all standing in the same wind.

But the question deserves a real answer, not just a reframe, so here is where I plant my feet.

If we are wrong, we are wrong about a tomb. Tom Holland, who is no apologist, spent a long secular career assuming Christianity was a footnote and arrived at the opposite conclusion, that nearly everything the modern West believes about human dignity, the worth of the weak, the conscience of the individual, is downstream of a Galilean crucifixion that should have ended in nothing. It did not end in nothing. Something happened that turned a scattering of terrified Galileans into people who would die rather than recant a thing they claimed to have seen. People do not die for a story they know they invented. They die for a body they are sure was missing, and a face they are sure they saw. You can call them deluded. You cannot easily call them liars, and you cannot explain them away as cleanly as the secular story needs you to. The resurrection is either the worst-attested fraud in history or the hinge of it. There is no comfortable middle.

And notice what scripture does with doubt, because it is not what your fear expects.

It does not banish the doubters. It builds on one. Thomas says he will not believe unless he puts his hand in the wounds, and Jesus does not rebuke him from a distance. He shows up. *Put your finger here. See my hands.* The risen Christ accommodates the doubter, invites the hand into the wound, makes room for the man who needed evidence. The faith was never afraid of Thomas. The church that is afraid of its Thomases has lost its nerve and forgotten its own founding scene. And the father in Mark 9 says the truest sentence a doubter ever prayed, *I believe; help my unbelief,* holding both at once, faith and its absence in the same breath, and Jesus heals the boy anyway. He did not wait for the man to resolve the contradiction. He worked through the crack.

There is a second answer, and it is quieter than the tomb but it has kept me up on its own nights. If we are wrong, we are wrong about the things we cannot stop believing anyway. That the weak deserve protection. That a person is worth more than his usefulness. That cruelty is not merely inefficient but evil, actually evil, evil in the dark whether or not anyone is watching. The secular story cannot ground a single one of these and knows it. Tom Holland's whole argument is that we are running on borrowed Christian capital, spending convictions we did not earn and cannot account for, certain that human dignity is real while denying the only soil it ever grew in. The atheist who weeps at injustice is a believer with cold feet. He has kept the morality and burned the only mortgage that paid for it. I lived in that house. I know how warm it stays for a while after the fire goes out.

So what do you do with the question that is already in the room.

You let it sit. You do not medicate it with louder worship or kill it with a quick apologetic you do not really believe. You hold it the way the psalmists held theirs, out loud, addressed to a God you are not yet sure is listening. *Why do you hide your face?* You read the people who asked it harder than you and stayed. And you make a wager, because there is no position that escapes the wager, only positions that pretend to. The skeptic wagers his life on emptiness. You wager yours on the tomb. Both of you are betting. Only one of you is honest about it.

Here is what fifteen years of pastoring has shown me. The people whose faith finally held were never the ones who never doubted. They were the ones who took the question all the way down, hit the floor, and found it was not the bottom. Bonhoeffer wrote his deepest faith from a cell, expecting to be hanged, and was. Doubt did not destroy him. It refined him until what was left could not be taken.

You are afraid the question will end your faith. It might end a version of it. Let it.

What if we are wrong. And what if, having asked it honestly, you find you cannot get the stone to stay in front of the tomb.
