You did everything you were told and the math still did not work.

You prayed and the marriage ended anyway. You tithed and the diagnosis came anyway. You raised the kids in the faith and one of them walked away anyway, and somewhere in the wreckage you ran the equation again — the one you were handed in your first year of believing. Faithfulness in, blessing out. It was supposed to balance. It did not balance. And the not-balancing produced a quieter terror than open atheism ever did, because the God you doubted was not a God you had rejected. He was a God who had stopped adding up, and that is the question that drives a believer to type the words into the dark — why doesn't God make sense, why did I do everything right and lose anyway.

I came to faith from atheism, so let me be honest about what I expected on the way in. I expected the universe to make more sense once God was in it. I expected the equation to finally resolve, the loose ends to gather, the cruelty of things to acquire a reason. What I found instead was a God who would not be solved, and it took me years to understand that the unsolvability was not the problem with the faith. **It was the faith.** I had come looking for an answer and been handed a person, and at first I could not tell the difference between those two things, and the whole crisis lived in that gap.

## We were sold a calculation

Somewhere in American Christianity the gospel got reduced to a transaction, and the transaction promised coherence above all.

Tim Keller spent a career taking that apart. In *Walking with God through Pain and Suffering* (2013) he showed that the older, sturdier faith never promised the books would balance in this life — that the assumption of a clean moral ledger, good rewarded and evil punished on a schedule we can audit, is not Christianity but a folk religion wearing its clothes. The prosperity preachers promised the balance outright. The self-help-with-a-cross crowd promised it in softer language, the abundant life as a return on spiritual investment. But the actual Scriptures hand you something stranger and far less marketable. They hand you Job, and Job is the book the equation cannot survive.

Here is a man the text itself calls blameless, and the calamity falls anyway — all of it, in an afternoon. The livestock, the servants, the ten children, the health, the standing. His friends arrive, and they are the theology most of us were raised in, whether we noticed or not. They run the math out loud. Suffering means sin, they reason, so confess the sin and the suffering will lift, because that is how the equation works and the equation must work or the universe is unsafe. They are not cruel men. That is the part we miss. They are orthodox men, careful men, men defending the coherence of God against the obscene evidence of their friend's body — and they spend thirty-some chapters defending it, building the case, quoting the tradition. And at the end God turns to them and says they have not spoken rightly of him.

Read that slowly. The men who defended God's logic are the men God rebukes. The man who shouted at heaven and demanded an accounting is the man God commends. The book is an assault on the equation, written into the canon, left there on purpose by people who knew exactly how dangerous it was to the comfortable version of the faith.

## The answer that refuses to be an answer

And when God finally answers Job, out of the whirlwind, he does not answer the question.

He does not explain the suffering. He never mentions the wager in the prologue, never tells Job why, never produces the chart that would make the afternoon add up. He asks instead about the foundations of the earth, the storehouses of the snow, the wild donkey who scorns the city, the war horse who laughs at fear, the eagle who makes her nest on the crag. It looks like evasion. A frightened reader hears it as a divine non-answer, God changing the subject because he has no defense. It is not evasion. **It is God refusing to be reduced to a function that Job can compute.** And Job's response, when it comes, is not new information. It is relationship. *I had heard of you with the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.* He did not get the answer. He got the Answerer. Those are not the same thing, and the entire book turns on the difference.

This is harder for us than it was for Job, and Charles Taylor told us why. In *A Secular Age* (2007) he describes the buffered self — the modern person sealed inside his own mind, for whom meaning is something we manufacture rather than something we receive. We are the first people in history trained from birth to expect the cosmos to be transparent to reason. We assume that if a thing is real it can be explained, and that if it cannot be explained it is probably not real, or not worth our trust. So when God does not make sense, we hear it as evidence against him, a failure of the system to deliver the transparency the system owed us. Job did not have that reflex. He had the older reflex, the one we lost — the capacity to stay in the room with a God he could not parse, to remain in relationship with an unexplained Person rather than walking out in search of an explanation that fit. We have lost the reflex, and the losing of it is why our doubt cuts deeper and heals slower than his ever did.

## The chart is always cold

I have stood at enough hospital beds to know the equation does not comfort anyone. It only condemns.

When you tell a grieving mother that God has a reason, you have made yourself one of Job's friends, and you have not noticed that the book you are quoting from is the very book that silences you. I have done it. Early on I did it from a kind of fear — the fear that if I could not explain God I would lose him, that an unexplained God was a God halfway to vanishing. So I explained him. I explained him to people who needed his presence and got my chart instead, and the chart was cold, the chart is always cold, because it is the thing we reach for precisely when we cannot bear the silence. We hand the grieving a reason because a reason gives us something to say, and our terror of having nothing to say is so great that we will wound the suffering to relieve it. The friends of Job talked for thirty chapters. The kindest thing in the book is the seven days at the start when they sat with him on the ground and said nothing, because his suffering was very great. Then they opened their mouths, and the comfort ended.

There is a difference, though, between a God who does not make sense and a God who is senseless, and everything hangs on it. A senseless God is chaos with a name — caprice, a coin flip, a force with no coherence behind the silence. The God of Job is not chaos. He is coherent in himself and only opaque to us, the way the foundations of the earth are coherent and only opaque to a man who was not there when they were laid. The opacity is not in God. It is in the gap between his vantage and ours, and that gap is not an insult. It is simply the truth about a creature standing before its Creator. To demand that God be transparent to you is to demand that he be your size. He declines. The declining is mercy, though it does not feel like mercy at the time.

Keller pressed exactly this point, and it is worth following all the way down. A God small enough to be fully explained by you would be a God too small to save you. The very incomprehensibility that feels, in the hospital corridor, like abandonment is the flip side of the thing you actually need — a God whose purposes run deeper than your sight, which means deeper than your catastrophe. If you could audit his reasons, he would be a manager, not a maker. And a managed universe holds no rescue you did not already foresee. The unexplained God is the only kind worth crying out to, because he is the only kind who might be doing something in the dark that you, from inside the dark, cannot yet see. **The same opacity that wounds you is the only ground there is for hope.** Take away the mystery and you take away the rescue in the same motion.

This is the thing the equation can never hold, and why the equation finally breaks every honest person who clings to it. The equation assumes you can see the whole board. It assumes the relevant variables are the ones in your line of sight — your faithfulness, your prayer, your tithe, your obedience — and that the output should follow from those inputs on a timetable you can read. Job's friends believed they could see the whole board. That was their error, dressed up as orthodoxy. They thought God's justice had to be legible to them or it was not justice, and so when the evidence stopped fitting their math, they did not doubt the math. They blamed the man. The equation always ends there. When reality refuses to balance, the equation does not confess its own poverty. It finds someone to condemn — your friend, or God, or finally yourself.

## Lament is the language of people who still believe

The Psalms live in this exact gap, and they are louder about it than we are comfortable with.

*How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?* That is Psalm 13, and it is not a crisis of faith. It is faith doing the one thing only faith can do, which is to address the silence rather than walk away from it. The atheist does not write Psalm 13. The atheist has no one to shout at; the silence is just silence, the universe being what it always was. Lament is the native tongue of people who still believe and cannot make it add up, and Scripture is full of it — a third of the Psalter, by some counts — because God is not embarrassed by it. He canonized it. He put the angry prayers in the book and called them prayer. The church that has no room for *how long* has a smaller God than the one who inspired the question, a God who can only be praised and never wept at, and that God will not survive a hospital corridor at midnight.

So what do you do when God does not make sense. You stop demanding that he do — not because the demand is wrong, since Job demanded and was praised, but because the demand was never the point and resolution was never the promise. You learn the difference between an explanation and a presence, and you learn it the hard way, usually at a graveside, usually after the charts have all failed you. You pray the angry Psalms instead of swallowing them, because the swallowed prayer rots into the unspoken doubt that finally walks you out the door. And you wait in the silence the way Job waited — not because the silence is comfortable but because the alternative is to walk out of the room where God actually is, into a room where everything makes sense and no one is home. That second room is on offer. It always is. It is clean and bright and explicable and empty, and a great many people have moved into it and called the emptiness honesty.

The cross is the place this all converges. The most God-forsaken cry in Scripture comes from the mouth of God himself — *my God, my God, why have you forsaken me* — and it is a question, and it is not answered from the sky, and three days later the answer turns out to be a Person and not a sentence. That is the pattern. It has always been the pattern. **The God of the Bible does not hand you the equation. He climbs into the part of it that does not balance** and dies there, and rises, and the rising is not an explanation either. It is a presence that outlasts the question.

You wanted a God who would make sense. You were given a God who makes scars, and shows them to the doubter, and says, put your hand here.

Stop solving him. Start following him into the dark, where the seeing finally happens.
