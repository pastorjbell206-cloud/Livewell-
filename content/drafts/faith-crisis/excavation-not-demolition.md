The word arrives across a table, usually with the coffee going cold, and it arrives braced. Someone I have pastored for a decade, or someone who drove forty minutes because they could not risk saying it at their own church, looks down and says it the way people report a diagnosis. *I think I'm deconstructing.* Then they wait, because they have learned to wait. They know the two speeches. There is the alarm speech, where the pastor hears the word as a positive test result and reaches for the apologetics shelf, and there is the celebration speech, available these days one podcast over, where the same word is treated as a graduation. Panic or applause. Both speeches make the same mistake. Neither one asks what the person is actually doing with the crowbar in their hands.

I have never been able to give the first speech, because I am disqualified. I came to faith from atheism, which means I met Christianity the way an inspector meets a building he has been assured is condemned. Every argument the deconstructing Christian discovers at two in the morning — the contradictions, the crusades and the cover-ups, the suspicion that the whole thing is a coping mechanism with a steeple on it — I did not have to discover. It was my native language. I stood outside the house for years agreeing with the demolition order, and when I finally went in, I went in swinging. I hit everything in that house as hard as any insider mid-crisis ever could, because I had no sentiment to protect and no grandmother watching. So the word does not frighten me.

But I cannot give the celebration speech either, and this is the harder confession. I have been a pastor for fifteen years, and in some of those years I preached a faith that could not survive the questions my own people would eventually ask it. I taped things together — the gospel and the country, the gospel and the party, the gospel and the quiet promise that God's chief interest was the smooth operation of a middle-class life — and I called the bundle Christianity, and I handed it to families who trusted me, and when the bundle failed in their hands, some of them concluded that God had failed. Some of the deconstruction happening in my county is the disassembly of sermons with my name on them. I do not write about this from a safe distance. There is no safe distance.

**So this essay stands on the one claim I owe every person at that table: deconstruction has a direction problem, not a tool problem. Demolition and excavation use the same instruments — the pry bar, the shovel, the refusal to respect a wall just because it is standing. From the street the two look identical. The difference is what you are digging for. One digs to get out. The other digs to get down — because under the additions, under everything the last few centuries nailed onto the house, there is something older, and the digging can be the way down to it rather than the way out of it. Demolition, or excavation. Nearly everything anyone will tell you about deconstruction sits downstream of that distinction, and almost nobody names it.**

## The grief is the honest part

Start where the tearing starts, because it rarely starts with an argument. It starts with a wound, and the wounds are not imaginary. In May 2022, Guidepost Solutions released its independent investigation into the Southern Baptist Convention's Executive Committee and found that leaders of the largest Protestant denomination in America had kept a private list of ministers accused of sexual abuse — hundreds of entries, maintained for years — while telling survivors who called for help that nothing could be done. The year before that, a posthumous investigation commissioned by his own ministry confirmed that Ravi Zacharias, the most celebrated apologist of his generation, a man whose books sat on my shelf, had used the cover of that ministry to prey on women. Beneath the covered cases run the unreported local ones: the church that protected the deacon and exiled the girl, the youth group where a kid's honest question was treated as a discipline problem.

And the pulpits that should have named all of it were often busy with other business. Over the last half century, large sections of the American church bound themselves to partisan politics so tightly that millions can no longer find the seam between the faith and the platform. I have written about that fusion elsewhere. Here it is enough to say that when a young woman concludes the church is a voting bloc with a music budget, she did not reach that conclusion irrationally. We handed her the evidence.

Then there are the answers, which for many people were the deepest wound, because they came from the people who were supposed to know. You asked why your father left, and got a slogan about God's plan. You asked how a good God watches a children's oncology ward, and were handed a verse like a coupon. I asked the first of those questions myself, as a boy growing up without a father, and I remember the coupons. A faith that answers its hardest questions with merchandise is training its most serious people to leave, and then blaming them for the training.

I know the counter-speech, and it deserves its strongest form: some deconstruction is appetite looking for an alibi — a decision already made, out shopping for respectable reasons. That is true of some. I have met them. But it is not true of most of the people at my table, and a diagnosis that flatters the diagnostician should always be checked twice. Because the person taking the faith apart is very often the one who took it most seriously. You do not tear at a thing you never expected to hold. The kid who actually read the whole Bible and noticed what is in it. The woman who actually believed the church was the body of Christ and therefore could not shrug when it behaved like any other corporation protecting its brand. The pew beside them can relax precisely because it never leaned its full weight on any of it. The deconstructor leaned, and something cracked, and the cracking sound was faith being taken seriously. Deconstruction is not usually rebellion. It is usually grief with a crowbar.

## The same tools

The word itself carries thirty seconds of useful history. Jacques Derrida coined *deconstruction* in the 1960s as a term of art for a way of reading texts; by the time it reached the hashtag it meant something simpler and sadder — the taking apart of an inherited evangelical faith, piece by piece, usually in public, usually in grief. I am not interested in policing the vocabulary. I am interested in the direction of the digging.

Because the alarm speech omits a fact it cannot afford to admit: most of what American deconstruction removes deserved to be removed. In 2005 the sociologist Christian Smith and his colleague Melinda Lundquist Denton published *Soul Searching*, the largest study of American teenage religion yet conducted, and gave the country's actual folk faith a name — moralistic therapeutic deism. Its creed, as they summarized it, runs roughly so: a God exists who watches over things; he wants people to be good and nice; the point of life is to be happy and feel good about yourself; God does not need to be involved unless you have a problem; good people go to heaven when they die. Read that list slowly and notice what is missing — sin, cross, resurrection, judgment, grace, cost — which is to say, notice that Christianity is missing. That is the deity a great deal of deconstruction is deconstructing. Beside him stand his colleagues: the tribal god who blesses one party's platform, the prosperity god who pays out for faith like a slot machine, the national god whose cross doubles as a flagpole. When a person takes that pantheon apart, they are not attacking the faith. They are agreeing with its first commandment. The church has a word for the demolition of false gods, and the word is not apostasy. Most of the rubble deserved to be rubble.

But now the question that decides everything, the one nobody at the demolition site wants to stop and answer: what are you digging *for*? A wrecking crew and an archaeological dig look identical from the road — the same dust, the same debris pile, the same disrespect for the existing structure. The difference is entirely in what the diggers believe is down there. The demolisher digs to clear the lot. The excavator digs because he has reason to think the ugly additions were additions — that under the vinyl siding and the drop ceilings and the seasonal flags there is a structure older than everything he hates about the house. Same shovel. Opposite prayer.

And I have watched what happens when the digging has no downward direction, so I will name it, gently, because I have loved some of these people. The crater becomes an address. There are men and women ten years into the demolition whose entire creed is now the list of things they no longer believe, who have made a settled identity out of the taking-apart — and taking apart is genuinely easier than building: the same feeling of progress at a fraction of the cost, and always an audience for the sound of breaking glass. Rubble is honest. Nobody can live in it. And the voices selling permanent demolition as freedom have usually found a way to bill by the swing.

## What the shovel hits

Dig down through the American additions and you do not reach empty dirt. The shovel rings. There is stone under this house, and the stone has dates carved into it.

In the year 325, the emperor Constantine convened bishops at Nicaea — among them, tradition remembers, men still carrying the marks of Diocletian's persecution, a missing eye, a maimed leg — to settle the teaching of an Alexandrian presbyter named Arius. Give Arius his due, because he was no cartoon. He was guarding the majesty of God the only way he could see, reasoning that the Father alone is without beginning, and that the Son, however exalted, must therefore be the first and greatest of creatures — nearest to God, but on our side of the line. It is a clean, plausible, reverent-sounding position; forms of it get reinvented every century. The council heard it and answered with a word chosen precisely because it could not be bent back into cleanliness: *homoousios* — the Son is of one being with the Father. Not the greatest creature. God from God, light from light. The church looked at the most reasonable reduction on offer and refused it, at a moment when some of the men refusing had already bled for less.

In 451 the Council of Chalcedon did the same service at the other pole of the mystery, confessing Christ as one person in two natures — fully God, fully man — and fencing the confession with four negations, usually translated *without confusion, without change, without division, without separation*. Notice what kind of sentences these are. They are not the product of comfortable men smoothing over a quarrel. They are the product of a church under pressure refusing every available simplification, from every direction, at cost, because each simplification would have made the faith easier and false.

That is the bedrock, and it has been under demolition for seventeen centuries. It is still there. Arius swung at it. The philosophers of the eighteenth century swung at it. The scholars who voted on the sayings of Jesus with colored beads swung at it. Empires that outlawed it and empires that domesticated it swung at it from opposite sides. Durability is not proof. But an honest demolisher, hearing the shovel ring like that, should at least grow curious about what kind of stone survives every crew that ever pronounced it finished.

And notice how small the floor is. Nicaea says nothing about your politics. Nothing about worship styles, the age of the earth, the timeline of the end, the schooling of children, or most of the fence lines the church that hurt you treated as matters of salvation. The creed that defines the faith is shorter than that church's membership covenant. Which means a large share of what is currently falling off your house was never load-bearing. Much of it was never in the blueprints at all.

## A monk with a pry bar

The church has been through this before, and the record is worth reading before you decide how your own dig ends. On October 31, 1517, an Augustinian friar and university professor named Martin Luther posted ninety-five theses for academic debate in Wittenberg — in Latin, addressed to scholars, opening with the claim (I paraphrase his first thesis) that when Christ said "repent" he meant the whole of a believer's life, not a transaction at a booth. Luther was not founding a movement. He was filing a repair request. He aimed at something specific — the indulgence trade, the machinery that had turned grace into revenue — and he expected the house to accept the repair. It excommunicated him instead, in 1521, and what we call the Reformation is in large part the story of a repair the institution refused.

What Luther declined to demolish tells you what kind of digger he was. He kept the creeds — all of them. Kept the Trinity of Nicaea and the two natures of Chalcedon, kept baptism and the Supper, kept the shape of the worship service with the false parts cut out, and put the Scriptures back at the center in the people's own language. The movement's slogan was a digging word: *ad fontes*, back to the sources — down through the medieval additions to Scripture and the fathers underneath. Luther swung as hard at the church of his day as anyone has ever swung, and he swung downward, toward a floor he was certain was there. It was an excavation that the institution experienced as a demolition, and the difference between those two descriptions is the entire argument of this essay.

I will not romanticize it. The digging cracked Europe. Wars followed it, and the fragments are still fragmenting five centuries later; an honest telling holds the wreckage in the same hands as the recovery. Excavation is not safe. Nothing about any of this is safe. The claim was never that digging costs nothing. The claim is that direction decides what the cost buys.

There is an older scene that catches the strange grief of rebuilding. When the exiles came home from Babylon and laid the foundation of the second temple, Ezra records that the young men shouted for joy while the old men who had seen the first house wept aloud, and the sound of weeping could not be told apart from the sound of joy (Ezra 3:12–13). Every rebuilt faith sounds like that. What rises on the old foundation will not look like what you lost, and there will be weeping mixed into the shouting, and the mixture is not failure. It is what recovery has always sounded like.

## Six verbs, one calling

There is one place in Scripture where God himself hands a man the wrecking bar, and it repays slow reading. In the thirteenth year of King Josiah — around 627 BC — the word of the LORD comes to a young man in the priest-town of Anathoth, and the commission runs like this:

> "See, I have set you this day over nations and over kingdoms, to pluck up and to break down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant." (Jeremiah 1:10, ESV)

Count the verbs. Six. Four of them are demolition — pluck up, break down, destroy, overthrow — and two of them are construction — build, plant — and all six are one calling, issued in one breath, to one man. God did not commission an optimist. He commissioned a demolisher, and he put the demolition first, because what Judah called faith had grown additions that had to come down before anything true could stand: the fusion of God and nation, the certainty that the temple guaranteed their safety, a religion that had become the state's chaplain. Jeremiah spent his life swinging at it. God sends him to the temple gate to say to the worshippers, "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD'" (Jeremiah 7:4, ESV). Their holiest slogan. Their deepest security. A lie, and he was sent to break it in the doorway of the building it advertised. No one who has read the prophets imagines that, walking into an American sanctuary this Sunday, they would leave the additions standing.

But the commission does not stop at overthrow, and here the whole matter turns. The last two verbs govern the first four. *To build and to plant* — and notice what kind of verbs they are: the slow ones. Anyone can overthrow in an afternoon. Demolition is loud, fast, and photogenic. Building takes years, and planting takes seasons, and the planter has to stay — through the winters, after the audience that gathered for the breaking glass has drifted off. Four verbs of wrecking, two of building, and the two are the point of the four, because a prophet authorized only to tear down would be half a prophet, and God does not commission halves. The demolition is in the calling. It is not the calling.

## What was under my house

I told you I went into the house swinging, so I owe you the end of it, though I will keep it short, because I promised no speech. When my demolition was finished — and it was thorough, years of it — what I found at the bottom was not the Christianity I had refused. That religion, the suburban chaplaincy, the mascot god of the comfortable, the vending machine with a fish on the side: my demolition was right about it, and I have never rebuilt one plank of it. What was under the house was older and stranger and harder. A first-century rabbi who could not be recruited by anyone's empire, including the ones that fly his cross. A God who demands death before he gives resurrection. A grace that costs nothing and then claims everything. A tomb that the best counterarguments I owned could not keep sealed. I did not find a faith that answered every question I had carried in. I found one that could hold the questions' weight, which is different, and better, and nobody had told me it existed.

And I found that the only prayer a rebuilder gets had already been written, by a man out of arguments, standing in front of Jesus with a dying son: he "cried out and said, 'I believe; help my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:24, ESV). Both halves in one breath, and the miracle came anyway. That is not the prayer of a finished man. It is the prayer of a standing one. It has held my weight ever since.

So, to the person at that table: no speech, and no deadline. Nobody standing in a half-taken-apart house needs a schedule shouted through the window, and the pressure you feel to land somewhere — anywhere — so that everyone around you can exhale, is not coming from God. Paul's instruction to a young church holds both edges of your situation in one sentence: "but test everything; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21, ESV). Test everything — the verse authorizes your crowbar. Hold fast what is good — the verse assumes the testing finds something, and that you will need your hands free to grip it.

You are allowed to keep the question inside the faith. Whatever they taught you, doubt is not the exit, and certainty was never the entrance fee — the father in Mark 9 got his answer mid-sentence, unbelief still on his lips. And you are allowed to dig past the point where the podcasts stop, past the crater where demolition sets up its gift shop, down through the additions, through the politics and the merchandise and the coupons they handed you instead of answers, down to where the shovel rings.

I do not know how long your dig takes. Mine took years, and I started with more wreckage than most. I only ask you to notice, some night when the house is quiet and the pile of what you no longer believe has grown taller than what you still do, that there are two ways to hold a shovel, and that they look identical from the street, and that they are not the same. One is how you leave. The other is how you get to the bottom.

Take it apart, then. Take it all the way apart — toward something. The bedrock is not going anywhere. It never has.
