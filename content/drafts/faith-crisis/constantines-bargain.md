People keep telling me they have lost their faith, and most of them have not.

They have lost something. The losing is real and the grief is real and I would not minimize either for a moment. But when I sit long enough with the person across the table, what they describe leaving is almost never the God of the Gospels. It is a church fused to a political party. It is a faith that defended the powerful and policed the weak. It is a Christianity that looked, on close inspection, like an empire wearing a cross as a brooch. They are walking away from that, and they think they are walking away from Jesus, and the confusion is not their fault. The two have been welded together for so long that almost no one alive remembers them apart. The weld has a date, and once you know the date you start to see the seam. The year is 312.

I came to faith from atheism, and I want to be honest about why that matters here. When I looked at Christianity from the outside, what I saw first was the bargain, not the Gospel. I saw the entanglement with power, the wars fought under the sign, the comfortable alliance with whoever happened to be winning, and I assumed that was the thing itself — that this was what the word *Christian* finally meant. **Most skeptics are reacting to Constantine, not to Christ.** They have never met Christ. They have only ever met the bargain, and the bargain is genuinely repellent, and their revulsion is closer to the prophets than they will ever be told. The person deconstructing in disgust at a church that blessed cruelty is doing something Amos would have recognized. He is just aiming it at the wrong target, because no one ever showed him the other one.

## The weld has a date

The year 312 is when Constantine claimed to see a cross in the sky before the battle at the Milvian Bridge, fought under the sign, won, and concluded that the Galilean god was useful.

Within a century the faith of the martyrs became the faith of the magistrates. The religion that had been illegal, hunted, scattered in house churches and catacombs, walked into the halls of power and never fully walked back out. This is the bargain, and it is worth stating both halves plainly because both sides genuinely believed they had gained. The church got protection, property, prestige, exemption, a seat at the imperial table, an end to the lions. The empire got a god to bless its wars and sanctify its order, a unifying cult for a fracturing world, divine cover for the sword. Both sides thought they had won the exchange. One of them was catastrophically wrong, and it was not the empire.

Because here is what the church handed over in the trade, without ever quite noticing the size of it. It handed over the cross as the shape of its power. For three centuries the followers of Jesus had conquered by being conquered, persuaded by dying, won by refusing to fight back — and in doing so they had been unmistakably his, because that was exactly how he had done it. The bargain inverted all of it. The cross stopped being the thing you suffered and became the thing you carried into battle ahead of the troops. The same symbol, turned inside out. A torture instrument that had meant *the powerless God who dies for you* came to mean *get out of the way or be destroyed.* No one signed a document. The meaning just drifted, decade by decade, until the inversion was complete and invisible, and the church could no longer remember that its Lord had refused the very thing it now wielded in his name.

## I am an heir of the bargain

This is the indictment, and I am standing inside it. I am a pastor in America, which means I am an heir of the bargain whether I chose it or not.

The American church did not invent the fusion of faith and power. It inherited it, refined it, and gave it a flag. James Davison Hunter, in *To Change the World* (2010), traced the whole modern impulse — the conviction, on the left and the right alike, that the kingdom of God arrives by capturing the levers of culture and the state, that faithfulness means winning the institutions and writing the laws and holding the offices. We learned that from Constantine. We have been running his play for seventeen centuries, and we run it instinctively now, without deciding to, the way you breathe. And every time the church reaches for the sword to defend the Lamb, it loses the very thing it was reaching to protect, because the Lamb was never defended that way and never once asked to be.

I have stood in rooms where the cross and the flag were treated as a single object, where to question the fusion was to be suspected of disloyalty to both, and I did not always say what needed saying. The bargain is comfortable. That is the whole point of it. It is comfortable, and it is a betrayal, and I have been comfortable. I have let the seam stay hidden because pointing at it costs something, and I have watched people walk out the door in disgust at a thing I could have helped them name, and did not, in time. So I am not writing this from above the problem. I am writing it from inside the church that made the deal and keeps making it, and benefits from it, and calls the benefit blessing.

## Look at what the bargain replaced

So look at what came before the bargain, because the contrast is the whole argument.

Before 312 the church had no power and changed the world anyway. The early Christians did not run the empire; the empire ran them, into the arenas. And yet they out-loved it. When the great plagues came and the pagans fled the cities, leaving even their own families to die in the streets, the Christians stayed and nursed the dying — their own and the strangers, the ones who had persecuted them included — and many of them caught the sickness and died too, and the watching world could not account for it. There was no incentive structure that explained it. Tom Holland, a secular historian who expected to find the faith a footnote to the real history of the West, found instead, in *Dominion* (2019), that the entire moral architecture we now take for granted — the dignity of the weak, the worth of the slave, the conscience that insists power is not the same as right — came from these powerless people and their crucified God.

They had no bargain. They had a tomb they swore was empty and a willingness to die for it, and that combination was the engine. **Constantine did not turn the engine on. He bolted it to a chariot and pointed it at his enemies**, and the engine has been misfiring in that chariot ever since, doing damage in the name of the one who built it to do the opposite. The power the church spent the next seventeen centuries accumulating did not make it more effective at what it had originally done. It made it worse at it. The church was never more world-altering than in the years it had nothing but love and a grave it could not explain.

There is a hard lesson buried in that history, and it cuts against every instinct a frightened church has. The early Christians were not winning. By every metric a strategist would use, they were losing — outlawed, outnumbered, periodically slaughtered, with no institutions, no armies, no legal standing, no plan to acquire any. And they were transforming the moral imagination of the most powerful civilization on earth. The two facts sit together and refuse to be pried apart. The powerlessness was not an obstacle the love had to overcome. The powerlessness was the medium the love worked in, the thing that made the love legible as love rather than as a bid for control. When the bargain handed the church power, it did not hand it a better tool. It took away the only condition under which its witness had ever been believed. A nurse who dies in a plague she could have fled means something. A magistrate who enforces orthodoxy with the sword means something else entirely, and the something else is the very thing the watching world had no reason to trust.

## Jesus was offered this exact bargain, and refused it

The thing most worth knowing is that Jesus was offered the bargain himself, at the start, and turned it down in the desert.

The devil takes him to a high place and shows him all the kingdoms of the world in their glory and says, *all this I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me.* The kingdoms, the power, the very levers Hunter described — offered up front, no battle required, no cross necessary. The whole thing, for one act of allegiance. And Jesus says no. He refuses the exact thing that Constantine would later accept on his behalf. Sit with that for a moment. The temptation Christ rejected in the wilderness is the foundation the imperial church was built on. What the Son of God named as satanic, the church three centuries later received as providence and called it the triumph of the faith.

He says it again at the end, standing bound before the only earthly power in the room. *My kingdom is not of this world. If it were, my servants would fight.* The whole logic of the bargain — that the kingdom comes by force, by holding the levers, by winning — is the logic Jesus refused at the beginning and refused again at the trial, with his life on the line, when fighting would have been the obvious play. He would not be that kind of king. The empire offered him the kingdoms in the desert and he said no, and then it offered them again three centuries later, and this time the church said yes on his behalf, and has been apologizing for it ever since to people who can no longer hear the difference between the deal and the man.

Lesslie Newbigin saw where this leaves us, and saw it as a gift rather than a catastrophe. He came home to England in the 1970s after nearly four decades as a missionary in India, and found that the West itself had become a mission field — that Christendom, the long arrangement Constantine began, was finally collapsing around him. And he did not mourn it the way the culture warriors mourn it. In *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society* (1989) and the work surrounding it, he argued that the collapse might be the best thing to happen to the church in seventeen hundred years, because a church stripped of its imperial privileges might rediscover that it never needed them — that it was always meant to persuade by love and not compel by law, to witness rather than rule, to win the way it won at the start. What looks like the death of Christianity may be the death of the bargain. Those are not the same funeral, and confusing them is the great mistake of our anxious moment.

## Make sure you know what you are leaving

So here is the word for the one at the table, ready to go.

Make sure you know what you are leaving. If you are leaving a faith that baptized cruelty, that confused God with country, that defended the strong and crushed the doubting and called the crushing holiness — then go, and go with God, because that thing deserved to die and the prophets would have torched it before you got there. But do not mistake it for him. The bargain is not the Gospel. The empire is not the kingdom. The cross became a sword in 312, and you are right to hate the sword, and the man it was made for would hate it more than you do. He already turned it down once, in a desert, hungry, with everything to gain and the whole world on offer.

Much of what we are calling deconstruction is just the bargain finally coming apart, seventeen centuries late, and the coming-apart is not the enemy of the faith. It may be the mercy of it — the long poison working its way out, the seam splitting so the two welded things can at last be seen as two. The danger is only that you will throw out the wrong half: keep the disillusionment, discard the man, and never learn that the man was the one who told you to be disillusioned in the first place.

Let it come apart, then. Underneath it, if you can bear to look past the rubble of the thing you were right to leave, there is a powerless man on a cross who never made the deal, and was never the problem, and is still, after all of this, the only thing in the wreckage worth keeping.
