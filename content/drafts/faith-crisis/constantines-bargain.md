People keep telling me they have lost their faith, and most of them have not.

They have lost something. The losing is real and the grief is real and I would not minimize either. But when I sit long enough with the person across the table, what they describe leaving is almost never the God of the Gospels. It is a church fused to a political party. It is a faith that defended the powerful and policed the weak. It is a Christianity that looked, on close inspection, like an empire wearing a cross as a brooch. They are walking away from that, and they think they are walking away from Jesus, and the confusion is not their fault. The two have been welded together for so long that almost no one alive remembers them apart. The weld has a date. The year is 312.

That is the year Constantine claimed to see a cross in the sky before the battle at the Milvian Bridge, and won, and decided the Galilean god was useful. Within a century the faith of the martyrs became the faith of the magistrates. The religion that had been illegal, hunted, scattered in house churches and catacombs, walked into the halls of power and never fully walked back out. This is the bargain. The church got protection, property, prestige, a seat at the imperial table. The empire got a god to bless its wars and sanctify its order. Both sides thought they had won. One of them was wrong.

I came to faith from atheism, and I want to be honest about why that matters here. When I looked at Christianity from the outside, what I saw first was the bargain, not the Gospel. I saw the entanglement with power, the wars fought under the sign, the comfortable alliance with whoever was winning, and I assumed that was the thing itself. Most skeptics are reacting to Constantine, not to Christ. They have never met Christ. They have only ever met the bargain, and the bargain is genuinely repellent, and their revulsion is closer to the prophets than they know.

This is the indictment, and I am inside it. I am a pastor in America, which means I am an heir of the bargain whether I chose it or not.

The American church did not invent the fusion of faith and power. It inherited it, refined it, and gave it a flag. James Davison Hunter, in *To Change the World*, traced the whole modern impulse, the conviction on the left and the right alike that the kingdom of God arrives by capturing the levers of culture and the state. We learned that from Constantine. We have been running his play for seventeen centuries. And every time the church reaches for the sword to defend the Lamb, it loses the thing it was reaching to protect, because the Lamb was never defended that way and never asked to be. I have stood in rooms where the cross and the flag were treated as one object, and I did not always say what needed saying. The bargain is comfortable. That is the point of it. It is comfortable, and it is a betrayal, and I have been comfortable.

So look at what the bargain replaced, because the contrast is the whole argument.

Before 312 the church had no power and changed the world anyway. The early Christians did not run the empire; the empire ran them, into the arenas. And yet they out-loved it. When the plagues came and the pagans fled the cities leaving their own to die, the Christians stayed and nursed the dying, their own and the strangers, and many of them caught it and died too, and the watching world could not account for it. Tom Holland, a secular historian who expected to find the faith a footnote, found instead that the entire moral architecture of the West, the dignity of the weak, the worth of the slave, the conscience that says power is not the same as right, came from these powerless people and their crucified God. They had no bargain. They had a tomb they swore was empty and a willingness to die for it. That was the engine. Constantine did not turn the engine on. He bolted it to a chariot and pointed it at his enemies.

Jesus was offered this exact bargain, and he refused it in the desert.

The devil takes him to a high place and shows him all the kingdoms of the world and says, all this I will give you, if you will bow. The kingdoms, the power, the levers Hunter described, offered up front, no battle required. And Jesus says no. He turns down the very thing Constantine would later accept on his behalf. Sit with that. The temptation Christ rejected in the wilderness is the foundation the imperial church was built on. *My kingdom is not of this world,* he tells Pilate, standing bound before the only earthly power in the room, *if it were, my servants would fight.* The whole logic of the bargain is the logic Jesus refused at the start and refused at the end. Constantine offered him the kingdoms again, three centuries later, and this time the church said yes on his behalf.

Lesslie Newbigin saw where this leaves us, and saw it as gift rather than catastrophe.

He came home to England after decades as a missionary in India and found that the West had become a mission field, that Christendom, the long arrangement Constantine began, was finally collapsing. And he did not mourn it the way the culture warriors mourn it. He thought the collapse might be the best thing to happen to the church in seventeen hundred years, because a church stripped of its imperial privileges might rediscover that it never needed them, that it was always meant to persuade by love and not compel by law, that the bargain had been a long slow poisoning all along. What looks like the death of Christianity may be the death of the bargain. Those are not the same funeral.

So here is the word for the one at the table, ready to leave.

Make sure you know what you are leaving. If you are leaving a faith that baptized cruelty, that confused God with country, that defended the strong and crushed the doubting and called it holiness, then go, and go with God, because that thing deserved to die and the prophets would have torched it first. But do not mistake it for him. The bargain is not the Gospel. The empire is not the kingdom. The cross became a sword in 312, and you are right to hate the sword, and the man it was made for would hate it too. He already turned it down once, in a desert, hungry, with everything to gain.

Much of what we call deconstruction is just the bargain finally coming apart, seventeen centuries late.

Let it come apart. Underneath it, if you can bear to look, there is a powerless man on a cross who never made the deal, and was never the problem, and is still, after all of this, the only thing in the wreckage worth keeping.
