The prayers stopped landing somewhere, and you cannot say when.

There was no decision, no scandal, no loss of belief you could point to. The faith is intact on paper. You would still sign the creed. But the warmth is gone, the sense of being heard is gone, the God who used to feel near at the altar or in the car or in the small hours has receded into a silence you cannot interrupt no matter how hard you reach. You read the chapter and nothing moves. You sing the song and feel nothing. And the worst part is not the silence. The worst part is the suspicion that the silence is your fault, that you have done something, that the warmth left because you failed and now God is gone.

He is not gone. I want to put that down first, flatly, because the not-knowing it has wrecked more faith than any argument ever did. The church handed you a vocabulary for conversion and a vocabulary for sin and gave you nothing for this, and the silence about the silence is its own quiet cruelty. There is a name for what you are in. There has always been a name. You were just never told.

John of the Cross gave it to us in the sixteenth century, in a Spanish prison cell, where the most devastated thing he ever wrote turned out to be a love poem.

He called it the dark night of the soul, and he did not mean what we have made it mean. We use the phrase now for any bad season, a hard year, a depression, a stretch of grief. John meant something stranger and more hopeful. He meant a deliberate withdrawal of felt presence by God himself, not as abandonment but as surgery. The new believer, John says, falls in love with the feeling of God, the consolation, the warmth, the goosebumps at the high point of the song. And the feeling becomes the thing he seeks. He starts chasing the experience of God instead of God, the way a man can fall in love with being in love and lose the actual person. So God, who is not content to be loved for his gifts, removes the gifts. He takes away the warmth so that you will learn to love him in the dark, for himself, with nothing in it for you. The dark night is not God leaving. It is God refusing to be reduced to a feeling.

That reframe alone has pulled people back from the cliff. But it asks something hard of you. It asks you to keep walking toward a God you cannot feel, on the bare word that he is there.

The Psalms knew this country long before John mapped it. Psalm 88 is the one they do not put on the coffee mugs.

It is the only psalm that ends in the dark with no turn toward the light. *You have put me in the depths of the pit, in the regions dark and deep.* The last word in the Hebrew is *darkness.* That is where it ends. And the church kept it, set it in the middle of the book, prayed it for three thousand years, because someone in their wisdom understood that a person in the dark needs a prayer that stays in the dark with him, not a prayer that hurries him toward a sunrise he cannot see yet. Psalm 88 is permission. It tells you that the silence is not outside the faith. It is inside the scriptures, canonized, prayed by the people of God in every century, including the century that produced you.

There is also a colder comfort, and it is the coldest and the truest one. Even the Son went through it.

On the cross the cry is not serene. *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me.* That is the dark night in its absolute form, the felt absence of the Father at the one moment it should have been impossible, spoken by the one person who had never sinned and could not have caused it by failing. Which closes off the lie at the root of your fear. If the sinless Son could feel forsaken, then the felt absence of God is not proof of your guilt. Jesus did not lose the Father at Golgotha. He felt the loss while remaining held, and that gap, between what was felt and what was true, is the exact gap you are standing in. He has been here. The ground under your feet has footprints in it.

So what do you do in the dark, when there is nothing to feel.

You do the unglamorous thing the tradition has always counseled, which is to keep the practices you built in the light. You pray when prayer is dust in your mouth. You read when the page goes flat. You come to the table when you feel like a hypocrite for coming, because the table was never about your feelings; it was about his promise. Eugene Peterson called the whole Christian life a long obedience in the same direction, and he meant precisely this, the plodding faithfulness that keeps walking when the scenery gives nothing back. The faith is not the feeling. The faith is the walking. The feeling will come and go like weather; the walking is the relationship.

And you tell someone. The monks always had a guide for this, a director who had walked the dark night and could say, calmly, this is that, you are not losing your faith, you are being matured by its absence. We stripped the guides out of our churches and called it freedom and left people to drown in private. Find the person who has been here. Let them name it over you. The naming is half the cure.

One more thing, and it is the hardest to receive. The dryness is not wasted. John insisted on this and the saints confirmed it with their lives. The faith that comes out the other side of the dark night is not the faith that went in. It is quieter, sturdier, less dependent on weather, harder to take away. The people I have buried with the most peace in their faces were almost never the ones whose faith ran warm their whole lives. They were the ones who had been through a long dark and learned to hold God's hand without being able to feel it.

The silence you are in is not the absence of God. It is the absence of the feeling of God, and the two are not the same, and learning the difference may be the most important thing your faith ever does.

Keep walking. The dark is not the end of the road. It is the part of the road where you finally learn whose hand you are holding.
