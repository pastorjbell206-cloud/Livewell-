The inversion happened quietly. At some point in the past several decades of American Christianity, the word *justice* came to carry a political valence — heard by significant portions of the evangelical church as a term of the cultural left, code language for a progressive agenda, something to be resisted or at minimum treated with suspicion. The inversion is complete now, which means almost no one notices it anymore.

It is also one of the most significant theological failures of contemporary Christianity. Because *mishpat* — the Hebrew word most often translated "justice" in the English Old Testament — does not belong to any political movement. It belongs to God.

*Mishpat* appears more than two hundred times in the Hebrew scriptures [cite — confirm]. It describes God's own character: "The LORD is a God of justice; blessed are all those who wait for him" (Isaiah 30:18). It describes God's activity in history: the Exodus is an act of *mishpat*, a righting of what was wrong. It describes the specific disposition that God requires of God's people — not as an optional expression of political preference, but as a core demand of the covenant relationship. When Amos announces God's indictment of Israel, the charge is not that the people have adopted wrong positions. The charge is that they "sell the innocent for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6). They have violated *mishpat*. They have broken the world's right order, which God made and God will restore.

The church that treats this word as politically contaminated has not protected itself from partisan capture. It has surrendered one of the oldest and most central terms of its own theological inheritance to people who did not coin it, do not own it, and have not always used it well.

I want to be careful here, because the steel-man case for suspicion deserves a hearing. The word *justice*, as deployed in certain recent cultural moments, has sometimes carried freight that is not self-evidently biblical — frameworks rooted in particular sociological theories rather than Scripture, programs that a thoughtful Christian might have genuine reasons to question. That discomfort is not paranoia. It is not unreasonable. But the response to a word being used badly is not to abandon it. The response is to reclaim it with greater precision than those who weaponized it.

Reclaiming *mishpat* means starting where the prophets started: with God's own character as the source of justice, not with any political coalition. God does justice. God loves justice. God requires justice. The prophet Micah does not instruct Israel to vote for the justice platform. He says: "He has shown you, O mortal, what is good. And what does the LORD require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). The requirement is given to every person in the community, without exception, as the baseline description of what faithfulness looks like.

The other Hebrew word that belongs here is *tsedaqah* — often translated "righteousness," but carrying the weight of right relationship, the restoration of what should be between people and between people and God. *Mishpat* and *tsedaqah* appear together repeatedly in the prophets [cite — confirm], as a pair, because justice and righteous relationship are not separable. You cannot establish right order externally while leaving broken relationships intact. You cannot restore relationships while tolerating structural arrangements that crush the vulnerable. The two words interpret each other.

The church that treats justice as political rather than theological eventually produces a congregation that assumes its political allegiances are its theological ones — that voting for the right candidates is the substance of faithful Christian life. This is the confusion that has done enormous damage to the church's witness in recent decades. Not because politics is irrelevant to faith. Because politics has swallowed faith whole, and few people noticed when it happened.

The alternative is not political withdrawal. It is theological seriousness. It is recovering the full weight of what *mishpat* and *tsedaqah* describe, bringing those terms to bear on the specific conditions of a specific community's life — the poor, the vulnerable, those without advocates — and asking what faithfulness requires there.

This will not be comfortable. Prophetic faithfulness rarely is. Amos was expelled from Bethel for his trouble (Amos 7:10-13). The prophets who were heard pleasantly were usually the ones who said what the king already wanted to hear.

Justice is not a political category. It is a description of what God is doing in history and what God requires of us while we are in it. The church that has lost hold of this has lost something it cannot afford to lose.
