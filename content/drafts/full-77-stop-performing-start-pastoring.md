It was a Tuesday afternoon when I finally admitted it to myself. I was preparing a sermon on grief — Psalm 22, the one Jesus quoted from the cross — and I was not praying. I was workshopping. I was testing the lines. I was listening to how it would land in the room on Sunday morning and editing accordingly. The sermon was about a man's abandonment by God, and I was producing content about it. The distance between those two things had opened without my permission, and I had not noticed because the sermons were still good. People were still moved. The machine was running.

That is the particular danger of performance-driven ministry. It works for a long time before it destroys you, and during the time it is working, you have almost no way to know the difference from the inside. The congregation cannot tell. The metrics cannot tell. Your own emotional experience cannot tell, because the performance generates its own adrenaline, and adrenaline is a reasonable counterfeit of joy if you do not inspect it too closely.

Performance creeps in through the smallest doors. It begins, often, with a good Sunday — a sermon that connected, a response that gratified, a moment in the room where you felt the thing you entered ministry hoping to feel. And then, imperceptibly, you begin to aim for that feeling rather than for the truth that produced it. You begin to construct the sermon not from Scripture outward but from the desired emotional landing backward. You begin to measure the week by how the congregation felt on Sunday rather than by whether you prayed on Monday. The craft, which is a real thing and matters, starts to replace the conversation with God that the craft is supposed to serve.

I am describing my own history. I am not offering a theory.

The recovery did not come with a single crisis or a dramatic confrontation. It came slowly, the way the drift had come. It began when I started meeting weekly with two other pastors who had made a specific commitment to tell each other the truth. Not pastoral colleagues sharing sermon illustrations. Two men who agreed, explicitly, that the pastoral face was not allowed in the room. We prayed for each other with the specificity that requires actual knowledge of actual circumstances. We named what was happening inside the work, not just in the work. It was ordinary and it was essential.

The second thing was a decision to re-order the week so that sermon preparation was not the week's first priority. Prayer was. Not prayer in the professional sense — the pastoral prayer, the staff meeting invocation — but the unscheduled, unproductive, honestly formative sitting with God that has nothing to do with Sunday. Eugene Peterson [cite — confirm] called it the pastor's first responsibility, the formation of the pastor's own soul. I had known this. I had not been doing it. The reorganization of the week felt like loss at first and then felt like coming back to myself.

The third thing was harder. I had to ask whether the image I was projecting from the platform was the same person who existed Monday through Saturday. Not whether I was lying — I was not. But whether the pastoral persona had developed its own internal logic that was not fully accountable to the actual man. There was a gap. Smaller than I feared, larger than I had admitted. Closing it required years, not a weekend.

There is a steel-man for the performance argument. The pastor's role is partly theatrical in the sense that the liturgy has a shape, the sermon has a structure, the public self must be ordered enough to bear the congregation's projections without shattering. You are not simply yourself on Sunday morning. You are also a vessel, a representative, a person performing an office. These things are real and not spiritually corrupt in themselves.

But the vessel must be full of something. The representative must be representing something real. The office must be inhabited by an actual man whose interior life is not a performance. When the performance consumes the person, there is nothing left to offer but a better and better-crafted emptiness.

Stop performing. Not today, not all at once. But begin. One honest conversation. One un-workshopped prayer. One week where the sermon is a letter to God before it is a communication to the congregation.

That is where pastoring starts.
