C.S. Lewis wrote, in his 1944 introduction to Athanasius's *On the Incarnation*, that the contemporary Christian reader should read one old book for every three new ones, and ideally one old book for every new one. Lewis was writing eighty years ago. The ratio he recommended has not been maintained, and the failure to maintain it has had consequences for the formation of Christian readers in our generation that Lewis predicted with substantial accuracy.

I want to write this essay for the Christian reader who has been buying books from the Christian-bookstore section for years and has been wondering why the books, on the whole, do not seem to be doing the formative work the reader had hoped they would do. The wondering is appropriate. The books have generally been shaped by the contemporary moment in ways the reader cannot fully see, and the reading of them is producing, in many cases, a kind of Christian formation that does not survive the next cultural moment when it arrives. The recovery of reading older books is one of the most concrete things the contemporary Christian reader can do to deepen the formation that current reading is mostly not producing.

## What Lewis actually said

Lewis's introduction is short and is worth reading in full. The substance of his argument can be summarized.

Every age has its own assumptions that are invisible to the people living in that age. The contemporary reader is shaped by assumptions he is not aware of, because the assumptions are the air he breathes. The reader who reads only contemporary books reads only books that have been shaped by the same assumptions. The reader who reads old books reads books that were shaped by different assumptions, and the difference makes visible to the reader the contemporary assumptions that the contemporary books cannot make visible.

Old books are not better in every respect. They have their own blind spots — sometimes obvious to us in retrospect, sometimes still hidden. The old books were shaped by the assumptions of their own age. The value of reading them is not that they are right and the new books are wrong. The value is that they expose the assumptions of our age by presenting different assumptions that we have to grapple with.

The reading of old books, Lewis argues, is therefore a kind of liberation. The reader who reads only contemporary books is trapped inside the current moment without knowing he is trapped. The reader who reads old books gains the ability to see his own moment from outside, which is what allows him to be critically engaged with his own moment rather than uncritically absorbed by it.

Lewis's argument applies to all reading, not only to Christian reading. The argument applies with particular force to Christian reading, though, because the Christian tradition has a substantial body of older literature that has been doing theological work for centuries before contemporary categories arrived, and the older literature provides resources for theological discernment that contemporary Christian publishing rarely produces.

## What contemporary Christian publishing produces

I want to be honest about what the contemporary Christian publishing industry is, because the honesty matters for how the reader should engage it.

The contemporary Christian publishing industry produces, primarily, books that meet contemporary felt needs. The books address topics that are current cultural concerns. The books are written in a vocabulary that contemporary readers will recognize. The books advance arguments that fit broadly within the assumptions of contemporary American evangelicalism. The books are designed to sell within the current market.

This is not a moral indictment of the industry. Publishing is a business. The publishers select books based on what they project will sell. The authors write books that fit the format the publishers want. The result is a body of literature that does excellent work within the contemporary frame but that rarely steps outside the frame to ask whether the frame itself is theologically adequate.

The body of literature has costs that I want to name.

The first cost is that contemporary Christian books, on average, reproduce the contemporary cultural assumptions that the books should be examining. The therapeutic culture has shaped much of contemporary Christian writing on emotional health. The political polarization has shaped much of contemporary Christian writing on civic engagement. The consumer culture has shaped much of contemporary Christian writing on the spiritual life. The shaping is often invisible to the authors and to the readers, because the shaping is the air they all breathe. The result is a body of literature that addresses contemporary topics in contemporary terms, often without the critical distance that older literature would have provided.

The second cost is that contemporary Christian books, on average, do not engage with the deep history of Christian thought. Most contemporary Christian books cite other contemporary Christian books. The interlocutors are recent. The arguments build on recent arguments. The references are to authors who are still publishing. The body of literature is, in significant respects, a closed conversation within the present generation, with limited engagement with the larger Christian tradition that has been having related conversations for two thousand years.

The third cost is that contemporary Christian books, on average, have shorter half-lives than the older literature. Most contemporary Christian books are forgotten within a decade. The reasons are partly that the cultural moment they addressed has shifted, partly that the arguments they made were not the strongest available, partly that the new contemporary books have replaced them in the market. The reader who has built a library of contemporary Christian books from the past two decades has, on the whole, built a library that has substantially aged. The reader who has built a library that includes older works has built something more durable.

## What old books actually offer

I want to be specific about what older Christian books provide that contemporary publishing largely does not.

The first thing is depth of theological engagement. The Church Fathers — Augustine, Athanasius, Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Chrysostom, the Cappadocian and Antiochene traditions taken together — wrote with a depth of theological engagement that contemporary popular Christian publishing rarely achieves. The Fathers were addressing the foundational theological questions for the first time, with the resources of classical philosophy and Hebrew thought at their disposal, and they produced literature that has been one of the church's most consistent sources of theological depth for sixteen hundred years.

The second thing is unfamiliarity. The medieval theological tradition — Anselm, Aquinas, Bonaventure, Julian of Norwich, the various mystics and scholastics taken together — operated in conceptual frameworks that are unfamiliar enough to the contemporary reader that engaging the material requires the reader to think outside his own assumptions. The unfamiliarity is the value. The reader who has worked through Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* or Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* has done work that no amount of contemporary reading would have required.

The third thing is the historic engagement with crisis. The Reformers wrote during a crisis of the church — when the institutional church was in obvious need of reform and when faithful Christians had to figure out how to respond. The Reformers' writings — Luther's *Bondage of the Will* and *Heidelberg Disputation*, Calvin's *Institutes*, the various confessions and catechisms — are particularly valuable for the contemporary reader because the contemporary American Christian context has features in common with the late-medieval crisis the Reformers addressed.

The fourth thing is the historic engagement with empire. Augustine's *City of God*, written in response to the fall of Rome, is one of the most important theological treatments of the relationship between the church and political power. The book is twelve hundred pages and demands sustained engagement. The book is one of the most important resources available for the contemporary post-Christendom moment, and very few contemporary Christian books address the same questions with anything like the same depth.

The fifth thing is the historic engagement with suffering. The various mystical and contemplative traditions — Julian of Norwich's *Revelations of Divine Love*, John of the Cross's *Dark Night of the Soul*, Teresa of Ávila's *Interior Castle* — address the soul's experience of suffering, doubt, and the apparent absence of God with a depth that contemporary therapeutic Christian writing rarely matches.

## What to actually read

I want to be concrete about where the reader interested in older books should start, because the historic body of Christian literature is vast and the reader without guidance can easily be overwhelmed.

Augustine's *Confessions* is the foundational autobiography of the Western Christian tradition. The book is accessible — written in the form of a prayer addressed to God, with substantial autobiographical narrative — and it is theologically rich. The reader new to Augustine should start with the *Confessions* rather than with the *City of God*. The Henry Chadwick translation is the standard English text.

Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* is the brief and accessible introduction to patristic theology that Lewis was introducing in 1944. The book is short — about a hundred pages in standard editions — and is one of the most important early statements of orthodox Christology. Read it.

John Calvin's *Institutes of the Christian Religion* is the foundational systematic theology of Reformed Protestantism. The book is long, but the reader who works through it slowly over months has done substantial theological work. The McNeill edition, edited by Battles, is the standard English version.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* (1937) and *Life Together* (1939) are the twentieth-century classics on the cost of discipleship and the practice of Christian community. Bonhoeffer wrote them under conditions far harder than ours, and they read differently as a result. They are not optional.

C.S. Lewis's own work, while contemporary in our timeframe, has aged well and serves as a transitional bridge between contemporary writing and older works. *Mere Christianity*, *The Great Divorce*, *The Screwtape Letters*, and *The Weight of Glory* are the obvious starting points.

The Book of Common Prayer, in its 1662 form or in modern versions that preserve the historic structure, is one of the great prayer-books of the Christian tradition. The reader who prays through the Daily Office for several months has participated in a liturgical formation that contemporary publishing rarely provides.

Beyond these starting points, the reader should pick a specific area of interest and find the older voices in that area. For political theology: Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Bonhoeffer, Yoder. For prayer and contemplation: the desert fathers, the medieval mystics, the Puritan devotional writers. For the church's life under pressure: the Reformation literature, the literature from the Confessing Church under the Nazis, the literature from the Soviet underground church. Each area has its own older voices that contemporary publishing has largely not replaced.

## The verdict

Most contemporary Christian reading is current. The current reading is shaped by the present moment in ways the reader cannot see. The reader who reads only current books reads only the assumptions of the present, and the formation produced is correspondingly limited. The reader who reads older books reads outside the assumptions of the present, and the reading produces formation that has more durability.

Lewis recommended one old book for every three new ones. The ratio is, in my reading, conservative. The contemporary American Christian context, with its accelerated cultural change and its publishing industry that produces enormous volumes of contemporary literature, would benefit from a ratio closer to one old book for every new one. The recovery is concrete. Pick one old book to read this season. Augustine, Athanasius, Lewis himself, Bonhoeffer, the Book of Common Prayer — pick one. Read it slowly. The reading will form you in ways no contemporary book will.

I am writing this for the Christian reader who has been disappointed by the books on his shelf and has not been sure what would be different. The different is older. The older is available. The older has been doing the formative work for two thousand years, and the work is available to the reader who is willing to do the slower reading the older books require. The slower reading is, in my pastoral experience, one of the most consistent features of mature Christian readers. The maturity is not produced by the volume of reading. The maturity is produced by the depth and the range — and the range has to include the older voices that the contemporary publishing industry has largely stopped recommending.

Begin with Augustine. The *Confessions* is short enough to finish in two months of evening reading. The book has been doing its work since the fifth century. The work will continue, in you, after you have finished the reading. The investment is small. The return is substantial.
