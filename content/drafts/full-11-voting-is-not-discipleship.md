An election will not save the church and an election will not destroy it. The church that thinks otherwise has stopped being a church and started being a constituency. The starting was gradual. The stopping was almost complete by the time anyone noticed.

I want to write this essay short and direct, because the confusion it names is one of the simplest to identify and one of the most consequential to fix. The confusion is this: American evangelicalism, over the past forty years, has gradually treated voting as if it were a form of discipleship. The treating has hollowed both the voting and the discipleship. Voting cannot bear the weight that discipleship is supposed to carry, and discipleship cannot survive being reduced to voting. The two are different categories of act. Pretending otherwise has been killing the witness.

## What discipleship actually is

Discipleship is the long process by which a person is formed into the image of Christ. It is what Eugene Peterson, borrowing Nietzsche on purpose, called *a long obedience in the same direction.* It is the work of decades. It involves the formation of the affections, the disciplining of the body, the catechesis of the mind, the long obedience of the will. It includes prayer, scripture, sacrament, worship, the sustained practice of the works of mercy, the formation of character through ten thousand small choices made over years.

Discipleship is what happens at the dinner table. It is what happens in the pew on Sunday morning. It is what happens when you visit the dying friend in the hospital. It is what happens when you tell the truth in a meeting where the truth is unwelcome. It is what happens when you forgive the person who has wronged you and meant it. It is what happens when you pray for your enemies and mean it. It is what happens when you give money to a person who cannot pay you back and intends not to. It is what happens when you go home to your spouse, after the bad week, and choose to be present, again, when the easier choice would be to scroll. Discipleship is most of life. It is what life is, if life is a Christian one.

Voting is one act, performed once every two or four years, in a process that involves no formation at all. You enter the booth. You make some marks. You leave. The act takes ten minutes. It costs nothing. It forms nothing in you. It does not require you to forgive anyone. It does not require you to pray for anyone. It does not require you to love an enemy or visit a sick friend or give to a poor neighbor. Voting is a civic act. It can be a thoughtful civic act, informed by your discipleship, but it is not itself discipleship. It is one of ten thousand civic acts you will perform in your life as a citizen, and it has the moral weight of an informed civic act, which is real but not large.

When American evangelicals talk about voting as if it were the central act of Christian witness — *vote your faith, vote the Bible, vote for life, vote for the Christian candidate* — we are committing a category error. The act of voting cannot carry that weight. The act of voting is too small. The act of voting takes ten minutes. The act of being formed into the image of Christ takes a lifetime. The two are not the same thing. Treating them as the same thing has produced two related distortions.

## What the confusion costs

The first distortion is that the sermons start sounding like campaign speeches. When voting becomes the central act of discipleship, the pulpit becomes the place where voting is shaped. The pastor's authority becomes political authority. The sermons start to be, in effect, voter-guidance documents, with the political conclusions buried inside Christian vocabulary. The congregation comes to expect this. The congregation begins to evaluate the pastor by whether his political conclusions match theirs. The pastor begins to know this and, often, to soften or sharpen his political statements based on what the congregation will tolerate. The whole apparatus of preaching becomes corrupted by its political function, and the deeper formation work that preaching is supposed to do becomes impossible. The congregation has not been catechized in the historic Christian doctrines, because the catechesis has been replaced by the voter guidance.

The second distortion is that the political opponent becomes the theological opponent. If voting is discipleship, then voting differently is a discipleship failure. The person across the aisle, who voted the other way, is not a fellow disciple with whom you disagree about contestable political questions; they are a heretic, an apostate, a failed Christian. This is the move that has made it impossible, in many American congregations, for people who voted differently to take communion together with any sense of shared identity. The political distinction has become the deeper distinction. The theological identity has been folded into the political identity, and the political identity has become the actual identity. The discipleship has been hollowed by the politics.

Both distortions have been doing damage for a generation. The damage is now widely visible. The exit of younger Americans from the church is, by the most thoughtful researchers, substantially driven by the perception that the church has become a partisan project. The younger Americans are not entirely wrong. The church has become, in many cases, a partisan project. The discipleship has been thinned to make room for the voting. The voting has been weighed down with theological significance it cannot carry. The whole thing has collapsed in slow motion, and the collapse is visible in the empty pews, the canceled missionary funding, the closed seminaries, the second-generation children who left and did not come back.

## What to do

Vote. I am not saying do not vote. I am saying vote with the recognition that voting is one civic act and not the substance of your discipleship.

Vote your convictions, including theological ones. The Christian who votes for what scripture commends — the protection of the unborn, the care of the poor, the dignity of the immigrant and the stranger, the integrity of marriage, the restraint of state violence, the limitation of arbitrary power — is voting in a way that is informed by his discipleship. The voting is downstream of the discipleship. The voting is shaped by the discipleship. The voting is not the discipleship.

Vote with humility about what voting can accomplish. Voting is a single act with limited effects. Voting elects officials who will make decisions in a complex institutional environment over which they have partial control. The official who promised what you wanted may not deliver. The official who threatened what you feared may not do it. The decisions made by elected officials are downstream of cultural conditions that voting did not produce and cannot quickly change. Voting is one of the smaller levers in the institutional machinery, not one of the larger ones. Treating it as one of the largest is a category mistake.

Vote and then go home and pastor someone. Go love your spouse. Go feed the hungry. Go form your children. Go bury the dead. Go open your home to the stranger. Go visit the prison. Go pray for your enemies. Go forgive the person who has wronged you. The discipleship is in the going. The voting was the small civic act on the way to the going. The going is most of life. The discipleship is, in fact, most of life — the slow accumulation of the obedience that is what scripture calls becoming Christ-like.

Stop preaching politics from the pulpit. Preach the gospel. Preach the historic Christian doctrines. Preach the works of mercy. Preach the formation of the affections. Preach the catechism. The political implications will follow. The political implications will be downstream of the theology, and they will be more theologically robust for not having been the primary content of the sermon. The pastor who preaches the gospel will produce, in his congregation, voters who vote with conscience. The pastor who preaches voting will produce, in his congregation, voters who have not been formed by the gospel.

Stop treating the political opponent as the theological opponent. The progressive Christian who voted differently than you is, if she has been baptized into the same body, your sister. The conservative Christian who voted differently than you is, if he has been baptized into the same body, your brother. You may disagree about politics. The disagreement is not the deeper disagreement, unless you have agreed to let politics be the deeper category. The deeper category is the gospel. The gospel does not require political agreement. The gospel requires the recognition that the Lord we all bow to is more important than the politicians we voted for, and the recognition has to be visible in how we treat each other across political difference.

## The verdict

Vote. Vote thoughtfully. Vote your convictions. Then close your laptop and go pastor someone. Go love your spouse. Go pray for the persecuted church. Go visit the dying friend. Go bury the dead.

Discipleship is what you do on the four years and the three hundred sixty-four other days of every election year. Most of discipleship has nothing to do with voting. The church that remembers this is the church that has a chance.

An election will not save the church. An election will not destroy the church. The church will be saved or destroyed by whether it is still a church — whether it still preaches the gospel, still administers the sacraments, still forms disciples, still cares for the persecuted, still buries the dead, still welcomes the stranger, still loves its neighbors, still tells the truth at cost. None of these are political acts in the partisan sense. All of these are the church being the church. The church being the church is what survives every election. The church that has become a constituency does not survive much of anything, because constituencies are organized around candidates, and candidates change, and the constituency that staked its identity on the candidate has, in every historical case I can think of, eventually been abandoned by the candidate and discredited by the change.

Be the church. Vote on the way. Do not confuse the voting with the church. The confusion has killed too many congregations already, and we are running out of pastors willing to say so. I am willing to say so. I am saying so here. The next essay in this series takes up the constructive question — what does practical theology look like after Christendom, when the political tools have been set aside and the disciplines of the historic church are recovered. That essay is the only one in this arc that tries to answer the question of what to do. It is the hardest one to write, and I have been putting it off, and I cannot put it off any longer. It is next.
