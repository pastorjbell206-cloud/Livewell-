The word justice appears in English Bibles so often that it has lost its edges. We read it and receive it without friction, without asking what the original word meant in its own context, in the throat of a Hebrew prophet addressing a community that had arranged its economic life to benefit those who were already comfortable. When we press into the Hebrew, we find not one word but two — and the difference between them is the difference between correcting a wrong and restoring a person.

*Mishpat* is the word usually translated justice in its procedural sense: right order, right judgment, the establishment of what is owed. It appears in legal contexts, in descriptions of the judge sitting at the city gate, in the call to hold court impartially. When Amos writes that Israel has turned justice (*mishpat*) into poison (Amos 6:12), he is describing the corruption of the systems that were meant to ensure that wrongs were corrected, that debts were honored, that the poor had access to the same law as the wealthy. Mishpat is the architecture of a society that works — the mechanisms that prevent the powerful from eating the weak.

*Tsedaqah* is harder to translate and harder to reduce. Usually rendered righteousness, it carries a relational weight that the English word has lost. Tsedaqah describes the condition of right relationship — not merely the absence of legal violation, but the presence of genuine flourishing, the restoration of what has been broken between persons and between a person and the community. Abraham was counted righteous (tsedaqah) not because he had met a legal standard but because he was in right relationship with God, a relationship of trust and fidelity (Genesis 15:6). The prophets use tsedaqah to describe what the society looks like when everyone in it is able to participate in the fullness of communal life — when the poor are not merely not defrauded but are genuinely included.

These two words appear together repeatedly in the prophetic literature. Together they describe the comprehensive vision of what God is working toward in the world: not simply the correction of specific wrongs, but the restoration of relationships and communities to the wholeness God intended. When Amos writes, *Let justice (*mishpat*) roll down like waters, and righteousness (*tsedaqah*) like an ever-flowing stream* (Amos 5:24), he is not calling for half a thing. He is calling for both: the structural correction of what is wrong and the relational restoration of what has been lost.

The church that has a theology of mishpat without tsedaqah produces charitable programs. People receive assistance but are not included. The wrong is somewhat corrected but the relationship is not restored. The wealthy feel good about their giving. The poor remain on the outside of the community looking in.

The church that has a theology of tsedaqah without mishpat produces warm sentiment with no structural honesty. People talk about relationship and belonging without naming the systems that are actively working against the belonging they celebrate. The poor are welcomed on Sunday and shut out by economic structures the congregation has never examined on Monday.

I have pastored in both directions. I have celebrated the food pantry without asking why the food pantry was necessary in the first place. I have preached about the beloved community without examining which members of the community had access to the beloved-ness I was describing. Both failures are mine to name.

The prophets held these two together without apology. They were not confused about the relationship between structural critique and relational restoration. Micah summarized the whole requirement in twelve Hebrew words: do mishpat, love hesed, walk humbly with your God (Micah 6:8). Note the sequence. The doing comes first — the active work of establishing right order, correcting what is wrong — and it is inseparable from the loving, the relational warmth that makes the correction life-giving rather than merely legal.

That is the comprehensive vision. Not one or the other. Both. Together. Rolling like water that does not run dry.

The church that accepts less than this has not yet begun to read the prophets.
