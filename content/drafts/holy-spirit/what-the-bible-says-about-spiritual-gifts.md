For a lot of people new to the faith, the doctrine of the Spirit goes along smoothly — he is a person, he convicts and indwells and comforts — right up until somebody mentions spiritual gifts. Then the ground gets strange. Tongues. Prophecy. Healing. Words of knowledge. A whole vocabulary that sounds either thrilling or alarming depending on which church you walked into first, and a Christian world that appears to be divided into two rooms that barely spoke to each other about it.

If that is where you are, I want to do two things in this essay. First, show you what the Bible plainly says about spiritual gifts, which is more than most people realize and less frightening than the arguments suggest. Second, give you an honest map of the disagreement — because the disagreement is real, it is held by serious and faithful people on both sides, and you deserve to understand it rather than be recruited into a camp before you know what the camps are.

Here is the thesis, and it is meant to lower the temperature. **Spiritual gifts are not exotic spiritual achievements for an elite tier of Christian. They are ordinary equipment the Spirit hands out so that ordinary believers can build up the church — and the fierce debate over which gifts are still active today is a real disagreement among people who agree on far more than they dispute.**

## The plain shape of the teaching

Start with the word. The New Testament word for spiritual gifts is *charismata*, which comes from the word for grace, *charis*. That root is the whole point. A gift is not a wage you earned or a rank you achieved. It is grace taking a usable form — something given, not attained, and given for someone other than yourself.

Paul lays it out most fully in 1 Corinthians 12, writing to a church that had managed to turn its gifts into a status competition. He heads the whole discussion off at the pass: "Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit ... To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good" (1 Corinthians 12:4, 7). Read that last phrase twice. *For the common good.* Not for your platform, not for your spiritual scoreboard, but for the building up of everyone else. Then Paul does something brilliant. He compares the church to a body — many parts, one body, the eye unable to tell the hand it is unnecessary, the head unable to say it has no need of the feet. The gifts are diverse on purpose, distributed on purpose, and worthless the moment they become a reason to rank one Christian above another.

He returns to it in Romans 12, with a slightly different and very down-to-earth list: prophecy, service, teaching, encouraging, generous giving, leadership, acts of mercy (Romans 12:6–8). Notice how ordinary most of that is. Generosity is a spiritual gift. Quiet acts of mercy are a spiritual gift. The dramatic ones get the headlines, but Paul buries them in a list that also includes the person who is simply, reliably good at encouraging discouraged people. The Spirit's gifts are not all fireworks. Most of them look like a faithful church member doing the unglamorous thing well.

And in Ephesians 4, Paul names the gifts as gifted *people* given to the church — apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds, teachers — and says why: "to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ ... until we all attain to the unity of the faith" (Ephesians 4:11–13). The aim of every gift, every time, is maturity and unity. A gift that divides the church has been turned against its own purpose.

So before any argument about which gifts are for today, here is what the Bible says without dispute: the gifts are grace, they are diverse, they are for others, and they exist to make the church mature and one. Hold that. It is the ground both sides of the coming argument stand on.

One more undisputed point deserves saying, because it dismantles the status game before it starts. Paul insists in 1 Corinthians 12 that the Spirit himself distributes the gifts "as he wills" (1 Corinthians 12:11). You do not select your gift from a menu, and you do not earn it by spiritual seniority. It is apportioned to you by a person who knows the body's needs better than you do. That single fact knocks the legs out from under both pride and envy. You cannot be proud of a gift you did not choose and could not generate, and you have no grounds to envy someone else's, because the same Spirit who gave them theirs gave you yours, on purpose, for a job only your part of the body can do. The hand does not get to resent the eye. The Spirit assigned both.

## The harder chapter, and the order Paul insists on

Then there is 1 Corinthians 14, the chapter people fight over, and it repays a careful look because Paul is not doing what either camp sometimes wants him to do.

Corinth was infatuated with the gift of tongues — speech in a language the speaker did not learn, which the New Testament treats as real. Paul does not forbid it. He says plainly, "I want you all to speak in tongues" — and then immediately, "but even more to prophesy" (1 Corinthians 14:5). Why prophecy over tongues in the gathered church? Because prophecy can be understood, and what can be understood can build people up, and building people up is the entire point. His governing rule for the whole chapter is one sentence: "Let all things be done for building up" (1 Corinthians 14:26). He wants the gifts used. He wants them used in order. "God is not a God of confusion but of peace" (1 Corinthians 14:33). Paul is neither the cheerleader nor the prosecutor of the dramatic gifts. He is the pastor insisting that whatever the Spirit gives, it serves love and order or it is being misused.

That balance is the thing to hold onto when you walk into the disagreement, because both camps can quote this chapter, and both are reading a real Paul.

## The two rooms, fairly described

Now the divide. For most of church history this was not a live debate in the way it is now, but since roughly the start of the twentieth century, with the rise of Pentecostal and then charismatic movements, the question has sharpened: are gifts like tongues, prophecy, and miraculous healing still given today, or did they belong to a founding era of the church and then cease? Two main answers, and I will state each as its best defenders would.

The **cessationist** view holds that certain gifts — particularly the sign gifts like tongues, prophecy, and miraculous healing — served a specific purpose in the founding age of the church. They authenticated the apostles and the message before the New Testament was complete, and once the foundation was laid and Scripture finalized, those particular gifts ceased, having done their job. Cessationists are not denying the Spirit's power; they will tell you, rightly, that the Spirit is intensely active today in conviction, conversion, sanctification, and the ordinary gifts. They are making a narrower historical claim about a specific set of miraculous gifts, and they are often motivated by a real pastoral concern — protecting people from manipulation, from manufactured experiences, from leaders who claim a "word from God" to control them. That concern is not paranoia. It has a long record behind it.

The **continuationist** view holds that nothing in Scripture says the gifts would stop before Christ returns, and that the burden of proof lies with anyone claiming they did. Continuationists point out that Paul's instructions in 1 Corinthians 14 assume ongoing use, that the New Testament never schedules an expiration date for any gift, and that the church has accounts of these gifts in many times and places since. They too are not naive; the careful ones insist on the same order Paul demanded — testing, accountability, building up, never chaos. They simply expect the Spirit to give today what he gave then, while submitting every claim to Scripture.

D. A. Carson wrote a careful study of 1 Corinthians 12 through 14 in 1987 called *Showing the Spirit*, and what makes it valuable is that Carson — no wild-eyed enthusiast — concluded the text does not support drawing a hard line that shuts the gifts down. Gordon Fee, in *God's Empowering Presence* (1994), made a similar exegetical case at length. But here is the point I most want a newcomer to absorb: even scholars who land on the continuationist side write with deep respect for their cessationist brothers and sisters, because they know the people across the aisle love the same Lord, read the same book, and want the same thing — a church built up rather than carried away.

The verse the two camps most often square off over is in the middle of the love chapter, where Paul says that prophecies will pass away, tongues will cease, and knowledge will pass away, "but when the perfect comes, the partial will pass away" (1 Corinthians 13:8–10). Cessationists have sometimes read "the perfect" as the completed Bible, which would mean the gifts ceased once Scripture was finished. Continuationists, including Carson, read "the perfect" as the return of Christ and the world to come — the day when we see "face to face" and know fully, which is plainly not yet. This is the kind of honest interpretive disagreement that mature Christians can hold without questioning one another's faith. Notice, too, what the passage assumes either way: the gifts are temporary. They are scaffolding, not the building. A day is coming when prophecy and tongues and partial knowledge will all be unnecessary, because the thing they pointed toward will have arrived in person. The gifts matter enormously now and will matter not at all then. That alone should keep anyone from treating them as the center of the faith.

It also helps to notice that this is a relatively recent argument in the church's long life, which should make everyone humble. For many centuries Christians simply did not frame the question in these terms. The sharp modern division traces largely to the twentieth century, and a newcomer who feels behind for not having an opinion should take heart: the wisest believers across two thousand years got along without one. You are not required to settle in your first year what the church spent most of its history not even asking.

## What you do not have to do

You do not have to resolve this before you can follow Jesus. That sentence is worth its own paragraph. The gifts question is what the older theologians called a secondary matter — a real issue, worth thinking through, but not the dividing line between faith and unbelief. Whole communions of faithful Christians land on each side. You can be wrong about cessationism and continuationism and still be saved, still be filled, still be useful to God. The enemy would love nothing more than to convince a new believer that they must pick a tribe and despise the other before they have learned to pray.

What you cannot opt out of is the part that is not in dispute. You have a gift. Scripture says so plainly: "To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit." Not to some. To each. Somewhere in the ordinary list and the dramatic list there is grace the Spirit has put in you for the good of people around you, and the tragedy is not getting the gifts debate wrong. The tragedy is letting the debate become a reason to bury the gift you were given. Far more Christians sin by neglect than by excess here. The body has parts that have decided they are unnecessary, and they are wrong.

How do you find your gift? Not by taking a quiz in isolation and waiting for a feeling. The gifts were given to a body, and they surface in the life of a body. You find what the Spirit has given you the same way a hand discovers it is a hand — by being put to use among the other parts. Serve. Show up where the church is doing its work. Pay attention to where your service actually builds people up, where others keep telling you something helped them, where you find a strange durability of joy in a task that would exhaust someone else. That convergence — a thing you can do, that helps the body, that you are drawn to do again — is usually the Spirit's gift coming into view. It rarely announces itself in the abstract. It shows itself in the doing, confirmed by the people you served. A gift you never use is a gift you may never identify, which is its own argument against sitting on the sidelines until the cessationism debate is settled to your satisfaction.

## The test that outranks the argument

Paul knew his Corinthians would argue about the gifts forever, so he set a chapter right in the middle of the discussion to outrank the whole thing. Between chapter 12 and chapter 14 stands chapter 13, the one read at weddings, which was never about weddings. Tongues without love is a clanging gong. Prophecy and knowledge without love is nothing. Giving your body to be burned without love gains nothing. Whatever gifts you have or do not have, whichever room you end up worshiping in, the test is the same and it is merciless: is love being built, or is the self being displayed?

That is the question the Spirit cares about, and it is the same in both rooms. A continuationist church that uses every gift to feed its own spectacle has failed Paul's test. A cessationist church so afraid of excess that it has quietly stopped expecting the Spirit to do anything has failed it from the other direction. The gifts were given for the common good. Love is how you tell whether they are doing their job.

Walk into either room, then, slowly and humbly. Learn what your gift is and use it. Hold the argument with an open hand and the love with both. The Spirit gave the gifts to build a body, not to draft an army against the other half of his own church.
