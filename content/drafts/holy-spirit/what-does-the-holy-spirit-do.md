> [PERSONAL STORY — James to supply: a moment you can name where, looking back, you suspect the Spirit was at work in you before you had any vocabulary for him — perhaps in the slow undoing of your atheism. Drop it in after "I had no name for it then."]

Suppose you grant the first claim — that the Holy Spirit is a person and not a force, the third member of the Trinity rather than a divine atmosphere. A fair next question, and the one a newcomer asks almost immediately, is blunter than the doctrine usually expects: so what does he *do?* What is he *for?* It is one thing to be told the Spirit is real and personal and worthy of worship. It is another to be able to point at anything in your actual life and say, *that was him.*

The honest answer is that the Spirit does a great deal, almost all of it without a name tag. He does not announce himself. He works the way light works — you do not see the light, you see what the light lets you see. So the question is not really *what does the Spirit do* but *where have I been failing to recognize him.*

Here is the thesis. **Nearly everything you would call genuinely good in a Christian's inner life — the conviction that you needed God, the sense that he is near, the slow change in what you want, the steadiness when life caves in — is the Spirit at work. He is not the dramatic exception to the ordinary Christian life. He is the engine of it.** Let me walk through five things he does, because the list itself is a kind of argument.

## He convicts

Start where the Spirit usually starts in a person, which is before they would ever describe themselves as religious.

Jesus said the Spirit, when he came, would "convict the world concerning sin and righteousness and judgment" (John 16:8). That word *convict* is the place most of us first met him without knowing it. It is the uninvited sense that something is wrong with you that no one put there — the discomfort that arrives in the middle of a good day, the quiet that turns loud when you lie down, the suspicion that the problem is not your circumstances but you. People spend enormous energy explaining that feeling away. I did. I had no name for it then, and I assumed it was guilt I had absorbed from a culture I had already rejected.

Conviction is not the same as condemnation, and the difference matters more than almost anything in this essay. Condemnation says *you are worthless and there is no way back.* Conviction says *you are wrong and there is a way home.* The first crushes. The second draws. The Spirit's conviction always has a door in it — it shows you the sin in order to show you the Savior, never to leave you pinned to the floor. When you feel the floor of your own self-justification giving way, that is not despair closing in. That is, very often, a person drawing near.

It is worth lingering on what Jesus actually said the Spirit would convict the world about, because it is broader than a guilty conscience over a particular sin. He named three things — sin, righteousness, and judgment (John 16:8). The Spirit convicts the world of *sin*, yes, but he also convicts it of *righteousness* — of the fact that there is such a thing as real goodness, embodied in Christ, against which our counterfeits show up as counterfeits. And he convicts of *judgment* — of the settled truth that evil has already been defeated and will not have the last word. Put together, the Spirit's convicting work is not merely making you feel bad. It is waking a numb conscience to the whole moral shape of reality: that sin is real, that goodness is real, that a reckoning is real. People who have never darkened a church door feel the edges of this all the time and call it by other names. The first stirrings of *something is wrong and something is right and it matters* are, more often than not, a person beginning his work.

## He indwells

Then comes the strangest thing the New Testament says about the Spirit, and it deserves a slow read.

Paul writes to a difficult, divided church in Corinth, "Do you not know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you?" In Romans he says it even more directly: "You ... are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if in fact the Spirit of God dwells in you" (Romans 8:9). The word is *dwells.* Not visits. Not hovers nearby. Lives in. The same God who once filled a temple in Jerusalem with a glory so heavy the priests could not stand now takes up residence inside ordinary people. The temple is no longer a building. The temple is you.

This is what Christians mean, technically, when they speak of *indwelling*, and Romans 8 is where it reaches its height. The same chapter says the Spirit testifies with our spirit that we are children of God, that we cry "Abba, Father" — the intimate word a child uses — not because we worked up the feeling but because the Spirit himself prompts the cry from inside (Romans 8:15–16). Gordon Fee, the New Testament scholar who spent a career on this material, titled his great 1994 study *God's Empowering Presence*, and the phrase is exact. The Spirit is not God's gift left on the doorstep. The Spirit is God's *presence*, moved in, taking up the cry of *Father* in a heart that used to have no such word.

Consider what indwelling answers. The deepest fear underneath most religion is that God is far off — that he exists, perhaps, but at a distance, watching, to be appeased across a gap you can never close. The indwelling of the Spirit is the direct contradiction of that fear. The gap is closed, and it is closed from God's side, by God moving in. Paul tells the Romans that if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to Christ at all (Romans 8:9) — which means the Spirit's indwelling is not an advanced upgrade for spiritual veterans. It is the basic mark of belonging to Jesus in the first place. Every ordinary Christian, the moment they belong to Christ, has become the dwelling place of God. That is not a feeling you have to generate. It is a fact about your address. The God you were afraid lived far away has, it turns out, moved into the house.

## He empowers

But the Spirit does not move in to make you comfortable. He moves in to send you out.

The last thing Jesus told his followers before he left was a promise of power with a direction attached: "You will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). Notice the shape of it. The power is real, but it is not power for its own sake, not power to impress or dominate or feel electric. It is power *to witness* — power to do the specific, costly, often unglamorous work of telling people about Jesus and living in a way that makes the telling credible.

This is where Fee's title earns its keep again. The empowering presence is what turned a band of frightened deserters — men who had run from a servant girl's question on the night Jesus was arrested — into people who would stand before the same authorities that killed their Lord and refuse to stop talking. Nothing in their personalities explains the change. Something came upon them. The empowering work of the Spirit is the difference between knowing the truth and being able to bear its weight in public. You will recognize it less as a surge of feeling than as an unaccountable ability to do the thing you were too afraid to do yesterday.

A newcomer should hear plainly what this power is not, because the word *power* gets badly abused. The Spirit's power is not a force you wield to get your way, to dominate a room, to perform, or to make your life go smoothly. The same Spirit who empowered the apostles to speak boldly also empowered them to be beaten for it and keep their joy. Spirit-power in the New Testament is most often power to endure, to love a difficult person, to forgive an enemy, to keep faith when faith costs everything. It looks far more like strength under pressure than like fireworks on demand. The strongest evidence that the Spirit has empowered someone is rarely something impressive they did. It is something hard they bore without breaking.

## He comforts

And when the cost of all that comes due, the Spirit does the work his oldest title names.

Jesus called him the *Paraclete*, the one called alongside, and one of the things a person called alongside does is comfort — not the thin comfort of distraction but the thick comfort of company. Paul says that when we do not even know what to pray, "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). Sit with that. In the seasons when prayer collapses into silence, when you cannot assemble a single coherent sentence to send heavenward, the Spirit is not waiting for you to recover your composure. He is praying *for* you, from inside you, in a language deeper than language. The comfort of the Spirit is not that he removes the dark. It is that he is in the dark with you, doing for you what you have lost the strength to do for yourself.

## He sanctifies

Which brings us to the longest and least dramatic work of all, the one that takes a lifetime.

The Spirit makes you holy. The technical word is *sanctification*, and it simply names the slow process by which a person actually changes — not their behavior managed by willpower, but their wants reordered from the inside. Paul tells Titus that God "saved us ... by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit" (Titus 3:5). Regeneration is the start, the new birth; renewal is the long work after, the gradual remaking of a self. Romans 8 ties the whole thing together: "If by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live" (Romans 8:13). The Spirit does not hand you a better moral résumé. He puts something to death in you and raises something else in its place, and most of the time it is so gradual you only notice it by looking back across years.

J. I. Packer wrote a book in 1984 with a title taken straight from Paul — *Keep in Step with the Spirit* — and his central insight was that holiness is not a heroic solo act but a partnership you keep in step with. The Spirit leads; you walk. He does not drag, and he does not stroll off and leave you. Sanctification is what it looks like to live a whole life learning the Spirit's pace.

Packer was also careful to puncture two false expectations that have done a lot of damage to ordinary believers. The first is the idea that the Spirit's sanctifying work should feel constantly dramatic — that if you are not having intense experiences, nothing is happening. Packer answered that the Spirit's deepest work is usually the quietest: the slow death of a resentment, the gradual loosening of a grip a sin had on you for years, the unspectacular growth of patience in a person who used to have none. The second false expectation is the opposite error — that holiness is a self-improvement project you run on your own steam, with God consulted occasionally. Packer's whole point in keeping the word *Spirit* in the title was that you are never the engine. You are the one keeping step with the engine. The sanctified life is neither a passive wait for God to zap you nor a grim solo march of willpower. It is a walk, with a companion who sets the pace and never abandons the road.

## Why you keep missing it

Run back over the five and a pattern emerges. The Spirit convicts you when you would rather feel fine. He moves into a life that was not asking for a tenant. He hands out power you cannot trace to your own talent. He comforts you in a dark you cannot pray your way out of. He changes you so slowly you credit the calendar instead of him. Every one of these is designed to be missed — to be attributed to your own conscience, your own resolve, your own good day, anything but a person at work.

There is one more thing the Spirit does that ties the other five together, and it is the thing Jesus stressed most. He bears witness to Christ. "He will glorify me," Jesus said, "for he will take what is mine and declare it to you." Every other work — convicting, indwelling, empowering, comforting, sanctifying — finally aims here: at making Jesus real, near, and trusted. This is why the most reliable sign of the Spirit's work is never how spiritual a person feels but how large Christ has become to them. A movement that draws all the attention to the Spirit himself, to experiences and manifestations and the people who have them, has in a sense misunderstood the Spirit, because the Spirit's own passion is to point past himself to the Son. The third person of the Trinity is, you might say, the least self-interested person in the universe. His entire ministry is to make someone else glorious. When you find yourself, after a hard season, loving Jesus more than you did before and trusting him more than you knew you could — that, more than any feeling, is the fingerprint of the forgotten God.

Packer's image is the one to keep. You do not feel the Spirit the way you feel a current. You recognize him the way you recognize a companion on a long walk — by the fact that you are still moving, still upright, still pointed somewhere good, when by every account of your own strength you should have sat down in the road a long way back.

That is what he does. He has, very likely, been doing it in you longer than you have had a name for him. The grief of the *forgotten God* is not that he is absent. It is that he has been present the whole time, working in the very places we were sure we were managing on our own.
