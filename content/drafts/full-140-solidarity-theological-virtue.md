The word solidarity carries freight it did not earn in every room where it is spoken.

In some rooms it sounds like a union hall. In some rooms it sounds like a protest slogan. In American evangelical Christianity it has acquired, through a process of cultural association, the smell of progressive politics — which means that the word itself, before the argument is made, has already been sorted by many listeners into "not our category, not our language, not our concern."

The irony is severe. The concept the word describes is one of the most thoroughly biblical claims about the character of God and the calling of the church that the tradition contains.

The Greek word for it, in its New Testament vicinity, is sympatheo — to suffer with. The God of Scripture does not observe human suffering from outside. The entire arc of the Old Testament is the story of a God who hears the cry of the enslaved (Exodus 3:7), who descends to deliver, who is not neutral between the oppressor and the one being crushed, who dwells with the contrite and lowly in spirit (Isaiah 57:15). The incarnation is the logical completion of this pattern. The Word became flesh and dwelt — literally, pitched a tent — among us (John 1:14). Not among us in the sense of proximity. Among us in the sense of solidarity. God did not send a message about human suffering. God entered human suffering, in the body, in the conditions of poverty and marginality, in a province occupied by an empire.

That is not a political statement. It is a description of what God did. And it carries irreversible implications for what God requires of the people who claim to follow this God.

Solidarity, as a theological virtue, means this: to stand with people in conditions you did not create and may not share, not as a manager of their problem but as a participant in their reality. This is different from charity, which typically moves from a place of security to a place of need with a gift in hand. Charity is good. Solidarity is more demanding. It requires proximity. It requires the willingness to let your own status be affected by association with those whose status costs them.

Paul names something like this in Philippians 2: "Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others." He grounds it immediately in the christological hymn that follows — Christ, who was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but made himself nothing, taking the form of a servant. The downward movement of Christ is the model for the downward movement the church is called to make. Not to manage the needy from a distance. To move toward them, which means moving away from the securities that distance provides.

I have not done this consistently. I have been charitable without being solidary — which means I have helped from a position that protected my own comfort and social location while occasionally distributing from my surplus. That is not the same thing as standing with. It is closer to standing near.

The early church attracted the attention of the Roman world not primarily through its teaching but through its practice. Pagan observers noted that Christians cared for their sick during plagues when others fled [cite — confirm], took in exposed infants others had discarded, and shared their resources across class lines in ways that had no precedent in the social arrangements of the empire. This was not a program. It was a theology worked out in the body — the theology of a God who had refused to remain at a safe distance from human need.

Solidarity is costly in ways that charity is not. Charity can be anonymous. Solidarity requires presence. Charity gives from where you are. Solidarity goes to where they are. Charity addresses need. Solidarity addresses the loneliness of being in need — the particular weight of carrying a condition that no one around you seems to share or be willing to share.

The virtue the gospel requires is not the benevolent distribution of surplus. It is the willingness to say: your condition is my concern, not because I have resources that might help, but because you are my neighbor and your struggle is mine.

That is theology. It has always been theology.

The political language borrowed it. We should not let the borrowing become an excuse to abandon the original.
