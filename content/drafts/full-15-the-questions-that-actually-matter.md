Most defenses of Christianity I read at twenty-three did not answer the questions I was actually asking. They answered the questions Christians wished I were asking. The questions I was actually asking were harder, and more honest, and not addressed by the bad apologetics on my shelf. I came to faith despite the apologetics, not because of it. I want to write the apologetics I needed then.

I am writing this for the version of me that was twenty, sitting in a university library in the middle of an essay on Russell's *Why I Am Not a Christian*. I am also writing it for any version of you who is in roughly that position now — the skeptic, the agnostic, the deconstructing former Christian, the person who left the church for reasons that seemed sufficient and has not been given a reason to come back. The first thing I want to do, before I make any argument, is to name what the questions actually are. Most of the rest of this series will be devoted to engaging them one at a time. This first essay just names them, takes them seriously, and gestures at what kind of answer is even possible.

## Five questions

The first question is the hiddenness question. If God exists, why does he stay so hidden? Why does he not make himself unmistakably evident, in the way the existence of the sun is unmistakably evident? Why is there a serious case for atheism at all, if God is real? Why does it take work to find him? Why do thoughtful, honest people end up in radically different places on the question? The pop apologetic answer — *he is right there, you just have to look* — is not a serious answer. The pop apologetic answer is what a person says who has not taken the question seriously. The real answer, if there is one, has to account for the hiddenness without explaining it away.

The second question is the moral evidence question. The universe contains moral evil of a magnitude that, if there is a God, makes God look either weak or cruel. The Holocaust. Cancer in children. Tsunami. Random meaningless suffering of innocent creatures. The Christian standard apologetic answers — the free-will defense, the soul-making theodicy, the greater-good frame — each have something to say, but each is intellectually incomplete in the face of an actual sufferer. The question is not whether there is *some* possible answer. The question is whether the answer that scripture and the tradition actually offer is livable in the face of the suffering, and whether the answer makes a Christian intellectually honest. I have spent twenty years thinking about this, and I have a better answer than I had at twenty, but it is not the answer the pop apologetics gives.

The third question is the submission question. What do I owe a story that requires my submission? The Christian faith is not, in its serious historic form, an invitation to add a religious feature to my otherwise autonomous life. It is a demand for the surrender of my autonomy to a Lord who, if his account of himself is correct, has the right to rearrange my life completely. That demand is what the pop apologetics tends to soften — the modern American gospel pitch is often the pitch that Jesus will improve my life without requiring me to surrender it. The pitch is dishonest. The real Christian demand is more total, and the skeptic who is taking the question seriously is right to want to know exactly what is being asked of him before he considers saying yes.

The fourth question is the religious-plurality question. Why this religion and not the others? The world contains Hinduism and Buddhism and Islam and various forms of indigenous religion and the increasingly elaborated spiritual-but-not-religious of the post-Christian West. Each makes claims. Each has its serious thinkers. Each has its experiences and its arguments. Why is the Christian story the right one, and not one of the others? The pop apologetic move is often to refute one of the others — usually atheism, less often Islam, almost never Buddhism — without addressing why the Christian story would be selected over the spectrum of alternatives. The real question is comparative. The real answer has to be comparative. Most Christians I know have never done the comparative work seriously, and the skeptic who has done some of it is rightly suspicious of an account that has not.

The fifth question is the problem-of-love question. This one is mine, in the sense that I am not sure I have seen it formulated quite this way elsewhere. The standard problem of evil is logical — how can an omnipotent and good God permit evil? My version of the problem is harder. The problem is that the universe, if there is a God who is love, does not look like a universe made by love. It looks like a universe made by something more ambivalent. The horrors are not edge cases; the horrors are pervasive and structural. If God is love, what kind of love produces a world like this? The skeptic asking this question is not asking whether God's permission of evil is logically defensible. The skeptic is asking whether the God who would permit a world like this is worth loving back. I think the question has an answer, but the answer is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is part of why I trust it.

## What kind of answer is possible

I want to say something about what kind of answer is possible to these questions before I take them up one at a time in subsequent essays.

The kind of answer that is not possible is the proof. There is no proof. There is no argument that compels the rational skeptic to conclude that Christianity is true. The history of apologetics is littered with claims to such proofs, and none of them has succeeded. The medieval ontological argument and the cosmological argument and the design argument and the more recent versions developed by Plantinga and Swinburne and Craig — each contributes something to the cumulative case, and none is finally compelling on its own. The skeptic who is waiting for an iron-clad demonstration will wait forever. The waiting is not a sign of the skeptic's intellectual seriousness; the waiting is, sometimes, a sign of the skeptic's unwillingness to consider that the question may not be settled by the kind of evidence the skeptic has decided to require.

The kind of answer that is possible is the cumulative case. The cumulative case is what scripture itself offers. Romans 1 is one form of it — the recognition that the structure of the world points to something, that the experience of moral seriousness points to something, that the deep human longing for meaning points to something. The book of Acts is another form — the testimony to the resurrection appearances, the historical change in Paul, the rapid emergence of a movement that the surrounding political conditions cannot account for. The Gospels are another form — the historical record of a particular life whose claims, if true, have an enormous explanatory weight. The historic creeds and the centuries of saints and the rationally reasoned theological tradition is another form. None of these is a proof. All of them together are a cumulative case, and the cumulative case is what an honest decision about Christianity has to be made on.

The honest skeptic and the honest Christian agree on more than the lazy versions of either admit. They agree that the question is not closed by the best argument of either side. They agree that the choice is a choice, in which evidence is weighed and intuitions are interrogated and a finally personal commitment is made. They disagree about which way the cumulative case finally points. The honest skeptic looks at the cumulative case and concludes that the secular alternative is more probable. The honest Christian looks at the cumulative case and concludes that the Christian explanation is more probable. Both are making a decision under conditions of incomplete evidence. Both are betting, in some sense. The bet has consequences. The next six essays are about the consequences.

## The structure of the series

I want to be honest about what I am going to do over the next six essays, because the structure of the series matters and I want you to know what you are signing up for.

I will take up the hiddenness question and the moral-evidence question together in the next essay — what secular explanations still have to explain. I will take up the problem of suffering, including my version of it, in the essay after that. I will take up the historical Jesus in the fourth essay of the track. I will take up the question of how to read the Bible — what the Bible is actually for, given that it is not the document the pop apologetics has been selling — in the fifth. I will take up the moral question, including the question of whether morality without God is possible and what difference morality with God makes, in the sixth. And I will close the track with what following this actually costs — the honest accounting of what conversion to serious Christianity will require of you, because if I am going to ask you to consider it, I have to tell you the cost up front.

The series will not, I am pretty sure, convince every skeptic who reads it. The series will give the skeptic enough to think about that, if he stays with it, he will at least know what he is rejecting more clearly than he did when he started. That is a worthy outcome. The Christianity I came to from atheism at twenty-four was a Christianity I knew I was choosing because I had counted what the choice cost. The pop apologetics had not let me count the cost. I am writing this for the readers who want to count, and who are willing to do so with me, and who are willing to grant me the benefit of the doubt that I am writing in good faith and not running a long-form bait and switch.

I am not running a long-form bait and switch. I want you to know what you are reading. I am writing the apologetics I needed at twenty. I am writing it with the awareness, gained over twenty years of pastoral work, that the audience for serious apologetics is smaller than evangelical institutions have generally assumed, and the audience is also more theologically capable than evangelical institutions have generally assumed. I am writing for the smaller and more capable audience. If you are in it, stay with me. If you are not, I will not feel insulted if you stop reading. The honest reading and the honest stopping are both legitimate responses.

## The verdict, such as it is

The questions are real. The questions are harder than most apologetic literature has been willing to acknowledge. The questions deserve serious answers. Most of the work of these seven essays is the work of giving them serious answers — not proofs, but the cumulative case that I myself have found persuasive, in the hope that some of you will find it persuasive too.

I came to faith from atheism. I did not come because of an argument; I came because of a particular complex of arguments and experiences and providential encounters that, taken together, made the Christian explanation of my life more credible to me than the secular one. I have not regretted the decision. I have, in the twenty-two years since, found the decision more justified rather than less. The cost has been real. The benefit has been larger. The honest writing of this series is part of how I pay back the debt I owe to the people who took my questions seriously when I was twenty-three and did not have what I have now.

The next essay takes up what the secular worldview still has to explain. I want to make the strongest case for the secular alternative that I can, before I name the explanatory remainders. The honest reader will, I hope, recognize that I am steel-manning the position, not strawmanning it. The honest skeptic will, I hope, recognize that the eventual Christian conclusion is reached through the most difficult version of the question, not the easy version. Stay for the second essay. We will not get to the resurrection until the fourth. We have to do the philosophical preliminaries first, because the resurrection itself, even if defended well, lands differently depending on whether you have done the underlying work. We are doing the underlying work now. Begin.
