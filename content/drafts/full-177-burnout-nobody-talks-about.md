The burnout we talk about is dramatic. The pastor who quits. The one who has the affair. The one who leaves the faith entirely, writes a book about it, goes on a podcast. The one whose congregation finds out about the financial irregularities or the pattern of manipulation. We talk about those because they give us a narrative — a clear sequence of events, a visible collapse, an identifiable moment when it all fell apart.

The burnout I want to talk about is quieter than that.

It is the pastor who is still in the pulpit. Still in the building on Sunday. Still doing the visits, running the meetings, answering the emails. From the outside, he looks like a faithful man doing the work. From the inside, he has been running on empty for two years, and he has gotten so good at performing faithfulness that he can barely tell the difference between performing it and living it anymore.

That is the burnout nobody talks about. And it is far more common.

## What it actually looks like

It does not look like exhaustion, exactly. Exhausted people want rest. This is different. This is a kind of interior flatness — a place where the preaching that once felt like something you were compelled to do now feels like a task to complete, where the prayer that once felt like conversation now feels like obligation, where the people whose needs once moved you now feel, at the worst moments, like demands you are trying to manage.

I know this because I have been inside it. I have stood in the pulpit and preached a sermon that I could see was working — the congregation was engaged, the truth was landing, the Spirit was moving in the room in ways I could perceive — and felt almost nothing. Not because the sermon was bad. Because the distance between what the sermon announced and what my interior life could access had grown to the point where the gap was more present to me than the sermon.

That gap is the burnout nobody talks about. It does not announce itself as a crisis. It presents as a gradual lowering of the room temperature of your soul.

## How it develops

The development is almost always slow and almost always related to the structural dynamics of pastoral ministry that we refuse to examine honestly.

The first is the expectation of availability. The theology of pastoral calling has, in many congregations, been constructed in a way that makes any limit on the pastor's availability feel like faithlessness. The congregation's need is real. The pastor's need for rest, for solitude, for relationships that are not ministry relationships, for the ordinary non-professional human experiences that sustain a person's interior life — these are treated as luxuries that the faithful pastor will sacrifice and the self-indulgent pastor will insist upon. This is not biblical. Jesus withdrew from the crowds. He sent the disciples away. He slept in the boat during a storm. The pattern of withdrawal and return is present throughout the Gospels, and it is not a failure of ministry commitment. It is the condition of ministry that does not destroy the minister.

The second is the accumulation of unexpressed grief. Pastoral ministry produces exposure to suffering that is extraordinary in its density. The funerals, the hospital rooms, the marriages that are falling apart, the prodigals who do not come home, the people who leave the church in anger or drift away without explanation — these accumulate. And the pastoral role does not generally provide a legitimate space for the pastor's own grief about them. The pastor is the comforter. He is not supposed to need comforting. So the grief accumulates, unexpressed, and eventually it produces a numbness that presents as stability.

## What the slow burnout costs

It costs the congregation more than the dramatic burnout does, in many cases, because it is invisible and therefore unaddressed. The pastor who collapses publicly is at least known to have collapsed. The congregation can respond. The systems can mobilize. The crisis is named.

The pastor who is quietly disappearing for years is not named. He is present. He is functioning. His sermons are prepared. He is simply no longer fully there — no longer able to bring the weight of his own soul to the work, because the weight of his soul has been slowly depleted by demands that were never matched by the replenishment that serious ministry requires.

The congregation pays for this in ways that are hard to measure. The pastoral care that lacks genuine empathy. The preaching that is technically sound but does not carry the weight of lived conviction. The leadership that is competent but not inspired. The person in the hospital room who leaves the visit feeling cared for but not actually reached.

## What turns it around

Not a sabbatical, by itself. A sabbatical without structural change is a vacation from a system that will produce the same burnout again in three years. What turns it around is naming it — to someone who can hold the truth of it without needing you to be stronger than you are, and without needing the information to remain confidential from the people who could change the structures that produced it.

Naming it means saying: I have been performing faithfulness for two years while being empty. It means saying: the way this ministry is structured is not sustainable for a human being. It means saying: I need rest and I need to build the kind of relationships and practices that sustain ministry rather than simply sustaining ministry from the last of what I have.

The prophet Elijah, after the extraordinary confrontation on Mount Carmel, collapsed under a broom tree and asked God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4). He was not weak. He was depleted. The angel's response was not a call to recommitment. It was food and water and sleep. Twice. Then a long journey. Then a question: "What are you doing here, Elijah?" Not a reprimand. An invitation to name what had happened.

God already knew. The naming was for Elijah.

Name it. That is where it starts.
