The photographs from multi-ethnic Sunday services are real. The warmth in those rooms is real. No one who has experienced genuine worship across racial lines would dismiss what happens there. It is a glimpse — a real glimpse — of something the New Testament insists is essential to the church's identity: that the dividing wall of hostility between peoples has been broken down in Christ (Ephesians 2:14).

But a photograph of diversity is not reconciliation. A moment of warmth is not reconciliation. Reconciliation is a specific theological movement — from estrangement to restored relationship — and it has a specific requirement that the American church has largely tried to satisfy without meeting.

The requirement is repentance. Not acknowledged wrongdoing. Not regret for the way things turned out. Repentance — the Hebrew *shuv*, the Greek *metanoia* — is a turning. It is a turning away from the patterns that produced the estrangement, toward a different way of being that demonstrates in practice that something has actually changed. Without the turning, what you have is apology without amendment. The relationship does not change. The power arrangements do not change. The systems that produced the injury continue producing it. Only the language about them has shifted.

The American church's history with race is not a record of isolated individual failures that can be addressed one apology at a time. It is a record of institutional choices made consistently across centuries: white churches that supported slaveholding theology, that remained silent during the lynching era, that opposed integration, that used biblical arguments to justify arrangements the prophets would have recognized as the exploitation of the vulnerable by the powerful. That history is documented. It is not in dispute among historians. [cite — confirm]

The response to that history that the American church has most often attempted is what we might call reconciliation-without-turning: diverse programming, cross-cultural preaching events, statements of acknowledgment, partnerships that are more symbolic than structural. These are not nothing. But they are not repentance, and they tend to ask the same communities that were harmed to do the labor of making the institution feel better about itself.

Genuine repentance would require something harder. It would require white congregations to examine what they have materially benefited from — in wealth, in land, in social standing — through the historical arrangements they now apologize for, and to ask what restoration might require. Not as an abstract question but as a concrete one: what do we actually owe, and to whom, and are we willing to pay it? It would require those same congregations to examine their present structures — who leads, who makes decisions, whose cultural preferences shape worship, whose comfort is protected — and to make the changes that genuine inclusion requires, not the changes that produce inclusion's appearance while leaving the existing arrangements intact.

I am not writing about other churches. I am writing about my own failures in this territory. I have been in rooms where the language of reconciliation was fluent and the structural change was absent. I have been part of conversations that produced warmth and produced nothing else. I have preached texts about unity across difference without examining the ways my own congregation's culture was organized around the comfort of the people who were already comfortable there. That is not neutral. It is complicity in a milder register.

The New Testament's vision of the church as a community where there is neither Jew nor Greek (Galatians 3:28) was not a description of a feeling shared at a gathering. It was a description of an economic and social reality that was, in the communities Paul was writing to, visibly and practically true: the wealthy sharing meals with the poor, the educated with the uneducated, the Jew with the Gentile, in ways that violated every social expectation of the first century. The church was scandalous precisely because the reconciliation was actual.

That is the standard. Not a better photo. An actual turning.

Until the turning, the language of reconciliation is something else. The word for it is marketing.
