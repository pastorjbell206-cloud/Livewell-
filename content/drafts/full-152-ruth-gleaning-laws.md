The gleaning laws in Leviticus 19 and 23 are among the most underpreached passages in the Old Testament. They commanded Israelite farmers not to harvest their fields to the edges, not to gather fallen grain, not to go back through their vineyards a second time. The margins of the field, the corners, the dropped fruit — these were to be left for the poor and the stranger.

This was not optional generosity. It was written into the legal code.

What Leviticus commands in law, the book of Ruth shows in practice. Ruth does not beg Boaz. She exercises a legal right — the right of the poor and the foreigner to glean from the harvest that the landowner was commanded to leave. When Boaz asks about her, his workers describe her activity not as charity-seeking but as working from early morning to the evening (Ruth 2:7). She is not passive. She is industrious. The gleaning law is not a welfare program. It is a framework for dignified labor — access to the means of survival without the dependency of pure charity.

Boaz's extraordinary kindness in the story is precisely that: extraordinary. He goes beyond what the law requires. He instructs his workers to leave extra grain, to let her drink from their water, to share their meal. The law created a floor. His generosity built a house on top of it. The story requires both — the legal structure that created the right and the human generosity that exceeded it.

We have almost entirely lost the category of structured access. The contemporary church thinks in terms of charity: we have resources, you have need, we will give you some of ours. The gleaning model thinks differently: the harvest belongs first to God, the poor have a prior claim on its margins, and the landowner is accountable for the structure he builds around his field. This is not charity. It is a form of justice built into the economy's architecture.

I have given to food banks. I have organized coat drives and Thanksgiving baskets and all the good works that constitute what we call mercy ministry. I have not often asked the more demanding question that the gleaning laws imply: are the structures of my economic life built in ways that create access for the poor, or do they concentrate benefit at the center and leave the margins empty? That is a different question than whether I am generous with what I accumulate. It is a question about design.

The book of Ruth is also a story about kinship. The Hebrew concept of the *goel* — the kinsman-redeemer — describes someone whose social obligation is to restore what the vulnerable have lost. Boaz functions as this redeemer. He restores Ruth and Naomi not just through grain but through legal protection, economic security, and full inclusion in the covenant community. The restoration is comprehensive. It does not address only the hunger. It addresses the whole condition of exclusion that the hunger represents.

The church has been better at the immediate — food, clothing, emergency relief — than at the structural. We have built many food banks and few programs that address why the food bank is always full. This is not a criticism of food banks. It is a diagnosis of the church's imagination.

The gleaning laws assume that the economy must be structured to include the poor — not as recipients of surplus charity, but as participants with a genuine stake in the harvest. The landowner does not have unlimited control over what his field produces. He has obligations. The field's edges belong, by covenant design, to those who have nothing of their own to harvest.

An economy designed around that principle would look different from what we have. Preaching that takes the gleaning laws seriously would sound different from what we usually preach. It would name not just the duty to give but the obligation to ask whether our fields are built with margins for those who need them.

Ruth picks up what Boaz left. The question is what we are leaving.
