When the disciples tried to keep the children away from Jesus, he was indignant (Mark 10:14). That word — indignant — is the same word used elsewhere to describe his response to the disciples' dullness, to hypocrisy, to misuse of the temple. It is not a mild correction. It is the kind of anger that comes when something important has been violated. He says: let the children come. Do not hinder them. The kingdom of God belongs to such as these.

He said this. He meant it in full. And then we built a civilization in which children born into poverty face measurably shorter lives, worse health outcomes, lower educational attainment, higher rates of exposure to violence and environmental toxins, and systematically reduced chances of economic mobility. [cite — confirm] We built it. We maintain it through policy choices and spending priorities and political alliances. And on Sunday mornings many of the people who built and maintain it sing about the one who said the kingdom belongs to children, and we do not experience this as contradiction.

That is remarkable. We should sit with how remarkable it is.

The prophetic tradition of Israel returns again and again to the orphan — the yatom, the child without a father to advocate and provide — as a specific measure of the community's faithfulness. Not as a category of charity. As a test. In Deuteronomy 24, the command to leave the edges of the field unharvested, to leave the olive branch for a second beating, to leave what falls from the vineyard — it is always "for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow." Three times in three verses. God is not uncertain about this. The child without protection is not a footnote to the covenant community's obligations. It is central.

Isaiah 1 — the opening chapter, the first thing God says in that prophetic collection — attacks Israel's worship: your offerings are meaningless, your assemblies are a burden to me, your appointed feasts my soul hates. Why? Because "your hands are full of blood." And then the corrective: seek justice, correct oppression, bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause (Isaiah 1:17). The fatherless. Among the first items listed when God describes what justice actually looks like in practice.

I have five sons. I know something about what it takes to raise them well — the time, the money, the presence, the advocacy at school meetings, the medical appointments, the emotional expenditure of thousands of unremarkable evenings. I have had the resources to give them those things. The children born two miles from where I live — same city, different zip code — are growing up with mothers stretched to a breaking point by two jobs and no partner and no savings and housing that is structurally unsafe. Those children bear the same image of God mine do. The conditions they are growing up in are not accidents. They are the product of choices made by people with the power to make different ones.

The steel-man here is about complexity: the causes of child poverty are multiple, the solutions contested, and the appropriate role of government versus church versus family is genuinely disputed. I accept that the policy debate is real. But the theological claim is not complex. A society that has resources sufficient to prevent children from going hungry, and chooses not to deploy them for that purpose, has made a theological statement about whose children matter. The church that sits inside that society and directs its moral energy primarily toward private behavior while remaining largely quiet about the conditions of the poorest children has made a complementary theological statement. Both statements are wrong. Scripture does not offer much ambiguity on this point.

Matthew 18 is the passage that should keep every Christian leader up at night. Jesus calls a child, stands a child in the center of the disciples, and says: "Whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea" (Matthew 18:6). The violence of the metaphor is intentional. Causing children to stumble — exposing them to conditions that damage and diminish them — is among the most serious offenses in the catalog of things Jesus says he cares about.

The statistics about children in poverty are a prophetic indictment. Not of poor parents. Of us. Of the church. Of the systems we sustain through our silence and our votes and our choices about where our money goes.

The kingdom belongs to such as these.

That sentence still reads as a verdict.
