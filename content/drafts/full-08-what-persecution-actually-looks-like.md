Linda was sixty-three when the men came to her village in Plateau State, Nigeria. They came at three in the morning. They killed her husband first, in front of her, in the kitchen. They killed the pastor next, two doors down, with his Bible still open on his desk. They burned the church. Three of Linda's grandchildren died in the burning, in the small back room of the parsonage where the older children had hidden them. The men were Fulani militants. They came back the next week. They are still coming, intermittently, into the Christian villages of the Middle Belt, and Linda is still living in the camp the survivors built, and her surviving children have been in that camp for two and a half years now, and the camp has no plumbing and irregular food and no plan for what comes next.

This is what persecution is.

I want to start there, with one specific person, because the abstraction is what has let us misuse the word. The persecution conversation in American evangelical life has been carried on at the level of abstraction — court cases, cultural slights, vague feelings of marginalization — and the abstraction has let us pretend that what is happening to American Christians is in any way analogous to what is happening to Linda. It is not. It is not even close. The use of the same word for both is a category error, and the category error is doing damage I want to name in this essay.

## What it is

Persecution is when the state, or a non-state armed group, imposes material harm on people because of their religious identity. The material harm is the load-bearing part. Persecution is when Pakistani Christians are jailed under blasphemy laws on the word of a single accuser, with no real prospect of legal defense. Persecution is when North Korean Christians are sent to labor camps where most of them die. Persecution is when Iranian house-church members are tortured to give up the names of fellow believers. Persecution is when Chinese pastors like Wang Yi — sentenced in 2019 to nine years in prison for *inciting subversion of state power*, which in his case meant pastoring an unregistered church and writing about it — sit in cells for years, separated from their families, their children growing up without them, their congregations dispersed under threat. Persecution is when the Coptic communities of Egypt are shot in their churches during liturgy. Persecution is when Indian Christians in Manipur and Odisha and parts of Karnataka are beaten by Hindu nationalist mobs while local police look the other way.

The Open Doors World Watch List documents this annually. The 2024 list estimated that over 365 million Christians worldwide live under high levels of persecution and discrimination. That number is large because it includes a wide range of contexts; the number of Christians under truly severe persecution is smaller and is concentrated in about fifty countries, almost all of them outside the West. Of the roughly 4,500 Christians killed for their faith in the most recent reporting year, Nigeria accounted for more than seventy percent. Plateau State, Linda's state, accounts for a substantial fraction.

These are real numbers. These are real people. They have names. They have congregations. Most of them are pastors and laypeople you have never heard of, holding the faith in conditions you have never been required to hold it in, paying a price you have not been asked to pay. The Voice of the Martyrs has been publishing their stories for decades. Open Doors and International Christian Concern have been documenting the conditions. The literature is available. The people are knowable. The reality is well-established.

This is what we mean when we use the word *persecution* honestly. This is the standard.

## What it isn't

American evangelical life in 2026 does not look like this.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters when the word matters. American Christians are not jailed for their faith. They are not killed in their churches. They are not sent to camps. They are not tortured to name fellow believers. They are not, by any honest accounting, persecuted in the sense the global persecuted church understands the word.

What American evangelicals are: marginalized, in certain professional contexts. Mocked, in certain media contexts. Inconvenienced, in certain regulatory contexts. Sometimes fired for refusing certain ceremonies. Sometimes sued, with mixed results in court. Sometimes excluded from honors and platforms they once would have been included in. Sometimes called names they would not call themselves.

These are real hardships. I do not want to minimize them. There are individual American Christians who have lost careers because they would not violate their consciences. There are bakers and florists and small-business owners who have faced expensive litigation. There are professors who have been pushed out of universities. There are nurses who have been ostracized for refusing to participate in procedures they consider wrong. These are not nothing. These deserve pastoral care. These deserve, in many cases, legal defense.

These are not persecution.

The distinction is not pedantic. The distinction matters because the word *persecution* has carried, in Christian moral imagination, a particular weight for two thousand years. It means the cross. It means Polycarp in the fire saying he had served the Lord for eighty-six years and would not blaspheme him. It means the disciples of Lyon thrown to the beasts in 177. It means Perpetua and Felicity in the arena. It means Hugh Latimer and Nicholas Ridley at the stake in 1555, with Latimer's line to Ridley — *we shall this day light such a candle, by God's grace, in England, as I trust shall never be put out.* It means Bonhoeffer at the gallows in Flossenbürg in April 1945, a few weeks before the Allied liberation. It means, in our own time, Linda in the Nigerian camp.

When we use the word for what is happening to American evangelicalism, we are taking a word that belongs to that company and applying it to our discomfort. The discomfort is real. It is not in that company. The misapplication does three kinds of damage.

## What the misapplication costs

The first thing the misapplication costs is solidarity with the actually-persecuted. American evangelicals talk about persecution constantly and do, on the whole, very little for the brothers and sisters who are paying its actual price. The amount of money American Christians spend on books about their own persecution is, by every measure I have been able to find, substantially larger than the amount of money American Christians give to the work of supporting persecuted Christians elsewhere. The amount of pulpit time given to American religious-liberty concerns is, in most evangelical churches, larger than the amount of pulpit time given to praying for Linda and her congregation. The disproportion is sinful, and it has been sinful for decades, and it is one of the reasons our witness to the global church has been weakened. We have been talking about our own marginalization while the brothers and sisters who actually need our help have been paying the price the word was meant to describe.

The second thing the misapplication costs is the integrity of our political theology. When you describe cultural defeat as persecution, you create a framework in which any political development that does not favor your group is read as persecution, and any political response to such a development is read as resistance to persecution. That framework licenses very serious overreach. It licenses, in some American evangelical circles, the embrace of strongman politics as a defensive response to imagined persecution. It licenses the willingness to abandon democratic norms because the alternative is read as catastrophic. It licenses a political imagination that is, in many cases, indistinguishable from the political imagination of the nationalist right — except that it carries Christian vocabulary, which makes it more durable and more dangerous.

The third thing the misapplication costs is the honest understanding of what we are actually facing. We are facing the end of cultural privilege. We are facing the dissolution of a particular cultural arrangement that benefited us for several generations. We are facing the same condition the church has occupied in most of its history. None of that requires the language of persecution to describe. All of it requires the language of repentance, recovery, and patient witness. When we use the persecution framing for what is actually the loss of cultural privilege, we make it harder to do what the actual situation requires.

I want to be honest about my own complicity here. I have used the persecution language from my own pulpit. I have used it less often as I have gotten older and have spent more time with brothers and sisters who actually know what the word means. The using was wrong. The repenting is part of why I am writing this essay.

## What we should say instead

There is a Christian language adequate to the situation we are actually in. The language is older than the persecution framing, and it has, on the whole, been more pastorally useful through the centuries.

We are losing cultural Christianity. This is the language of the previous essay. It is the right language. It names what is happening accurately. It does not borrow weight from a word that does not belong to us.

We are entering a post-Christendom moment. This is the language of Lesslie Newbigin and Stanley Hauerwas. It is the language scholars have used for forty years to describe the shift. It is not a controversial framing. It is the standard description of what is happening.

We are facing increased cultural marginalization. This is honest and accurate. It is also bearable. The early church faced more than this and built parallel institutions; the global persecuted church faces enormously more than this and continues to build the church.

We are being called to costly discipleship in a way the previous generations of American Christians were not called. Bonhoeffer's phrase is the right one. The costly discipleship is real. It does not, in American conditions, generally include martyrdom. It includes loss of cultural standing. It includes social cost. It includes economic cost in some cases. These are real costs. They are smaller than the cost the word *persecution* names. We can be honest about both.

## What to do

Pray for the persecuted. Pray specifically. Pray for Linda. Pray for the Coptic communities. Pray for the Wang Yi family. Pray for the Pakistani families holding the faith under blasphemy law. Pray for the North Korean Christians whose names we do not know. Pray every week from the pulpit, with specificity, with names when names are available. The historic prayer of the church has included the persecuted by name for centuries. Recover the practice.

Give. The organizations that serve the persecuted church need funding. Open Doors. Voice of the Martyrs. International Christian Concern. Barnabas Aid. The Tim Tebow Foundation's persecuted-church work. The amounts the American church gives to these organizations are small relative to the amounts we give to our own institutions. Increase the proportion. Make the giving a discipline of the household budget.

Be in personal contact when you can. Some of you will know missionaries serving in these contexts. Some of you will know diaspora Christians who came out of these conditions. Be friends with them. Visit them when you can. Listen to their stories. Let their reality reshape your imagination about what the word *persecution* names.

Stop using the word for yourself. The stopping will feel strange at first. The stopping is necessary. The stopping is part of how we recover the integrity of our political theology and the proportion of our pastoral language.

## The verdict

Persecution is what is happening to Linda. Persecution is what is happening to Wang Yi. Persecution is what is happening to the Pakistani families and the North Korean believers and the Coptic communities and the Indian Christians under Hindu nationalist mobs. These are our brothers and sisters. They are paying a price we have not paid. They deserve, at minimum, our prayers, our money, and the integrity of our language.

We are not persecuted. We are losing cultural privilege. The losing is hard. The losing is bearable. The losing is, in the long historical view, normal. The losing requires honest naming and patient response. It does not require, and is in fact diminished by, the borrowing of a word that belongs to the company of martyrs.

Give the word back. Pray for the people it actually describes. And let us take up the actual work of being faithful in a post-Christendom America — which is hard work, real work, costly work, and is not, by any honest measure, the work of the persecuted. The persecuted have work harder than ours. They are doing it without our help. That can change. The change starts with the integrity of our language and the redirection of our resources. The next essay in this series takes up another piece of the same recovery — the long historical pattern in which the church, every time it has won political power, has paid for the winning more than the winning was ever worth.
