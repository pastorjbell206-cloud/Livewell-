American Christianity owns a great deal of property.

Sanctuaries with professional lighting rigs. Multi-campus facilities with commercial kitchens and gymnasium floors. Endowments, investment portfolios, media budgets. The precise aggregate is debated, but no one disputes the basic fact: the institutional church in America controls resources on a scale that would have been incomprehensible to the communities in which the New Testament was written. Those early communities shared possessions, sold property, and distributed to anyone in need (Acts 2:44–45). They were also, in most cases, economically marginal — a gathering of artisans, freedpersons, and the occasional household of modest means, meeting in borrowed rooms.

The contrast is not a reason for guilt. It is a reason for examination.

I have sat in budget meetings where we spent more time discussing the replacement of aging pew cushions than we spent on benevolence allocations. I have done that. I have approved facilities expenditures while hearing, in the very next agenda item, about the family in the congregation facing eviction. I am not pointing from a distance. I know what it costs to maintain a building and to keep a staff and to run the programs that form the visible life of a congregation. I know those costs are real and those programs are not meaningless. I am saying they are not the whole conversation. And we have made them the whole conversation.

The question the New Testament will not let us avoid is not "can a wealthy church be faithful?" but "what does faithfulness require of a wealthy church?" The answer the early church gave was concrete. When the Jerusalem community faced famine, the Gentile churches took up a collection — Paul describes it in 2 Corinthians 8–9 with language about equality and surplus meeting need. "Your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need" (2 Corinthians 8:14). This is not an abstract principle. It is a description of how wealth was expected to move: from where there was more, toward where there was less.

John says it plainly. "If anyone has the world's goods and sees a brother or sister in need, yet closes his heart against them, how does God's love abide in him?" (1 John 3:17). The Greek word is bios — the material conditions of living. To have bios and withhold it from someone who lacks bios is not merely stinginess. It is evidence that God's love does not actually abide in us. This is a strong claim. John intends it strongly.

The steel-man of the wealthy church: institutional resources exist for institutional purposes, and a church must maintain itself to serve at all. Programs require staff. Staff require salaries. Buildings require maintenance. A congregation that gives away its operational budget has no operational capacity. This is true. And the question is not whether to have buildings or budgets. The question is proportion. What percentage of our collective resources reaches people outside our walls? What percentage disappears into the maintenance of the institution that is supposed to serve them? When those numbers are examined — and many churches resist examining them — the results often call for theological reckoning rather than institutional satisfaction.

James is the harshest voice in the New Testament canon on this question. "Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten. Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence against you" (James 5:2–3). He is not writing to pagan merchants. He is writing to people who assembled in Jesus's name. The wealth he indicts is Christian wealth held while brothers and sisters lacked.

The world's poverty is not an abstraction. It is specific people in specific places who lack what we have in surplus. The church's wealth is not a neutral fact. It is a theological statement — about what we believe, about who we think God's resources are for, about where we imagine the Kingdom's priorities lie.

We cannot claim to follow Jesus through a building he wouldn't recognize while the people he specifically named as the measure of our faithfulness sleep outside.

The audit is not comfortable. I have not conducted it with the honesty it deserves.

That is also a theological statement.
