Revelation 7:9 describes a vision John received of the throne of God: a great multitude from every nation, tribe, people, and language, standing before the Lamb. John describes it as a vision of what is coming. The white evangelical church in America has often treated it as a distant promise rather than a present obligation — something to aspire to eventually, while continuing to operate in ways that make the aspiration functionally empty.

I am a white pastor. I lead a predominantly white congregation. What I am about to say implicates me more than anyone.

The error that predominantly white churches make when they engage the question of racial diversity is not usually malicious. It is structural. The church decides it wants to be more diverse — a desire that is real and, in many cases, genuinely motivated by the text — and then proceeds to pursue diversity in ways that ensure it will fail. The worship style does not change. The leadership structure does not change. The cultural assumptions embedded in every program and process and piece of communication do not change. What changes is a marketing effort: the website gets updated, some stock photos are diversified, a visiting preacher from another ethnicity is invited for Black History Month.

And then the pastor is confused when people of color do not stay.

They do not stay because they were not invited. They were recruited. There is a difference. Invitation says: come as you are, and this community will be changed by your presence. Recruitment says: come as you are, and you will be expected to become like us. People of color in America have developed a finely tuned capacity for detecting the difference between those two messages, because the cost of misreading it has historically been very high.

The second error is that the conversation about diversity gets framed as a conversation about aesthetics rather than power. The predominantly white church that adds a diverse worship team, or adjusts the music mix, or makes the welcome language more inclusive — and then calls that diversity work — has mistaken the surface for the structure. The structural question is: who makes decisions? Whose theological instincts shape the sermon series? Whose cultural assumptions are written into the governance documents? Whose discomfort, when expressed, gets taken seriously?

If the answers to those questions are all the same — if power rests entirely with the same people it rested with before the diversity conversation began — then the worship set is decoration on an unchanged building.

The third error is the most subtle and the most damaging: treating diversity as a gift the majority culture offers to the minority rather than a gift the minority culture offers to the whole body. The white church that approaches diversity with the posture of benefactor — we are welcoming you in — has already positioned the conversation wrong. The New Testament vision is not of majority culture opening its doors to minority culture. It is of a new humanity being created in Christ, in which the categories that once defined who was in and who was out have been fundamentally reorganized.

Galatians 3:28 is not a statement about tolerance. It is a statement about ontology. What we are in Christ reorganizes what we were.

This means the predominantly white church pursuing genuine diversity must be willing to be changed by it. Not changed at the level of marketing, but changed at the level of power, culture, and leadership. It must be willing to hear the critique that its worship style is culturally particular rather than culturally universal. It must be willing to name the ways its governance reflects majority culture assumptions. It must be willing to do the slower, harder work of building genuine relationships with leaders and communities of color before it starts a diversity initiative — because without those relationships, every diversity initiative will be received as what it often is: a program designed to make the white church feel better about itself.

I have not done this well. I have been the pastor who felt good about the intentions while being defended from the feedback.

The feedback is the beginning. Not the goal — the beginning.
