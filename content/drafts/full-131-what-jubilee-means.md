Leviticus 25 is the kind of text that gets skipped in Bible reading plans. It is detailed, it is agricultural, and on the surface it seems to address a land tenure system so foreign to our own that application appears impossible. So we move on. And in moving on, we miss one of the most radical economic visions in the entire canon of Scripture.

The Jubilee arrived every fiftieth year. In that year, land that had been sold reverted to its original family. Debts were cancelled. Israelites who had sold themselves into indentured labor to pay their debts were freed and allowed to return to their families. Every fifty years, the economic arrangements of Israel were reset — not dramatically, but structurally, built into the law itself so that the accumulation of wealth and the deepening of poverty had a ceiling. You could accumulate for forty-nine years. The fiftieth year, the whole thing reset.

The theological rationale is stated explicitly in Leviticus 25:23: *The land must not be sold permanently, because the land is mine and you reside in my land as foreigners and strangers.* The Jubilee rests on a claim about ownership. The land belongs to God, not to Israel, not to the individuals within Israel. This means that what we call property rights — the permanent, transferable ownership of land — did not exist in the same form within the Israelite covenant. What existed was stewardship: the use of land entrusted to you for a season, to be returned when the time came.

The Jubilee is not presented as charity. It is presented as the working out of a theological premise: if the land belongs to God, then arrangements that result in some families owning all of it and other families owning none of it are, by definition, a distortion of what God intended. The mechanism for correcting that distortion was built into the law. You did not need to appeal to the generosity of the wealthy. The law itself required the correction.

Scholars debate whether the Jubilee was ever actually practiced in Israel's history. [cite — confirm] The prophets' denunciations of land accumulation and the enslavement of debtors suggest that if it was practiced, it was not practiced consistently. What is not in doubt is that God commanded it — that this vision of periodic economic reset, of limits on accumulation, of the liberation of the indebted, is not a marginal idea in the biblical text. It is law. It has the same authority as the commandments against murder and theft.

Jesus opened his public ministry in Luke 4 by reading from Isaiah 61 in the synagogue at Nazareth: *The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor. He has sent me to proclaim freedom for the prisoners and recovery of sight for the blind, to set the oppressed free, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor.* The phrase translated *year of the Lord's favor* is a direct reference to the Jubilee. Jesus announced his ministry in Jubilee language — liberation of the bound, restoration of what had been lost, freedom for those whose debt had consumed them. The Jubilee is not merely Old Testament background. It is the shape of what Jesus came to do.

What does this demand of the church? Not a literal implementation of ancient land law. But something more demanding than dismissal. It demands that the church take seriously the biblical conviction that accumulation has limits, that debt can be a form of bondage, and that God is concerned with the economic conditions that determine whether people can participate in the fullness of community life. It demands that we ask who in our communities is living in a permanent state of indebtedness from which there is no visible exit — and whether the church has anything to say to that, or whether we have decided that the Jubilee was only ever metaphorical.

The text will not allow the metaphor to do all the work. There were real fields. Real families. Real liberation.

The year of the Lord's favor requires something of the years before it. We are in those years now.
