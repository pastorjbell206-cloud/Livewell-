Gen Z is the most religiously disaffiliated generation in American history. They did not leave the church the way I left the church — angrily, with the door slammed. They left quietly, the way you leave a party you were not enjoying. Most of them are not coming back. Some of them will come to a church we have not yet become. I want to describe what that church looks like.

I have been pastoring through the entire millennial and Gen Z exit, watching the patterns up close in my own congregation and in the broader denominational conversations I am part of. The patterns are consistent enough to talk about with some confidence. The standard explanations are mostly wrong. The honest explanation is harder, and the path forward is narrower than American evangelical institutions want it to be. This essay is the careful version.

## What the data actually shows

The numbers, briefly, because the numbers are the necessary starting point. Pew Research's most recent data, alongside the work of Ryan Burge and the Public Religion Research Institute, shows that approximately forty percent of Gen Z adults identify as religiously unaffiliated, which is the highest disaffiliation rate of any American generation on record. The trajectory has been steepening across the most recent cohorts; the youngest portion of Gen Z is, on current trends, projected to be even more disaffiliated than the older portion when they reach adulthood.

The disaffiliation is not the same as atheism. Most of the religiously unaffiliated Gen Zers describe themselves as spiritual but not religious, as believing in some kind of higher power, or as undecided rather than committed atheists. The category of confident atheists is small. The category of *I have not figured out what I think and I am not interested in finding out from the church* is much larger.

The disaffiliation is concentrated in former evangelicals, former Catholics, and former mainline Protestants in roughly that order of severity. The Black church has lost some Gen Z, but at lower rates than the white traditions. The Hispanic church has lost less than the white traditions but more than the Black church. The patterns differ by community in ways that suggest the institutional dynamics are doing significant explanatory work.

The disaffiliation does not look temporary. The previous patterns of generational return to religious affiliation around the time of family formation have been declining for several cohorts. Millennials returned at lower rates than Gen X. Gen Z is not, on current evidence, returning at the rates millennials did. The drift is structural.

## Why Gen Z is actually leaving

I want to take the standard explanations seriously and then offer the explanation that, in my judgment, is closer to the truth.

The standard institutional evangelical explanation is the *secular culture* story I addressed in the church decline essay. The story has some truth — American culture is less broadly supportive of Christian institutions than it was in earlier generations — but the story does not explain the differential disaffiliation across traditions, and it does not explain the specific reasons Gen Zers themselves give when they are asked.

The actual reasons Gen Zers give cluster in patterns that are remarkably consistent across multiple interview studies. The patterns are documented in the work of David Kinnaman, Jean Twenge, the Springtide Research Institute, and the Pew Religion Landscape Studies, and they line up across the various methodologies.

Gen Zers leave because of the church's politicization. The conversion of evangelical institutions into wings of partisan political coalitions has been one of the most consistent reasons cited. Gen Zers watched their parents' churches absorb partisan political identity in ways that struck them as theologically incoherent and morally compromising. The willingness of evangelical institutions to defend morally indefensible political figures because the figures delivered on certain policy preferences was particularly corrosive. Gen Zers concluded, correctly, that the church had subordinated its moral witness to its political coalition. The exit was rational.

Gen Zers leave because of the church's handling of sexuality. This is not, primarily, a disagreement about doctrine. Most of the Gen Zers I have talked with about this are open to traditional Christian moral commitments on sexuality, and many hold them. What they are not open to is what they perceive as the church's exclusive focus on certain sexual sins to the exclusion of others, the church's harsh treatment of LGBTQ people compared to its treatment of other moral categories, the church's complicity in purity culture that they watched harm their friends, and the church's failure to address the sexual abuse that has been exposed in evangelical institutions. The exit is not, primarily, an exit from doctrine. The exit is an exit from what they perceive as institutional cruelty and hypocrisy on sexual questions.

Gen Zers leave because of the church's anti-intellectualism. The willingness to dismiss serious scientific work, the unwillingness to engage hard biblical questions honestly, the suspicion of higher education, the bad arguments deployed against good arguments — Gen Zers watched all of this and concluded that the church was not a serious intellectual home. Many of them are themselves serious intellectually. They wanted a faith that could be held with intellectual integrity, and they were given a faith that asked them to defend positions they could not defend on intellectual grounds.

Gen Zers leave because of the church's complicity in racism. The institutional record of white evangelicalism on race — historical and contemporary — was visible to Gen Zers in ways it was not visible to earlier generations. The internet made the history accessible. The contemporary controversies made the failures visible. Gen Zers watched white evangelical institutions struggle to articulate any coherent position on racial questions, and many concluded that the institutions were not theologically serious enough to address what they saw.

Gen Zers leave because of the church's smallness in the face of suffering. The Gen Z mental health crisis is real. Anxiety rates, depression rates, suicidal ideation rates have all spiked in ways that have been documented across multiple studies. The church's response to the crisis has, on the whole, been inadequate — quick spiritual answers to deep psychological wounds, dismissive treatment of mental health professionals, simplistic readings of complex situations. Gen Zers needed more than the church was offering. They went elsewhere.

These are the actual reasons. They are not, primarily, theological objections. They are pastoral and institutional objections. The Gen Z exit was, in significant respects, the rational response to what they watched the church be over the past two decades.

## What will not work

Most of what the church has been trying to do to address the Gen Z exit will not work.

Louder worship music will not work. The musical sophistication of contemporary worship is high. The musical sophistication is not the problem. The problem is what the music is delivering, not how loud it is delivering it.

Cooler pastors will not work. The branding of pastors as relatable and culturally savvy was the strategy of the last twenty years. The strategy produced the cultural intelligibility that the megachurch needed for its growth. The strategy is exhausted. Gen Zers do not want cooler pastors. They want pastors who tell the truth.

More relevance will not work. The church has spent forty years pursuing cultural relevance and has produced churches that are difficult to distinguish from secular institutions on most measurable dimensions. The pursuit of relevance has failed in its own terms. The institutions that pursued it have not stopped the decline; the institutions that have continued doing more traditional Christian work have, in many cases, fared better.

A better social media presence will not work. The presence of evangelical institutions on social media is already substantial. The presence is not the problem. The problem is what is being presented and how it is being received.

These strategies have been the dominant institutional responses for two decades. They have not worked. They will not work. They mistake what is being rejected.

## What might work

The honest answer to *what would bring Gen Z back* is that most of them are not coming back to anything that currently exists. Some of them might come to something we have not yet built. The building is the work.

The work has several pieces, and I want to name them concretely.

The church has to do honest repentance for what it has been. The repentance is specific. It is the repentance for the sexual abuse cover-ups. The repentance for the racial blindness. The repentance for the political compromises. The repentance for the anti-intellectualism. The repentance for the failure to address mental health honestly. The repentance has to be public, specific, and structurally meaningful. It cannot be vague apologies followed by business as usual. It has to be reform that actually reforms.

The church has to recover intellectual seriousness about the hard questions. The Gen Zers who have been leaving are, in many cases, intellectually capable people who watched the church refuse to engage their questions seriously. The recovery requires pastors and teachers who can actually engage scientific questions, philosophical questions, historical-critical questions about scripture, ethical questions about contemporary issues, with intellectual honesty rather than defensive dismissal. The recovery is happening in some institutions and is not happening in others. The institutions that do not recover will continue to lose Gen Z.

The church has to refuse the partisan trap. The political polarization of evangelicalism has to be reversed. The third position I have written about in the politics essays of this writing project has to be articulated, defended, and embodied by particular pastors and institutions. The polarization will not be reversed by appeals to civility; it will be reversed by the costly recovery of theological identity that is not subordinate to political coalition.

The church has to offer hospitality the surrounding culture cannot offer. The Gen Z mental health crisis, the Gen Z loneliness crisis, the Gen Z housing crisis, the Gen Z economic insecurity — all of these are conditions the surrounding culture is not addressing well. The church can address them, in concrete ways, through household hospitality, through mutual aid, through small groups that actually function as communities of care. The church that does this will look different from most American evangelical churches now. The church that does this will produce the kind of public witness that Gen Zers will recognize as Christian in ways the current institutional witness does not produce.

The church has to be willing to be small. The strategies that have produced large evangelical institutions are not the strategies that will produce faithful Christian witness in the post-Christendom moment. The institutions that pursue size will continue to pursue what has produced the current crisis. The institutions that accept smallness as a condition of faithfulness will be free to do the harder, more sustainable work that the current crisis is calling for.

## The verdict

Gen Z is not coming back to the church the church spent thirty years trying to build for them. The seeker-sensitive megachurch did not keep them. The hip urban church plant did not keep them. The reformed young-restless movement did not keep them. The post-evangelical progressive church is not keeping them. They are leaving everything.

Some of them will return — not to what we are, but to what we have not yet become. The becoming is a generation's work. The becoming requires repentance, intellectual recovery, refusal of partisan capture, hospitality, and willingness to be small. The becoming requires institutional honesty that most American evangelical institutions have not yet been willing to do. The becoming is what the next generation of pastors and lay leaders will be working on, and the work has barely begun.

I am hopeful about the church on the other side of this. I am not hopeful about most of the institutions on this side. The institutions that figure out how to repent, recover, and rebuild will be smaller and more faithful. The institutions that continue the current strategies will continue to decline. The choice is being made now, in particular congregations, by particular leaders. The Gen Zers who left are watching, and they are surprisingly willing to come back to something they have not yet seen but that they have been hoping for. The hoping is what the next generation of faithful churches will be built from. The building is the work. The work is yours.
