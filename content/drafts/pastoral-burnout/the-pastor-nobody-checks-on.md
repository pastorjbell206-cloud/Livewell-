A man stands at the back door on Sunday and shakes two hundred hands. He asks each person how they are. He means it. He remembers the surgery, the wayward son, the layoff. He files it away to pray over on Monday. Then he drives home and no one asks him anything.

This is not an anomaly. It is the design.

We have built a vocation around a single, unexamined assumption. The pastor is the one who checks on people. The arrow runs one direction. He initiates the call to the hospital, the visit to the widow, the text to the family in crisis. He is the subject of the verb. He is rarely its object. And a man can go years inside that grammar before he notices that the sentence has no place for him.

I have sat with pastors who could not remember the last time someone called to ask how *they* were doing. Not how the building campaign was going. Not whether attendance had recovered. How they were. The question landed like a foreign language. One of them wept before he could answer it, and then apologized for weeping, which tells you everything about the shape of his last decade.

Eugene Peterson spent his life arguing that the pastoral vocation had been quietly traded for a managerial one. He was right, and the trade has a cost no one printed on the receipt. The manager is surrounded by people who report to him and depend on him. The manager is also, by structure, alone. The men under him do not pastor him. They cannot. He is the one they come to when the thing breaks.

This is not how it always worked, and the history matters because we have forgotten there was ever another way. For most of the church's life the man who cared for souls was himself under care. The desert fathers of the fourth century would not let a monk direct others until he had submitted himself to a spiritual father. The Celtic church called that figure the *anam cara*, the soul friend, and Brigid of Kildare is supposed to have said a person without one was like a body without a head. The medieval priest confessed to a confessor. The Puritan pastor met with a circle of brethren who knew the state of his soul and asked after it. The expectation ran in both directions. The shepherd was also a sheep, and somebody was appointed to know it.

We kept the giving and dropped the receiving. We built a profession in which the pastor confesses to no one, is directed by no one, and is checked on by no one, and we did it within a single century, and we did it without noticing what we had removed. The asymmetry is new. It is not the historic shape of the office. It is a modern American invention, and like most of our inventions it is efficient and it is killing people.

So the pastor learns to perform fineness. He gets good at it. He has to be good at it, because the congregation does not actually want a fragile shepherd, and he knows this, and he adjusts. The smile at the door is partly real and partly armor. By the time he is forty-five he can no longer tell which part is which.

This is the loneliness that does not look like loneliness. He is never alone. His calendar is full of people. He is in more rooms with more faces than almost anyone in his town. And he is starving in the middle of the crowd, because attention is not the same as care, and being needed is not the same as being known.

Consider Elijah under the broom tree. First Kings nineteen. He has just won. Mount Carmel, fire from heaven, the prophets of Baal routed in front of the nation. It is the great public victory of his career. And the next thing we see is a man a day's walk into the wilderness, sitting under a single tree, asking God to let him die. *It is enough now, Lord. Take my life.*

Read what comes next slowly, because the church usually rushes it. God does not rebuke him. God does not hand him a productivity plan. God lets him sleep. Then God feeds him. An angel touches him and says, *Arise and eat, because the journey is too great for you.* Twice. He sleeps and eats again before a single word of correction or commission is spoken. The body before the assignment. The care before the rebuke.

We have inverted this. We hand the exhausted pastor the assignment and skip the bread. We tell him the work is too great and offer him a conference. We say *the journey is too great for you* and mean it as a motivational slogan instead of what it was — a diagnosis that required God to act before the man could move.

And notice the lie Elijah believed under that tree. *I, even I only, am left.* He was wrong. There were seven thousand who had not bowed the knee. The isolation was partly real and partly a distortion of exhaustion. This is worth saying plainly to ourselves. When we are this depleted, our reading of our own situation cannot be trusted. The pastor who is certain no one would understand, no one would stay, no one could be told — that pastor is doing math under the broom tree. The numbers are off. They are always off in that chair.

I am not writing this from above the problem. We pastors built it together. We celebrated the man who never took a day off. We made *tired* a synonym for *faithful*. We told the young guy entering ministry that the work would cost him everything and we said it like a recruiting pitch instead of a warning. We did this. I have done this. I have praised exhaustion in another man because praising it was easier than asking whether he was alright.

The structure cannot generate the cure on its own. Someone has to break the grammar. The arrow has to reverse, and it will not reverse by accident, because the whole system is built to keep the pastor as subject and never object. Somebody has to decide that this man, the one who asks everyone, gets asked.

It might be a fellow pastor who refuses to talk shop for one lunch. It might be an elder mature enough to pastor his pastor instead of evaluating him. It might be the discipline of a spiritual director, a confessor, the old structures the church abandoned and now pretends it never needed. It will not be the congregation as a body, because the congregation needs him to be the strong one, and that need is not wicked, it is just real, and it will not feed him.

This is what the Pastors Connection Network exists for, and I will say it plainly because I have watched it work. A pastor cannot manufacture a peer out of his own congregation. He needs other men in the same office, far enough away that there is no rivalry and close enough that they will pick up the phone. Not a mastermind group trading church-growth tactics. Men who will ask the question the congregation cannot ask, and wait for the true answer, and not flinch when it comes. Most pastors do not have one such relationship. The data says so and so does every honest conversation I have had with a man at the end of his rope.

And it requires changing the question we are willing to ask each other. *How's the church* is not the question. *How's attendance, how's giving, how's the building* — these are the questions we have learned to ask, and they are all questions about the institution, and a man can answer every one of them while quietly dying behind his own face. The question Elijah needed was the one God finally asked him twice in the cave. *What are you doing here?* Not the church. You. What are you doing here, in this wilderness, at the end of yourself. We have stopped asking each other that question. We ask about the tower and never about the builder.

What it requires first is that the pastor stop performing. That he let one person see the broom tree. This is the hardest thing, because he has spent twenty years teaching everyone, including himself, that he does not sit there. He does. He sits there more than he will admit, usually after the biggest Sundays, which is exactly where Elijah sat.

The angel did not ask Elijah to be stronger. The angel asked him to eat.

There is a man in your network, your town, your phone, who shook two hundred hands this morning and will not hear his own name in a caring sentence all week. You know who he is. You might be him. The question is whether anyone in his life is willing to become the exception to the design.

Somebody fed Elijah. Nobody is feeding him.
