The question the lawyer asked is the one we all ask: "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29)

It sounds like a sincere request for clarification. It is not. The text tells us what it was: the lawyer, having already affirmed the two great commandments, "desiring to justify himself" asked the follow-up question. He was not confused about who his neighbor was. He was hoping for a definition narrow enough that he was already in compliance. A neighbor as someone who lives adjacent. A neighbor as someone who shares my ethnicity, my class, my religious tradition. A neighbor as someone I have explicitly chosen to be in relationship with. Any of these definitions would have left the man feeling satisfied that the law's demands on him were already met.

Jesus told a story instead of giving a definition. That is significant. A definition would have answered the question on the lawyer's terms. The story answers it on entirely different terms.

The Samaritan is not a random choice. In the ethnic and religious politics of first-century Judea, Samaritans and Jews maintained a hostility that was centuries old, rooted in the Assyrian deportations and resettlements following the fall of the northern kingdom. [cite — confirm] They did not associate. Their religious traditions had diverged around the question of the correct place of worship, among other things. When a Jew in Jesus's audience heard "Samaritan" they heard: the wrong kind of person. The person we do not help and who does not help us. The boundary case.

The priest and the Levite who pass by are not merely cold-hearted. There were plausible religious reasons for their choices — contact with a man who might be dead would have rendered them ritually impure and unable to serve. The law that governed their calling created categories that made stepping past the dying man defensible. They were managing competing obligations, as institutional religion always is, and they chose the obligation that served their continued function over the obligation that cost them their status.

And then the Samaritan, who owed the man nothing — not by ethnicity, not by religion, not by prior relationship — saw him, was moved in his viscera (the Greek word splanchnizomai points to the gut, not the heart, a physical response of compassion), bound his wounds, carried him, paid for his recovery, and promised to return. He crossed every category that would have justified walking past.

Jesus does not then ask: "Which of these was the true neighbor?" He asks: "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of robbers?" (Luke 10:36). The lawyer cannot say "the Samaritan." He says "the one who showed mercy." He cannot even speak the name of the category he has spent his life avoiding. Jesus tells him: go and do likewise.

The parable is not about individual charity. It is an argument about the structure of moral obligation. The question "who is my neighbor?" assumes that neighborliness is a category I apply to some people and withhold from others on the basis of prior relationship or shared identity. The parable deconstructs that assumption entirely. The neighbor is not a category I apply. The neighbor is the one in front of me who needs. My obligation is not defined by who they are. It is defined by what they face and what I have.

I have walked past on the other side. Not dramatically. Not with any clear moment of decision. But I have organized my life, my time, my resources, and my attention in ways that kept me on a road where I would not encounter the man who had fallen among robbers. I have lived in places and moved through schedules that made the question "who is my neighbor?" effectively answerable as "people like me." That is not neutrality. That is a choice. The parable names it.

The lawyer wanted a manageable definition. The church has spent centuries finding ones. We draw the lines at national borders, at zip codes, at church membership, at doctrinal agreement. The parable does not authorize any of them. It authorizes one criterion only: need, encountered, and what you did about it.

The boundary Jesus will not let the lawyer draw is the one we keep trying to draw.

Go and do likewise is still the command. The road is still the same road.
