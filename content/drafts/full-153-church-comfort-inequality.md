The American church has made peace with a level of economic inequality that the prophets would have named without hesitation. We have not named it. We have organized our congregations around it, built our worship experiences for it, and developed a theology that, if not defending it, at least refuses to disturb it.

This requires explanation, because the Scriptures do not cooperate.

Amos was not a radical. He was a farmer from Tekoa, a southern shepherd who had no particular interest in politics. God sent him north to Israel during a period of relative prosperity in the eighth century BC — a period when, as he describes it, the wealthy sold the poor for a pair of sandals and trampled the heads of the poor into the dust (Amos 2:6-7). Amos did not argue that poverty was regrettable. He argued that God would not forget it. He argued that the worship of a people who oppressed the poor was an offense to God — that the incense and the music and the offerings were not only worthless but actively disgusting to the God who had heard the cries of the poor (Amos 5:21-24).

The same God. The same offense. The question is why the contemporary church reads Amos and concludes that his indictment was for a different era.

I have been comfortable with inequality. I have preached in buildings that cost more than the median household income in my county, to congregations whose combined assets were significant, about a God whose concern for the poor I named without implicating myself or us in the structures that perpetuate poverty. This is a kind of homiletical cowardice I have practiced long enough that I mistook it for pastoral wisdom.

The early church was not comfortable with inequality. Acts 2 and 4 describe a community where no one claimed that any possession was their own, where distribution was made to each as any had need. This was not communism. It was not a political program. It was the community's natural response to the resurrection — the recognition that if death has been defeated, then the anxious accumulation of security against death has also been defeated, and something different is possible. The early church's economic practices were ecclesiological. They followed from their theology.

By the fourth century, when Constantine had made Christianity safe and then powerful, the church's relationship to wealth had changed substantially. John Chrysostom, Bishop of Constantinople in the late fourth century, preached against the wealth of the church with a ferocity that embarrasses most contemporary preachers [cite — confirm]. He called the person who has more than they need and does not give it away a thief. He was not considered a radical. He was considered orthodox. The tradition that now treats prophetic challenge to wealth as political overreach has simply forgotten where it came from.

What is the theological justification for the church's comfort with inequality? Several have been proposed. That wealth is a sign of blessing. That poverty is the result of individual choices. That Jesus said the poor will always be with you. Each of these is a distortion of the texts they invoke.

"You will always have the poor among you" (Matthew 26:11) is Jesus quoting Deuteronomy 15:11 — a passage that, in its original context, commands generosity precisely because the poor will always be present. It is not a license for indifference. It is a mandate for ongoing obligation.

The blessing theology has deep roots in American Protestantism, particularly its Calvinist strains — the idea that material prosperity is evidence of divine favor. This theology was already present in the Puritan settlements of the seventeenth century and had become dominant in certain streams of evangelicalism long before the prosperity gospel made it explicit. The prosperity gospel is not an aberration of the American church. It is its logical conclusion.

The church that is comfortable with inequality has developed a theology to support that comfort. The Scriptures are more uncomfortable than we have allowed them to be.

The feast Isaiah describes in chapter 25 — the feast on this mountain for all peoples, the rich food and well-aged wine — is not reserved for those who have succeeded under current economic arrangements. It is the feast that puts an end to those arrangements. It swallows up death. It wipes away tears.

That is the God the prophets announced. The church that has made peace with inequality has not yet made peace with him.
