When Jeremiah wrote to the exiles in Babylon, he did not tell them to keep their distance from the city until the rescue came. He told them to build houses, plant gardens, marry, raise children — and to "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7). Seek the shalom of Babylon. The place of captivity was also the place of calling.

This is the instruction that most of us have not fully received.

There is a particular version of Christian mission that locates the real work somewhere else — in the Global South, in the unreached people groups, in the countries where missionaries go and churches pray for and support financially. None of that is wrong. The global mission of the church is real and costly and necessary. What is wrong is the proportion — the amount of missiological imagination we pour into distant places relative to the amount we invest in the specific block where the church building sits.

I have sat in churches that sent teams to Africa while their immediate neighborhood had no Christian presence with any relationship to the people who actually lived there. I have sat with pastors who could name unreached people groups in Southeast Asia but could not name the dominant ethnic community within six blocks of their congregation's front door. I have been that pastor. I have been more conversant with global statistics than with the actual human beings whose daily life takes place in the shadow of our steeple.

The global mission does not excuse the local one. It actually depends on it. The New Testament pattern of mission is centrifugal — moving outward from the community where discipleship is practiced to the communities adjacent to it and then beyond. Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). The ends of the earth are the culmination of the movement, not the substitute for the beginning. A church that skips Jerusalem and Judea for the ends of the earth has confused the direction of the arrow.

The local mission is harder than the distant one in a particular way. Distant mission allows a kind of abstraction — you send money, you pray for names, you support work you will never see and people you will never know. That abstraction is not nothing. But it can also insulate you from the specific, demanding, human work of loving your actual neighbors — who are complicated, who may not be interested in what you are offering, who know things about your neighborhood that you do not, who have needs that your current programming is not designed to address.

The work of loving your city begins with knowing it. Not knowing about it — the demographics and the statistics and the economic profile — but knowing it the way you know a person: slowly, through presence, through repeated encounters that build the kind of familiarity where real conversation becomes possible. It begins with the question that Nehemiah asked when he arrived in Jerusalem: What is the actual condition of this place? Not what I assume the condition to be, but what I see when I stop performing activity long enough to look.

In most American cities, what you will see when you look is a profound isolation. Neighbors who do not know each other. Communities fractured along economic lines that the church building often reinforces rather than crosses. People in genuine need with no relationship to any institution with the capacity to help. And in the midst of that, a congregation that has the theological resources, the community structure, and the social presence to be a genuine force for human flourishing — if it is willing to direct those resources outward rather than inward.

The mission field is not abstract. It is the homeless man outside your building who sees the congregation arrive and depart each Sunday without eye contact. It is the apartment complex three streets over where no one from the congregation has ever been. It is the school where the neighborhood's children spend their days, where the teachers are desperate for partners.

These are not secondary mission concerns while the real work happens elsewhere. These are the first call.

Go where you are.
