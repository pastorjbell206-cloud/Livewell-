Most American men over forty have no real friends. The data is consistent and devastating. The recovery is the most important relational work most men can do, and the church is one of the few institutions positioned to support it if the church is willing.

I want to write this essay for the man reading it who has been carrying, for years, the sense that something is missing in his relational life and has not been able to name it. The naming is often the beginning of the recovery. The naming is also widely resisted, because the absence of real male friendship has become so normalized that many men do not recognize the absence as a problem. The recognition is the first work.

## What the data shows

Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone* (2000) documented the long-term decline in American civic and social engagement across multiple measures. The decline has continued and in some respects accelerated since the book was published. The friendship statistics have been particularly stark for men.

The Survey Center on American Life's 2021 report on friendship in America found that the percentage of American men who reported having no close friends had quintupled since 1990 — from three percent of men in 1990 to fifteen percent in 2021. The percentage who reported having ten or more close friends had collapsed from approximately one in three in 1990 to roughly one in eight in 2021. The collapse was steepest among men over fifty, but the trend extended across age groups. The American Family Survey, the American Time Use Survey, and several academic research traditions have confirmed similar findings using different methodologies.

The friendship recession is not a perception problem. The data is consistent across multiple research traditions. The friendship structures that organized American men's lives a generation ago — the workplace friendships, the neighborhood relationships, the civic and fraternal organizations, the church involvements that connected men to other men — have, on the whole, contracted. The contraction has produced a generation of American men whose primary emotional resources are their spouses, if they have spouses, and almost no one else.

## Why men's friendships collapsed

The collapse has had several causes. The causes are worth naming because they continue to operate.

The first cause has been the geographic mobility of contemporary American life. Men who would have stayed in one place for decades a generation ago are now expected to move for education, for work, for advancement. The mobility is structural — many jobs require relocation, many careers require multiple relocations across a working life. The mobility breaks friendships. The friendships that survive the mobility require sustained intentional work, and the intentional work has often not been done.

The second cause has been the rise of demanding work culture. The forty-hour week has become the fifty-hour or sixty-hour week for many men in professional positions. The work hours have consumed the time that would have been available for sustained friendship. The work expectations have also consumed the emotional energy that friendship requires. The friendships have, in many cases, been silently traded for career advancement, often without the conscious choice the trade represents.

The third cause has been the death of the third place. Ray Oldenburg's *The Great Good Place* (1989) coined the term *third place* for the social gathering spots — the cafés, the bars, the lodges, the clubs, the barbershops — that were neither home nor work but provided the regular gathering points for sustained casual relationship. The third places have collapsed in much of contemporary American life. The replacement has been screen-based engagement that does not produce the relational substance the third places produced.

The fourth cause has been the privatization of leisure. The leisure activities of contemporary American men have become increasingly individual — streaming, gaming, scrolling. The leisure activities of an earlier generation were more communal — bowling leagues, fraternal organizations, neighborhood sports, men's church groups. The communal leisure produced friendships as a byproduct. The individual leisure produces, as a byproduct, the isolation we are now measuring.

The fifth cause has been the masculine cultural script that discourages emotional vulnerability among men. The script tells men that emotional needs are feminine, that real men do not need close friends, that strength means independence. The script is false in ways that are documented across the developmental and clinical psychology literature. The script has produced, over decades, generations of American men who have internalized the false script and who consequently do not pursue or sustain the friendships they actually need.

## What the absence costs

The absence of close male friendships has costs that the men in the condition often cannot identify themselves.

The first cost is the structural overburdening of marriages. The man whose primary emotional resource is his spouse has loaded the marriage with an emotional weight the marriage cannot finally bear. The spouse is being asked to carry the relational load of a spouse plus the load of several friends. The asking is unfair to the spouse, who has her own needs and her own life, and the loading produces stress on the marriage that the marriage often cannot absorb. Many of the marital difficulties I have seen across twenty years of pastoral work have been substantially driven by this structural overburdening. The recovery of male friendship has, in many cases, been one of the most consequential moves a husband can make for his marriage.

The second cost is the impoverishment of fatherhood. The man without friends often parents in isolation. The shared wisdom that older men used to transmit to younger fathers — through fraternal organizations, through neighborhood relationships, through church involvement — has been substantially lost. The contemporary father often parents from his own resources and from the parenting books he can find, without the lateral relational input that historic male friendship would have provided. The parenting is harder than it has to be, and the difficulty contributes to the breaking of fathers that has been part of the contemporary parenting crisis.

The third cost is the mental health collapse. The data on male suicide rates, on death-of-despair rates, on midlife crisis indicators is consistent. American men in midlife are suffering at rates that previous generations did not. The suffering is causally related, in the careful research, to the friendship recession. The man without friends has no one to call when the suffering arrives. The suffering deepens in isolation. The deepening produces the outcomes the data is now measuring.

The fourth cost is the impoverishment of the church. The men who would have been the lay leaders of congregations a generation ago are now often disengaged from church life in ways that the previous generation was not. The reasons are partly the broader friendship recession; the church has not provided the structures of male friendship that would have kept the men engaged. The result is congregations whose lay leadership pipeline is thin and whose male membership is declining, and the result has been worse for the church than the church has yet fully recognized.

## How friendships actually form

I want to be concrete about how male friendships actually form, because the abstract conversation about loneliness does not produce friendships and the practical conversation has, in my experience, been more useful.

Male friendships form through repeated time and shared activity. The friendships do not, on the whole, form through intentional conversation about feelings. They form through participation in shared activities — sports, work projects, classes, hobbies, ministries — repeated over years. The intentional conversation about deeper things comes later, after the trust has been built through the sustained participation. Most contemporary American men who attempt to form friendships through direct emotional disclosure find the attempt awkward and unsustainable. The attempt is in the wrong order.

The friendships require sustained presence. The friendship at year three is not the friendship the same group of men will have at year fifteen. The deeper friendships are produced by sustained participation over years rather than by intensity over months. Most American men quit before year three. The quitting is part of why deeper friendships have become rare. The willingness to stay through the boring middle is what produces the friendship that eventually matters.

The friendships require shared loss as well as shared activity. The men who become close to one another over time are typically men who have walked through specific shared difficulties — a death, a job loss, a marital crisis, a child in trouble. The shared difficulty is what produces the depth that casual friendship cannot reach. The willingness to be present for one another through difficulty is part of what distinguishes deep friendship from casual acquaintance. The willingness is not always available in the American man's relational style, and the developing of the willingness is part of the work.

The friendships require some explicit conversation about deeper things, eventually. The friendships that remain superficial across decades have produced something less than what scripture and the historic tradition describe as Christian male friendship. The friendships that grow deeper include, eventually, explicit conversations about faith, about marriage, about the work being carried, about the failures being lived through. The explicit conversation can be awkward. The explicit conversation is part of what makes the friendship real.

The friendships are protected by structure. The men who maintain deep friendships across decades typically have structural protections for the friendships — regular meetings, sustained activity together, the explicit commitment to keeping the relationship alive across the various transitions of life. The structural protection compensates for the natural drift that destroys most friendships over time.

## What to do this month

I want to give specific counsel for the man reading this who has recognized the condition and wants to begin the recovery.

Pick one activity you will attend regularly. The activity should bring you into sustained contact with the same men over time. Church small groups, men's Bible studies, recreational sports leagues, regular volunteer commitments, classes you take at the local community college, hobby groups — any of these can work. The activity should be one you can commit to for at least a year. The committing is the foundation.

Be the person who suggests the additional gathering. The men in your activity have not generally been waiting for you to suggest that you grab dinner after the activity, that you do something together on a Saturday, that you keep in touch between the regular meetings. They are waiting for someone else to suggest it, in the same way you have been waiting. Be the someone else. The willingness to be the initiator is what moves the acquaintance toward friendship.

Take the friendships seriously over years. The friendship at year one is not the friendship that will eventually matter. The friendship at year five may be the friendship that saves your life in some specific crisis. The willingness to invest in friendships across the boring middle is what produces the friendships that matter.

Address the cultural script directly. The internalized scripts that have been keeping you from friendship can be addressed. Read about it. Talk to your spouse about it. Get a therapist if you need help. The cultural script is not theology. The cultural script is a recent invention that has produced enormous harm, and the willingness to refuse it is part of the recovery of what the historic Christian tradition has consistently honored as a central part of the male life.

Pray for friendships. The friendships you need are friendships that God has, in many cases, already been preparing for you. The praying is part of how you become available to receive them.

## The verdict

You were not made to do this alone. The cultural moment in which men are supposed to be lonely, self-reliant, and stoic is a cultural lie. The biblical pattern, the historical pattern, the human pattern is that men have other men in their lives at a depth that takes years to build. Build it. Build it now. Begin by attending the same gathering, the same gym, the same study, the same league, repeatedly, for years. The friendship at year fifteen is the friendship that saves men's lives. It cannot exist without the boring middle.

I am writing this for the American man who has been carrying loneliness without permission to name it. The naming is here. The recovery is available. The recovery requires sustained effort across years rather than intensity across months. The recovery is what your marriage needs, what your children need, what your church needs, what your own soul needs.

Begin this month. Pick one activity. Show up consistently. Stay through the boring middle. The friendship is there waiting to be built, and the building is yours to do, and the building will change the rest of your life if you commit to it.
