Most Bible reading plans make people quit by February. The reason is structural. The plans were designed for the wrong reader. The reader the plans assume is the reader who already reads the Bible fluently and wants a sustained framework for ongoing reading. The reader the plans actually need to address is the one who has not been reading the Bible regularly, who has tried before and quit, and who is approaching the book with a mix of curiosity, intimidation, and the residual guilt of previous failures. The mismatch produces the predictable result. The annual cycle of January enthusiasm followed by February abandonment is so widely observed it has become almost a Christian joke. The joke is not funny. The joke is the visible sign of a strategy that has been failing for a generation.

This essay is for the reader who wants to start reading the Bible and who has been intimidated by the plans, the apps, the well-meaning Christian-bookstore literature, and the previous failed attempts. The strategy I want to offer is older than the modern reading-plan industry. It is the strategy the Christian tradition has been using for most of its two thousand years. It is more sustainable than the plans, and it produces, in my pastoral experience, the kinds of readers who keep reading rather than the kinds who quit.

## Why the standard plans fail

The standard Bible reading plans — *Read the Bible in a Year*, *McCheyne's Plan*, the various denominational variations — share a structural feature that I want to name. They are oriented toward coverage rather than formation. The implicit goal is the reading of the whole Bible within a specified time frame. The reading itself is the metric. The plan succeeds when you have read all the assigned passages; the plan fails when you have not.

The orientation toward coverage produces several predictable problems. The pace required to read the Bible in a year — roughly three to four chapters daily, with longer days for the historical books — does not allow for the kind of slow reading the difficult passages require. The reader who hits Leviticus, the genealogies, the more obscure parts of Ezekiel, or the apocalyptic sections of Daniel and Revelation often finds himself either skimming to keep up or falling behind and quitting. The pace also does not allow for any sustained reflection on what is being read. The reader is moving through text quickly enough to maintain the schedule, and the moving-through-quickly does not, on average, produce the kind of formation the reading was supposed to produce.

The orientation toward coverage also misframes what the Bible is for. The Bible is not a textbook to be mastered through coverage. The Bible is a library of texts meant to be read slowly, reflectively, in conversation with the church's two-thousand-year interpretive tradition, with attention to what God is saying through the reading rather than what can be checked off the list. The historic Christian reading practice has been almost the opposite of the modern reading-plan industry. The historic practice has been slow, repetitive, contemplative, and centered on the deep absorption of relatively small amounts of text rather than the rapid coverage of large amounts.

## What lectio divina actually is

I want to introduce a historic practice that has been one of the most useful pastoral tools I have offered to people who want to start reading the Bible and have been failing under the standard plans. The practice is *lectio divina* — sacred reading — and it has been the standard Christian devotional reading practice for most of the church's history, including in Protestant traditions that have, in some cases, forgotten that they have inherited it.

*Lectio divina* has four movements, classically described as *lectio, meditatio, oratio, contemplatio* — reading, meditation, prayer, contemplation. The movements are not a checklist. The movements are a rhythm that the reader gradually internalizes over months and years of practice. The rhythm operates on a small amount of text, usually four to ten verses, and takes ten to twenty minutes rather than the hour or more that the standard reading plans require.

The first movement, *lectio*, is the slow reading of the passage. The reader reads the passage once, slowly, to take in what is there. The reader reads it again, more slowly, attending to the words that seem to carry weight. The reader reads it a third time, even more slowly, letting particular phrases settle. The reading is unhurried. The reading is repetitive. The reading is not aimed at coverage; the reading is aimed at receiving what the text has to say.

The second movement, *meditatio*, is the deliberate consideration of what the text means. The reader asks what the passage is saying, what it is asking of him, what it is showing him about God or about himself or about the world. The meditation can include the use of study tools — a good study Bible, a brief commentary, the cross-references — but the meditation is not primarily an academic exercise. The meditation is the reader's sustained attention to what the text is doing in his particular life on this particular day.

The third movement, *oratio*, is prayer in response to the reading. The prayer can be brief or extended. The prayer is shaped by what the reading and the meditation have produced. The reader may pray for the gift of understanding, for the grace to obey what he has been shown, for the people the passage brings to mind, for the situations in his life that the passage addresses. The prayer is the reader's response to the God who has been speaking through the text.

The fourth movement, *contemplatio*, is the contemplative resting in what has been received. The reader stops his active engagement and rests in the presence of God. The resting may produce nothing apparent. The resting is part of the practice. The historic tradition has consistently honored the contemplative dimension of devotional reading, and the contemporary American evangelical tradition has, on the whole, neglected it.

## How to actually start

I want to be concrete about how the practice translates into the actual daily rhythm of someone starting from nothing.

Pick a single book of the Bible. Not the whole Bible. One book. The Gospel of Mark is a good starting point — it is short, narrative, and theologically rich. The Gospel of John is another. The book of Philippians is the shortest viable option for someone who wants to start very small. Pick one book. Stay in it for several weeks.

Pick a small passage. The passage should be four to ten verses, sometimes shorter. The first paragraph of Mark 1 — verses 1 through 8 — is a fine starting passage. The opening of John 1 — the first eighteen verses, the prologue — is another. The beginning of Philippians — the first eleven verses — is another. The passage should be small enough to read slowly and to return to multiple times within the session.

Set a consistent time. The historic devotional practice has been more sustainable when it has been at the same time each day. Many Christians find early morning works best — before the day's interruptions arrive, before the phone has been opened, when the mind is relatively fresh. Some find evening works better — at the end of the day, in the quiet after the household has settled. The specific time matters less than the consistency. The consistency is what allows the practice to become a habit.

Begin with five to fifteen minutes. The historic practice of *lectio divina* often took longer when the practitioner was deeply formed in it, but the contemporary reader starting from nothing should begin with a duration that is sustainable on busy days. Five to fifteen minutes is a reasonable target. The duration can grow over months and years as the practice deepens.

Do the four movements briefly. Read the passage three times slowly. Sit with it for a few minutes, asking what it is saying to you. Pray briefly in response. Sit in silence for a minute or two at the end. The whole rhythm should take ten minutes at most for someone starting out. The brevity is part of the sustainability. The depth comes from repetition over years rather than from length on any given day.

Move slowly through the book. The reader who has settled into the rhythm I am describing might cover four to ten verses per session, and might cover one chapter per week or per two weeks. The pace seems slow compared to the year-long reading plans. The pace is what allows the reading to actually form the reader. The pace is what the historic tradition has consistently honored as the right pace for devotional reading. The reader who reads slowly through Mark over three months will know that gospel in a way the reader who covered the whole New Testament in a year does not.

## What to add over time

The basic practice I have described will sustain the reader through the first year of returning to Bible reading. As the practice settles and the reader gains confidence, several additions can deepen the reading.

A good study Bible. The ESV Study Bible, the NIV Application Commentary series, the Faithlife Study Bible, or any of several other contemporary study Bibles provide brief commentary, cross-references, and historical context that enrich the reading. The study Bible should be a resource for occasional consultation rather than the primary reading text. The reading should remain the focus; the study Bible serves the reading.

A brief commentary on the book being read. The N.T. Wright *For Everyone* series is accessible and theologically rich. The Tyndale Old and New Testament Commentaries are slightly more technical but still accessible. The Bible Speaks Today series is good. Pick one commentary on the book you are reading, and consult it briefly when the meditation phase raises questions you cannot resolve on your own.

The practice of memorization. The historic Christian tradition has consistently included the memorization of scripture as part of devotional formation. The memorization need not be ambitious. A short psalm a quarter, or the Lord's Prayer learned by heart, or a substantial passage from one of the Pauline letters, can over years produce an accumulated store of scripture that the reader carries with him through life. The memorization deepens the reading in ways nothing else does.

The integration of scripture into household and congregational worship. The reading is supported by, and supports, the worship of the household and the congregation. The reader who is reading scripture devotionally in the morning and hearing it preached in the congregation on Sunday and reading it with his household in the evening is forming a comprehensive engagement with the text that produces the kind of biblical literacy and biblical formation the historic Christian tradition has consistently honored.

## The verdict

The Bible reading habit that survives is not the one that finished the annual plan. The Bible reading habit that survives is the one that has settled into a sustainable daily rhythm of slow attention to small passages over years. The historic practice the church has been using for most of its history is more sustainable than the modern industry's coverage plans, and it produces the kind of readers who keep reading rather than the kind who quit.

I am writing this for the reader who has tried before and quit, for the reader who has been intimidated by the plans, for the reader who wants to read the Bible and has not yet found the practice that works for him. The practice I have described will work, in my pastoral experience, for most readers who give it a sustained chance. The chance has to be measured in months rather than in days. The first three weeks may feel awkward. By the third month, the reader who has stayed with the practice will usually find that the rhythm has settled into his life and that the reading has begun to do what reading the Bible is supposed to do.

Begin tomorrow morning. Mark 1, verses 1 through 8. Ten minutes. The rest is months of consistent return. The return is what the Christian tradition has been doing for two thousand years, and the return is what produces, over time, the readers who actually know what scripture says rather than the readers who have read it once quickly and forgotten.
