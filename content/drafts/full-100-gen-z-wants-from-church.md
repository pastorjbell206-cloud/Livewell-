The church's typical answer to losing young people has been, for thirty years, a version of the same thing: better production, more relevant music, a preacher who wears sneakers and speaks their language. Some of this helped, for a time, with certain generations. With Gen Z — roughly those born between the mid-1990s and early 2010s — it is landing wrong.

Not because young people today do not respond to quality. But because Gen Z has a highly developed sense of when they are being marketed to, and the church that reorganizes itself around attracting them tends to read to them as exactly that: a sales strategy dressed in theological language.

What research on Gen Z's religious attitudes consistently shows [cite — confirm] is not a generation that has abandoned the desire for transcendence, community, or moral grounding. These are not people who are indifferent to meaning. They are people who have a profound suspicion of institutions that claim to offer meaning while demonstrating, through their behavior, something other than what they advertise. The scandals of the last two decades — financial, sexual, ecclesial — have not passed unnoticed by a generation that grew up with social media making institutional failures visible in real time. They are not uninterested in the church. They are skeptical of it for specific reasons that deserve specific responses.

What they are looking for is not a more entertaining version of what their parents had. They are looking for something genuinely different from the consumerism that surrounds them everywhere else. They want a community that is actually a community — that knows its members, that bears one another's burdens in the Galatians 6:2 sense of the phrase, that does not simply gather for a weekly event and disperse back into isolation. The church that has perfected the Sunday morning experience and done nothing about the Monday morning loneliness of its members will not hold Gen Z. Because they can identify that gap. They live in it.

They also want, more than the church generally expects, intellectual honesty. This is the finding that most surprises pastors: Gen Z is not asking for faith made easier. Some of them are asking for faith that takes its hardest questions seriously — that does not treat doubt as a spiritual failure, that does not paper over the genuine difficulties of Christian intellectual claims, that can say "I do not know" without appearing to lose confidence in the whole structure. The church that presents a version of Christianity that has been pre-sanitized of its complications will lose the intellectually serious among Gen Z before the first month is out.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from prison in 1944, described what he called "religionless Christianity" — a form of faith stripped of its bourgeois cultural trappings and expressed in terms that could be inhabited by people who had seen through the accommodations of institutional religion. [cite — confirm] He was not calling for less theology. He was calling for a theology embedded in actual human reality rather than protected from it. That is closer to what Gen Z is looking for than anything currently described as "Gen Z ministry."

The congregation that will retain young adults is not the one that has the best band. It is the one that has taken the hardest questions seriously, built genuine community across the week rather than just on Sunday morning, and demonstrated — in its actual life, not its marketing — that the gospel it announces is the thing it lives.

That is not a generational program. That is just the church being the church.

It has always been what young people needed. We have simply been too busy being relevant to notice.
