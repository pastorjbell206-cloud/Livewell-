The biblical position on the poor is not ambiguous. It is not one theme among many, balanced against equal and opposite themes. It is among the most consistently stated, most emphatically repeated, most specifically applied commands in all of Scripture — and somewhere along the way the American church turned it into a department. A line item. A ministry category with its own budget and its own volunteers, safely contained so that the rest of the church's life need not be disturbed by it.

That containment is the error. The poor are not a category the church manages. They are the test the church is given.

Consider how much of the text is taken up with this. The Law builds care for the poor into the structure of the economy itself — gleaning laws, the sabbath year, the jubilee, the prohibition on charging the poor interest (Leviticus 25, Deuteronomy 15). The Prophets measure the nation's faithfulness almost entirely by its treatment of the widow, the orphan, the stranger, the destitute. Jesus opens his public ministry announcing good news to the poor (Luke 4:18) and stakes the final judgment on whether we fed the hungry and visited the prisoner — *as you did it to one of the least of these, you did it to me* (Matthew 25:40). James says religion that God accepts is to visit the orphan and widow in their affliction (James 1:27). This is not a fringe emphasis you have to dig for. It is the main road.

So how did we make it optional? We did it the way the church usually domesticates a hard command: we spiritualized it, and we departmentalized it. We decided "the poor" mostly meant the poor in spirit, a tidy interior condition that requires nothing of our wallets. And what we could not spiritualize away, we walled off into a benevolence fund and a quarterly outreach, so that the church could care for the poor the way a corporation has a philanthropy arm — real, funded, and entirely separate from how the institution actually lives.

I have done this. I have preached the spiritualized version because it asked nothing costly of comfortable people, myself included. I have pointed proudly at the benevolence line in the budget as though writing a check were the same as the life the text describes. I am not exposing someone else's evasion. I am confessing the shape of my own.

Here is why it matters that the poor are a test and not a category. A category can be managed at the edge of an institution without changing its center. A test goes to the center. The question Scripture keeps pressing is not *does your church have a ministry to the poor* — almost every church can answer yes. The question is *what does the way you treat the powerless reveal about the God you actually worship.* Because the God of the Bible has identified himself with them to a degree that should frighten us. He did not say the poor are near his concerns. He said what you do to them, you do to him.

This is the test, and it is not graded on the existence of a program. It is graded on something harder to fake: whether the church's ordinary life — its budget, its friendships, its sense of who belongs — actually includes the people the world has discarded, or whether it has found a respectable way to keep them at the edge while feeling generous about it.

To recover this is not to swap the gospel for social work. The cross is still the center; people need to be reconciled to God and not merely fed. But the same gospel that reconciles us to God reconciles us to each other, and a reconciliation that leaves the poor exactly where it found them is not the biblical kind. The vertical and the horizontal were never meant to come apart. We pried them apart because keeping them together costs too much.

The poor are not your church's project. They are the exam. And the text has already told you the question, asked the same way every time: when you saw the least of these — did you see me?
