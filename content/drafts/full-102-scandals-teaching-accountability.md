The names accumulate. Pastors of megachurches. Founders of parachurch organizations. Denominational leaders with decades of respected service. The pattern has become familiar enough that a segment of the public now reads each new revelation without surprise — as confirmation of what they already suspected rather than as an aberration that demands explanation. That readiness to file the scandal as expected is, in its own way, a form of damage that the church has done to its own witness.

The temptation when these failures become public is to treat them as individual moral failures of specific individuals with specific character defects, who happened to end up in positions of authority. This reading is not wrong — individuals make choices, and the choices these men made were real and their consequences are real and the people they harmed deserve more than a systemic analysis. But the individual-failure reading, by itself, is insufficient. Because the structural conditions that allowed these failures to persist — in some cases for years, in some cases for decades — were not accidents. They were features of systems that had been built in ways that made accountability structurally difficult.

The concentration of authority in a single charismatic leader with no meaningful accountability structure around him. The culture of deference that frames questioning the pastor as questioning God. The financial arrangements that give a leader personal control over resources without independent oversight. The complaints that were received, taken seriously for a moment, and then managed into silence by institutions more committed to their reputation than to the protection of vulnerable people. These are not the conditions of individual moral failure. They are architectural. They were built. They can be rebuilt differently.

The biblical warnings about the corruptions of unchecked power are not incidental to the tradition. They are among its most persistent themes. Samuel warns Israel that a king will take your sons, your daughters, your fields, your servants, and your harvest (1 Samuel 8:10–18). The prophets return again and again to the specific abuses committed by leaders who were accountable to no one but themselves. The elder qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1 are not a list of spiritual virtues in the abstract — they are a description of a man whose character has been tested in contexts where character can actually be observed: in his household, in his community, over time. The New Testament does not trust personal charisma as a sufficient basis for leadership authority.

The church that reads the current reckoning and responds primarily by better vetting individual character has not understood what the reckoning is about. Character matters, enormously. But character without structure is insufficient. Every leader, whatever his character, benefits from accountability — not because he cannot be trusted, but because human beings are not designed to operate without it. The argument for accountability structures is not that leaders are untrustworthy. It is that trustworthiness is better sustained with oversight than without it.

I have been in ministry long enough to know how accountability gets resisted. It gets framed as a failure of trust, as an insult to a leader's proven track record, as an institutional imposition on the freedom of the Spirit. I have made some version of that argument myself. It is not a good argument. It is a self-protective argument dressed in theological language.

The people who were harmed by pastoral failure in these institutions did not need a better background check on the man who hurt them. They needed a system where someone beside that man was empowered to act on what they reported.

Build the structures that could catch you if you fell.

And build them before you need them.

The church that waits for a scandal to take accountability seriously has waited too long.
