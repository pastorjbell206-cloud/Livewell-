The word "missionary" carries a specific image in the American evangelical imagination: a Western Christian, usually white, who leaves a comfortable life in the developed world to carry the gospel to a people who do not yet have it. That image is not entirely wrong. It describes something that has happened, at real cost, for centuries — from William Carey wading into Bengal in 1793 to the thousands of cross-cultural workers who serve today in difficult conditions. The sacrifice is real. The fruit has been real.

The image is also, increasingly, an incomplete account of where the missionary movement actually lives.

The center of global Christianity has shifted. The largest Christian populations are now in Africa, Asia, and Latin America [cite — confirm]. The fastest-growing churches are in Nigeria, Brazil, China, and South Korea. The countries that were once "mission fields" receiving workers from the West now send more missionaries than many Western nations. The Korean church, to name the most dramatic example, has become one of the world's largest missionary-sending communities [cite — confirm]. The Ethiopian church, the Nigerian church, the Brazilian church — these are not young communities being nurtured by Western missionaries. They are mature, rooted communities producing their own theologians, their own missionaries, their own pastors who go to places that the Western church cannot access.

This shift has consequences for how the American pastor understands his role in global mission.

The older model placed the American or British missionary at the center: he raised support in the West, went to the field, planted a church, trained local leaders, and eventually handed off. That model assumed a particular asymmetry — Western resources and expertise flowing toward a less developed church in need of formation. In many contexts that model is now not only outdated but actively counterproductive. The national pastor who leads a congregation of five hundred in rural India does not need the American church to send him a church planter. He may need the American church to send him resources, to advocate for his community, to learn from his theological insights, or to partner in the specific mission he is already executing with a depth of cultural competence that no outsider can replicate.

That reorientation requires genuine humility from the American church, and humility about mission is not one of our strongest institutional instincts. We are accustomed to being the ones who send. The logic of becoming the ones who receive — who learn from the global church rather than primarily teach it — requires a reordering of our assumptions about what we have to offer and what we need.

The national pastor working in a context of persecution has things to teach the American church about faith under pressure that our comfort has prevented us from learning any other way. The Nigerian pastor whose congregation prays with a desperation that is not performance but necessity has things to teach the American congregation whose prayer life is anemic not from lack of theology but from lack of felt need. The Indian pastor who plants churches in communities where conversion is socially and sometimes physically dangerous has things to teach the American congregation whose evangelism is limited by the fear of mild social awkwardness.

None of this diminishes the call to send. The American church still has resources — financial, relational, logistical — that the global church needs, and there are still contexts where the cross-cultural worker from outside carries something the local community cannot provide. The sending vocation is not over.

What is over is the assumption that the sending runs in one direction. The missionary movement of the twenty-first century is a conversation between the global church and the church in the West, and the American pastor who has not yet begun listening to his end of that conversation is missing something essential.

The gospel traveled from Jerusalem to Antioch to Rome. It did not stop there.

It has been traveling ever since. Often without our help.
