I have been describing the problem for nine essays. This is the only one in the arc that tries to describe the way through. I have been putting this essay off because it is the hardest one to write. The diagnosis essays were easier; the patterns are visible, the historical analogies are abundant, the prophetic register is the register the previous essays have lived in. The constructive register is different. The constructive register requires me to say not only what has gone wrong but what to do, and the saying-what-to-do is precisely the place where most pastoral writing collapses into either programmatic optimism or vague encouragement. I have been guilty of both. I am going to try to avoid both here, and the trying will not be entirely successful, and you will have to grant the unsuccess and read the essay anyway.

What I want to say is this. The way through the loss of Christendom is older than the loss of Christendom. The way through has been here the whole time. The way through is the slow recovery of practices the church has held in every period of its history but has, in the Christendom era, allowed to atrophy. We do not need to invent the post-Christendom church. We need to recover the church that was here all along, hidden inside the costume. The recovery is hard. The recovery is what the next generation of faithful Christians will be doing. I want to describe what the recovery looks like, in terms practical enough to act on this week.

## Four practices, recovered

I am going to name four practices. They are not original. They are the practices the early church used to survive the Roman Empire, the medieval church used to survive the dark periods between revivals, the Reformation churches used to rebuild Christian formation after centuries of nominal Catholicism, and the global church is using right now to survive in places where Christendom never existed. The four are Sabbath, hospitality, catechesis, and liturgical formation. None is new. All have been atrophied in mid-twentieth-century American evangelicalism. All can be recovered, in households and congregations, beginning now.

I want to describe each in turn, and then say something about what holds them together.

## Sabbath

Sabbath is not primarily about rest. Sabbath is primarily about refusing to be ruled by the empire's time.

Walter Brueggemann wrote a small book about this called *Sabbath as Resistance* (2014). I have used it in three different small groups, with three different sets of laypeople, and the book has done more theological work in my congregation than most of the larger books I have used. Brueggemann's argument, briefly, is this. The first Sabbath command in Exodus 20 is grounded in the creation rhythm — God rested on the seventh day. The repetition of the Sabbath command in Deuteronomy 5 is grounded in liberation — *remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt.* The two groundings together make Sabbath a political practice. Egypt was the empire of productivity, the empire that worked the slaves seven days a week, the empire whose Pharaoh could not stop demanding bricks. Sabbath is the refusal to live by Pharaoh's calendar. Sabbath is the practice of remembering that we have been liberated from the empire of endless production and have been given a rhythm that allows us to stop, weekly, to acknowledge that we are not the empire's slaves.

The American empire is not Pharaoh, but the American empire has its own demand for endless production. The forty-hour week has become the fifty-hour week, the sixty-hour week, the always-on week. The phone in the pocket has erased the distinction between work and home. The kids' activities have absorbed Sunday afternoons. The Sabbath that used to structure the week in American Protestantism — the slow Sunday, the long lunch, the afternoon walk, the evening reading — has, for most American Christians I pastor, simply dissolved. It is one of the most consequential losses of the late-Christendom period.

The recovery is concrete. Twenty-four hours a week, beginning Saturday night at sundown if you want to keep it the way the Jewish tradition kept it, or Sunday morning to Monday morning if the practical rhythm of your household requires that. Phone off. Screens off, with limited exceptions. Work off. Errands not done. Time for worship, for slow meals, for conversation, for reading, for prayer, for being with your family in a way that is not optimized for productivity. The first three months of trying to keep Sabbath will be painful. Most people I have walked through this discover that they did not know how exhausted they were, because they had never given the body a chance to rest. The Sabbath is the chance. The Sabbath also forms, in the people who keep it, a body that knows it is not the empire's slave. The knowing is what makes the rest of the political theology I have been writing in this arc actually livable. You cannot resist the empire's narrative if your body is running on the empire's rhythm. The body has to be retrained. The retraining is Sabbath.

## Hospitality

Hospitality is the practice of caring for the people the empire does not care for. I have written about this in the early-church essay. I want to make it concrete here.

The historic Christian practice of hospitality is not the modern American practice of entertaining your friends. The Greek word is *philoxenia* — love of the stranger. Hospitality in the New Testament and the early church meant taking in the traveler you did not know, the widow without family, the abandoned infant, the released prisoner, the foreigner, the sick. The early Christian household was structured to receive guests. The bishops' duties included managing the welfare of the local widows, orphans, and travelers. The eucharistic celebration included an explicit collection for the people who could not feed themselves. Hospitality was the daily practice that made the persecution-era church visible to its pagan neighbors as something other than a private club for like-minded people.

The recovery of hospitality requires the household to be different than the average American household is now. The average American household is structured around the nuclear family, with strangers received only for short visits and with elaborate hosting protocols. The historic Christian household received people for weeks and months and sometimes years. It absorbed unmarried relatives, widowed parents, traveling missionaries, students, recently-released prisoners trying to rebuild their lives. The Christian household was, in some real sense, the parish's basic unit of mercy. Most American evangelical households are not.

I want to be honest. Our household has been one of the more hospitable in our church, and we have still been bad at this. We have hosted the dinners, but we have not absorbed the lives. We have taken in friends for a week, but we have not taken in strangers for a year. The retreat into the privacy of the nuclear family has been one of the great unnamed losses of late-Christendom American Christianity. The recovery is slow. The recovery begins with one specific person you are willing to welcome into your household for longer than is convenient.

The hospitality recovery is the practice that gives the post-Christendom church its public witness. People will not, on the whole, be argued back into Christianity. They will sometimes be loved back into it. The love has to be specific, embodied, structured into the rhythms of the household. The hospitality practice is the structure that makes the love possible.

## Catechesis

Catechesis is the slow teaching of the faith. Mid-twentieth-century American evangelicalism could assume the surrounding culture would catechize the basics — the existence of God, the moral seriousness of sin, the dignity of the human person, the historical Christian narrative. The surrounding culture is not doing this anymore. The church will now have to teach everything from the ground up. Most of us are not prepared to.

The catechetical recovery has two pieces. The first is the household. The second is the congregation.

The household catechesis recovery requires the parents to be the primary teachers of the faith. This is the historic Protestant position. The Westminster Larger Catechism in its exposition of the fifth commandment makes the household the basic site of religious formation. The Heidelberg Catechism was designed to be memorized in households over fifty-two Sundays. Wesley's hymnal was, in part, a catechetical tool for the singing of doctrine in homes. The mid-twentieth-century evangelical assumption that catechesis could be outsourced to the youth pastor and the Sunday school teacher was an aberration. The aberration has produced two generations of Christian young adults who do not, in any sustained way, know what their tradition holds. The aberration cannot continue. The household has to teach again.

The household teaching does not require parents to be theologically sophisticated. It requires consistency and rhythm. Five minutes a day, every day, of the basics — the Lord's Prayer, the Apostles' Creed, a small chunk of one of the historic catechisms, an evening scripture reading, a goodnight prayer. The historic Christian household maintained this for centuries. The recovery is what the next generation of faithful Protestant households will be doing, and the doing will produce children who know the faith in a way the youth-group generation did not.

The congregational catechesis recovery requires the church to do what the early church did. Adults are catechized before baptism, or before becoming full members, or before having children dedicated. The catechetical class is six to twelve weeks of explicit teaching of the basics — God, scripture, Christ, the cross, the resurrection, the church, the sacraments, the moral life. The basics are taught with seriousness. The basics are tested, lightly. The basics are integrated into the household rhythm of the new family. The basics are returned to, again, every few years, for the same family, as their children grow and the questions deepen.

The catechetical recovery is the slowest of the four practices, because it requires institutional commitment and consistent leadership over years. It is also the most important. The church that has not catechized its members will, in the post-Christendom moment, lose them — because the surrounding culture is not going to provide them the theological vocabulary they need to hold their faith against it.

## Liturgical formation

Liturgical formation is the recovery of worship that forms people. James K.A. Smith's *You Are What You Love* (2016) is the best one-volume articulation of this argument. Smith's claim, briefly, is that worship is formative whether we intend it to be or not. The shape of the worship shapes the people. The thin entertainment-driven worship that has dominated American evangelicalism for forty years has, on the whole, formed people who are entertained but not formed. The deeper worship of the historic Christian tradition — the eucharist celebrated with weight, the historic prayers prayed slowly, the Psalms sung in their full range from praise to lament, the liturgical year observed in its seasons — forms people more deeply.

The recovery does not require every evangelical church to become high-church Anglican or Catholic. The recovery requires every evangelical church to ask, honestly, what its worship is forming people into. The praise-band concert that opens many evangelical services is forming people into consumers of religious experience. The motivational sermon that occupies much of the central portion is forming people into self-help seekers. The brief altar call that closes is forming people into makers of one-time decisions rather than long obedience.

The historic liturgies were not perfect. They had their own forms of formation problem. But they had something most American evangelical worship does not have — they had the recognition that worship is formative, and they had the historical accumulation of practices that have proven to form Christian disciples over centuries. The recovery requires evangelical churches to read the historic liturgies seriously, to import what serves the formation of disciples, and to discard the entertainment-driven additions that have been displacing the formative core.

The eucharist celebrated weekly with weight. The Lord's Prayer prayed slowly. The Psalms used in their full range. The liturgical year observed in its seasons — Advent that genuinely waits, Lent that genuinely fasts, Eastertide that genuinely celebrates for fifty days. The recovery is slow. The recovery requires pastoral leadership willing to lose, in the short term, the entertainment-driven attendees who came for the concert. The recovery produces, in the medium term, the formed disciples who can carry the church through the post-Christendom moment. The recovery is, by every historical measure I can find, the more durable strategy.

## What holds them together

The four practices are not a program. They are an integrated practice. Sabbath gives the body the rhythm. Hospitality gives the household the structure. Catechesis gives the mind the content. Liturgical formation gives the worship the depth. The four together form a Christian person and a Christian household and, over time, a Christian congregation that can hold its faith without depending on the surrounding cultural support that Christendom used to provide.

What holds them together is the recognition that the church has to do, intentionally and slowly, what the surrounding culture used to do without our help. The catechesis used to happen by default. The Sabbath used to be enforced by the surrounding civic rhythm. The hospitality used to be supported by neighborhood density and lower mobility. The liturgical formation used to be carried by the broader Christian cultural inheritance. None of this is happening by default anymore. The church has to do it deliberately.

The deliberate doing is, in some ways, the recovery of what the early church did under conditions much harder than ours. The early church catechized for years before baptism. The early church kept Sabbath under an empire that did not honor it. The early church practiced hospitality in cities that were hostile to them. The early church worshipped with weight in homes because they had no buildings. We have buildings. We have the historic catechisms. We have the historic liturgies. We have two thousand years of accumulated practical wisdom. We have, in a real sense, more resources than the early church had. We have, in another real sense, much less courage than the early church had. The recovery is the slow process by which we use the resources we have to build the courage we have lost.

## The verdict

The post-Christendom church is not a problem to be solved. It is the church being given back the practices that the Christendom arrangement had let us neglect. The four practices — Sabbath, hospitality, catechesis, liturgical formation — are the church's historic core, and they have been atrophied in our generation, and they can be recovered, beginning this week, in your household and my household and the households of every congregation that is willing to be patient about the work.

The work is generational. The work will not produce the political wins American evangelicalism has been chasing for forty years. The work will produce, over time, the kind of church that can hold its faith without political wins, the kind of household that can form children who know what they believe and why, the kind of congregation that can outlast the regimes that did not love it and the regimes that did.

This is the constructive answer. This is the church on the other side of Christendom. This is not a new thing. This is the church being itself again, after a long period of being something thinner. The being-itself is the recovery. The recovery is the work. The work is yours. The work is mine. The work is what the rest of my pastoral life will be about.

The next series of essays in this writing project moves to other questions — politics, the American church in detail, the everyday-life applications of the same theological commitments to marriage and parenting and money and manhood and womanhood. The After Christendom arc closes here. The constructive answer is the four practices. The four practices are recoverable. The recovery starts this week, in your household, with one practice you have not yet been keeping. Pick one. Begin. The next generation of the faithful church will be the generation that did this. Let us be the parents of that generation. The generation does not yet exist. The generation will exist because of the practices we are willing to recover now, while there is still time.
