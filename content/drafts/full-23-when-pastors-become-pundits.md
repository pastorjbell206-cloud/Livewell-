The pastor who becomes a pundit has stopped being a pastor. The transformation is gradual and almost always invisible to the pastor doing it.

I have watched this happen up close over twenty years of pastoral ministry. I have watched it happen to friends. I have watched it happen, in seasons, to myself. The slide from pastor to pundit is not announced. It does not arrive on a particular Sunday. It arrives in small substitutions, one after another, until the role has changed and no one — including the pastor — has noticed when the change became permanent.

This essay is about the substitutions. It is also about how to reverse them, while there is still time.

## Why pastors get pulled toward punditry

The seduction is real. Pastoring is hard, slow, often invisible. Punditry is fast, visible, rewarded. The pull is not surprising. It is exactly what you would expect, given the economic and attention structures of American religious life in our generation.

Pastoral work happens in slow conversations across decades. The fruit shows up at funerals, at weddings, in the third generation that came through your church. Most of what you do as a pastor will not be visible to anyone who has not been formed by it over years. You will preach a sermon. Eleven people will say *good sermon, pastor* on the way out. None of them will mention what you actually said. Two of them will think about it on Wednesday. One of them, eighteen months from now, will tell you that the sermon changed something in her marriage that she has not been able to name. That is the rhythm. That is what the work feels like.

Punditry has a different rhythm. A pundit posts a take. The take gets engagement within hours. The numbers come back fast. The applause is immediate. The criticism is also immediate, but the criticism functions, in the attention economy, as further engagement, and the engagement is itself rewarding. The feedback loop is short. The dopamine is reliable. The pundit knows, within a day, what worked and what did not.

The pastor who is also producing content for a public platform — the Substack, the podcast, the social-media presence that has become standard equipment for pastoral identity in our generation — feels the pull of both rhythms. The pastoral rhythm is slow and frustrating. The pundit rhythm is fast and rewarding. Over time, almost without exception in the cases I have watched up close, the faster rhythm starts to dominate. The pastor begins to think in pundit terms. The sermons begin to sound like takes. The pastoral conversations begin to feel less interesting than the public commentary. The substitution is underway.

## What is a pundit, and why is it a different category from a pastor

I want to be precise about the distinction, because the categories are different in ways that matter theologically.

A pundit's authority is borrowed from the audience. The pundit has the platform he has because people pay attention to him. If they stop paying attention, the platform shrinks. The pundit's incentive structure rewards the production of takes that hold attention. The takes that hold attention, in our current information environment, are the takes that confirm what the audience already believes, that name the audience's perceived enemies, and that produce the emotional reactions the audience came for.

A pastor's authority is given by Christ through the church. The pastor has the authority he has because he has been ordained, called, set apart by a body, accountable to that body, formed in a tradition that has been working at this for two thousand years. The pastor's incentive structure, when functioning, rewards faithfulness to the gospel, care for the souls under his charge, and the slow formation of disciples over decades. The two authority structures are not the same. They are not even adjacent. They are different categories of authority, with different sources, different accountabilities, different time horizons, and different goods.

When a pastor adopts pundit mode, he has not added a function to his pastoral identity. He has substituted one category of authority for another. The substitution is the problem. The pundit-pastor is borrowing his authority from his audience rather than receiving it from Christ. The borrowing is invisible to him in most cases, because the audience is also Christian and is using Christian language. But the structural relationship has changed. The pundit-pastor needs his audience in a way the pastor does not need his congregation. The dependence has reversed.

## What gets lost

The first thing that gets lost is the local congregation as the primary site of ministry.

A pastor's congregation is, in the historic Christian understanding of the role, the people in front of him on Sunday morning. They are particular people, with particular sins and particular sufferings and particular hopes. The pastor is responsible for *them*, not for an abstract national audience. The pastor's sermon should land on the people who came to hear it, not on the followers who will see the clip on Tuesday. The pastoral visits should be made to actual hospital beds, not to virtual followers. The pastoral funerals should be performed for actual neighbors, not for content.

The pundit-pastor begins to think of his actual congregation as a small subset of his real audience. The real audience, in his mind, is the larger public his content reaches. The local congregation begins to feel limiting. The sermon begins to be written for the broader audience, with the local congregation as incidental beneficiaries. The local pastoral work — the hospital visits, the funerals, the small conversations after service — begins to feel like time taken away from the more important content production. The substitution has reached the sermon and is reaching the rest of the work.

The second thing that gets lost is the pastoral authority that depends on knowing people.

A pastor's authority on Sunday morning depends, in significant ways, on the congregation's recognition that he knows them. He has visited the dying. He has performed the funerals. He has counseled the marriages. He has watched the children grow up. The Sunday morning preaching is the public moment of a much larger relationship that has been built across decades, in private rooms, with particular people. The authority is earned in those private rooms. The authority is exercised on Sunday morning. The two are linked.

The pundit-pastor's authority is not earned in those rooms. The pundit-pastor's authority is earned by the consistency and sharpness of his takes. The pundit-pastor's followers do not need him to know them; they need him to articulate what they already think more clearly than they can articulate it themselves. The authority is performative rather than pastoral. The performative authority is fragile in ways the pastoral authority is not.

The third thing that gets lost is the pastoral imagination.

The pastor who is forming his congregation over decades is asking different questions than the pundit who is producing content for a national audience. The pastor is asking: *what does this specific person need to hear from scripture today, given the specific situation they are in, in language they will understand?* The pundit is asking: *what take will land best with my audience this week?* The two questions produce different sermons, different counseling, different writing. The pastoral imagination, when atrophied, leaves a pastor who can no longer think pastorally. He thinks instead in takes, in platforms, in audiences. The local congregation, which used to occupy his imagination, now occupies the margins.

## How to tell if you are slipping

I want to offer diagnostic questions, because the substitution is gradual and most pastors who have made it do not know they have. Five questions. Ask them honestly. The honest answers will tell you where you actually are.

When was the last time you visited a member of your congregation in the hospital? Not the elder you were already meeting with for other reasons. A regular member. Someone whose name you have to look up in the database.

When was the last time you preached a sermon on something that was not in the news cycle of the week you wrote it?

When was the last time someone in your congregation told you something embarrassing about themselves, in a private setting, with the expectation that you would hold the confidence?

When was the last time you spent an unhurried afternoon with one of your own children, without producing content about it afterward?

When was the last time you wrote a sermon for the eleven people who would be in the third pew, rather than for the twenty thousand who would see the clip?

If the honest answers are *I do not remember* or *not recently*, you have slipped further than you have noticed. You can still come back. The path back is the path of the original work — quiet visits, slow conversations, the funeral of someone whose family does not subscribe to your podcast, the wedding of two people who have no idea what your Twitter bio says. The path is unglamorous. The path is the work. The path is what pastoral authority has always been built from.

## The verdict

The pastor who has stopped being a pastor and become a pundit cannot return easily. The path back is the path of the original work, slowly and painfully restored, often with congregants and friends who have been hurt by the substitution along the way. Most of the pastor-pundits I know are not coming back. The path is not closed. The path is just hard, and they have grown used to a different kind of reward.

I am writing this for the pastor who is still inside the work and is feeling the pull of the platform. I want to name what the pull is, what it costs, and what is at stake in the resisting of it. The resistance is countercultural in our generation. The resistance is also pastorally necessary. The church that has lost its pastors to punditry has lost something difficult to recover, and the recovery starts one pastor at a time, with one honest accounting of where the substitution has already happened.

Visit someone in the hospital this week. Without filming it. Without writing about it. Without telling anyone. Sit with them. Listen. Pray. Go home. That is the work. The work is what pastoral authority has always been built from. There is no shortcut. The shortcut is the punditry, and the punditry has been emptying our churches of the pastors they actually need.
