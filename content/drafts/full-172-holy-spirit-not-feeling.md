The pneumatology we have actually practiced — as opposed to the one in our doctrinal statements — runs something like this: the Holy Spirit is the part of Christianity that feels good. The Spirit is the warmth in worship, the sense of presence in prayer, the emotional uplift that distinguishes a spiritual experience from a merely intellectual one. When people talk about being "moved by the Spirit," they mean moved emotionally. When a worship service is described as "Spirit-filled," the evidence is usually the intensity of the feeling in the room.

This is not entirely wrong. It is dangerously incomplete.

The Spirit in the New Testament is not primarily a source of pleasant religious experience. The Spirit is the active, working presence of God in the world, accomplishing specific things that could not otherwise be accomplished — convicting the world of sin and righteousness and judgment (John 16:8), producing in believers the character of Christ (Galatians 5:22-23), interceding with sighs too deep for words (Romans 8:26), distributing gifts for the building of the body (1 Corinthians 12), guiding the church into truth (John 16:13). These are not emotional states. They are actions. The Spirit acts.

## The reduction and its cost

The reduction of the Spirit to emotional experience has a history. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Protestant evangelicalism was shaped by a series of revivals — the First Great Awakening in the American colonies, the Methodist movement in England — that put enormous weight on the felt experience of conversion. [cite — confirm] The warmth that John Wesley described feeling at Aldersgate, the trembling and crying that Jonathan Edwards documented and cautiously defended in his account of the Northampton revival — these were evidence, in the revivalist imagination, that the Spirit had genuinely moved.

The evidential function of emotion created a problem that subsequent generations have had to manage: what happens when the emotion fades? If the Spirit's presence is confirmed by the feeling, then the absence of the feeling suggests the absence of the Spirit. This produces a spirituality that is constantly chasing the next experience, constantly measuring the health of one's faith by the intensity of one's feelings. It produces what C.S. Lewis called, somewhere in his letters [cite — confirm], the error of treating feeling as the measure of reality rather than as an unreliable servant of it.

Paul's account of his own experience includes affliction, confusion, persecution, and abandonment by collaborators he had trusted (2 Corinthians 4, 2 Timothy 4). He does not describe these as periods when the Spirit was absent. He describes them as periods of extraordinary spiritual formation in which the life of Jesus was made manifest in his mortal body. The formation was not comfortable. The Spirit was present through the discomfort, not despite it.

## What the Spirit actually does

The fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23 — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control — is largely not emotional. Joy is the closest to an emotional state, and even joy in the New Testament (chara) is closer to settled confidence in God's goodness than to subjective exhilaration. Patience is the capacity to endure what is difficult without being destroyed by it. Faithfulness is reliable consistency in time. These are character qualities — durable orientations of the will and the habitual self — not feelings that come and go with the quality of last Sunday's worship set.

The formation of these qualities is the long, slow work of the Spirit in a person's life, and it looks almost nothing like what we describe when we say someone is "Spirit-filled." The most Spirit-formed people I know are not the most emotionally expressive people in the room. They are often the quietest — the ones whose patience in difficulty, whose consistency in faithfulness, whose capacity to love people who are difficult to love, marks them as something other than what the surrounding culture produces on its own.

That is pneumatological evidence. It is just not the evidence we have been trained to look for.

## What this means for the church

A church that has reduced the Spirit to feeling will inevitably evaluate its own spiritual health by metrics of emotional intensity — by whether people are crying during worship, whether the services feel alive, whether there is a quality of atmosphere in the room that suggests something beyond the ordinary. These are not nothing. But they are not the primary evidence.

The primary evidence is transformation. The Spirit was given, Peter announces on Pentecost (Acts 2:38), for the forgiveness of sins and the gift of the Spirit — a gift inseparable from a life that turns in a new direction. The new direction is the evidence. Not the tongues in the upper room, extraordinary as those were. What happens after the tongues. The community of Acts 2:42-47 that shared possessions, that devoted itself to the apostles' teaching, that cared for its poor and welcomed strangers — that is the pneumatological community. The Spirit produced it. It would not have happened otherwise.

The Spirit is not a feeling. The Spirit is the power of the age to come invading the present age, making possible in human beings what is otherwise simply not possible — love for enemies, patience in affliction, generosity in scarcity, faithfulness in obscurity. That is not an emotional state. That is the life of God.

We should want that more than we want to feel good. They are not the same thing.
