It's seven-thirty in the evening, and a family is trying to pray together. The two-year-old wants to hold the Bible, then throw it, then climb into the lap of whoever is reading. The seven-year-old has a question about whether Jonah could breathe inside the fish. The fifteen-year-old is present in body and elsewhere in mind, visibly enduring a devotional book written for children half his age. The parents can feel the whole thing tilting. If they pitch it to the toddler, the teenager checks out. If they pitch it to the teenager, the toddler melts down in ninety seconds. Most nights they give up, and many families quietly stop trying, or split the children up and do separate, age-appropriate devotions with each, which takes three times as long and happens a third as often.

Parents searching for how to do family worship with children of different ages usually assume the problem is a matter of finding the right material: a book that somehow works for everyone, a curriculum designed for a wide spread. There are good resources, and some help. But the difficulty runs deeper than material. It lies in an assumption most of us hold without knowing it, one that shapes nearly everything the modern church does with children: that worship must be pitched at a person's level of understanding in order to form him. (The case for household worship itself, from Deuteronomy 6 to the catechisms, is made in [How to Do Family Devotions and Teach Your Kids to Pray](/writing/family-devotions-authentic). This essay takes up the narrower problem of the age spread.)

The distinction this essay turns on is between worship pitched at a child and worship a child grows into. Pitched worship is designed around what the child can grasp right now. It is sorted by age, simplified for comprehension, and measured by attention. Worship a child grows into is designed around what is true and good, and it gives the child a place in it before he understands it, trusting that understanding will come. The first treats the child as an audience to be reached. The second treats him as a member to be included. Scripture, for the most part, does the second, and that opens a way through the problem of the toddler and the teenager at the same table.

## How worship got sorted by age

The habit of dividing people by age for religious instruction is recent, and it grew from real insights. In the nineteenth century, schools began sorting children into grades by age, and the church's instruction followed, with graded curricula and classes. In the twentieth century, developmental psychology described childhood as a sequence of stages, each with its own capacities. It became common sense that a four-year-old and a fourteen-year-old should be taught differently, and in important respects they should. The modern church responded with nurseries, children's church, junior high and senior high ministries, each tailored to its age.

Much of this was wise. Children do have different capacities at different ages, and a sermon aimed at adults can pass over a small child entirely. Some of the best work on children's spirituality has come from taking those differences seriously. Sofia Cavalletti, an Italian Hebrew scholar who began working with young children in Rome in 1954 alongside the Montessori educator Gianna Gobbi, developed what became the Catechesis of the Good Shepherd. In *The Religious Potential of the Child* (1979), she described how children between about three and six responded with deep and quiet joy to the parable of the Good Shepherd, the image of a shepherd who knows each sheep by name. She argued that young children have a real capacity for relationship with God, not a lesser version of adult faith, and that they need an environment suited to them. Her work is a powerful case for taking children's particular needs seriously.

But the age-sorting had a cost that no one intended. Families began to assume that faith could only be shared with a child in the form designed for his age, and that worship across ages was a kind of compromise, too simple for the older ones and too hard for the younger. Those of us who lead churches built much of that arrangement, and I include myself. Without meaning to, we taught parents that real formation happened down the hall, in a room sorted by birth year. Holly Catterton Allen and Christine Lawton Ross, in *Intergenerational Christian Formation* (2012), traced how thoroughly American churches had separated the generations and argued that something important was lost when children rarely worshiped with adults and adults rarely knew children. Their case was not against age-specific teaching but against a church in which the generations never met.

Kara Powell and Chap Clark, in *Sticky Faith* (2011), drew on research at the Fuller Youth Institute following young people from high school into college, and reported that students who had experienced worship and relationships across generations tended to carry a more durable faith into adulthood. They urged churches to surround each young person with several adults who knew them by name. The household is the first place where that can happen. A home where the toddler and the teenager worship together, with parents and sometimes grandparents, is already an intergenerational congregation. The question is whether we treat that as a problem or a gift.

## Little ones in the assembly

Scripture assumes, again and again, that children are present when God's people gather, and that their presence matters even when their understanding is small.

When Moses gave instructions for the reading of the law every seventh year, he specified who should come. "Assemble the people, men, women, and little ones, and the sojourner within your towns, that they may hear and learn to fear the LORD your God, and be careful to do all the words of this law, and that their children, who have not known it, may hear and learn to fear the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 31:12–13). The little ones are in the assembly. The purpose is that children "who have not known it" may hear and learn. They were not sent to a separate program. They stood with everyone else, hearing words they could not yet fully grasp, and learning to fear God by watching their parents fear him.

When Ezra read the law after the return from exile, the assembly included "both men and women and all who could understand what they heard," and he read "from early morning until midday" (Nehemiah 8:2–3). There's an interesting note here: the phrase "all who could understand" suggests that children old enough to follow were included, and that the ability to understand was valued. Both instincts are present. Scripture doesn't despise comprehension. But it doesn't make comprehension the price of admission either. When the prophet Joel called Israel to repentance, the summons went out to everyone: "gather the people. Consecrate the congregation; assemble the elders; gather the children, even nursing infants" (Joel 2:16). Nursing infants understand nothing of repentance. They were gathered anyway, because they belonged to the people who were repenting.

The New Testament keeps children in the room. Paul's letters were written to be read aloud to the gathered church (Colossians 4:16), and in two of them he addresses children directly: "Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right" (Ephesians 6:1). He assumed they were listening. When the children in the temple cried out their hosannas to the Son of David, the chief priests were indignant, and Jesus answered by quoting a psalm: "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise" (Matthew 21:15–16). When his disciples tried to keep children away, "he was indignant and said to them, 'Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God'" (Mark 10:14).

There is even a young man in the book of Acts, one Luke also calls a boy, and his story will be familiar to any parent of a teenager. Paul was preaching late into the night at Troas, and "a young man named Eutychus, sitting at the window, sank into a deep sleep as Paul talked still longer" (Acts 20:9). He fell from the third story and was taken up dead, and Paul went down, embraced him and restored him. Then Paul went back upstairs, broke bread and kept talking until daybreak. "And they took the youth away alive, and were not a little comforted" (Acts 20:12). The church at Troas had a young man drifting off during a long service. Nobody suggested he'd be better off in a separate youth meeting. He was part of the assembly, and when he fell, the whole assembly stopped for him.

And there is Samuel, the boy who served in the house of the LORD under the old priest Eli, in a time when "the word of the LORD was rare" (1 Samuel 3:1). When a voice called Samuel in the night, the boy didn't recognize it. It was Eli, old, half-blind and a failure as a father to his own sons, who understood that God was calling the child and taught him what to say: "Speak, LORD, for your servant hears" (1 Samuel 3:9). The old man and the boy, in the same house of worship, each needing the other. That is a picture of worship across generations, and it isn't tidy. Eli had failed badly. But he knew enough to help a child answer God.

## Words big enough to grow into

If children are to worship with adults, they need forms of worship that can hold every age at once, and the church has always had them, though we have often forgotten them. The most important is the Psalms.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote *Life Together* (1939) out of his years leading an illegal seminary of the Confessing Church in Nazi Germany, which the Gestapo closed in 1937. In it he describes the day of a Christian household or community beginning with shared worship, and the heart of that worship is the Psalter prayed together, along with a substantial reading of Scripture, a hymn sung in unison and prayer. He argued that the Psalms are the prayer of the whole church and, above all, the prayer of Christ himself, who prayed them on earth and prays them still in his people. The next year, in a short book called *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* (1940), he compared learning to pray to a child learning to speak: a child learns to talk because his father speaks to him, and we learn to pray because God has spoken to us first and given us his own words to say back to him.

That image is the key to family worship across ages. A toddler learns to speak by being surrounded by a language far richer than anything he can produce. No one waits until he understands grammar to talk to him. He hears full sentences, stories and songs long before he can reply, and slowly he grows into the language. The Psalms work the same way. A two-year-old can't understand Psalm 23, but he can hear it every night until its rhythm is part of him, and one day he'll realize he knows it by heart. A fifteen-year-old can understand the Psalms far better than a children's devotional, and he will find in them things no children's book would dare to say: anger at God, despair, doubt, fear of enemies, the sense of God's absence. "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1). The same psalm serves both children, at different depths.

Thomas Cranmer understood this when he composed *The Book of Common Prayer* (1549). His preface set out an order in which the Psalter would be read through every month, and the whole of Scripture over the course of the year, in the language of the people, so that ordinary Christians would be steeped in it. Fixed words, repeated daily, are one of the great gifts to a family with children of different ages. The Lord's Prayer, the Apostles' Creed, a doxology, a psalm prayed often enough to be known by heart: these are forms that a three-year-old can join in and a seventeen-year-old can mean. The doxology sung in many churches, "Praise God from whom all blessings flow," was written in the seventeenth century by Thomas Ken for the scholars of Winchester College, that is, for teenage boys. Toddlers sing it now.

There's a story in Augustine's *Confessions* that makes the point in another way. In a garden in Milan in 386, Augustine was in anguish over his inability to give his life to God. He heard a child's voice from a nearby house, chanting over and over, "Take up and read." He couldn't remember any children's game with those words. He took it as a command, opened the letters of Paul, and read the passage that ended his long resistance. God used a child's sing-song to speak to one of the greatest minds in the history of the church. Children are not only receivers of worship. Their voices are part of it.

## Giving every age a part

In practice, worship with a wide range of ages works best when it is short, regular and shared, and when every person, from the smallest to the oldest, has something to do.

The toddler's part is mostly bodily. He can light a candle with help, or blow it out at the end. He can hold the Bible, hand out the song sheets, stand for the reading, fold his hands, say "Amen." He can sing the parts of a song he knows. Small children participate through their bodies long before they participate through their minds, and a family that gives them a physical role in worship includes them in it far more than one that asks them to sit still and listen.

The grade-school child's part is often the question. Children between about six and ten are natural askers, and a family can build worship around their questions. After a short reading from Scripture, the parent can simply ask what they noticed, or what they wondered about. The seven-year-old's question about whether Jonah could breathe inside the fish isn't a distraction. It's the beginning of the kind of attention Scripture deserves. Children of this age can also memorize readily, and a psalm or a catechism answer learned now will stay with them for decades.

The teenager's part is leadership. A fifteen-year-old who is asked to endure a children's devotional will resent it. A fifteen-year-old who is asked to read the Scripture, choose the psalm, lead the prayer, or explain a passage to his younger siblings is being treated as a member of the household's worship, not a child being managed. Teenagers often rise to what is asked of them. Asking one to lead worship, even once a week, tells him that his parents see him as someone who can carry the faith, not merely receive it. And the younger children watch. A toddler who sees his older brother reading Scripture aloud is learning something no parent can teach him as well.

The parents' part is to hold it all together without making it a performance. Family worship doesn't need to be long. Ten minutes, even five, kept regularly, will form a family more than an hour kept occasionally. It doesn't need to be polished. It will be interrupted, often. The toddler will wander off. The teenager will sigh. Someone will laugh at the wrong moment. None of that means it has failed. It means it is happening in a real household. A reasonable pattern might be a candle lit, a psalm read or sung, a short passage of Scripture with a question or two, a prayer that gathers up the day and names people in need, the Lord's Prayer together and a blessing. Families will adapt it endlessly, and should.

## When the teenager won't come

Every family with a teenager will eventually face the evening when the teenager refuses. He has homework, or friends, or simply no interest. He may say that it's for little kids, or that he doesn't believe it anymore. This is the hardest part of family worship across ages, and the options deserve careful thought.

One view holds that parents should insist. The household worships together, and every member participates. Scripture calls children to obey their parents, and a teenager who is allowed to opt out of family worship learns that faith is optional. There's truth here. Children who are excused from everything they dislike grow into adults who can't sustain any commitment. And a family that abandons worship the moment a teenager objects has taught him that it was only ever for the little ones.

The other view holds that coercion breeds resentment, and that a teenager dragged to worship will associate God with compulsion. Faith cannot be forced, and a young person's growing autonomy is part of God's design. Pressing too hard may drive him away altogether. There's truth here too. Many adults who left the faith trace it partly to a childhood of forced religious performance.

Many families find a path between them, and neither of the first two views is foolish. They can expect a teenager to be present, at the table, as a member of the family, while not demanding that he perform a faith he doesn't feel. They can invite him to lead rather than requiring him to follow. They can ask for his input on what the family reads or sings. They can make room for his doubts, and even bring them into the worship: the Psalms are full of complaint, and a teenager who is angry at God may find that the Bible has already given him words for it. And the parents can keep worshiping whether he joins or not, because their own faith doesn't depend on his participation. A teenager who watches his parents continue to pray, with or without him, learns that their faith is real.

It's also true that a teenager's withdrawal is sometimes about more than faith. Adolescence can bring depression, anxiety and struggles a family cannot see. If a teenager's refusal comes with a darker change, withdrawal from friends, hopelessness, talk of not wanting to be alive, that is a matter for help, not argument. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text, and a doctor or counselor should be involved. A family's worship can hold a hurting child, but it doesn't replace care.

And when the teenager drifts, the story of Eutychus is worth remembering. He fell asleep during the service and fell out of the window. The church didn't lecture him. It stopped everything, went down to him and brought him back. Then it went on worshiping, with him in the room.

## A multitude no one could number

The psalmist pictured the whole creation praising God, and when he came to human beings, he named every age: "Young men and maidens together, old men and children! Let them praise the name of the LORD, for his name alone is exalted; his majesty is above earth and heaven" (Psalm 148:12–13). The praise of God is not sorted by age. It is a chorus in which the old and the young sing together, each voice needed.

The final picture of worship in Scripture is the same. John saw "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). There is no children's church in that vision, no separate youth service. The great assembly of God's people is every nation and every generation gathered together. Family worship across ages is a small rehearsal of that assembly. The toddler throwing the Bible and the teenager sighing at the table are practicing, whether they know it or not, for the day when they will stand together before the throne.

"One generation shall commend your works to another, and shall declare your mighty acts" (Psalm 145:4). That happens in a household when the generations are in the same room, the older declaring God's works to the younger and the younger, sometimes, reminding the older why it matters. It happens imperfectly, in ten-minute pieces, with interruptions. It happens on the nights when no one seems to be paying attention.

The family at seven-thirty in the evening doesn't need a better book. It needs to believe that the two-year-old and the fifteen-year-old both belong in the room, that the words of Scripture are big enough for both of them, and that God is at work in the worship of a household even when it looks like chaos. The children will grow. The words will wait for them. The question is whether, when they are grown, they will remember a home where everyone, from the smallest to the oldest, had a place before God.
