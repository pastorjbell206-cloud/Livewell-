Imagine a fifteen-year-old boy who has been, in the space of three years, a serious baseball player, a gamer who barely left his room, a kid who wore only black, a youth group regular with a Bible full of underlining, and now, apparently, a runner who talks about little besides his mile time. His parents are not sure which of these was the real one. Neither is he. At dinner he's funny and warm. By the time the dishes are done he's behind his bedroom door, and his mother can hear him on a call with someone, sounding like a person she has never met. She doesn't worry that he's in trouble, exactly. She worries that he's tired in a way a fifteen-year-old shouldn't be, as though being someone has become a job he has to keep performing and can't quite afford to stop.

Parents of teenagers usually hear the question behind that bedroom door without being told what it is. It's the oldest question of adolescence: who am I? What most of us don't hear, because we've been breathing the same air as our children, is that the culture has already handed them a particular way of answering it, and that the answer is heavier than any previous generation of teenagers was asked to carry. This essay is about that weight, where it came from, what the church has sometimes done to make it worse, and the older question that can make it bearable. It isn't about doubt, or phones, or gender, though each of those touches it and each has its own essay on this site. It's about the ordinary work every adolescent has to do, and about what a parent can give a child who is trying to do it.

## How adolescence became its own country

It helps to remember that the long, distinct stage of life we call adolescence is younger than we think. For most of history, children moved into adult work and adult responsibility in their early or middle teens, usually alongside the adults they would eventually replace. Apprentices lived with their masters. Farm children did farm work. There were young people, of course, and they were restless, but they were not a separate population with a separate culture.

That began to change around the turn of the twentieth century. In 1904 the psychologist G. Stanley Hall published a massive two-volume work titled *Adolescence*, which popularized the idea of the teenage years as a time of "storm and stress," a turbulent passage requiring special protection and special treatment. At nearly the same time, American schooling was transformed. Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz, in *The Race Between Education and Technology* (2008), describe what they call the high school movement: between roughly 1910 and 1940, secondary school went from being the experience of a minority of American young people to being the experience of most of them. For the first time, millions of teenagers spent their days mostly in the company of other teenagers, sorted by age and away from adult work. Thomas Hine traces the result in *The Rise and Fall of the American Teenager* (1999). By the 1940s the word *teenager* had come into common use, and by the 1950s there was a teenage market, teenage music and a teenage culture that adults could observe but not enter.

The psychologist who gave this new country its map was Erik Erikson. In *Childhood and Society* (1950) he described a sequence of developmental tasks across the whole of life, and he assigned to adolescence the task of forming an identity as opposed to remaining in what he called role confusion. In *Identity: Youth and Crisis* (1968) he gathered his work around a phrase the culture has used ever since: the identity crisis. Erikson thought adolescents needed what he called a psychosocial moratorium, a protected period in which they could try on roles and commitments before settling into adult ones. It was a humane idea, and it described something real. But it also named, and in some ways licensed, a new expectation: that each young person would have to discover or construct who they were, rather than simply growing into who they had always been told they were.

## Choosing a self

That expectation grew heavier as the century went on, because the culture around teenagers changed its understanding of what a self is.

Robert Bellah and his colleagues, interviewing Americans for *Habits of the Heart* (1985), gave a name to what they found: expressive individualism, the conviction that each person has a unique core of feeling and intuition that must be expressed if life is to be meaningful. Their most famous interviewee was a young nurse named Sheila Larson, who described her faith as her own little voice and called it "Sheilaism." Bellah's team saw that this was not a fringe view. It had become the default grammar of the American self. Charles Taylor, in *The Malaise of Modernity* (1991), gave the same development a sympathetic but searching reading. He argued that the modern ideal of being true to oneself is a genuine moral achievement, not mere selfishness, but that it collapses when it is cut loose from what he called horizons of significance, the background of things that matter regardless of what I choose. A self that chooses everything has nothing to choose against, and its choices mean less and less.

In a later essay, "The Politics of Recognition" (1992), Taylor made a point that parents of teenagers will recognize instantly. Identity, he argued, is never formed alone. It is worked out in dialogue with others, and it depends on their recognition. We come to know who we are partly by seeing ourselves reflected in the eyes of people who matter to us. When recognition is withheld, or when it is given in a distorted form, a person can be genuinely harmed. That is why a teenager's sense of self is so fragile. The expressive ideal tells him to find his true self within, but his actual experience is that he doesn't feel real until someone sees him. And in a world where the old sources of recognition, the family, the town, the congregation, the trade, have thinned, much of the seeing is done by peers, and increasingly by a screen full of strangers who are always watching and never satisfied.

It is important to be fair to the modern self before criticizing it. The refusal to be simply assigned an identity by others has real moral force. For centuries, identities were imposed by class, sex, race and caste in ways that crushed people, and many of those crushed were crushed in the name of God. A teenager who says "I will not simply be what you've decided I am" is sometimes saying something true and brave. The problem is not that the modern self wants to be free. The problem is what freedom has come to mean, which is the obligation to generate your own meaning from scratch and then to keep proving it to a watching audience. That is not liberation. It is a full-time job, and the fifteen-year-old behind the bedroom door is working it.

## Foreclosure and the church's temptation

If the culture's temptation is to hand teenagers an impossible task, the church's temptation is to spare them the task altogether, and that turns out to be its own kind of failure.

In 1966 a psychologist named James Marcia, building on Erikson, published a study that sorted young people into four identity statuses according to two questions: have they explored alternatives, and have they committed to something? Those who had done neither he described as diffused, drifting without direction. Those who were exploring but not yet committed were in moratorium, Erikson's protected season of searching. Those who had explored and then committed he called achieved. And those who had committed without ever exploring, usually by adopting their parents' values and plans whole, he called foreclosed. Foreclosed young people often looked like the most stable of the four. They were confident, settled and cooperative. But their identities had never been tested, and an identity that has never been tested can prove brittle when life finally presses on it.

Christian parents and churches have a strong temptation toward foreclosure, and it usually comes from love. We know what we want our children to believe and who we want them to be, and the idea of a moratorium, a season in which they might try on other possibilities, frightens us. So we answer every question before they can ask it, we manage their environments, and we measure our success by how smoothly they affirm what we affirm. Christian Smith and Melinda Lundquist Denton, surveying American teenagers for *Soul Searching* (2005), found that the faith most of them actually held, across denominations, was not historic Christianity but something they called moralistic therapeutic deism: a God who exists, wants people to be nice, wants them to feel good about themselves, and mostly stays out of the way unless needed. Smith and Denton also found that parents were the most important influence on their children's religious lives. The teenagers had learned their parents' faith very well. The trouble was what that faith had become. Kenda Creasy Dean drew the conclusion in *Almost Christian* (2010): the church had not failed to transmit its faith to teenagers. It had succeeded in transmitting a thin one.

A foreclosed identity and a thin faith often go together. A teenager who has inherited Christianity as a set of family rules and cultural habits, without ever having to ask what it's for, will usually hold it until the first real pressure and then find nothing underneath. That is a different problem from the teenager who loses faith after asking hard questions, which this site takes up in [When Your Teenager Says They Don't Believe Anymore](/writing/teenager-losing-faith). It is the quieter problem of the teenager whose faith was never tested because nobody let it be. The answer is not to push children into doubt. It is to recognize that a faith worth having can bear being examined, and that a teenager who asks "why do we believe this?" is not attacking the family but beginning to take it seriously.

That distinction is the hinge of the whole matter. There are three ways a young person can come to a Christian identity: by inventing it, by inheriting it without examination, or by receiving it. Invention is the culture's way, and it produces fragile selves who must keep performing. Unexamined inheritance is the church's temptation, and it produces brittle selves who crack under pressure. Reception is different from both. It means being given something by others that you did not make, and then coming, over time and through testing, to own it as your own. It is what happens when a child grows up in a family that loves her and then, as a young adult, chooses that family again. It is also, according to the New Testament, how every Christian identity is formed.

## Whose am I

The Gospels tell us almost nothing about Jesus between his infancy and his baptism, with one exception, and it's a story about a twelve-year-old.

Luke tells us that when Jesus was twelve, his family went up to Jerusalem for Passover, as they did every year. On the way home, Mary and Joseph realized he wasn't in their traveling party. They searched for three days and found him in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening and asking questions. His mother said what any parent would say: his father and she had been searching for him in great distress. And Jesus answered, "Why were you looking for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?" (Luke 2:49). Luke adds that they didn't understand what he meant. Then comes the part that is easy to skip: "And he went down with them and came to Nazareth and was submissive to them. And his mother treasured up all these things in her heart. And Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:51-52).

The only adolescent scene in the Gospels is about the question of whose house a young person belongs in. And notice how Jesus answers it. He knows whose he is. His identity is grounded in his relationship to his Father, and that knowledge is so secure that he can sit among the teachers and ask questions without anxiety. And then, knowing whose he is, he goes home and submits to his parents and grows. The knowledge of his deepest belonging doesn't make him contemptuous of his earthly family. It gives him the freedom to be patient with them and to keep growing. About eighteen years later, before he has preached a sermon or healed anyone, he hears his identity spoken from heaven at his baptism: "You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11). The Father's pleasure comes before the work, not as its reward.

This is the inversion the gospel offers a teenager. The culture asks "who am I?" and expects the answer to be achieved by choosing and performing. Scripture asks a prior question: whose am I? "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 opens with the same claim. Asked what is our only comfort in life and in death, it answers, "that I am not my own," and goes on to say that I belong, body and soul, to my faithful Savior Jesus Christ. To a modern ear that sounds like a loss of freedom. To a tired fifteen-year-old, it may be the first good news he's heard in a year. If I am not my own, I don't have to make myself. I don't have to prove who I am to the people watching. I have already been claimed.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote a poem in his prison cell at Tegel in 1944 that has become one of the most honest things a Christian has ever written about identity. It's titled "Who Am I?" He describes how his guards and fellow prisoners see him: calm, cheerful, steady, stepping out of his cell like a squire from his country house. Then he describes how he feels inside: restless, longing, sick, like a caged bird struggling for breath. Which is the real one? he asks. Is he the man others see, or the one he knows himself to be? Or both at once? He doesn't answer. Instead, he ends the poem by handing the question to God: whoever I am, God knows me, and I am his. That is not a dodge. It's the only answer that can hold a self that is divided and unfinished. Bonhoeffer didn't need to resolve who he was, because he knew whose he was.

A man raised without a father learns early that "whose am I" is not an abstract question. Every child needs to belong to someone, and a child who doesn't know whose he is will spend a long time trying to earn a place with anyone who might claim him. That is true of every teenager, fathered or not. The difference the gospel makes is that the claim comes first. John wrote, "Beloved, we are God's children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). Notice the structure. Belonging is settled now. What we will be is not yet visible. That's the Christian version of Erikson's moratorium, and it's better than his, because it isn't a waiting room where you must find yourself before time runs out. It's a secure home in which you are free to grow into a self that God is still revealing. The book of Revelation even promises, to those who overcome, "a white stone, with a new name written on the stone that no one knows except the one who receives it" (Revelation 2:17). Our deepest identity is not something we invent or achieve. It's a name we will be given, and it will surprise us.

## What a parent can give

What does this mean for the parent standing outside the bedroom door? It doesn't mean giving the teenager a lecture on the Heidelberg Catechism, though there's nothing wrong with reading it together. It means something harder, which is becoming the kind of home in which a young person can hear the answer to "whose am I" before he's figured out "who am I."

It starts with the recognition Taylor wrote about. A teenager needs to be seen, and the first place he should be seen is at home. That means paying attention to who he actually is, rather than who we hoped he'd be or who we're afraid he's becoming. It means noticing the things he loves and asking about them with real curiosity. It means recognizing the child as a person in his own right, not a project whose outcome reflects on us. Here the charge comes home to parents. Many of us have quietly made our children's performance the evidence of our own worth. The grades, the scholarships, the athletic success, the visible faith, even the church involvement, become our trophies, and our teenagers feel it. They know when they're being recognized and when they're being displayed. A parent who needs his child to succeed in order to feel successful himself has handed the child the same exhausting task the culture hands him: perform, or you're nobody. The first repentance may be ours.

It continues with giving the teenager a story larger than himself. Taylor's horizons of significance are not abstractions. They're the family's history, the church's memory, the long story of God's people that stretches back to Abraham and forward to the new creation. A teenager who knows that he belongs to a people older than his generation, who has heard the stories of grandparents and saints, who has sat in a congregation where people of every age confess the same faith, has somewhere to stand while he asks his questions. This is why it matters that teenagers have real relationships with Christian adults who aren't their parents, and why a youth ministry that keeps teenagers entirely apart from the rest of the church can inadvertently reinforce the very separation Hall and Erikson described.

It includes the freedom to explore within belonging. A parent who knows that the child's deepest identity is secure in God can afford to let him try on different interests, friendships and even questions, without panicking at each one. The baseball player, the gamer, the runner may all be part of a young person's search for what he loves. The parent's job is not to prevent the search but to be the stable home he returns to, the way Jesus returned to Nazareth. That doesn't mean there are no limits. A home can and should set boundaries around what is harmful. But the aim is not a foreclosed child who never asked anything. It's a young adult who has asked and found that the faith he was given can hold his questions.

It means taking the teenager seriously as someone God can use now, not only later. Paul told the young Timothy, "Let no one despise you for your youth, but set the believers an example in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity" (1 Timothy 4:12). When Jeremiah protested that he was too young to be a prophet, God answered, "Do not say, 'I am only a youth'; for to all to whom I send you, you shall go, and whatever I command you, you shall speak" (Jeremiah 1:7). Teenagers are not adults-in-waiting. They are members of the body of Christ with gifts the church needs, and a teenager who has real responsibility in the life of a congregation learns who he is partly by learning what he's for.

And it requires watching for the moments when the search turns dark. Adolescence is a season when depression, anxiety and self-harm often surface, and a teenager who seems lost may sometimes be in real danger. If your child talks about not wanting to exist, withdraws from everyone, or shows signs of harming himself, don't wait for it to pass. Talk to him directly, and talk to his doctor or a counselor. If you think he may be at risk of suicide, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour, and if he is in immediate danger, call 911. This is not medical advice, and a parent shouldn't try to be his child's therapist. But a parent can make sure the child is never alone with that question.

The fifteen-year-old behind the bedroom door will probably be someone else by seventeen, and someone else again by twenty-five. That's not a failure. It's what growing up looks like. But underneath all the selves he tries on, he needs one thing that doesn't change, and it isn't a personality or an achievement. It's a belonging. The deepest gift a parent can give him is not a finished answer to "who am I?" It is the steady, repeated, embodied message of a home that says: you are ours, and more than that, you are God's, and nothing you become will change the second of those. We won't give that message perfectly, because we're still learning it ourselves. Most of us parents are still performing for someone, still waiting for a voice from heaven we have trouble believing was spoken over us. But it was. And the teenager who watches his parents rest in that, even imperfectly, may learn it before they did. He may find, at the end of his searching, that the self he was looking for had been held for him all along by the One who knew his name before he was born and will give him, in the end, a name he hasn't heard yet.
