Imagine a six-year-old at bedtime, three days after his grandmother died. The adults have been on the phone, crying in the kitchen when they think he can't hear, making arrangements. Someone told him that Grandma went to be with Jesus. Someone else said she's sleeping now. Tonight, as his father turns out the light, he asks the question he has clearly been working on all day: When is Grandma coming back? And then, before his father can answer, a second one: Are you going to die too?

If you are the parent in that doorway, you may be grieving yourself, maybe more deeply than you've ever grieved. You may have lost your own mother, or your spouse, or, most terribly, another child. And now you're being asked to explain death to someone who doesn't yet understand it, at the exact moment you can barely hold it together yourself. You want to protect him. You want to find words that won't frighten him. You may be tempted to say whatever will get him to sleep tonight.

This essay is for that doorway. It tries to say plainly what children understand about death at different ages, what they need from the adults around them, why honesty usually serves them better than the gentle evasions we reach for, and what the Christian faith offers a grieving child that is neither a lie nor a platitude. It also says, early and clearly, where grief in a child can turn into something more dangerous. If a child or teenager in your care, or you yourself, are having thoughts of suicide or of joining the person who died, don't wait. Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour. Nothing that follows is medical advice. It is written to help you be present to a grieving child, not to replace the help a doctor or counselor can give.

## How we learned to hide death from children

For most of human history, children saw death. People died at home, usually in the same rooms where they had lived. Children were present at deathbeds, helped wash and dress the body, sat with it through the night, walked behind it to the churchyard. In the nineteenth century, even in prosperous Western households, the death of a sibling or parent was a common childhood experience, and the rituals of mourning, the black clothing, the drawn curtains, the months of formal grief, made death visible to everyone. Children's books of the period were often frankly about dying. None of this was easy, and a good deal of it was frightening. But death was not a secret.

The twentieth century changed that. The French historian Philippe Ariès, in *Western Attitudes Toward Death* (1974), described what he called the forbidden death: the modern tendency to remove dying from the home to the hospital, to hand the body over to professionals, to shorten mourning and hide grief, and above all to keep death away from children. The English sociologist Geoffrey Gorer had made a similar observation in 1955, in a short essay titled "The Pornography of Death." He argued that death had replaced sex as the great unmentionable subject of modern life. The Victorians had been reticent about sex and frank about death. His own contemporaries were becoming frank about sex and reticent about death. Children, who had once been present at the deathbed, were now sent to a neighbor's house during the funeral.

The instinct behind all this is not cruel. It comes from love. Parents want to shield their children from pain, and it's natural to think that the less a child sees of death, the less he'll suffer. But the hiding came with costs that took decades to understand. Children who aren't told the truth don't stop wondering. They fill the silence with their own explanations, which are often more frightening than reality. Children who are sent away from funerals often feel excluded from the family's grief, and sometimes conclude that the death was their fault or that they weren't important enough to be included. And children who never see adults grieve learn that grief is something shameful, to be hidden.

The church has sometimes added its own layer of evasion. Well-meaning Christians tell grieving children that God needed another angel in heaven, or that God took Grandpa because he loved him so much. The first is simply false: in Scripture, people who die don't become angels. The second is worse, because it makes God into the thief who took the person the child loved, and a child who hears it may conclude that God is someone to be afraid of, or that being loved by God is dangerous. We say these things because we don't know what else to say, and because silence feels unbearable. But the children remember them.

## What children can understand

Children's understanding of death develops over time, and the research on it gives parents a rough map, though every child is different and grief often doesn't follow the map neatly.

In 1948 the Hungarian psychologist Maria Nagy published a study of how children in Budapest thought about death. She found that the youngest children, under about five, tended to see death as a kind of departure or sleep, something reversible. Children between about five and nine often personified death, imagining it as a figure who comes to take people away. Only around nine or ten, in her study, did most children understand death as something that happens to everyone, according to natural laws. Later researchers refined her picture. In 1984 Mark Speece and Sandor Brent reviewed the research and identified three components that children need to grasp to understand death: that it is irreversible, that it means the body stops working entirely, and that it is universal, coming to every living thing. They found that most children come to understand all three sometime between about five and seven, though the timing varies widely.

This helps explain the six-year-old's questions. When he asks when Grandma is coming back, he may not yet fully grasp that death is permanent. When he asks whether his father will die too, he is beginning to understand that death is universal, and it frightens him. Both questions deserve honest answers, given gently and at his level.

John Bowlby, whose work on attachment reshaped child psychology, turned in the third volume of his *Attachment and Loss* (1980) to the question of how children grieve. He argued, against those who thought young children were too young to mourn, that even small children do grieve their losses, and that they are more likely to grieve in healthy ways when they receive prompt and accurate information about what happened, are allowed to share in the family's grief, and have the continuing comfort of a trusted adult. His conclusions have held up remarkably well. The things children need most are not complicated. They need the truth. They need to be included. And they need someone to hold onto.

Children also grieve differently from adults. A child may cry intensely one minute and ask to go play outside the next. This doesn't mean he doesn't care or has already gotten over it. Children often can only bear grief in small doses, and they step in and out of it. Grief may come back at unexpected moments, months or years later, when a child reaches a new stage of understanding and has to grieve the loss again in a new way. A child who lost a parent at four may grieve that parent freshly at ten and again at sixteen, as he understands more fully what he lost. Teenagers may grieve more like adults but often hide it, especially from their parents, and may turn to friends or withdraw. A parent should expect grief to be uneven and long.

## Honest words for the hardest things

The first and most important practical counsel is to use real words. Say that the person died. Say that she is dead. The euphemisms we reach for, that she passed, that we lost her, that she's sleeping, that she went away, are meant to soften the blow, but for young children they often confuse and sometimes terrify. A child told that Grandma is sleeping may become afraid to go to sleep himself. A child told that we lost Grandpa may wonder why no one is looking for him. The plain words are kinder because they are clearer.

There is a complication here for Christians, because the New Testament itself uses the language of sleep for death. Paul writes to the Thessalonians about "those who are asleep" (1 Thessalonians 4:13), and Jesus says of Lazarus, "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I go to awaken him" (John 11:11). But when the disciples misunderstand, John tells us, "Then Jesus told them plainly, 'Lazarus has died'" (John 11:14). The biblical language of sleep is not a euphemism to avoid the word death. It is a resurrection word, a claim that death is not the end, because those who sleep will wake. The early Christians called their burial grounds by a word meaning sleeping place, from which we get the word cemetery. A parent can use that language with an older child, as long as it's explained: Grandma died, her body stopped working and won't work again, and we believe that God will one day raise her up, the way you wake up from sleep. That's different from telling a four-year-old that Grandma is sleeping and leaving him to lie awake wondering.

Children will ask hard questions, and they deserve honest answers. When a child asks, "Will you die too?" the honest answer is yes, someday, but that you expect to live a long time and take care of him until he's grown, and that if anything ever happened to you, there are people who would take care of him. That's truthful without being frightening. When a child asks whether the death was his fault, and many children secretly wonder this, especially young ones who believe their thoughts or behavior can cause things to happen, the answer needs to be clear and repeated: nothing you did or thought or said caused this.

Some children will also ask whether the person who died is with Jesus, and not every family can answer that with the same confidence. Honesty applies here too. A parent can say what he does know, that God knew and loved this person more fully than any of us did and that God is both just and merciful, without claiming to know what he does not.

The hardest cases are deaths by suicide, by violence, or by overdose. Parents often want to hide the cause, and the instinct is understandable. But children usually find out eventually, from a cousin, from a classmate, from the internet, and finding out later that the adults lied can compound the grief with a sense of betrayal. Most people who work with grieving children advise telling the truth in age-appropriate terms. For a young child, that might mean saying that the person had a sickness in how their mind was working, one that made them feel very sad and confused, and that because of it they ended their life. For an older child or teenager, it means more direct honesty. What children don't need are the details of how it happened. And they need to hear, clearly, that it was not their fault, that the person was loved, and that if they ever have thoughts like that, they can always tell you and you will help them.

Children should generally be allowed to attend funerals if they want to, with preparation. Tell them beforehand what they'll see: the casket, the body if there is a viewing, people crying. Give them a trusted adult to sit with who can take them out if they need a break. Let them participate in small ways, drawing a picture to put in the casket or choosing a flower. The writer of Ecclesiastes says, "It is better to go to the house of mourning than to go to the house of feasting, for this is the end of all mankind, and the living will lay it to heart" (Ecclesiastes 7:2). Children need to be in the house of mourning too. It's part of how they learn that grief is shared, that death is real, and that the community of faith gathers around the grieving.

## Jesus wept, and children are watching

Children learn how to grieve largely by watching the adults around them. That puts a particular burden on grieving parents, and it's one of the reasons it matters what the church has taught us about grief.

C. S. Lewis, after his wife Joy Davidman died in 1960, wrote a raw journal of his grief that was published as *A Grief Observed* (1961). In it he recorded that he couldn't talk to his stepsons about their mother. Whenever he tried, he wrote, what appeared on their faces was not grief or love or fear or pity but "the most fatal of all non-conductors, embarrassment." It's a devastating observation, and an honest one. A grieving adult and a grieving child can end up hiding their grief from each other, each trying to protect the other, each alone. Lewis was one of the most gifted Christian writers of his century, and he couldn't find a way to break that silence. It's no shame if we struggle too. But the silence is worth trying to break.

The Gospels give us a picture of a different way. When Jesus came to the tomb of his friend Lazarus, he found Lazarus's sisters and their friends weeping. "When Jesus saw her weeping, and the Jews who had come with her also weeping, he was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled" (John 11:33). Then comes the shortest verse in the Bible: "Jesus wept" (John 11:35). Jesus knew he was about to raise Lazarus from the dead. He wept anyway. The hope of resurrection did not make grief unnecessary. It made it bearable, and it gave it a direction. A parent who weeps in front of a child, and then holds the child and talks about the person who died and about the hope of seeing them again, is teaching the child that grief and faith belong together.

Paul says the same thing to the Thessalonians. "But we do not want you to be uninformed, brothers, about those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). Notice what Paul doesn't say. He doesn't say that Christians shouldn't grieve. He says they grieve differently, with hope. The church has sometimes misread this verse as though Christian faith should make grief unnecessary or brief, and grieving people have been made to feel that their tears were a failure of faith. That was never Paul's point. Children especially need to hear that it's all right to be sad, that crying for someone you love is not a lack of faith, that Jesus cried too.

The Old Testament gives grieving people even more room. When King David's infant son died, he stopped his fasting and said something that has comforted grieving parents for three thousand years: "But now he is dead. Why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he will not return to me" (2 Samuel 12:23). The prophet Jeremiah pictured Rachel weeping for her children, and Matthew quotes it at the slaughter of the children of Bethlehem: "A voice was heard in Ramah, weeping and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted, because they are no more" (Matthew 2:18). Scripture does not rush the grieving to comfort. It sits with them.

Nicholas Wolterstorff, the philosopher, whose twenty-five-year-old son Eric died in a climbing accident in 1983, wrote in *Lament for a Son* (1987) that he would not try to explain the death, and that he found the easy explanations offered by well-meaning Christians more wounding than helpful. What he found instead was a God who suffers with those who suffer, and a hope that did not require him to pretend his son's death was good. That's the right posture for a parent explaining death to a child. We don't have to explain why it happened. We shouldn't say God did it because he wanted the person more than we did. We can say what the Bible says: that death is an enemy, "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26), that God hates it, that Jesus wept at it, and that Jesus has defeated it and will one day undo it entirely.

## When grief turns dark

Most children, with honest information and loving adults around them, will grieve and gradually find their footing again, even though the loss will stay with them. But some children and teenagers develop grief that becomes something more serious, and parents need to know the signs.

Watch for a child who can't function for an extended period: who stops eating or sleeping, who can't go to school, who withdraws from friends and activities for months rather than weeks. Watch for a child who seems stuck, unable to move forward at all. Watch for guilt that doesn't respond to reassurance. Watch especially for a child who talks about wanting to die, about wanting to be with the person who died, or who seems to have lost the will to live. Teenagers who lose a family member are at particular risk, especially if the death was by suicide. In 2022 the American Psychiatric Association added prolonged grief disorder to its diagnostic manual, recognizing that for some people, including children, grief can become persistent and disabling in a way that needs professional care.

If you see these signs, talk to your child's pediatrician, and look for a counselor who specializes in children's grief. Many communities have grief support groups for children, where they can meet other children who have lost someone and realize they aren't alone. The Dougy Center in Portland, Oregon, which has worked with grieving children since 1982, offers resources for parents and a directory of programs around the country. And if a child or teenager ever speaks of wanting to die, take it seriously every time. Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, and if the child is in immediate danger, call 911 or take him to an emergency room. Asking a child directly whether he's thinking of hurting himself will not put the idea in his head. It may be the question that saves him.

Grieving parents need care too. A parent who is drowning in grief can't carry a child through it alone, and there's no shame in asking for help, from a counselor, a pastor, a support group, friends and family. An adult whose own grief has not lifted after many months may find company in this site's essay on [grief that doesn't go away](/writing/the-weight-that-stays). The church can be a place where grieving families are held, not only in the week of the funeral but in the months and years that follow. That means remembering the anniversaries. It means saying the name of the person who died, rather than avoiding it for fear of causing pain. It means that the families who have lost someone are not left alone after the casseroles stop coming.

## The last enemy

Go back to the six-year-old at bedtime. What can his father say that is both true and bearable?

He can say that Grandma died, that her body stopped working and she won't come back to live with us here. He can say that he's very sad, and that it's all right to be sad, and that Jesus was sad when his friend died too. He can say that he isn't planning to die for a long, long time, and that there will always be people to take care of him. He can say that nothing the boy did made this happen. And he can say what Christians have always said at gravesides: that we believe Jesus died and rose again, that death is not the end, and that God will one day make everything new, when "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" (Revelation 21:4).

That promise is not a way of avoiding grief. It's what makes grief bearable, because it tells the truth about death. Death is not natural, not good, not God's plan for his creatures. It's the last enemy. The Christian hope is not that death doesn't matter but that it has been defeated, and the defeat is not yet finished. A child who learns that from his parents learns something that will hold him for the rest of his life: that loss is real, that grief is right, and that grief is not the final word.

"The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" (Psalm 34:18). When Jesus was on earth, parents brought their children to him, and "he took them in his arms" and laid his hands on them (Mark 10:16). The same Lord who wept at a friend's tomb is near to the grieving child in the dark bedroom and to the grieving father in the doorway. He does not ask either of them to stop crying. He asks them to cry toward him, and he promises that one day he will wipe the tears away himself. Until that day, the father's job is not to make his son stop grieving. It is to grieve with him, honestly and in hope, and to keep telling him the truth about the enemy that took his grandmother and the Lord who will, on the last day, give her back.
