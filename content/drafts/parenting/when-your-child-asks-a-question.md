It always comes at bedtime. The lights are off. You are exhausted and already mentally in the next room, and the small voice in the dark says the thing you have no answer for. Why did grandma have to die. Where do people go. If God is real why didn't he stop it. The question is enormous and you have ten seconds and you are not ready, and you will never be ready, because the question is older than every answer anyone has ever given it.

What you do in those ten seconds is the formation. Not the answer. The answer is almost beside the point. What forms the child is whether the question was safe to ask — whether it landed in a parent who could hold it, or a parent who flinched and reached for the lid.

This is not about having the answer. It is about what you do when you don't.

## The reflex to close

The untrained reflex is to close the question fast. We do it for the kindest reasons. The child is upset; we want the child unupset. The question is frightening; we want it defused. So we reach for the quick consolation — grandma's in a better place now, God has a plan, everything happens for a reason — and we hand it over like a pacifier, and the crying stops, and we tell ourselves we have helped.

We have not helped. We have taught the child that this kind of question gets a pacifier. The child, who is not stupid, files away that the parent did not actually engage the thing, that the parent produced a phrase, that the phrase was meant to end the conversation rather than enter it. The next time the question comes — and it will come, larger, at sixteen, at twenty-five, in a hospital corridor — the child will not bring it to you. You taught them at bedtime that you handle these with a phrase. They will go find someone who handles them with a face.

The phrases are not even true, most of them. Everything happens for a reason is not in the Bible; it is a folk belief wearing Christian clothes, and it makes God the author of the cancer, which is a heavier claim than the person saying it has thought through. God has a plan is true but cruel when deployed as an anesthetic, because it implies the plan required the dead grandmother, and the child can hear that implication even when the adult cannot. We reach for these because they let us out of the room. The child knows we left the room. That is the lesson that lands.

## Job's friends gave the right answers

The book of Job is the longest sustained argument in scripture about exactly this moment, and its verdict is devastating to everyone who likes tidy answers. Job's friends are not villains. They are theologically educated men who say largely correct things about the sovereignty and justice of God. They sit with Job in silence for seven days, which is the one thing they get right, and then they open their mouths, and everything goes wrong. They explain his suffering. They defend God's reputation. They produce the right answers.

And at the end, the voice from the whirlwind turns to the friends and says my anger burns against you, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has. Job, who shouted and accused and demanded and refused every tidy explanation, spoke rightly. The friends, who defended God with correct doctrine, did not. The book is brutal toward people who answer the unanswerable question too well, and the brutality is the point. There are questions where the right answer, deployed too fast, is itself the sin.

God never answers Job's question. Read it again — the why is never given. What Job receives is not an explanation but a presence, the whirlwind, the Lord himself arriving, and somehow the presence is enough where the explanation would not have been. This is the deepest pastoral truth available about the bedtime question. The child is not finally asking for information. The child is asking whether they are alone in the dark with this. The answer they need is not a sentence. It is a presence that does not leave when the question gets too big.

## What to do with the ten seconds

Stay. That is most of it. Do not solve it; stay in it. You can say I don't know — and you should, when you don't, because the child needs to learn early that the people who love them most do not have the bottom of this either, and have not left the faith over it. The I-don't-know spoken by a parent who is still kneeling by the bed, still present, still unafraid, is more catechesis than any answer. It teaches that faith is not the possession of all the answers. It is staying in the room with the unanswered thing.

You can be honest about the grief. Bonhoeffer, writing after the death of friends, refused the Christian instinct to fill the gap left by a lost person with a consolation. He said God does not fill the gap; God keeps it empty, and so helps us keep alive our former communion, even at the cost of pain. A child taught that grief should be quickly resolved has been taught to distrust their own heart, because their heart will not resolve it. A child taught that the gap stays, that even God leaves it open, that the ache is not a failure of faith but a form of love — that child has been given something to grow into rather than something to outgrow.

And you can hand them the lament. The Psalms gave Israel permission to bring the rawest version of the question straight to God. My God, my God, why have you forsaken me — that is not a faithless sentence. It is scripture. Jesus said it from the cross. The child who learns that you are allowed to ask God why, out loud, in anger, with no answer arriving, has been handed the actual prayer life of the actual people of God, instead of the press-release version that snaps the first time real darkness tests it.

There is a long Christian tradition that the church has mostly forgotten, in which not-knowing is treated as a stage of faith rather than a failure of it. The medieval writer of *The Cloud of Unknowing* insisted that God is finally met not in the bright clarity of explanation but in a darkness the intellect cannot pierce, and that learning to remain there, unresolved, is the deeper part of prayer. The negative theology of the Eastern church said the same — that the truest things about God are known by what cannot be said. We have raised children on a Christianity of total explanation, a faith with an answer slotted for every question, and then watched that faith shatter on contact with the first question the answers could not hold. A child given room to not-know, in the company of a parent who can dwell in the not-knowing without panic, has been handed a faith with somewhere to stand when the explanations run out. They will run out. They always run out. The only question is whether the child has been taught that the running-out is the end of faith or the beginning of its depth.

## The verdict

The question you cannot answer is not a failure of your parenting. It is the moment your parenting becomes about something other than information. You will not have the answer. You were never going to have the answer. Augustine did not have it. Job did not get it. Jesus cried it out into a sky that did not, in that hour, reply.

What you have is the choice to stay. To sit on the edge of the bed in the dark and not reach for the lid. To say I don't know, and I'm here, and we will carry this one together for now. The child will remember almost nothing you said. The child will remember that you did not leave.

That is the whole inheritance. Not the answer to the question. A parent who stayed in the room when the question had no answer — so that years from now, in a darker room, with a heavier version of the same question, the child knows that the dark is survivable, because once, at bedtime, someone they trusted survived it with them and did not pretend it was smaller than it was.
