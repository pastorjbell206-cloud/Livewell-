It always comes at bedtime. The lights are off. You are exhausted and already mentally in the next room, and the small voice in the dark says the thing you have no answer for. Why did grandma have to die. Where do people go. If God is real why didn't he stop it. The question is enormous and you have ten seconds and you are not ready — and you will never be ready, because the question is older than every answer anyone has ever given it. The hard questions kids ask about God do not arrive on a schedule that lets you prepare. They arrive in the dark, when your defenses are down, which is exactly why they matter.

What you do in those ten seconds is the formation. Not the answer. The answer is almost beside the point. What forms the child is whether the question was safe to ask — whether it landed in a parent who could hold it, or a parent who flinched and reached for the lid.

This is not about having the answer. It is about what you do when you don't. **The child is not finally asking for information. The child is asking whether they are alone in the dark with this.**

## The reflex to close the question

The untrained reflex is to close the question fast. We do it for the kindest reasons. The child is upset; we want the child unupset. The question is frightening; we want it defused. So we reach for the quick consolation — grandma's in a better place now, God has a plan, everything happens for a reason — and we hand it over like a pacifier, and the crying stops, and we tell ourselves we have helped.

We have not helped. We have taught the child that this kind of question gets a pacifier. The child, who is not stupid, files away that the parent did not actually engage the thing, that the parent produced a phrase, that the phrase was meant to end the conversation rather than enter it. The next time the question comes — and it will come, larger, at sixteen, at twenty-five, in a hospital corridor — the child will not bring it to you. You taught them at bedtime that you handle these with a phrase. They will go find someone who handles them with a face.

The phrases are not even true, most of them. Everything happens for a reason is not in the Bible; it is a folk belief wearing Christian clothes, and it makes God the author of the cancer, which is a heavier claim than the person saying it has ever thought through. God has a plan is true, but it turns cruel when deployed as an anesthetic, because it implies the plan required the dead grandmother, and the child can hear that implication even when the adult cannot. We reach for these phrases because they let us out of the room. The child knows we left the room. That is the lesson that lands, and it lasts.

## Job's friends gave the right answers and were condemned for them

The book of Job is the longest sustained argument in scripture about exactly this moment, and its verdict is devastating to everyone who likes tidy answers. Job's friends are not villains. They are theologically educated men who say largely correct things about the sovereignty and justice of God. They sit with Job in silence for seven days, which is the one thing they get right, and then they open their mouths, and everything goes wrong. They explain his suffering. They defend God's reputation. They produce the right answers, deployed at the worst possible moment, to a man who needed a presence and got a seminar.

And at the end, the voice from the whirlwind turns to the friends and says my anger burns against you, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has (Job 42:7). Job, who shouted and accused and demanded and refused every tidy explanation, spoke rightly. The friends, who defended God with correct doctrine, did not. The book is brutal toward people who answer the unanswerable question too well, and the brutality is the point. There are questions where the right answer, deployed too fast, is itself the sin.

Read the book to its end and notice what is missing. God never answers Job's question. The why is never given. What Job receives is not an explanation but a presence — the whirlwind, the Lord himself arriving — and somehow the presence is enough where the explanation would not have been. This is the deepest pastoral truth available about the bedtime question. The child is not finally asking for information. The child is asking whether they are alone in the dark with this. The answer they need is not a sentence. It is a presence that does not leave when the question gets too big.

## What to do with the ten seconds

Stay. That is most of it. Do not solve it; stay in it. You can say I don't know — and you should, when you don't, because the child needs to learn early that the people who love them most do not have the bottom of this either, and have not left the faith over it. The I-don't-know spoken by a parent who is still kneeling by the bed, still present, still unafraid, is more catechesis than any answer. It teaches that faith is not the possession of all the answers. It is staying in the room with the unanswered thing.

You can be honest about the grief. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from prison in 1943 after the deaths of people he loved, refused the Christian instinct to fill the gap left by a lost person with a consolation. He said God does not fill the gap; God keeps it empty, and so helps us keep alive our former communion with each other, even at the cost of pain. A child taught that grief should be quickly resolved has been taught to distrust their own heart, because their heart will not resolve it. A child taught that the gap stays, that even God leaves it open, that the ache is not a failure of faith but a form of love — that child has been given something to grow into rather than something to outgrow.

And you can hand them the lament. The Psalms gave Israel permission to bring the rawest version of the question straight to God. My God, my God, why have you forsaken me — that is not a faithless sentence. It is scripture (Psalm 22). Jesus said it from the cross (Matthew 27:46). The child who learns that you are allowed to ask God why, out loud, in anger, with no answer arriving, has been handed the actual prayer life of the actual people of God, instead of the press-release version that snaps the first time real darkness tests it.

**A child taught that the gap stays — that even God leaves it open — has been given something to grow into rather than something to outgrow.**

## The tradition knew that not-knowing is a stage of faith

There is a long Christian tradition the modern church has mostly forgotten, in which not-knowing is treated as a stage of faith rather than a failure of it. The fourteenth-century English author of *The Cloud of Unknowing* insisted that God is finally met not in the bright clarity of explanation but in a darkness the intellect cannot pierce, and that learning to remain there, unresolved, is the deeper part of prayer. The apophatic theology of the Eastern church said the same centuries earlier — that the truest things about God are known by what cannot be said, that to stop talking is sometimes the most faithful theology available.

We have raised children on a Christianity of total explanation, a faith with an answer slotted neatly for every question, and then watched that faith shatter on contact with the first question the answers could not hold. The shattering was not a flaw in the child. It was a flaw in what we built. We handed them a faith with no room in it for mystery, and then mystery arrived, as it always does, and there was nowhere for them to stand.

A child given room to not-know, in the company of a parent who can dwell in the not-knowing without panic, has been handed a faith with somewhere to stand when the explanations run out. They will run out. They always run out. Augustine, who wrote more about God than almost anyone in history, ended much of it in awe at how little he had finally said. The only question is whether the child has been taught that the running-out is the end of faith or the beginning of its depth. The parent who panics at the edge of explanation teaches the first. The parent who can stand calmly at that edge teaches the second.

This is why the ten seconds matter more than your theology. Your theology can be repaired later, expanded, corrected, deepened over a lifetime. What cannot easily be repaired is the lesson the child absorbs in those first seconds about whether the dark is a place you can be accompanied into, or a place you are sent into alone with a slogan for company.

## The incarnation is God refusing to answer from a distance

There is one more thing buried in the bedtime question, and it is the deepest reason staying matters more than solving. The center of the Christian claim is not that God sent down an explanation for suffering. It is that God came down into it. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:14) — not a memo about the dark, but a presence inside it. When the human race asked its oldest version of the bedtime question, God's answer was not a sentence handed down from a safe height. The answer was a body, in a manger and then on a cross, asking the question himself from the inside: my God, my God, why.

So when you sit on the edge of the bed and stay rather than solve, you are not failing to be theological. You are being more theological than the parent with the quick phrase. You are imaging the exact move God made — descent, presence, accompaniment into a darkness you cannot fix. The slogan offers the child a god who explains from a distance. Your staying offers the child a God who comes down and does not leave. The child cannot follow the argument yet. The child can feel the weight of someone who refused to leave the room, and that feeling is the first true thing they will ever know about the God who refused to leave the world.

The explanation, even a correct one, keeps you above the suffering, narrating it. The presence puts you down inside it, beside the small person who is afraid. One of those is the gospel. The other is theology used as an exit.

**The slogan offers your child a god who explains from a distance. Your staying offers a God who comes down and does not leave.**

This reframes the whole anxiety most Christian parents carry about the hard questions. We assume our job is to be the one with the answers, and we feel the failure acutely when the answer is not there. But the child was never built to receive the universe through a parent's explanations, and a parent who could answer everything would teach the child the most dangerous lie of all — that suffering is a problem to be solved by sufficient information, that the dark yields to the right words, that faith is a matter of having the explanation rather than having a companion in the unexplained. The child raised on that lie meets real suffering as an adult, finds no explanation equal to it, and concludes the faith has failed. It did not fail. It was misdescribed. It was never an explanation. It was a presence, and we taught the child to expect the wrong thing and call its absence a collapse.

## The verdict

The question you cannot answer is not a failure of your parenting. It is the moment your parenting becomes about something other than information. You will not have the answer. You were never going to have the answer. Augustine did not have it. Job did not get it. Jesus cried it out into a sky that did not, in that hour, reply.

What you have is the choice to stay. To sit on the edge of the bed in the dark and not reach for the lid. To say I don't know, and I'm here, and we will carry this one together for now. The child will remember almost nothing you said. The child will remember that you did not leave.

That is the whole inheritance. Not the answer to the question. A parent who stayed in the room when the question had no answer — so that years from now, in a darker room, with a heavier version of the same question, the child knows the dark is survivable, because once, at bedtime, someone they trusted survived it with them and did not pretend it was smaller than it was.
