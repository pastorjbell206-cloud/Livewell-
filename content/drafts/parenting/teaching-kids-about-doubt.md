There is a question already forming in your child, and they are not going to ask it. Not because they do not have it — they have it, it is sitting there gathering weight — but because they are watching to see whether this house has room for it. And the test they are running is silent. They float a small doubt, a low-stakes one, and they read your face. If your face tightens, the door closes, and the real questions go underground where you will never reach them. I want to talk to the parent who has been waiting for their kid to bring the hard questions, not realizing the kid is waiting on them.

The conversation about doubt is not one your child will start. The shame is too high and the risk is too great. They need you to start it. And there is a window for starting it, and the window is open now and will not stay open.

Not later. Now. Before someone else does.

## The market for your child's doubts

Here is what is true whether you act or not. Your child will encounter the hardest objections to the faith. The only variable you control is who is in the room when it happens.

If you start the conversation, you are in the room. You get to introduce the suffering of the innocent, the silence of God, the violence in the conquest narratives, the contradictions that are not really contradictions and the ones that genuinely are hard — and you get to do it as someone who loves them and has wrestled it and stayed. If you do not start it, someone else will. A video, an algorithm, a confident stranger whose entire content strategy is built on dismantling the faith of teenagers, and who has no stake in your child's flourishing. The objection lands either way. The only question is whether it arrives in your living room or in a dark bedroom at one in the morning, framed by someone who profits from their unmooring.

You are not protecting your child by keeping the questions out. You are surrendering the framing to whoever reaches them first.

## Doubt is in the Bible on purpose

We act as if doubt is the enemy of faith. The Bible treats it as a feature of the faithful. John the Baptist — the man who leapt in the womb, who baptized Jesus, who called him the Lamb of God — sends from prison to ask, "Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" That is the forerunner doubting the one he announced. And Jesus does not rebuke him. He sends back evidence and a blessing.

Thomas wants to touch the wounds. The father in Mark 9 cries, "I believe; help my unbelief," and his son is healed inside the same breath that admits the unbelief. The Psalms ask *how long* and *why have you forsaken me*. The doubt is not smuggled into Scripture against its will. It is canonized. It is in the prayer book. The tradition built a place for it because the tradition was made by people who actually walked with God and knew the walk includes the dark stretches.

So when you teach your child that doubt is permitted, you are not making a modern concession. You are handing them the actual Bible, the one with Job in it, the one where the most honest man gets commended over his friends who defended God with tidy answers. The friends had no doubts. God was angry at the friends.

## Why honesty about doubt produces stronger faith

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age*, described the condition your child is being raised inside — a world where belief is contested, where every faith knows there are intelligent people who reject it, where certainty itself has become harder to come by for everyone. Your child cannot be raised in the sealed world you may have grown up in. That world is gone. The faith that survives the world your child actually lives in is not the faith that was never questioned. It is the faith that was questioned and held.

A faith that has never met an objection is a faith that shatters on contact with the first one. A faith that has been pressure-tested at the kitchen table, by a parent who took the questions seriously and did not flinch, is a faith with a place to put the next objection. You are not weakening their faith by introducing the hard things. You are inoculating it. The small, survivable exposure now is what prevents the catastrophic one later.

There is a generational reason this matters more for your child than it did for you. Through the middle of the twentieth century, American childhood happened inside what sociologists called a sacred canopy — a shared cultural assumption that God was real and Christianity was the default, reinforced by the school, the neighborhood, the calendar. A child could absorb belief by osmosis because the whole environment leaned the same direction. That canopy is gone. Your child is being formed in an environment that leans the other way, where unbelief is the assumed sophistication and faith the thing requiring defense. The osmosis that worked on you will not work on them. They cannot drift into belief on a cultural current that now runs against it. They have to be given the faith deliberately, questions and all, by someone willing to carry the load the old canopy used to carry for free. That someone is you. The canopy is not coming back to do it for you.

## The doubt under the doubt

Notice, too, that most of what arrives dressed as an intellectual objection is wearing a disguise. The teenager who announces that the problem of evil disproves God is sometimes making an argument and sometimes saying that something evil happened to them or to someone they love and no one in the church would sit with it honestly. The objection is real and deserves a real answer. But the answer that satisfies is rarely the syllogism. It is the parent who hears the argument and asks, gently, whether something is underneath it. Job's friends had answers — tidy, theologically defensible answers — and God was angry with them precisely because they used good theology to avoid sitting with a suffering man. Do not be the friends. Give the argument its due, and then go looking for the wound the argument may be carrying. The wound is usually the actual question. Answer the syllogism and miss the wound, and you have won a debate while your child quietly decides you are not safe to bring the real thing to.

## How to actually open it

You do not need a degree in apologetics. You need to go first with your own. Say, out loud, at a moment that is not a crisis: *Want to know something I find hard about all this?* And then say a real one. Not a softball you have a slick answer for — a real one, the kind that has actually cost you a night's sleep. Watch what happens. The shame drains out of the room. If Dad has hard questions and is still here, then hard questions are not the exit door. They are part of the furniture.

Then you do the harder thing. You do not rush to resolve it. You let it sit. You say *I do not fully know, and here is what I lean on while I do not know.* The unresolved question, held by a present parent, teaches more than the tidy answer ever could. It teaches that faith and uncertainty can share a room, which is the single most important thing a modern believer has to learn, and the thing the church most often refuses to model.

## The verdict

Start the conversation. Do not wait for the question to surface on its own, because the shame will keep it submerged until it surfaces somewhere you are not. Go first. Name your own doubt. Make this the house where the hardest things can be said out loud, because the alternative is a house where they get said somewhere else, to someone else, framed by someone who wants your child gone.

The window is open. Your child is watching your face to find out if it is safe. Make it safe. Say the hard thing first. They have been waiting for permission, and the permission has to come from you, and it has to come before the algorithm beats you to the room.
