Every parent eventually stands in the checkout aisle with a child who wants something. It is placed at eye level for a reason. The child asks, then pleads, then argues, and the parent makes a decision in about four seconds that will be repeated, with variations, several thousand times over the next eighteen years. Yes, because it's easier. No, because we can't afford it, which may or may not be true. No, because you have enough, which the child experiences as arbitrary. Each answer teaches something. Most of the time we have no idea what.

Parents who search for how to teach their kids about money and generosity usually have practical questions. Should we give an allowance? Should it be tied to chores? How much should a child save? Should we make them give some of it away? Those are good questions, and this essay will get to them. But they sit on top of a deeper one that most families never ask aloud: what do our children already believe about money, from watching us? Because they believe a great deal, and they learned it long before anyone sat them down.

A child learns from the tone of voice in which the bills are discussed, or the silence in which they aren't. He learns from what his parents buy when they're anxious, from what they envy in the neighbors, from whether money is spoken of with fear or gratitude or contempt. He learns whether giving is something his parents do, and whether they do it gladly. He learns, in short, what money is for, and he learns it by watching what money does to the people he loves most. Most of our explicit lessons are corrections to a curriculum we're teaching without meaning to.

That's the uncomfortable starting point, and I include myself in it. Nearly every adult carries some disorder about money, whether it shows up as anxiety, extravagance, stinginess or shame. Our children are learning it. The question isn't whether we'll hand them a theology of money. It's which one.

A word before going further: nothing here is financial advice. On budgeting, debt, savings accounts, investing or taxes, a family should consult a qualified professional who knows its circumstances. What follows is about the formation of a child's heart, which no financial advisor can do for you.

## How children became customers

It helps to know that the way modern children relate to money is historically new.

For most of human history, children worked. On farms and in family shops, in cottage industry and later in mills and mines, children contributed to the household economy from an early age, and their value to the family was partly economic. The sociologist Viviana Zelizer traced what happened next in *Pricing the Priceless Child* (1985). Roughly between 1870 and 1930, American children passed from being economically useful to being economically useless and emotionally priceless. Child labor laws, compulsory schooling and a new sentimental ideal of childhood removed children from the workforce, culminating federally in the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938. The change was an enormous moral achievement. Children were rescued from factories that maimed and exhausted them. But it also changed the child's relation to money. A child who once earned became a child who received, and the question of how children should handle money shifted from how they contribute to how they spend. In the early twentieth century, child-rearing experts began promoting the weekly allowance precisely as training in spending: a way to teach the young consumer to make choices.

That was a reasonable response to a new situation, and it had a hidden consequence. The child was now being formed primarily as a consumer, and the consumer economy noticed. By the late twentieth century, children had become a market in their own right, and advertising aimed at them directly. Juliet Schor, in *Born to Buy* (2004), documented the scale of marketing to children and studied its effects among children in the Boston area. She found that the children more heavily involved in consumer culture tended to fare worse emotionally, with more anxiety and lower self-esteem, and her analysis suggested that the consumer involvement was helping to cause the distress, not merely accompanying it. The checkout aisle at eye level is the small, visible edge of a vast apparatus built to train children's desires. It works.

So a Christian parent is not teaching a child about money in neutral territory. The child's wants are being formed every day by people who profit from them, and the parent is trying to teach a counter-formation. That's worth knowing, because it clarifies the task. We're not just teaching skills. We're contesting a formation of desire that the child's whole culture is working hard to complete.

There's a second silence to name. In many families, money is the one subject no one discusses in front of the children. Parents who talk freely about sex and death and doubt go quiet about income, debt and giving. The reasons are understandable. Some are ashamed of how little they have or how much. Some fear burdening children with adult worries. Some were raised that way themselves. But the silence teaches too. It teaches that money is shameful and secret, and it leaves the child to learn about it from the only people who are talking to him constantly: the advertisers.

## What the marshmallow really measured

Anyone who has read about children and money has probably encountered the marshmallow test. In studies at Stanford beginning in the late 1960s, the psychologist Walter Mischel offered preschool children a choice: one treat now, or two if they could wait alone in a room for several minutes. Follow-up studies suggested that children who waited longer tended to have better outcomes years later, and the test became a cultural shorthand for the importance of self-control. Mischel's popular book *The Marshmallow Test* (2014) summarized decades of this work.

Then the story became more complicated, and more interesting. In 2018, Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan published a larger and more diverse replication. They found a much weaker link between waiting and later achievement, and most of it disappeared once they accounted for the child's family background and early abilities. Delaying gratification, it turned out, was bound up with circumstance.

An earlier experiment had shown why. In 2013, Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri and Richard Aslin ran the marshmallow test with a twist. Before the test, half the children met an adult who promised them better art supplies and then failed to deliver. The other half met an adult who kept the promise. When the marshmallow test began, the children who had experienced a reliable adult waited far longer than those who had been let down. The researchers titled their paper "Rational Snacking." A child who has learned that promises are broken is being perfectly rational when he eats the marshmallow now.

For a parent, this is one of the most important findings in the whole literature. Saving, at its root, is not a matter of willpower. It is a matter of trust. A child waits when he believes the future is real and the promise will be kept. A child who has learned that the adults around him are unreliable, or that there may be nothing tomorrow, grabs what he can today, and he isn't wrong to. Children raised in poverty or chaos who spend immediately are often responding sensibly to the world they know. Any Christian teaching on thrift that shames those children has misunderstood the problem.

Scripture saw this long before the psychologists. When Israel was fed with manna in the wilderness, the people were told to gather only what they needed for the day. Those who tried to hoard it found that it "bred worms and stank" (Exodus 16:20). The lesson of the manna was that God could be trusted to provide again tomorrow, and the only way to learn it was to not store up. Yet the same Scripture praises the ant who "prepares her bread in summer and gathers her food in harvest" (Proverbs 6:8), and observes that "whoever gathers little by little will increase it" (Proverbs 13:11). The line Scripture draws doesn't run between saving and spending. It runs between saving from trust and hoarding from fear. The ant gathers because the seasons are reliable. The hoarder in the wilderness gathers because he doesn't believe God will come through.

This gives parents a clear first task, and it isn't a lesson plan. It's being reliable. The child who learns to wait for a larger good is the child whose parents keep their promises, whose home is steady, whose tomorrow can be trusted. A parent who wants a child to save should first ask whether the child has reason to believe in the future the parent is asking him to save for.

## Use and enjoyment

The clearest Christian map for money was drawn not by a financial writer but by a bishop in North Africa. At the beginning of *On Christian Teaching*, begun around 397, Augustine distinguished two ways of relating to things: some are to be enjoyed, and others are to be used. To enjoy something, he said, is to cling to it with love for its own sake. To use something is to employ it in the service of obtaining what you love. The whole of human disorder, in Augustine's account, comes from confusing the two: trying to enjoy what should be used, resting our hearts in things that were meant to carry us toward God and one another.

Money is the purest example of a thing to be used. It has no value in itself. It exists only to be exchanged for something else. When a person begins to enjoy money, to cling to it, rest in it, find security and identity in it, the disorder is immediate. Jesus names it directly: "No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money" (Matthew 6:24). And he names the illusion beneath it: "Take care, and be on your guard against all covetousness, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15).

Augustine's distinction is useful for parents because it doesn't make money evil. It puts money in its place. A child doesn't need to be taught that money is bad, which would be false and would make him either guilty or secretive about it. He needs to be taught that money is a tool, that it is for something, and that the question of what it's for is the one that matters. Paul's instruction to Timothy about the wealthy captures the balance precisely:

> As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy. They are to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share, thus storing up treasure for themselves as a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of that which is truly life. (1 Timothy 6:17–19)

God "richly provides us with everything to enjoy." That line forbids the stinginess that treats every pleasure as suspect, and the rest of the passage forbids the hoarding that treats every pleasure as a possession. Money is used. God, and the good things he gives, and the people he gives them to, are enjoyed.

The church has said this in every generation, often more sharply than we'd like. Basil of Caesarea, preaching on the rich fool of Luke 12 during a famine in the 360s, told his congregation that the surplus sitting in their storehouses belonged by right to the hungry. John Wesley's sermon "The Use of Money" (1760) gave Methodists three rules, to gain, save and give all they could, and meant the third to govern the first two. That longer argument, from the fathers to Wesley's worry that his people kept the first two rules and forgot the third, is traced in [What Does the Bible Say About Money and Wealth?](/writing/what-the-bible-actually-says-about-money-and-wealth). The concern here is narrower, and it lives at the kitchen table. We teach our children to earn and to save, and we teach them far less about why.

## Allowance, chores and an argument between good parents

Now the practical questions, which are genuinely disputed among thoughtful Christian parents. None of them is settled by Scripture, and families should decide them without feeling that the other choice is unfaithful.

The first is whether a child's money should be tied to work. One view holds that it should. Scripture honors labor and connects it to provision: "If anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" (2 Thessalonians 3:10). A child paid for chores learns early that money comes from effort, that work has value and that nothing is simply owed. Many families find this clarifying, especially as children grow older and can take on real jobs, and there is dignity in a child earning his own money and deciding what to do with it.

The other view holds that ordinary household chores should not be paid, because they are what members of a family do for one another. Ron Lieber, a personal finance journalist, argues along these lines in *The Opposite of Spoiled* (2015). He recommends that parents give an allowance as a teaching tool, a small amount of money the child learns to manage, while expecting chores as part of belonging to the household, unpaid. Paying for every task, the argument goes, teaches a child that he contributes to the family only when compensated, and a child who can afford to skip the chore may simply decline the payment. Lieber also urges parents to talk with their children about money much more openly than most do, including about what the family earns, spends and gives, in terms suited to the child's age.

Both views have real wisdom, and many families blend them: ordinary chores unpaid, as part of belonging, and extra work paid, as a way of earning. The distinction beneath the choice is worth naming, whatever a family decides. There is work done because we belong, and there is work done to earn. A child who learns only the second will grow into an adult who can't imagine serving without being paid. A child who learns only the first may never grasp that money must be earned. The household is where a child learns both, and learns which is which.

Paul gives a third purpose for work that neither view always remembers. "Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need" (Ephesians 4:28). Work, in that verse, is not only for supporting oneself. It is for having something to give. A child who earns five dollars can be taught that part of the reason for earning is to share.

That leads to the second question: should children be required to give? Many Christian families teach children to set aside a portion of any money they receive, often a tenth, for giving at church or to people in need. Some use separate containers for giving, saving and spending, which makes the proportions visible to a young child. Others worry that required giving teaches compulsion, and point to Paul: "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7). Christians also disagree about whether the tithe binds believers under the new covenant as law, or is a wise pattern, or has been surpassed by a call to greater generosity. This essay doesn't settle that question. But with young children, the habit of giving often has to come before the heart for it, just as the habit of prayer does. A child taught to set aside part of his money for others, and allowed over time to choose where it goes, will often grow into the cheerfulness Paul describes. The practice is scaffolding, not the building.

## The tithe that was a feast

When Christians think of the tithe, they usually picture an obligation: a tenth handed over, grudgingly or dutifully. It's worth reading what Deuteronomy actually describes. Israel was to set aside a tenth of the harvest each year and bring it to the place of worship. If the place was too far to carry the produce, they could sell it and carry the money, and then spend it there on food and drink for a feast. "And you shall eat there before the LORD your God and rejoice, you and your household" (Deuteronomy 14:26). Every third year, the tithe was stored in the towns for those who had no land of their own: "And the Levite, because he has no portion or inheritance with you, and the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow, who are within your towns, shall come and eat and be filled, that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands that you do" (Deuteronomy 14:29).

The tithe in Deuteronomy is a meal. It is eaten by the family in God's presence with rejoicing, and it is shared with the poor, the foreigner, the orphan and the widow. A child in ancient Israel would have experienced the tithe not as a deduction but as the best feast of the year, at which the family ate before God and the people who had nothing ate alongside them. That is a far cry from an automated transfer that no child ever sees.

And that points to a practical problem that modern families face without knowing it. Generosity has become invisible. Many parents give faithfully, but online, by automatic withdrawal, on a screen the child never looks at. The child sees the parents buy groceries, pay for gas and order things online. He never sees them give. So he concludes, reasonably, that his parents don't. If a household wants its children to learn generosity, some of its giving has to be visible: discussed at the table, decided together, delivered by hand when possible. A child who helps choose which family in need the household will help this month, and who goes along to deliver it, learns more than a year of lessons could teach.

There is research, for whatever it is worth, suggesting that generosity is good for the giver. Christian Smith and Hilary Davidson, in *The Paradox of Generosity* (2014), drew on national surveys and interviews and found that Americans who give more of their money, time and attention to others tend to report greater happiness, better health and a stronger sense of purpose. They're careful to say the relationship is complex. But Scripture had already said it more simply. Paul reminded the Ephesian elders of "the words of the Lord Jesus, how he himself said, 'It is more blessed to give than to receive'" (Acts 20:35).

The same research tradition has an indictment for adults. Smith, with Michael Emerson and Patricia Snell, studied American Christian giving in *Passing the Plate* (2008) and found that most American Christians give away a small fraction of their income, far less than they could, and far less than their own traditions teach. That is not a finding about other people's households. It is a portrait of ours, and our children are learning from it.

The Macedonian churches, Paul told the Corinthians, gave "in a severe test of affliction," and "their abundance of joy and their extreme poverty have overflowed in a wealth of generosity on their part" (2 Corinthians 8:2). Their generosity came out of their poverty and their joy, not out of surplus. And Jesus watched a poor widow put two small coins into the temple offering and said she had given more than all the rich, "for they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on" (Mark 12:44). That matters for children, who have very little. A child's gift of a dollar from a small allowance may be, by Jesus' measure, more generous than his parents' check. Parents who treat a child's small gifts as real, and not as practice for the day when he'll have enough to give meaningfully, are teaching him to see money the way Jesus did.

## Where the heart follows

Paul grounds every exhortation about money in one sentence. "For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). Christian generosity is not finally a financial strategy or a character trait. It is a response to having been given everything by someone who emptied himself to do it.

That's the deepest thing a parent can teach a child about money, and it can't be taught in a lesson. A child learns it by being loved lavishly and freely by parents who were loved that way by God. He learns it by watching his parents treat money as a tool and people as ends. He learns it by seeing them give when giving costs something, by hearing them speak of their income as a trust rather than a trophy, by watching them refuse the checkout-aisle logic that says the next thing will satisfy. And he learns it, most of all, by watching his parents be content, not because they have everything, but because they know whose they are.

"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). Jesus did not say that where your heart is, your treasure will follow. He said it the other way around. The heart follows the money. That is why the way a family handles its money is never only a financial matter. Every dollar a household spends, saves or gives is shaping what its members will love. Our children are watching where we put our treasure, and they are learning where to put their hearts.

The child in the checkout aisle will grow up. One day he'll stand there with a child of his own, making the same four-second decisions. What he does then will depend less on any rule we gave him than on what he watched us love. The question worth carrying out of this essay isn't how much our children know about money. It's what they have seen money mean in our home, and whether it pointed them toward the Giver who gave them everything first.
