It's a Tuesday in October, a little after six, and dinner is happening in the car. One child has practice across town at six-thirty, another has a lesson that ends at seven-fifteen on the other side of the highway, and a third is doing homework on a knee in the back seat under the dome light. The weekend is already spoken for: a tournament two hours away that begins Saturday morning and, if the team keeps winning, runs through Sunday afternoon. Nobody in this family would say they chose this. They said yes to one good thing, then another, each yes reasonable on its own, and one day they looked up and discovered that the calendar had become the head of the household.

The parents in that car are not worldly or careless. They are, almost always, conscientious people who love their children and are trying to give them every good thing within reach. That's what makes the question so hard to ask out loud. When someone types "is my family too busy" into a search box late at night, it is rarely because they have stopped caring. It is because they care a great deal and suspect that the caring has turned into something else, and they can't quite name what.

Much of what they feel should be granted first, because a great deal of it is true. Sports are a good gift. The body is not an embarrassment to the Christian faith; it is a creature God made and called good, and the Son of God took one and rose in it. A child who learns to lose without sulking, to win without gloating, to be at the field at six in the morning for teammates, to take correction from a coach, and to feel the plain happiness of a well-struck ball is learning things that no Sunday school lesson can substitute for. Paul reached for athletic images without apology. "Do you not know that in a race all the runners run, but only one receives the prize? So run that you may obtain it. Every athlete exercises self-control in all things. They do it to receive a perishable wreath, but we an imperishable" (1 Corinthians 9:24–25). He also told Timothy that "while bodily training is of some value, godliness is of value in every way, as it holds promise for the present life and also for the life to come" (1 Timothy 4:8). Some value is not no value. For a great many children, especially girls who a few generations ago were given almost no chance to compete, the team has been a place of real dignity. For a child in a hard home, a coach can be the steadiest adult in the week.

So the problem isn't sports, and it isn't music lessons or robotics or dance. The distinction this essay turns on is simpler and harder than that. Some goods serve a household. Other goods are served by it. You can tell which is which by one test: whether the family can still tell the thing no. A good that can be interrupted for the sake of something higher is being loved in its place. A good that can no longer be interrupted, for dinner, for sleep, for worship, for a grandparent's birthday, for a child's plain exhaustion, has quietly stopped being a gift and started being a master. And children, who watch what their parents cannot refuse, learn from it what their parents actually worship.

## How play became an investment

The strange thing about the over-scheduled family is how new it is, and how old the temptation beneath it is.

Augustine, writing his *Confessions* near the end of the fourth century, remembered being beaten by his schoolmasters as a boy for playing ball when he should have been at his lessons. He admitted he loved the games and was a poor student for it. But he noticed something about the men who punished him. Their own pursuits, the ambitions and rivalries and civic honors, were no less a game than his; the difference was only that the grown-ups' trifles were called business, while a boy's were called idleness and whipped out of him. He didn't excuse himself. He saw that the adults were playing too, only with higher stakes and less honesty about it. That observation is sixteen centuries old, and it still describes a Saturday sideline.

The Dutch historian Johan Huizinga gave the modern world its classic account of what play is. In *Homo Ludens* (1938) he argued that play is older than culture, that animals play before human beings ever build a city, and that much of what we call civilization grew out of it. He described play by its marks: it is free, entered into voluntarily; it stands outside ordinary life, in its own time and space, with its own rules; it absorbs the player completely while remaining, by its own nature, not serious; and it is connected with no material interest, no profit to be gained. A game is played for the sake of the game. Then Huizinga said something that reads like prophecy. He worried that modern sport, as it became systematized, organized and professionalized, was losing its play-character. The more it was taken seriously as a means to something else, the less it was play at all.

That is exactly the turn American childhood took in the second half of the twentieth century. Little League began in Williamsport, Pennsylvania, in 1939, when Carl Stotz organized a few teams so neighborhood boys could play on a field their size. For decades youth sports in America looked roughly like that: local, seasonal, run by volunteer parents, and over by the time the leaves turned. What changed after the 1980s was not that children started playing. It was that play became an investment. The travel team, the year-round club, the private coach, the showcase tournament and the recruiting video all arrived on the promise that a child's future could be built early, one weekend at a time, and that a family that didn't build it was falling behind. The child's game became a portfolio.

The pediatricians noticed. In 2016 the American Academy of Pediatrics published a clinical report on sports specialization and intensive training in young athletes. Its authors advised that most children wait until adolescence before specializing in a single sport, and they warned of the overuse injuries, the psychological stress and the burnout that come from year-round training in one activity at an early age. The same report noted that playing several sports tends to serve a young athlete better than focusing on one. The medical profession, in other words, arrived by its own road at something like Huizinga's warning: when you turn play into production, even the production suffers.

The psychologist David Elkind had seen the broader pattern decades before. In *The Hurried Child* (1981) he described children pressed to grow up too fast, made to carry the anxieties and schedules of adults before they had the inner resources to bear them. And the sociologist Annette Lareau, in *Unequal Childhoods* (2003), observed a dozen families closely and gave a name to the middle-class version: concerted cultivation. In the families she studied, organized activities came to govern the household's time, children's schedules set the rhythm of the whole family, and parents treated each child's talents as something to be developed deliberately. Lareau did not sneer at this. She found that it gave children real skills, especially an ease with institutions and adults that would serve them later. She also found households that were frantic, exhausted and short on unstructured time, siblings who barely saw each other, and children who learned to expect that the family would organize itself around them. The wider story of how protective, investment-minded parenting took hold is told in [How to Raise Resilient Kids Without Overprotective Parenting](/writing/parenting-letting-them-fail); here the concern is narrower, and it is the calendar.

That last point is the one worth sitting with. The over-scheduled family doesn't only lose time. It teaches a theology of the self. A child who grows up watching every adult in his life rearrange their week around his development learns, without anyone saying so, that he is the project the household exists to complete. That is a heavy thing to lay on a nine-year-old, and he will not thank us for it at twenty-five, when the world declines to organize itself around him and he has never learned how to be one member of something larger.

## The oldest argument about Sunday

Somewhere in this story the Lord's Day appears, usually as a scheduling conflict. The tournament runs through Sunday morning. The club holds practice at nine on Sundays because the fields are free. And a family that wants to take both church and the team seriously finds itself choosing, again and again, which one will give way.

It's worth knowing that this argument is centuries older than travel ball, because the history clears away some of the heat. In 1618 King James I issued a declaration, later called the Book of Sports, ordering that his subjects not be hindered from lawful recreation on Sundays after divine service: dancing, archery, leaping, vaulting, May games and the like. His son Charles I reissued it in 1633 and required ministers to read it from their pulpits. Many Puritan clergy refused and were punished for it, and when Parliament gained the upper hand in the civil conflict, it ordered the declaration publicly burned in 1643.

The king's case deserves a hearing, because it is stronger than its reputation. The declaration argued that working people labored six days a week and had only Sunday for any recreation at all; that forbidding them honest games after church would drive them to the alehouse instead; and that a Protestantism which banned all mirth on the Lord's Day would hand Catholic critics a ready reproach. That is not a frivolous argument. It is a class argument, and a pastoral one, and it has a modern echo. The family whose parents both work weekday shifts, whose only free day is Sunday, may reasonably ask why the Christian calendar seems to assume a household with leisure on Saturday.

The Puritans answered from conviction, not from joylessness, though they were often accused of it. They believed the fourth commandment still bound Christians, carried over to the first day of the week by the resurrection, and that the whole day belonged to God's worship and to works of necessity and mercy. They lost the argument about the king and eventually won it for a century or two in Scotland and New England, and their heirs lost it again. By the time most of us were children, the question had simply stopped being asked. The fields were open, the malls were open, and Sunday was one more day. The longer history of that argument, and the ways faithful Christians have answered it, is traced in [What Is the Sabbath and Should Christians Keep It Today?](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it).

Then there is the case everyone knows. In 1924 the Scottish sprinter Eric Liddell went to the Paris Olympics as a favorite in the 100 metres and withdrew from it, because the heats were scheduled on a Sunday and he would not run on the Lord's Day. He ran the 400 metres instead, not his best event, and won the gold medal. The film *Chariots of Fire* (1981) turned this into a shipboard crisis of conscience, but in fact the schedule was known months in advance and Liddell made his decision quietly and without drama. He went on to serve as a missionary in China and died in a Japanese internment camp in 1945. What is most instructive about Liddell is not the refusal itself. It's that the refusal was possible because he had settled, long before the conflict came, what his running was for. He was not forced to choose between God and the race. The race had already been placed under God, and so when the two collided, there was nothing to agonize over.

I should be careful here, because Christians honestly disagree about how the Sabbath command binds us. Some hold, with the Westminster divines, that the Lord's Day is the Christian Sabbath and should be kept whole. Others read Paul's words, "One person esteems one day as better than another, while another esteems all days alike. Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind" (Romans 14:5), and conclude that no day is holier than another, though the gathering of the church remains a command. This is a question on which faithful families differ, and a household that lets its child play in a Sunday tournament twice a season has not thereby left the faith. What no Christian reading can support is the arrangement most of us have drifted into, in which the gathering of God's people simply loses every time it conflicts with anything else. The letter to the Hebrews was written to a small, pressured community tempted to drift away, and its instruction is plain: "And let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near" (Hebrews 10:24–25). The habit of some. The writer knew that people rarely abandon the assembly in one decision. They do it by habit, one reasonable absence at a time.

And the church has to be honest about its own part. Many congregations responded to the Sunday conflict by making church one more activity, competing on convenience, moving the youth program to fit the season and quietly teaching families that worship was an item on the family calendar rather than the thing the calendar is arranged around. I am a pastor, and I'm inside that indictment. We in the church told families that Sunday mattered and then scheduled ourselves like a club that hoped they'd fit us in. It should surprise no one that they did exactly that.

## Martha and the many things

Beneath the calendar there is almost always a feeling, and the feeling is fear. It is rarely spoken as fear. It sounds like responsibility: I don't want my child to miss her chance. Everyone on the team is doing the winter clinic. If he doesn't make the travel roster this year, he won't make the high school team, and then what? The fear has an economic edge. Many parents are quietly afraid that the world their children will inherit is narrower and harsher than the one they grew up in, that places are scarce, that a child must be made exceptional to be safe. The schedule is the form that fear takes when it is loved and well-financed.

Luke records a small domestic scene that Christians have argued over for centuries. Jesus comes to the home of two sisters. Mary sits at his feet and listens. Martha is "distracted with much serving" and finally asks Jesus to tell her sister to help. "But the Lord answered her, 'Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her'" (Luke 10:41–42). Read carelessly, the passage becomes a contest between two personalities, the doer and the contemplative. That misses it. Martha is doing a good thing. Hospitality to a traveling teacher was an honorable duty, and Jesus doesn't call her work sinful. What he names is its effect on her: anxious and troubled about many things. And what he names in Mary's choice is its durability: it will not be taken away. The many things Martha served would all be gone by morning. The one thing Mary received would outlast her.

The over-scheduled family is Martha's house in minivans. Nearly everything in it is good, and the good things have multiplied until the family is anxious and troubled about them, and the one thing that will not be taken away has been crowded to the edge. The psalmist saw the same pattern in a society of builders and watchmen. "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain. Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain. It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep" (Psalm 127:1–2). A household can eat the bread of anxious toil on behalf of its children, rising early for the six a.m. practice and going late to rest after the tournament, and believe the whole time that it is doing the children a kindness. The psalm doesn't condemn work. It condemns the anxiety that believes the house stands only because we keep building.

I have to turn this around, because it would be easy to read the last few paragraphs as a sermon aimed at the sports parents two rows over. Every parent carries some version of this fear, whether it is spent on travel teams or on test prep, on music competitions or on the careful curation of a child's reputation. Those of us who pride ourselves on a quieter calendar are often just anxious about different things. The busyness is a symptom, and the disease is common to all of us: a quiet disbelief that God can be trusted with our children's futures, and a conviction, rarely admitted, that the child's life is finally ours to secure.

Josef Pieper, writing in postwar Germany in *Leisure: The Basis of Culture* (1948), warned of a world of total work, in which even rest exists only to prepare for more work. The over-scheduled childhood is total work wearing a jersey. The child is never simply playing; he is developing. He is never simply resting; he is recovering for Saturday. The game has been drafted into the service of the résumé, and a child who is never allowed to do anything for its own sake will have a hard time, later, believing that God loves him for his own sake either. He will have learned too well that love follows performance.

"For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his soul?" (Mark 8:36). Jesus asked that about adults, but it can be asked about a childhood. What does it profit a child to gain a scholarship and lose the capacity for rest, for worship, for sitting at a table with people who don't care how he played? The question isn't rhetorical. Some families will look honestly at what they are buying and decide it is worth the price. Many will realize they never looked at the price at all.

## What a family can refuse

This is not a place for a rule, and I won't offer one. Families differ, children differ, and seasons of life differ. A household with one child and flexible work has choices a single mother with three children and two jobs doesn't have. A child for whom the team is the only structure keeping him out of real trouble is in a different place from a child for whom it is one of six enrichments. The gifted young musician or athlete who genuinely loves the work, not only the approval, may need more of the family's time for a season, and that can be a good and generous thing for the household to give. What follows is not a program. It is a set of questions that families have found clarifying, offered in the spirit of wisdom, not law.

Begin with the test this essay opened with. Can we still tell it no? Pick any activity and imagine declining it for the sake of something better: missing a practice for a grandparent's funeral, a tournament for a friend's baptism, a weeknight session because the whole family is worn to the bone. If the answer is that we couldn't possibly, that the coach would bench him, that the club would drop her, the activity has more authority in the household than the parents do. That may be a price a family decides to pay. It should at least be a price they know they are paying.

Then there is the table, and whether the family still eats at it together. The table is the most ordinary liturgy a household has, and it is usually the first casualty of an over-full week. Even a few protected dinners a week, with phones away and no one rushing out the door, preserve something that no enrichment can replace: a place where each child is simply a member of the family and not a talent under development.

Beside the table sits a quieter question: whether each child has any time that is genuinely unstructured, time in which nothing is being developed and no one is keeping score. Huizinga's free play, the fort in the backyard and the aimless bike ride, is not wasted time. It is where a child learns that life is a gift before it is a task.

The Lord's Day raises a question each household must answer before the conflict arrives, not in the car on the way to it. What does our family do when the schedule collides with the gathering of God's people? Some families decide that Sunday worship will never give way. Others decide it gives way rarely, for specific reasons, and that when it does, the family worships elsewhere, at an evening service or with another congregation near the tournament. What matters is that the decision is made in advance, in the light, as a family, and that the children hear the reasons. Liddell's clarity came from a question settled long before the Olympic schedule was published.

And some questions children can answer better than their parents. Ask them. Not in a way that loads the answer, but honestly: Do you love this? Would you miss it if you stopped? Are you doing it for yourself or for us? Many parents discover that a child has been dragging himself to an activity for two years out of a fear of disappointing them. Others discover that a child loves the thing far more than they had realized. Either discovery is worth the conversation.

A family may run through all of this and conclude that its calendar is right, that the season is demanding but the goods are real and the household can bear it. That is a legitimate conclusion. The point was never to shame the busy. It was to put the calendar back under the family, and the family back under God, so that when the children look at how their parents spend the week, they learn that their parents love them, and not that their parents are afraid.

## The streets full of boys and girls playing

The prophet Zechariah was given a vision of the restored Jerusalem after the exile, and among its images is one that is easy to pass over. "Old men and old women shall again sit in the streets of Jerusalem, each with staff in hand because of great age. And the streets of the city shall be full of boys and girls playing in its streets" (Zechariah 8:4–5). When God describes the peace he intends for his people, one of the signs is children at play. Not children at practice. Not children being developed. Children playing in the street, while the old sit and watch, with nowhere to be and nothing to prove.

The Christian hope is not that our children will finally be exceptional. It is that they will finally be at home. The kingdom is pictured again and again as a feast, a city, a garden and a wedding: places where the striving is over and the thing that remains is delight in God and in one another. Ecclesiastes put it with a farmer's plainness: "Better is a handful of quietness than two hands full of toil and a striving after wind" (Ecclesiastes 4:6). A childhood can be two hands full, every hour accounted for, and still miss the one handful that lasts.

What parents most want for their children is a future. What they most need to give them is a present in which they are loved before they have achieved anything, and a God who can be trusted with the rest. That was always the gift of the Lord's Day, whatever we conclude about how it binds: it is the day of resurrection, when the decisive work was done while every disciple was hiding, and nothing they produced contributed to it.

So the question for the family in the car on that Tuesday in October isn't finally about any one team. It is whether anything in the life of the household is still beyond the schedule's reach, whether the children will grow up having seen, at least sometimes, their parents say no to a good thing for the sake of a better one. They will remember the tournaments. They will remember far longer what the family was willing to miss, and why.
