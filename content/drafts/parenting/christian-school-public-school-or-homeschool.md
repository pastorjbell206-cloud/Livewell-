Think of a kitchen table in February. The enrollment deadline for next fall is three weeks away, and two parents are sitting with a laptop open to the website of the Christian school across town, a spreadsheet of tuition figures, a brochure from the local public elementary school, and a library book on homeschooling that one of them checked out and neither has finished. They've prayed about it. They've asked friends. The trouble is that their friends' answers were not really answers. One couple, who homeschool, spoke about it with a quiet certainty that made the public school sound like a surrender. Another, who teach in the public schools, spoke about Christian schools as though they were a retreat from the mission field. A third couple, whose children attend the Christian school, seemed to assume that any serious Christian family would do the same if they could afford it. Each answer came wrapped in a faint judgment, and the couple at the table can feel it. They're not only choosing a school. They're choosing which of their friends will quietly think less of them.

If that table is yours, it's worth saying plainly at the start what this essay will and won't do. It won't tell you which choice to make. The question of how to school your children is what theologians call a second-order question: a matter on which faithful, orthodox, Scripture-honoring Christians disagree, and on which the Bible does not give a direct command. It is not a small question, and it's not a question without better and worse answers for a particular child in a particular place. But it is not a test of faith, and the church has done real harm by treating it as one. What this essay will do is explain how the question got so charged, state the strongest Christian case for each option in a form its own defenders would recognize, and then say what Scripture does bind, which turns out to be more demanding than any of the three camps usually admits.

## How the question got here

Christians have been founding schools for as long as there have been Christians who could read, but the modern shape of the question goes back to the Reformation. In 1524 Martin Luther wrote an open letter to the councilmen of the German cities urging them to establish and maintain Christian schools for both boys and girls, at public expense. His argument was that the gospel had come back into the light through Scripture, and a people who couldn't read Scripture would lose it again. Luther wanted schooling to be public, Christian and universal all at once, and he saw no contradiction among the three. The Puritans of Massachusetts Bay agreed. Their school law of 1647, known to history by its preamble about "that old deluder, Satan," who sought to keep people from the knowledge of the Scriptures, required towns of fifty households to appoint a teacher and towns of a hundred households to maintain a grammar school.

For the next two centuries, American schooling was Protestant schooling, more or less by default. When Horace Mann became secretary of the new Massachusetts Board of Education in 1837 and led the movement for common schools, free and open to all children, the schools he championed were meant to be nonsectarian, but nonsectarian meant broadly Protestant: the King James Bible read without comment, Protestant hymns and prayers, a moral curriculum shaped by the Protestant majority. Catholics noticed. In 1844, disputes over which Bible should be read in Philadelphia's public schools helped set off riots in which churches were burned and people were killed. The Catholic bishops eventually concluded that the common schools would never be neutral toward their faith, and in 1884 the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore directed that every parish should establish a school. The great American Catholic school system was, in part, a response to a Protestant public school. Charles Glenn, in *The Myth of the Common School* (1988), argues that the common school was never neutral at all. It was designed to form a particular kind of citizen with a particular set of beliefs, and those who didn't share the beliefs were expected to conform.

By the twentieth century the beliefs were changing. John Dewey, the most influential philosopher of American education, closed his *My Pedagogic Creed* (1897) by describing the teacher as "the prophet of the true God and the usherer in of the true kingdom of God." Dewey's God was not the God of the King James Bible, and his kingdom was a democratic society shaped by scientific intelligence. His vision did not remove religion from the schools so much as replace one religious vision with another. Then, in two decisions, *Engel v. Vitale* in 1962 and *Abington School District v. Schempp* in 1963, the Supreme Court ruled that state-sponsored prayer and devotional Bible reading in public schools violated the Constitution. Many evangelicals experienced these rulings as the moment the public schools stopped being theirs.

The Christian school movement grew rapidly in the decades that followed, and honesty requires naming a painful part of that history. In parts of the South, some private schools, including some church schools, were founded after desegregation to allow white families to avoid integrated public schools. The Supreme Court upheld the government's power to deny tax exemption to a racially discriminatory religious school in *Bob Jones University v. United States* in 1983. Many Christian schools had nothing to do with that history and were founded out of sincere theological conviction, and many today are deeply committed to racial reconciliation. But the history is real, and a Christian school movement that doesn't know it can't fully understand why some of its neighbors regard it with suspicion.

Homeschooling came back into American life from two directions at once. On the left, the educator John Holt, in *How Children Fail* (1964) and later books, argued that schools themselves damaged children's natural curiosity, and he eventually urged parents to teach their children at home. On the right, Raymond and Dorothy Moore, in *Better Late Than Early* (1975), argued that early formal schooling was harmful for young children and that the home was the best place to learn. Christian families took up homeschooling in growing numbers. The Home School Legal Defense Association was founded in 1983 to defend them in court, and by the early 1990s homeschooling was legal in every state.

Two other threads belong to the story. Abraham Kuyper, the Dutch theologian and statesman, argued in his address at the founding of the Free University of Amsterdam in 1880 that Christ claims sovereignty over every square inch of human existence, and that different spheres of life, including the family, the church and the school, each have their own God-given authority. The Dutch fought for decades over religious schooling, and in 1917 they reached a settlement under which religious and public schools received equal public funding. And in 1947 the English writer Dorothy Sayers gave a lecture at Oxford titled "The Lost Tools of Learning," arguing for a return to the medieval trivium of grammar, logic and rhetoric. Her lecture helped inspire, decades later, the classical Christian school movement that has grown quickly in America since the 1980s.

All of this history lands at the kitchen table in February. The couple choosing a school are not making a merely practical decision. They are standing inside four centuries of argument about what schools are for, whose schools they are, and what they should teach children about God.

## The case for each

Each of the three options has a serious Christian case behind it. It is worth stating each in its strongest form, so that a family who chooses differently can understand why their friends chose as they did.

**The case for the public school** begins with the calling of God's people to seek the good of the place where they live. When the exiles of Judah were carried off to Babylon, Jeremiah wrote to them, "But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (Jeremiah 29:7). The public school is one of the most important shared institutions of any community, the place where the children of the rich and the poor, the believing and the unbelieving, are formed together. Neil Postman, in *The End of Education* (1995), argued that the deepest purpose of public schooling is not to serve a public that already exists but to create one, to give children of different backgrounds a common story and a shared life. Christian families who withdraw from the public schools, the argument goes, take with them their children's example, their own involvement and often their resources, and the schools and the children left behind are poorer for it. Scripture shows God's people educated in pagan institutions without losing their faith: Moses "was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts 7:22), and Daniel and his friends were trained in "the literature and language of the Chaldeans" (Daniel 1:4) and remained faithful. Defenders of public schooling add that the primary responsibility for a child's spiritual formation belongs to parents and the church in any case, and that a child who learns to hold his faith among classmates who don't share it may be better prepared for adult life than one who has never had to. Many public school teachers are faithful Christians who see their classrooms as a place of genuine service.

**The case for the Christian school** begins with the conviction that education is never neutral. Every school teaches not only facts but a vision of what matters, what a human being is and what life is for. James K. A. Smith, in *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009), argued that education forms desires as much as it transmits information, and that the habits and rituals of an institution teach children what to love whether or not anyone intends them to. Jesus said, "A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone when he is fully trained will be like his teacher" (Luke 6:40). Children spend some thirty hours a week in school, far more than they spend in church or in family devotions, and it is naive, the argument goes, to think that what happens in those hours won't shape who they become. Kuyper's conviction that Christ claims every square inch applies here: mathematics, history, literature and science are all part of God's world, and a Christian school can teach them as such, integrating faith and learning rather than confining faith to Sunday. A good Christian school also provides a community in which teachers and parents share the same fundamental commitments and reinforce each other's work. Defenders note that this is exactly what the early Reformers, the Catholic bishops and the Dutch Calvinists all concluded when they built their own schools.

**The case for homeschooling** begins with the conviction that God gave children to their parents and charged parents with their formation. "And these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). Homeschooling families take that instruction as a picture of education woven through the whole of ordinary life, which is hard to achieve when children are away from home most of the day. Homeschooling allows teaching tailored to each child's pace and gifts, which can be a great mercy to children who struggle in conventional classrooms or who are far ahead. It keeps children in a world of mixed ages rather than the age-segregated peer culture that has dominated adolescence since the high school became universal. It allows a family to shape its days around worship, service, rest and one another. (The legal requirements for homeschooling differ from state to state, and families should learn their own state's law; this is not legal advice.) Defenders often point out that for most of Christian history, and indeed most of human history, children were formed chiefly at home, and that the modern school is the novelty. Rod Dreher, in *The Benedict Option* (2017), urged Christian families to think seriously about withdrawing their children from institutions they believe are forming them against the faith, and pointed to both homeschooling and classical Christian schools as ways of doing so. His proposal, and the case against it, get a fuller hearing in this site's essay on [raising Christian kids in a secular culture](/writing/raising-kids-post-christian).

## What each case has to answer

Each of these cases is strong, and each has to answer a serious objection, which its most thoughtful defenders acknowledge.

The public school has to answer the question of formation. If Smith and Glenn are right that no school is neutral, then parents who choose the public school have to reckon honestly with what it is teaching their children, not only in the curriculum but in the habits, the culture and the unspoken assumptions of the place. Daniel was faithful in Babylon, but he was a young man with a formed conscience when he got there, and he was willing to refuse the king's food. Defenders of the other two options put the objection bluntly: children are not missionaries sent to evangelize their classmates; they are children being formed. Families who choose the public school, if they are wise, don't assume the school will form their children for them and don't assume it will do no harm. They take up that work themselves with more intensity, not less.

The Christian school has to answer three questions. The first is cost. Tuition puts Christian schooling out of reach for many families, and a Christian education available only to those who can pay can quietly turn into a class marker, which sits uneasily with a faith whose Lord said that the gospel is preached to the poor. The second is the bubble. A school where every family shares the same commitments can shelter children from the questions they will eventually face, and some graduates meet those questions for the first time in college with nothing to answer them. The third is whether the school actually forms what it promises. Smith and Denton's research on moralistic therapeutic deism found that thin, therapeutic faith was common among American teenagers of every tradition, including those raised in churches. A Christian label on a school doesn't guarantee Christian formation.

Homeschooling has to answer questions about capacity, isolation and accountability. Not every parent has the gifts, training, time or emotional resources to teach well, and the burden of homeschooling usually falls heavily on one parent, most often the mother, who may sacrifice a great deal. Children educated at home can be isolated from peers and from adults outside the family, which matters for their development and occasionally for their safety, since a child who sees no one outside the home has no one to tell if something is wrong at home. Most homeschooling families are nothing like that, and many are deeply connected to co-ops, churches and communities. But the objection is real, and the best homeschooling families build accountability and community deliberately. Research comparing outcomes across the three sectors is limited and contested, partly because homeschooling families are difficult to study, and none of the three camps can honestly claim that the evidence settles the question in its favor.

## What Scripture actually binds

If Scripture doesn't tell parents which school to choose, what does it tell them? More than we might expect, and something harder than any single choice.

It binds parents to the formation of their children. "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). That charge does not transfer to a school, whether public, Christian or kitchen table. A family that hands a child to a Christian school and assumes the school will produce a Christian has misunderstood the command as badly as a family that hands a child to a public school and assumes the school will do no harm. Deuteronomy 6 imagines formation happening "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise," which is to say, everywhere, all the time, in the ordinary life of the household. No choice of school removes that responsibility or fulfills it.

It binds the church to share that formation. In most Christian traditions, when a child is baptized or dedicated, the whole congregation makes promises to help raise the child in the faith. That means the church is responsible for the children of the public school family, the Christian school family and the homeschool family alike, and it should be a place where children from all three are formed together.

And it binds Christians to charity toward one another on questions like this. Paul wrote to a Roman church divided over food and holy days: "One person esteems one day as better than another, while another esteems all days alike. Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind" (Romans 14:5). And a few verses earlier: "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another?" (Romans 14:4). Paul did not think the questions dividing the Romans were unimportant. He thought they were questions on which faithful people could reach different conclusions before the same Lord, and he forbade them to despise or judge each other for it.

None of us stands outside this charge. The judgment the couple at the kitchen table felt from their friends is real, and most of us have dealt it out. We make our schooling choice into a badge of seriousness and quietly read other families' choices as compromise or fear or pride. And beneath the choice there are often idols we would rather not name: the idol of achievement, which asks which school will get my child into the best college; the idol of safety, which asks which choice will guarantee that nothing bad ever reaches my child; the idol of purity, which imagines that the right environment can produce a faithful child without the risk of a child's own heart; and the idol of status, which asks what the choice says about us. Every option can be chosen for idolatrous reasons, and every option can be chosen in faith. The question for any family is not only which school but why.

That still leaves the couple with a decision to make, and Scripture's freedom doesn't make it easier. It does change the questions they should be asking. What does this particular child need, with her particular gifts and struggles? What are the actual schools available to us like, not in the abstract, but the specific public school down the street, the specific Christian school across town, our specific capacity to teach at home? What can our family sustain, financially, emotionally and spiritually, without breaking a marriage or a parent? Where are our neighbors, and what do we owe them? And whatever we choose, how will we take up the work of formation ourselves, and who in our church will help us? The answers may be different for different children in the same family, and different at different stages. Many families have used all three options at one time or another.

The strongest objection to leaving the question open deserves to be heard. Some Christians believe that the public schools, or the culture they carry, have become so hostile to the faith that sending a child there is simply unwise, and that treating the question as open is a failure of nerve. Others believe that withdrawal from public institutions is a failure of love for the neighbor, and that treating the question as open excuses a retreat. Both objections contain real conviction, and a family persuaded by one of them should act on it. But neither has the force of a biblical command, and a church that elevates either into a test of faithfulness has made a second-order question into a first-order one, which is its own kind of error.

The couple at the kitchen table will make their decision, and they may second-guess it for years. Whatever they choose, no school will do the whole work, and none can relieve them of the part Scripture gives to them. Much of their children's formation will happen in the car on the way home, at the dinner table, in the way the parents speak to each other and about their neighbors, in whether the family prays when things go wrong, in whether the children see their parents repent. "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6). That proverb is wisdom, not a guarantee, and it says nothing about which school. It speaks about the way, and the way is walked at home long before and long after any school day. The children at that table belong first to God, who loves them more than their parents do and who will be at work in them in whatever classroom they sit. The parents' task is not to find the choice that makes them safe. It is to be faithful in the choice they make, to hold it with humility, and to extend to the family across the street, who chose differently, the same grace they hope to receive.
