Your son is twelve and the algorithm has already found him. It found him faster than you did, knows his attention better than you do, and it is whispering one of two scripts into his earbuds while you are still deciding whether he is old enough for the conversation. He is going to internalize one of those scripts unless you give him something truer first. The work of raising sons in this particular decade is the work of getting to him before the feed finishes the job.

The first script says strength is dominance — get yours, dominate the room, never show the wound, treat softness as a tax the weak pay to the strong. The second script says masculinity itself is the disease — apologize for the shape of your body, distrust your own impulse to protect and build, make yourself as inoffensive as possible and call it growth. This essay is for the father, or the mother raising a boy alone, who can feel both scripts pulling at a son and does not know what to put in their place.

The culture hands your son two counterfeits and demands he choose. One inflates him. One erases him. Both are lies, and **the thing neither counterfeit can imagine is a man strong enough to lay his strength down on purpose.** That is the third thing. That is the only manhood that is both strong and able to be saved.

Not the strongman. Not the apology. The cross.

## The crisis is real, and it is old

I was raised without a father, so I learned early what the absence does. It does not produce nothing. That is the lie people tell — that a boy simply adapts, fills in, turns out fine. He builds a model of manhood out of whatever is lying around. Television. The angriest man on the block. The loudest voice on the screen. The silence where a father should have been standing to answer the questions, which the boy reads as an answer in itself. A boy cannot tolerate a vacuum where his identity is supposed to be, so he fills it with whatever is available. Right now, what is most available, most engineered to find him, and most profitable to whoever made it, is monetized rage.

The data is not subtle. A large share of American boys are growing up with fathers absent or present-but-unavailable, and the under-fathered boy is the one most exposed to the counterfeits, because he has no living model against which to measure them. He cannot tell the salesman from the father, because he has never closely watched a father. Robert Bellah, in *Habits of the Heart* (1985), traced how American individualism hollowed out the inherited institutions that once formed people into a shared and durable self. The boy used to be apprenticed into manhood by a whole community of men — uncles, neighbors, the men at work and at worship who corrected and modeled and expected. That apprenticeship has largely collapsed. What replaced it is a feed, and the feed does not want him to become a man. The feed wants him to keep scrolling.

## Why both counterfeits are theological failures

The strongman script fails because it builds a self that cannot be saved. A man who has organized his entire identity around never being weak, never being wounded, never needing anyone, has built a fortress the gospel cannot enter. The gospel comes to the weak. It comes to the one who knows he needs rescue, who has run out of his own resources and admitted it. The strongman has made himself, by definition, un-rescuable. He has armored the exact place the grace was supposed to get in. He will succeed at dominance and then starve to death inside it, and he will not be able to name what is killing him, because naming it would breach the one rule he was given.

The apology script fails for the opposite reason and lands in the same ditch. It tells the boy that the strength itself — the drive to protect, to provide, to build, to fight for something larger than himself — is a defect to be medicated and managed down. But Scripture does not ask men to surrender their strength. It asks them to crucify it and receive it back, aimed now at something worth dying for. The problem was never that men are strong. The problem is always what the strength is made to serve.

Here is the third thing, the one the feed cannot sell because there is no money in it. It is the pattern Paul describes when he says that Christ, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, and humbled himself to the point of death (Philippians 2:6–8). Power that refuses to grasp. Strength that empties itself on purpose. And it is what Jesus said outright to men arguing about who was greatest — that the rulers of the nations lord it over them, but it shall not be so among you; whoever would be great must be a servant (Mark 10:42–44). That is the directive given specifically to men reaching for status, and it inverts the entire strongman script at the root. The summary verse your son most needs to watch you live is the marriage one: *Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her* (Ephesians 5:25). Read what that verse actually demands of a man. It does not tell him to dominate, and it does not tell him to disappear. It tells him to spend his strength to the point of death on behalf of someone else. That is not weakness, and it is not domination. It is power laid down on purpose, which is the hardest thing a strong man can do and the one thing the strongman is constitutionally unable to do. The model is a man who had the strength to call down legions of angels (Matthew 26:53) and chose instead to be nailed to a board by people who were mocking him as he died. That is the manhood. It is harder than both counterfeits combined, which is exactly why neither counterfeit will ever offer it to your son.

## What a son actually learns from

He does not learn this from a lecture, and he will not learn it from this essay, and he will not learn it from a sermon. He learns it from a body in front of him doing it. Boys learn manhood the way they have always learned it — by watching a man closely and copying him, mostly without knowing they are doing either. This is the part no algorithm can replace and no curriculum can shortcut. Your son is learning what a man is from how you handle being wrong, how you speak to his mother when you are tired and the day went badly, whether you keep your word when keeping it costs you something, what you do with your anger when it has somewhere tempting to go, and whether you can apologize without it appearing to kill you.

**The cross has to be embodied before it can be taught.** If your son never watches a man lay his strength down for someone weaker, he will not believe it is possible, and he will default to the script that at least makes him feel like something rather than nothing. Bonhoeffer wrote, in *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937), that when Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die. A boy needs to watch a man die a hundred small deaths — to his ego, his comfort, his need to win the argument at the dinner table, his desire to be right more than to be close — before the word *sacrifice* means anything to him beyond a noise that happens in sermons.

## The protection he needs from you now

You cannot keep the feed away from him entirely. That war is mostly lost on the level of devices, and the parent who stakes everything on screen restriction is fighting the last war while the current one is happening inside his son. But you can do the one thing the feed structurally cannot. You can know him. The boy who is deeply known by a present father or a fierce mother is far harder for the counterfeits to capture, because he is not starving, and the counterfeits feed entirely on the starvation. They are food for a vacuum. Fill the vacuum with real presence and the whispers lose most of their power. Not all of it. Most.

And give him men. If you are a single mother, this is not a failure to confess; it is a need to meet, and meeting it is one of the strongest things you can do. Find him faithful men — an uncle, a coach, a pastor, a deacon, the kind of man you would actually want him to become — and put them in his life on purpose, not by accident, not someday. The community apprenticeship has collapsed at the cultural level, but you can rebuild a small piece of it around your specific son with enough intention. I was raised by a mother, without a father in the house, and I can tell you plainly that the men who stepped into that gap did more for me than they ever knew they were doing. A boy can be substantially fathered by men who are not his father. It takes intention, and it takes a parent humble enough to ask other people for help with the one thing she most wishes she could do alone. Ask anyway.

## Why he needs the wound permitted

There is one more thing the strongman script gets exactly backwards, and your son is absorbing the lie right now, today, while you read this. The script says a man does not feel, does not break, does not weep, does not need. And then the people who built that script stand around wondering why men are dying — by their own hand at rates that should stop the country cold, isolated, unable to name what is killing them because naming it would break the one rule. A boy taught that the wound is shameful does not stop being wounded. No one does. He just learns to bleed where no one can see it, and a wound that bleeds in secret for long enough becomes something far worse than a wound.

The man your son needs to watch is a man who can be wounded out loud. The shortest verse in Scripture is two words: *Jesus wept* (John 11:35). He wept at a grave, in public, in front of everyone who would later write it down, and the text does not treat the tears as a lapse in his masculinity that needs explaining away. It treats them as the love of God made visible in a man's face. And then — ten verses later, the same man, the same scene — he walks into the tomb and undoes death. The strength and the tears are in one man, in one passage, separated by a few sentences. That is the integration the counterfeits cannot offer and cannot even picture. The strongman has the strength and forbids the tears. The apology script permits the tears and distrusts the strength. Your son needs to see both, in one man, and the most available candidate to be that man, the one already standing in his house, is you.

## What this looks like before he can name any of it

None of this arrives in your son as theology. It arrives as instinct, built up from ten thousand small observations he is not aware he is making and could not articulate if you asked. He is watching whether you serve his mother when you are tired and no one would notice if you did not. He is watching what you do with your phone when you think you are alone, because boys read the gap between a man's public face and his private one with brutal accuracy, and they file it. He is watching whether your strength is for sale to the highest bidder or spent on the people who cannot pay you back. By the time he can put words to manhood, the words will mostly be describing what he already absorbed from a man in his house years before he had the language.

This is the part that should sober every father, including me. You are teaching your son what a man is right now, today, whether or not you have decided to, and you are teaching it most powerfully in the moments you are not performing — the tired moments, the cornered moments, the moments you lost the argument and had to choose between your pride and your child. The strongman script is seductive to fathers too, not only to twelve-year-olds, because dominance is easier than sacrifice and feels like strength while it is quietly teaching your son that love is a transaction conducted from a position of power. The apology script tempts the other kind of father, the one who confuses gentleness with absence and goes quiet exactly when his son needs him to stand up and be a wall between the boy and something. Your son needs the wall and the tenderness from the same man, and the only way he learns it is possible is to watch it in you on an ordinary day.

## The verdict

The world will offer your son a throne and a cage and tell him those are the only two doors. Hand him a cross instead, and let him watch you carry one first, on an ordinary Tuesday, when no one is filming and there is no applause for it. Strength that protects rather than dominates. Power that serves rather than performs. A man who can be wounded, can be wrong, can lay it all down, and is somehow not diminished by any of it but enlarged. That is the only manhood that can be both strong and saved, and it is the one your son will never find on the feed.

He is going to become a man either way. The clock is already running and the scripts are already playing. The only question left open is which one he is copying. Be the one worth copying. Then get out of the way, and watch what God does with a boy who was actually fathered.
