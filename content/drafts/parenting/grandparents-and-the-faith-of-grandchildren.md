Picture a grandmother in her late sixties waiting in a school pickup line on a Tuesday afternoon. She collects her two grandchildren three days a week because both of their parents work. Her daughter stopped going to church in college and married a kind man who doesn't believe in God. The children, seven and ten, have never been to a service. On the drive home the seven-year-old asks why Grandma always closes her eyes before she eats. The grandmother feels her heart speed up. She wants to tell him everything. She also remembers the evening, two years ago, when her daughter said, carefully, "Mom, please don't preach at the kids." She answers the question in a sentence, and then asks him about his spelling test, and spends the rest of the drive wondering whether she said too much or not nearly enough.

Other grandparents carry the question differently. Some have grandchildren who are raised in the faith and want to strengthen what the parents are building. Some see their grandchildren only through a screen, a thousand miles away. Some are raising their grandchildren full time because their own son or daughter can't, and find themselves doing at seventy what they thought they had finished at forty. All of them are asking some version of the same thing: what part do I have in whether these children come to know God?

This essay argues that the part is real, often larger than grandparents think, and different from the part that belongs to parents. Scripture names one grandmother whose faith outlived her, and its account of her is brief enough to be read in a breath. Her whole record is a single verse. It turns out to be enough to build a vocation on.

## A faith that dwelt first in Lois

Second Timothy presents itself as the last letter Paul wrote. He is in prison in Rome, and he knows how it will end: "For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come" (2 Timothy 4:6). He's writing to a younger man he loves, a pastor who seems to have been timid and who is facing opposition, and near the beginning he reaches back three generations. "I am reminded of your sincere faith, a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well" (2 Timothy 1:5).

The context gives the verse its weight. Acts tells us that Timothy was "the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer, but his father was a Greek" (Acts 16:1). Timothy had not been circumcised as a child, most likely because of his father, and Paul had him circumcised before taking him along, "because of the Jews who were in those places, for they all knew that his father was a Greek" (Acts 16:3). Timothy grew up in a religiously divided home. The faith that reached him came through his mother and his grandmother, in a household where the father didn't share it. The grandmother in the pickup line lives in a version of that household, and Scripture's one named grandmother lived there first.

Paul says the faith was "sincere." The Greek word means, quite literally, unhypocritical, without a mask. What reached Timothy was not a performance of faith but the thing itself, visible in two women's lives. And Paul says the faith "dwelt" in them. It's a word for living in a house, and Paul uses it again a few verses later of the Holy Spirit: "By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit entrusted to you" (2 Timothy 1:14). Faith, in this picture, is not a set of opinions passed along like an heirloom. It's something that takes up residence in a person, and through that person, in a household.

Later in the letter Paul reminds Timothy "how from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (2 Timothy 3:15). He tells him to continue in what he learned, "knowing from whom you learned it" (2 Timothy 3:14). Timothy learned the Scriptures as a child from particular people. Paul expects him to remember their faces.

But notice the most important phrase in the verse. The faith dwelt first in Lois, then in Eunice, "and now, I am sure, dwells in you as well." Paul doesn't say Timothy inherited his grandmother's faith. He says the same faith came to live in him too. That is the distinction this whole question turns on. There's a difference between transmission and inheritance. Property is inherited. It passes automatically, by law, whether the heir wants it or not. Faith is transmitted, handed from one person to another, and it has to be received, and it lives in each generation only when it takes up residence there. Lois couldn't believe for Timothy. What she could do was live a faith without a mask, teach a child the Scriptures, and leave the rest to God.

Lois appears nowhere else in the Bible. Her entire record is that her faith was sincere and that it lived on. Most grandparents will be remembered for less. It's hard to think of a better epitaph.

## What the research shows

The sociologist Vern Bengtson spent decades leading the Longitudinal Study of Generations, which followed hundreds of Southern California families across four generations beginning in 1970. His book *Families and Faith* (2013), written with Norella Putney and Susan Harris, reported findings that surprised many readers. In a culture that often assumes each generation walks away from the faith of the last, the study found more continuity across generations than expected. The strongest predictor of whether faith was passed on was not the strictness of the parents but the warmth of the relationship, and the father's warmth mattered a great deal. And grandparents turned out to have real influence. In some families, a grandchild took up a grandparent's faith even when the parents in between had set it aside, what Bengtson's team described as a skipped generation.

Christian Smith and Amy Adamczyk, in *Handing Down the Faith* (2021), drawing on large national surveys and interviews, reached a conclusion that should shape how grandparents understand their role. Parents, they found, are by far the most important influence on whether children carry their religion into adulthood, much more than churches, youth groups or schools. Parents who practice their faith, talk about it naturally at home and combine warmth with clear expectations pass it on most reliably. For grandparents this means that the first and best thing they can usually do is strengthen the parents. A grandmother who supports her daughter's marriage, helps with the children so the parents can rest, and speaks well of them in front of the grandchildren may be doing more for those children's faith than any conversation she has with them directly.

David Kinnaman and Mark Matlock, reporting on research among young adults raised in the church in *Faith for Exiles* (2019), found that those whose faith proved resilient were far more likely than others to have had close relationships with older adults in their congregation, adults who weren't their parents. This is where grandparents, and older people in general, have a place no program can supply. Children need adults who know them, have time for them and aren't responsible for enforcing bedtime.

None of these findings guarantees anything. Research describes patterns, and every family is a particular family. But taken together they suggest that grandparents are neither powerless nor in charge. They are one of the ways faith reaches a child, usually not the main one, often a decisive one, and almost never through pressure.

## How the generations came apart

For most of history, grandparents didn't have to think about how to reach their grandchildren. They lived nearby, often in the same house or the same village, and the children grew up around them. The Scriptures assume this. "Tell your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children to another generation" (Joel 1:3). The psalmist describes a chain of telling in which each generation hands the story on: "We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD, and his might, and the wonders that he has done" (Psalm 78:4), "that the next generation might know them, the children yet unborn, and arise and tell them to their children, so that they should set their hope in God" (Psalm 78:6–7).

The early church kept this pattern. Basil of Caesarea, one of the great theologians of the fourth century, credited his faith in part to his grandmother, Macrina the Elder. She had survived the persecutions, and Gregory of Nazianzus, Basil's friend, recorded that Basil's grandparents had spent years hiding in the forests during them. In one of his letters Basil recalls that she taught him as a child the words of Gregory the Wonderworker, the bishop who had first planted the faith in their region a century before, words she had carefully kept. Her grandsons Basil and Gregory of Nyssa went on to help shape the Nicene faith the whole church still confesses. A grandmother's teaching reached further than she could have imagined.

The twentieth century changed the arrangement. Families moved for work, and grandparents were often left in another state. Social Security, beginning in 1935, made it possible for older Americans to live independently, which was a real good and also meant they lived apart. In 1960 the developer Del Webb opened Sun City, Arizona, one of the first large communities restricted to older adults. Marc Freedman, who has spent his career working to reconnect the generations, argues in *How to Live Forever* (2018) that America built a society in which the old and the young rarely meet, and that both have lost something as a result. The old lost purpose. The young lost the presence of people who had lived long enough to know what matters.

The church followed the same path. From the middle of the century, congregations increasingly sorted people by age: nursery, children's church, youth group, young adults, senior adults, each with its own room, its own leaders and sometimes its own service. There were reasons for it, and some of the work done that way was excellent. But the result is that many children now grow up in church without ever having a real conversation with anyone over seventy except their own relatives, and many older Christians have no idea what the children in their own congregation are facing. We built churches in which the generations worship in the same building and rarely meet.

Wendell Berry's novel *Hannah Coulter* (2004) is narrated by an old woman looking back over her life in a Kentucky farming community, watching her children leave for cities and careers and her grandchildren grow up at a distance. Berry doesn't make her a sentimental figure. She grieves what has been lost, and she keeps faith with what Berry calls the membership, the web of people bound to one another and to a place, who remember and are remembered. The novel is a picture of what grandparents have always carried: the memory of a people, which can only be handed on if someone is there to receive it.

## When your children don't share your faith

The hardest case is the grandmother in the pickup line. Her daughter has set the faith aside, and her son-in-law never had it. She loves her grandchildren and believes their souls matter more than anything, and she has been asked, gently, not to preach at them.

There are two real goods in tension here, and each deserves its strongest statement. The first is the authority of parents. Scripture places the raising of children in the hands of their parents: "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). Even when parents don't share their own parents' faith, they are the ones God has made responsible for their children. A grandparent who goes around them, who takes the children to church without permission, contradicts their teaching behind their backs or tells the grandchildren that their parents are wrong, is undermining the very authority Scripture establishes. And as a practical matter, she will usually lose access to the grandchildren altogether. Parents who feel their authority is being subverted protect their children from the person subverting it.

The second good is the grandparent's calling to tell the next generation. The psalmist said, "So even to old age and gray hairs, O God, do not forsake me, until I proclaim your might to another generation, your power to all those to come" (Psalm 71:18). Grandchildren are persons, and the gospel is true, and a grandmother who says nothing about the most important thing in her life to children she loves has not respected them. She has hidden something from them.

The way through is witness within honor. It begins with honesty toward the parents. A grandmother might say to her daughter, plainly: "My faith matters to me, and the children are going to ask me about it. I won't go around you. When they ask, I'd like to be able to answer honestly, and I'll tell them that you and their dad see things differently. Is that all right with you?" Many parents who don't believe will agree to that, because they don't actually want their children lied to. They want them not to be pressured. The grandmother can then live her faith openly: praying before meals at her own table, keeping a Bible on the counter, telling the stories of her own life, including how God has met her. When the children ask, she can answer simply and truthfully, and add, "Your mom and dad believe something different, and you can ask them too." If the parents allow it, she can take the grandchildren to church with her. If they don't, she can pray for them by name every day, which no one can forbid.

This is a prudential question, not a doctrinal one, and families will land in different places. Some parents will welcome more than this. Some will allow less. The grandparent who can't abide any limit should ask whether her insistence is really about the children's souls or about her own need to win an argument with her daughter.

That question points to something else the grandparent may need to face. Sometimes an adult child's distance from the faith has something to do with how that child was raised. A home that was harsh, hypocritical or joyless may have taught the child to associate God with those things. Not always, and never entirely, since adult children make their own choices. But a grandparent who wants to be trusted with the grandchildren's questions may first need to have an honest conversation with her own child about what she got wrong. Repentance toward our own children is one of the most powerful testimonies we can give our grandchildren, even if they never hear about it. That subject has its own essay, [When Your Adult Child Leaves the Faith](/writing/when-your-adult-child-leaves-the-faith). And some grandparents have been cut off entirely, for reasons fair or unfair. Their calling is harder still: to pray, to keep the door open, to refuse bitterness, and to wait.

## When grandparents raise their grandchildren

A growing number of grandparents aren't picking their grandchildren up from school three days a week. They're raising them. The reasons are usually painful: a son's addiction, a daughter's incarceration or death, a household that became unsafe. The grandparents who step in are often doing it on fixed incomes, with bodies that tire more easily, while grieving their own child. They are a quiet army in the American church, and many of them have told almost no one how tired they are.

Scripture has a picture of this too. At the end of the book of Ruth, after years of famine, exile and the death of her husband and both sons, Naomi holds a grandson. "Then Naomi took the child and laid him on her lap and became his nurse" (Ruth 4:16). The women of the town had already spoken over her: "He shall be to you a restorer of life and a nourisher of your old age" (Ruth 4:15). Naomi had come home bitter, telling the town to call her by a name that meant bitterness, and her restoration came through a child she held on her lap. The child was Obed, the grandfather of David.

Grandparents raising grandchildren need practical help, and they should seek it without embarrassment. Questions about custody, guardianship and benefits differ by state, and this is not legal or financial advice. Many areas have kinship care programs and support groups specifically for grandparents raising grandchildren, and a local agency can explain what's available. If a son or daughter is struggling with addiction, the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 is free, confidential and open every day. If the weight becomes more than you can carry, call or text 988.

And the church should see them. A congregation that runs a senior adult ministry built around trips and luncheons might ask how many of its older members are raising children again, and what they need. It might be a ride, a meal, a night off, a younger couple willing to be the backup for school pickup, or simply someone who asks how they're doing and waits for a real answer.

## Simeon's arms

When Mary and Joseph brought the infant Jesus to the temple, the first people to recognize who he was were two old people. Simeon had been told he would not die before seeing the Lord's Christ. He "took him up in his arms and blessed God and said, 'Lord, now you are letting your servant depart in peace, according to your word; for my eyes have seen your salvation'" (Luke 2:28–30). Then Anna, a widow of eighty-four who had spent decades praying in the temple, came up "and began to give thanks to God and to speak of him to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem" (Luke 2:38).

Luke places the old at the threshold of the gospel. They had spent their lives waiting and praying, and when the child came they recognized him, held him and told others about him. That is close to the grandparent's calling. Grandparents are not the ones who raise most children, and they are not in charge of what their grandchildren believe. They are the ones who have waited and prayed long enough to recognize what God is doing, who can hold a child and speak a blessing over him, and who tell the story to anyone who will listen.

Simeon held Jesus as an infant and then departed in peace. He did not live to see the ministry, the cross or the empty tomb. He saw the beginning and trusted God with the rest. Many grandparents will be in the same place. They will pray for grandchildren whose faith they won't live to see. We don't know whether Lois lived to hear of the letter that named her. Her faith dwelt in her, and then in her daughter, and then in a grandson who became a pastor, and Scripture never tells us how much of that she saw.

The grandmother in the pickup line may never know either. She'll answer the seven-year-old's question honestly and briefly, and pray for him by name that night, and maybe take him to church at Easter if her daughter says yes. She'll keep her Bible on the counter where he can see it. The resurrection is the reason her waiting isn't foolish. The God who raised his Son keeps every promise across every generation, including the ones made to grandmothers who didn't live to see them kept. "Grandchildren are the crown of the aged" (Proverbs 17:6), and the crown is not the grandmother's to place. It belongs to the one who can make faith dwell in a child long after the woman who first prayed for it is gone.
