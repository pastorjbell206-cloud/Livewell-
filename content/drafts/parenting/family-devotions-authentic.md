Somebody is crying, somebody is hiding under the table, and you are reading Leviticus in a voice that does not sound like your own, trying to land an application point before the four-year-old detonates. This is family devotions. This is the thing the parenting books promised would knit your household to God. And every fiber of you suspects it is theater, and the suspicion is correct, and you keep doing it anyway because stopping feels like spiritual failure. If you have been searching for family devotions that aren't forced, start here: the problem is not your discipline, and it is not your children.

The problem is the form. You took something meant to be a meal and ran it like a program. And children, who have no defenses but excellent instincts, can smell the difference between a meal and a program from across the room — and they are building their picture of God out of whichever one you are actually serving. **A program teaches them that God is a scheduled appointment. A table teaches them that God is the air the house breathes.** You have been working hard at the wrong thing, and the hard work is exactly what makes it feel fake.

Not a program. A table.

## Where the awkwardness comes from

The model most of us inherited is essentially a small church service performed in the living room. An opening, a reading, an explanation, a closing prayer, and a quiet quality-control check on whether everyone absorbed the point. It borrows the architecture of corporate worship and shrinks it to fit the den, and then we are surprised that a den-sized worship service feels strained, because we have never once felt natural watching anyone do liturgy in a recliner.

But the household is not a small church. It is its own thing, with its own shape, and Scripture never asked it to mimic the assembly. Deuteronomy 6 is the founding text for forming faith in children, and it describes something nobody would recognize as a devotional. *You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise* (Deuteronomy 6:7). That is not a program. That is a saturation. It happens in the car, at the sink, on the walk, in the dark before sleep. It has no agenda, no closing prayer, and no quality control. It is faith woven into the ordinary hours, not extracted into a special one and quarantined there.

We took a way of life and compressed it into a fifteen-minute slot, then felt guilty when the slot felt fake. The slot was always going to feel fake. The slot was never the form. The form was the whole day.

There is a longer history under the awkwardness, and it is worth seeing, because it explains why this feels so unnatural to us specifically. For most of Christian history the household was the primary seminary. The Puritans of the seventeenth century called it the little church, and the father catechized at the hearth because there was nowhere else to send the work and no specialist to outsource it to. Then the church professionalized. The Sunday school arrived in England in the 1780s, originally to teach literacy to working children, and slowly the formation of the young migrated out of the home and into a building staffed by experts. By the time you and I were raised, the household had largely handed the discipling of its own children to the youth pastor, the curriculum, the camp. Family devotions are, in part, a guilty attempt to claw a little of that back — which is good and right. But we are trying to recover a whole way of life by importing the building's methods into the den, and the methods do not transfer. The home was never supposed to run like the institution. The home is the older thing. The institution was the borrowing.

## The corruption hiding inside the performance

When devotions become a performance, three things rot at once, quietly, while you are congratulating yourself for keeping the habit.

The children learn that faith is a thing you do in a designated block and then return to real life — that God is a scheduled meeting, not the atmosphere the house lives inside. The parents learn to measure faithfulness by whether the block happened, which means a stressful, joyless, white-knuckled block still gets counted as a win, and the counting hides the rot. And the whole household quietly learns to fake reverence on cue, to arrange their faces into worship the moment the book opens, which is the precise opposite of everything you wanted to produce.

James K. A. Smith has argued, across *Desiring the Kingdom* (2009) and *You Are What You Love* (2016), that we are formed less by what we are told than by what we repeatedly do — that our loves are shaped by liturgies, the small repeated practices that train the heart long before the mind catches up to what is happening. Which cuts both ways, and the second way is the one nobody mentions. A forced, resented, performed family devotional is also a liturgy. It is also forming someone. It is teaching, with great repetition, that God-time is the dreaded part of the day, the part you endure to get to dinner. You are forming your children faithfully and reliably. You are simply forming the wrong thing into them, night after night, and calling it discipleship.

## What a table actually does

Consider that the central practice Jesus left his people was a meal. Not a lecture. Not a curriculum. A table — bread broken, a cup poured, words said over food among people who loved and irritated each other in roughly equal measure. The theology traveled through the ordinary act of eating. And nobody at that table performed reverence. They argued, that very night, about which of them was the greatest (Luke 22:24). He fed them anyway. He washed their feet anyway. The chaos at the table did not disqualify the table. The chaos was the table.

So lower it. Read less, talk more. Read four verses and ask a real question — not a question with a known answer you are quietly fishing for, but a question you actually wonder about yourself. *What do you think God is like, after hearing that?* And then let the answer be wrong, and unfinished, and theirs. Pray for the actual things: the math test, the sick grandmother, the friend who was cruel at recess today. Sing one song badly and on purpose. Light a candle, because children remember light. Let the toddler be a toddler, and do not treat the chaos as a failure of the practice. The chaos is the household. The household is the point. There was a toddler's worth of chaos at the first table too.

Some nights it will be two minutes at the foot of a bed, and a whispered prayer, and nothing else. That counts. That is Deuteronomy 6 — *when you lie down*. The goal was never the production. The goal was the saturation, and saturation is mostly made of small ordinary moments that do not feel like anything while they are happening.

## Let the form change as the children change

The table that worked for a five-year-old will insult a fifteen-year-old, and the parent who keeps running the toddler liturgy for a teenager is teaching the teenager that faith is a children's thing one is meant to outgrow — which is exactly the conclusion the culture is already whispering to them, now confirmed at home.

With the young, you read the story and let the chaos be chaos and keep it short and warm. With the older ones, you put a hard question on the table and let them push back, and you do not win every round, and — this is the part that disciples them most — you let them watch you not need to win. A teenager who watches a parent hold a real objection without panic learns that the faith is large enough to contain the objection. A teenager who watches a parent flinch and shut it down learns the opposite, and files it away.

Eugene Peterson spent his pastoral life, including the whole argument of *The Contemplative Pastor* (1989), insisting that the spiritual formation of a person is unhurried and organic — more like the slow growth of a tree than the assembly of a machine. You cannot manufacture it on a schedule, and the harder you push for a visible result tonight, the more certainly you damage the soil. **The pressure you feel to produce a measurable spiritual outcome by bedtime is the exact pressure that ruins the practice.** Plant. Water. Wait. The growth is not your department. Paul said it plainly to a church anxious about its own results: one plants, another waters, but God gives the growth (1 Corinthians 3:6–7). The growth was always God's department. Your department is the soil and the table and your own presence at it.

## The guilt that keeps you running the program

Underneath the dread is usually guilt, and the guilt deserves to be named before it can be put down, because it is the engine driving the whole joyless machine. You feel that a faithful Christian household has formal worship every evening, that the families who have it together have it, and that your inability to sustain it is evidence of a deeper failure. So you keep restarting the program out of guilt, run it badly out of exhaustion, quit it again out of shame, and restart it a month later out of guilt — and the cycle itself teaches your children that God-time is a source of tension between their parents, an obligation perpetually being failed.

The guilt is mostly counterfeit. It comes from comparison, not from Scripture. Deuteronomy 6 does not prescribe a nightly service; it prescribes a saturated life, and a saturated life has no attendance sheet to fail. The family that talks about God in the car and prays over the actual crises and reads a little when it can and lets the form breathe is not a less faithful version of the family with the immaculate evening liturgy. It may well be the more faithful one, because it has refused to trade the saturation for the performance. Jesus reserved his sharpest words for people who had perfected the religious performance and lost the thing it was supposed to carry — *you tithe your herbs and have neglected the weightier matters* (Matthew 23:23). A household can keep a flawless devotional schedule and neglect the weightier matter, which is a child who actually knows that God is near and that grace is real and that the people who love him most also love God. Keep the weightier matter. Let the schedule serve it or get out of its way.

## The thing you are actually building

You are not building a child who can recite the catechism on command, though that may come, and there is nothing wrong with it. You are building a child for whom God is not a department of life but the atmosphere of it — woven into the meal, the car, the bedtime, the ordinary fight and the ordinary forgiveness that follows it. That child grows up structurally unable to picture a life with God walled off into a fifteen-minute slot, because they never once knew him that way. They knew him at the table. They knew him in the talking by the way and the lying down and the rising.

And here is the relief buried in all of it, for the parent who quit the formal devotional in shame when it collapsed into chaos for the hundredth time. You are not behind. You have not failed. The most ordinary parts of your day — the drive to school, the dishes, the question whispered in the dark before sleep — were always the real curriculum, and you have been teaching it whether you meant to or not. The question was never whether you would form your child's faith in the ordinary hours. You already are. You have been all along. The only question left is what you are saying in them.

## The verdict

Stop running a service. Set a table. Read a little, ask honestly, pray for the real things, sing one song badly, and let the household be a household while you do it. Saturation, not production. Presence, not performance. The slot was always going to feel forced, because the slot was never the form.

The form was your whole house — talked through, walked through, lived in front of them in the ordinary hours nobody thinks to count. That is what they will remember when they are grown and the catechism answers have faded. Not the program. The table they were always welcome at, no matter the day they had, and the God who was somehow always already there before anyone opened a book.
