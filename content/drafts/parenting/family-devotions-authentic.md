Somebody is crying, somebody is hiding under the table, and you are reading Leviticus in a voice that does not sound like yours, trying to land an application point before the four-year-old detonates. This is family devotions. This is the thing the books promised would knit your household to God. And every fiber of you suspects it is theater, and the suspicion is correct, and you keep doing it because stopping feels like spiritual failure. I want to talk to the parent who dreads the thing they are supposed to love.

The problem is not your discipline. The problem is the form. You took something that was meant to be a meal and ran it like a program. And children, who have no defenses but excellent instincts, can smell the difference between a meal and a program from across the room.

Not a program. A table.

## Where the awkwardness comes from

The model most of us inherited is essentially a small church service performed in the living room. An opening, a reading, an explanation, a closing prayer, a quality-control check on whether everyone absorbed the point. It borrows the architecture of corporate worship and shrinks it to fit the den.

But the household is not a small church. It is its own thing, with its own shape, and Scripture never asked it to mimic the assembly. Deuteronomy 6 is the founding text, and it describes something nobody would recognize as a devotional. "You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise." That is not a program. That is a saturation. It happens in the car, at the sink, on the walk, in the dark before sleep. It has no agenda and no closing prayer and no quality control. It is faith woven into the ordinary hours, not extracted into a special one.

We took a way of life and compressed it into a fifteen-minute slot, and then felt guilty when the slot felt fake. The slot was always going to feel fake. The slot was never the form.

There is a longer history under the awkwardness, too. For most of Christian history the household was the primary seminary. The Puritans of the seventeenth century called it the little church, and the father catechized at the hearth because there was nowhere else to send the work. Then the church professionalized. Sunday school arrived in the late 1700s, originally to teach literacy to working children, and slowly the formation of the young migrated out of the home and into a building staffed by specialists. By the time you and I were raised, the household had largely outsourced the discipling of its own children to the youth pastor, the curriculum, the camp. Family devotions are, in part, a guilty attempt to claw a little of that back — which is good. But we are trying to recover a whole way of life by importing the building's methods into the den, and the methods do not transfer. The home was never supposed to run like the institution. The home is the older thing. The institution was the borrowing.

## The corruption of the performance

When devotions become a performance, three things rot at once. The children learn that faith is a thing you do in a designated block and then return to real life — that God is a scheduled appointment, not the air the house breathes. The parents learn to measure faithfulness by whether the block happened, which means a stressful, joyless, white-knuckled block still counts as a win. And the whole household learns to fake reverence on cue, which is the precise opposite of what you wanted.

James K. A. Smith has argued that we are formed less by what we are told than by what we repeatedly do — that our loves are shaped by liturgies, the small repeated practices that train the heart before the mind catches up. Which cuts both ways. A forced, resented, performed devotional is also a liturgy. It is teaching something. It is teaching that God-time is the dreaded part of the day. You are forming them. You are just forming the wrong thing.

## What a table actually does

Consider that the central practice Jesus left his people was a meal. Not a lecture. A table, bread broken, wine poured, words said over food among people who love and irritate each other. The theology travels through the ordinary act. Nobody at that table performed reverence. They argued about who was greatest. He fed them anyway.

So lower it. Read less, talk more. Read four verses and ask a real question — not a question with a known answer you are fishing for, but a question you actually wonder about. *What do you think God is like, after hearing that?* Let the answer be wrong and unfinished and theirs. Pray for the actual things: the math test, the sick grandmother, the friend who was cruel today. Sing one song badly. Light a candle. Let the toddler be a toddler and do not treat the chaos as a failure of the practice. The chaos is the household. The household is the point.

Some nights it will be two minutes at the foot of a bed. That counts. That is Deuteronomy 6 — when you lie down. The goal was never the production. The goal was the saturation.

And let the form change as the children change. The table that worked for a five-year-old will insult a fifteen-year-old, and the parent who keeps running the toddler liturgy for a teenager is teaching the teenager that faith is a thing for children that one outgrows. With the young, you read the story and let the chaos be chaos. With the older ones, you put a hard question on the table and let them push back, and you do not win every round, and you let them see you not need to win. Eugene Peterson spent his life insisting that the spiritual formation of a person is unhurried, organic, more like the growth of a tree than the assembly of a machine. You cannot manufacture it on a schedule. You can only keep the soil good and keep returning to the table and trust the slow work to a God who is not in the hurry you are in. The pressure you feel to produce a visible result tonight is the exact pressure that ruins the practice. Plant. Water. Wait. The growth is not your department.

## The thing you are actually building

You are not building a child who can recite the catechism on command, though that may come. You are building a child for whom God is not a department of life but the atmosphere of it — woven into the meal, the car, the bedtime, the ordinary fight and the ordinary forgiveness. That child grows up unable to picture a life with God walled off in a fifteen-minute slot, because they never knew him that way. They knew him at the table. They knew him in the talking by the way.

And here is the relief in it. You are not behind. You have not failed because the formal devotional collapsed into chaos and you quit it in shame. The most ordinary parts of your day — the drive to school, the dishes, the question whispered in the dark before sleep — were always the real curriculum, and you have been teaching it whether you meant to or not. The question was never whether you would form your child's faith in the ordinary hours. You already are. The only question is what you are saying in them.

## The verdict

Stop running a service. Set a table. Read a little, ask honestly, pray for the real things, and let the household be a household while you do it. Saturation, not production. Presence, not performance.

The slot was always going to feel forced, because the slot was never the form. The form was your whole house, talked through, walked through, lived in front of them in the ordinary hours. That is what they will remember. Not the program. The table they were always welcome at, and the God who was somehow always already there.
