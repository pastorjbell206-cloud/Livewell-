You are putting your child to bed and she asks if God is real, and you do not know what to say, because some nights you are not sure yourself. So you say the line. The confident one. The one you do not fully believe tonight. And you lie there afterward feeling like a fraud who just handed his fraud to the next generation. I want to talk to the parent in that bed.

The fear underneath it is specific. You believe your job is to give your child certainty, and you do not have certainty to give, so you manufacture it, and the manufacturing feels like betrayal. The fear assumes that doubt is the contaminant and confidence is the cure. The fear has it backwards.

Not the doubt. The hiding.

## What you think you are protecting them from

The standard evangelical instinct is to present children with an unbroken front. Mom and Dad are sure. God is good, the Bible is true, prayer works, no follow-up questions. The instinct comes from love. We think a child needs solid ground, and we think solid ground means we cannot show a crack.

But children are not building their faith on your conclusions. They are building it on your relationship to your conclusions. A child can tell the difference between a parent who believes something and a parent who is reciting something. They cannot always name the difference. They can always feel it. And what they feel, when you recite, is that the faith is a script — that the real adult life happens somewhere behind the script, in a room they are not allowed into.

So they leave. Not in rebellion. In disappointment. They conclude the faith was a children's program, and they have outgrown the program, and the adults were just better at pretending. Christian Smith's research traced exactly this exit — a generation walking out not in crisis but in quiet, having decided the thing was never load-bearing to begin with.

## What honesty actually sounds like

There is a difference between *I have doubts, so who knows* and *I have doubts, and here is why I still believe*. The first is abandonment dressed as honesty. The second is faith doing its actual work in front of a watching child.

The second sounds like this. *I wonder about that too. Some nights I am not sure either. Here is what keeps me here: that the world feels made and not random, that the man Jesus said things no one else has matched, that I have known a presence I cannot explain away. I do not have it nailed down. I am staying anyway.* That is not weakness handed to a child. That is a working model of how a grown person holds faith — with both hands, including the hand that trembles.

Thomas, in John 20, says he will not believe without the wounds. He says it inside the room of the disciples, not outside it. Jesus does not throw him out. Jesus comes back and offers the wounds. The doubt was processed inside the community of faith, not exiled from it. That is the home you are trying to build. The room where the doubt is allowed to be spoken, because the One being doubted is secure enough to take it.

## The Psalms already gave you the script

We act as if honest faith is a modern problem the ancients never had. Open the Psalms. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me." "How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?" These are not the prayers of people without faith. They are the prayers inside the book of faith, canonized, sung, handed down. The tradition built a place for the complaint and put it in the hymnal.

Somewhere we decided children should only be taught the grateful prayers. We cut them off from half the Psalter and called it protection. Then we wondered why, when their own grief arrived, they had no language for praying through it and no model for staying. Teach your child to pray *how long*. Teach her that the question is not the end of faith. The question is in the Bible.

Walter Brueggemann spent decades arguing that the Psalms move through a pattern the modern church has tried to skip — orientation, then disorientation, then a new orientation on the far side. We want to raise children who go straight from the first to the third, who are settled and then more settled, who never have to pass through the dark middle where the old certainties come apart. But there is no road around the middle. There is only the road through it. The faith that has never been disoriented is a faith that has never grown up. When you hide your own disorientation from your child, you are not sparing her the middle. You are only making sure she has to cross it alone, with no one ahead of her on the path who has been there and come out.

## Why your certainty was never the point

Consider where certainty even comes from in a child. For the first years, it comes from you. You are the floor of the world. If you say God is real, God is real, the way the kitchen is real. But that borrowed certainty has an expiration date built into it, and it expires somewhere in adolescence, when the child discovers you are a person who can be wrong about other things and reasons, correctly, that you might be wrong about this one too. The faith that was propped entirely on your authority falls when your authority is rightly demoted from godlike to human. This is not a malfunction. It is supposed to happen. A faith that stays propped on a parent forever is not faith. It is dependence wearing faith's clothes.

So the question was never whether you could hand down certainty. You cannot. The borrowed kind always expires, and the manufactured kind expires faster, because the child can feel the manufacturing. The question is whether, when the borrowed certainty runs out, your child has watched a real adult hold real faith through real doubt — and so has some idea of how it is done. You are not the source of their faith. You are the demonstration of how a grown person carries one.

## The thing your uncertainty cannot do

Here is what should free you. Your child's faith does not finally rest on your certainty. It rests on God. You are not the bridge they have to cross to get to him; you are a witness pointing past yourself. The witness does not have to be airtight. The witness has to be honest about what he has seen.

I came to faith from atheism. Nobody argued me across with a perfect case. What moved me was watching people who held the faith without pretending it was easy — who admitted the cost, admitted the questions, and stayed. The pretending would have confirmed my suspicion that it was all theater. The honesty was the only thing that left a door open. Your honesty is the door. Your performance is the wall.

And there is mercy in this for you, the tired parent in the dark bedroom. You have been carrying a weight that was never yours to carry, the weight of being the guarantor of your child's faith, the one who must have it nailed down so they can stand on you. Set it down. You are a witness, not a foundation. The foundation is older than you and will outlast you and held you up on the nights you doubted, too. Tell your child the truth and trust the truth to a God who has been keeping people through their doubts since long before you had any.

## The verdict

Tell your child the truth about where you are. Tell her what you doubt and tell her why you have not left. Let her watch a grown person carry a real faith — not a stainless one, a real one, with weight and wear and a few cracks where the light gets in.

She will not inherit your certainty. You do not have certainty to give, and certainty was never the inheritance anyway. She will inherit how you held the uncertainty. She will inherit whether you stayed. That is what goes down. Give her the staying. The staying is the faith.
