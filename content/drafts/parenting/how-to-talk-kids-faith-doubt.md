You are putting your child to bed and she asks if God is real, and you do not know what to say, because some nights you are not sure yourself. So you say the line. The confident one. The one you do not fully believe tonight. And you lie there afterward feeling like a fraud who just handed his fraud to the next generation, wondering whether talking to your kids about faith honestly is even possible when the faith you are passing down has cracks you can see by daylight.

The fear underneath the question is specific. You believe your job is to give your child certainty, and you do not have certainty to give, so you manufacture it, and the manufacturing feels like betrayal. The fear assumes that doubt is the contaminant and confidence is the cure — that if you can just keep your wobble off your face, the faith will pass down clean. The fear has it exactly backwards. **It is not your doubt that endangers your child's faith. It is the hiding.** The doubt, spoken honestly, is survivable and even formative. The performance is the thing that teaches a child the faith is theater.

Not the doubt. The hiding.

## What you think you are protecting them from

The standard evangelical instinct is to present children with an unbroken front. Mom and Dad are sure. God is good, the Bible is true, prayer works, no follow-up questions accepted at this counter. The instinct comes from love. We think a child needs solid ground, and we think solid ground means we cannot show a crack, and so we caulk over every fracture before the children can see in.

But children are not building their faith on your conclusions. They are building it on your relationship to your conclusions. A child can tell the difference between a parent who believes something and a parent who is reciting something. They cannot always name the difference. They can always feel it. And what they feel, when you recite, is that the faith is a script — that the real adult life is happening somewhere behind the script, in a room they have not been allowed into, and that the script is what you say to children until they are old enough to be let backstage.

So they leave. Not always in rebellion. Often in disappointment. They conclude the faith was a children's program, that they have outgrown the program, and that the adults were simply better at maintaining the set. Christian Smith's research, across *Soul Searching* (2005) and *Souls in Transition* (2009), traced exactly this exit — a generation walking out not in dramatic crisis but in quiet, having decided somewhere along the way that the thing was never load-bearing to begin with. They did not lose an argument. They concluded there had never been a real argument, only a performance, and they stopped attending.

## What honesty actually sounds like when you talk to kids about doubt

There is a difference — and your whole task lives inside it — between *I have doubts, so who knows* and *I have doubts, and here is why I still believe*. The first is abandonment dressed as honesty. It hands a child the weight without the witness. The second is faith doing its actual work in front of a watching child.

The second sounds something like this. *I wonder about that too. Some nights I am not sure either. Here is what keeps me here: that the world feels made and not random, that the man Jesus said things no one else has matched, that I have known a presence I cannot explain away when I have tried to. I do not have it nailed down. I am staying anyway.* That is not weakness handed to a child. That is a working model of how a grown person holds faith — with both hands, including the hand that trembles. A child who watches that does not learn that faith is fragile. A child who watches that learns that faith is something adults actually do, with real weight, and not something they merely recite to children at bedtime.

Thomas, in John 20, says he will not believe without the wounds. Notice where he says it. He says it inside the room of the disciples, not outside it. He has not left. And Jesus does not throw him out for the demand. Jesus comes back and offers the wounds — *put your finger here* (John 20:27). The doubt was processed inside the community of faith, not exiled from it. That is the home you are trying to build. The room where the doubt is allowed to be spoken out loud, because the One being doubted is secure enough to take it, and so is the parent.

## The Psalms already wrote the script for you

We act as if honest faith is a modern problem the ancients never had — as if doubt is a contemporary contaminant the Bible never anticipated. Open the Psalms. *My God, my God, why have you forsaken me* (Psalm 22:1). *How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?* (Psalm 13:1). These are not the prayers of people without faith. They are the prayers inside the book of faith, canonized, set to music, sung in the assembly, handed down across three thousand years. The tradition built a place for the complaint and put it in the hymnal on purpose.

Somewhere we decided children should only be taught the grateful prayers. We cut them off from half the Psalter and called it protection. Then we wondered why, when their own grief finally arrived — and it always arrives — they had no language for praying through it and no model for staying inside a faith that was not currently delivering. Teach your child to pray *how long*. Teach her that the question is not the exit from faith but a room inside it, and that the room is in the Bible, and that people holier than either of you have prayed from it.

Walter Brueggemann spent decades — most directly in *The Message of the Psalms* (1984) — arguing that the Psalms move through a pattern the modern church keeps trying to skip: orientation, then disorientation, then a new orientation on the far side of the dark. We want to raise children who go straight from the first to the third, who are settled and then more settled, who never pass through the middle where the old certainties come apart. But there is no road around the middle. There is only the road through it. A faith that has never been disoriented is a faith that has never grown up. When you hide your own disorientation from your child, you are not sparing her the middle. You are only guaranteeing she crosses it alone, with no one ahead of her on the path who has been there and come out the other side.

## Why your certainty was never the point

Consider where certainty even comes from in a child. For the first years, it comes from you. You are the floor of the world. If you say God is real, then God is real, the way the kitchen is real and the morning is real and you are real. But that borrowed certainty has an expiration date built into it, and it expires somewhere in adolescence, when the child discovers you are a person who can be wrong about other things and reasons — correctly — that you might be wrong about this one too. The faith that was propped entirely on your authority falls the moment your authority is rightly demoted from godlike to human. This is not a malfunction in your child. It is supposed to happen. A faith that stays propped on a parent forever is not faith. It is dependence wearing faith's clothes, and it will not survive contact with an adult life.

So the question was never whether you could hand down certainty. You cannot. The borrowed kind always expires, and the manufactured kind expires faster, because the child can feel the manufacturing the whole time. The real question is whether, when the borrowed certainty runs out — and it will — your child has watched a real adult hold a real faith through real doubt, and so has some working idea of how the thing is actually done. **You are not the source of your child's faith. You are the demonstration of how a grown person carries one.** That is a smaller job than you have been carrying, and a truer one.

## What kind of doubt you are carrying

Not all doubt is the same, and the kind you are carrying changes what you owe your child. There is the doubt of the worn-out believer — the dark stretch where God feels absent, where prayer feels like talking to the ceiling, where you still believe in your bones but cannot currently feel any of it. That is the doubt of the Psalms, the doubt of nearly every saint who left a record, and it is safe to bring to the dinner table in age-appropriate honesty, because it is faith in its winter rather than faith in its grave. Your child can watch you walk through that and learn something they will need.

There is also the harder kind — the doubt that has moved into the foundations, where you are genuinely unsure whether the central claims are true and have been for some time. That kind deserves honesty too, but it also deserves your own attention before you make your child the audience for it. If you are in that place, the most loving thing is not to perform a confidence you have abandoned, and it is also not to narrate your unraveling to a ten-year-old as though they were your confessor. It is to find another adult — a pastor, a wise friend, someone who has been through the cold — and face it in a room built for it, so that what your child sees is not a parent in freefall but a parent taking the question seriously enough to pursue it. Even there, the honest sentence is available: *I am wrestling hard with this right now, and I am taking it seriously, and I have not let go.* That is true, and it is survivable for a child to hear, and it models the one thing that matters most: that you do not run from the hard thing, and you do not pretend it away. Jesus himself, in the garden, did not pretend the cup was easy — *let this cup pass from me* (Matthew 26:39) — and then did not let go. The wrestling and the staying were in the same prayer.

## The thing your uncertainty cannot do

Here is what should loosen the knot in your chest. Your child's faith does not finally rest on your certainty. It rests on God. You are not the bridge they have to cross to reach him; you are a witness pointing past yourself toward someone older and steadier than you. The witness does not have to be airtight. The witness only has to be honest about what he has actually seen.

I came to faith from atheism. Nobody argued me across the line with a flawless case, and a flawless case would have made me more suspicious, not less. What moved me was watching people who held the faith without pretending it was easy — who admitted the cost, admitted the questions, and stayed anyway. The pretending would have confirmed everything I already suspected, that the whole thing was a posture people kept up in front of each other. The honesty was the only thing that left a door open. Your honesty is the door. Your performance is the wall, and the wall is the thing they climb over on their way out.

And there is mercy in this for you, the tired parent in the dark bedroom. You have been carrying a weight that was never assigned to you — the weight of being the guarantor of your child's faith, the one who must have it nailed down so they can stand on you. Set it down. You are a witness, not a foundation. The foundation is older than you, will outlast you, and held you up on the very nights you doubted hardest. The God Jesus described receives the prayer *I believe; help my unbelief* (Mark 9:24) and heals the child anyway, in the same breath that admits the unbelief. Tell your child the truth about where you are, and then trust that truth to a God who has been keeping people through their doubts since long before you had any of your own.

## The verdict

Tell your child the truth about where you are. Tell her what you doubt, and tell her why you have not left. Let her watch a grown person carry a real faith — not a stainless one, a real one, with weight and wear and a few cracks where, as it turns out, the light gets in.

She will not inherit your certainty. You do not have certainty to give, and certainty was never the inheritance anyway. She will inherit how you held the uncertainty. She will inherit whether you stayed. That is the thing that goes down a generation. Give her the staying. The staying is the faith — and it is the one part of this you can actually give her, because it is the one part that was never about being sure.
