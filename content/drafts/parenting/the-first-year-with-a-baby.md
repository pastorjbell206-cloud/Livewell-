Picture a kitchen at three in the morning. The only light is the one over the stove, because the overhead light would wake the baby all the way up, and a mother is standing there with a bottle in one hand and her phone in the other, reading about sleep regressions while her son, eleven weeks old, finally drifts. Down the hall her husband is asleep, or pretending to be, and she knows exactly how many times he has gotten up this week, because she has been counting. He has been counting too. Tomorrow at breakfast neither of them will say the number out loud, but it will sit on the table between them next to the coffee. They love this child with a ferocity that surprises them. They are not sure they still like each other. And somewhere underneath both of those facts is a third one they haven't said to anyone, which is that they haven't prayed in a month, not really, and they don't know whether that means something is wrong with their faith or only that they are tired.

If that kitchen is yours, you probably already know that the advice available to you comes in two flavors. The first is practical: sleep schedules, swaddles, a chart for splitting the night feedings. Much of it is good, and none of it touches the thing that frightens you most. The second is spiritual: children are a gift, cherish every moment, these days go by so fast. It is also true, and when it is said to someone who hasn't slept in four hours at a stretch since spring, it lands as an accusation. What almost nobody tells new parents is that the strain they feel is ordinary, that it has causes older than their marriage, and that the Christian faith has something more to say to it than a verse on a nursery wall. This essay tries to say that something, starting with the plain facts and working down.

## What the research found

It helps to know that the kitchen at three in the morning is one of the best-studied places in family science. Carolyn Pape Cowan and Philip Cowan followed couples from pregnancy into the early years of parenthood and published what they found in *When Partners Become Parents* (1992). Their couples were not failing marriages. They were ordinary, hopeful people, and most of them reported more conflict and less satisfaction with their marriages after the baby came than before. Among the Cowans' most telling findings was that the division of work drifted, within months, toward a more traditional arrangement than either spouse had planned, and the gap between the plan and the reality became its own grievance. That drift is its own subject, taken up in this site's essay on [the mental load in marriage](/writing/emotional-labor-in-marriage).

John Gottman's lab took up the same question. In a study published in 2000, Alyson Shapiro, Gottman and Sybil Carrère followed newlywed couples for several years, some of whom became parents and some of whom did not. About two-thirds of the new mothers reported a decline in their satisfaction with the marriage, against roughly half of the wives who stayed childless. The more interesting finding concerned the other third. What distinguished the mothers whose satisfaction held steady was not the absence of stress. It was a husband who kept expressing fondness for his wife and stayed aware of her and of the marriage, and a wife who stayed aware of her husband in turn, two people still paying attention to each other's inner world while the baby consumed their outer one. Gottman and Julie Schwartz Gottman later built a book for new parents on that research, *And Baby Makes Three* (2007).

The broader studies point the same way. A meta-analysis led by Jean Twenge and W. Keith Campbell in 2003 found that parents, on average, report lower marital satisfaction than couples without children, and that the gap is widest for mothers of infants. Brian Doss and his colleagues, following couples for eight years in a study published in 2009, found that marriages without children also tend to decline slowly over time, but that couples who became parents showed a sharper drop right after the birth. None of this proves that children are bad for marriages, and the researchers never claimed it did. It proves something more useful. The strain in your kitchen is predictable. It has a shape. It happens to people who love each other and love their children. You are not uniquely failing.

That matters, because the first lie exhaustion tells is that your situation is a verdict. The fight about the dishwasher is evidence that you married the wrong person. The month without prayer is evidence that your faith was never real. The resentment you feel toward the person asleep down the hall is evidence of who you really are. The research can't tell you what your life means, but it can take away that particular lie. What you are feeling is what nearly every couple feels in this year. The question is not whether the strain comes. It is what you build while it's here.

## How new parents ended up so alone

The research explains that the first year is hard. It doesn't explain why it is harder for us than it had to be, and for that you have to go back further than your marriage.

For most of human history a baby was not born into a couple. It was born into a household, and the household was larger than two people and more crowded than we would find comfortable: grandparents, unmarried aunts, older siblings, servants in wealthier homes, neighbors who wandered in and out. The household was also an economy. People worked where they lived, and the work of feeding and minding children was folded into the work of the farm or the shop, shared among many hands, most of which belonged to someone other than the mother. Nobody romanticizes the conditions. Infant mortality was brutal, and the crowded household had its own cruelties. But a new mother in 1750 was rarely alone with a newborn for ten hours a day, and a new father was rarely asked to be the entire support system of another adult who had just been through childbirth.

Industrialization pulled work out of the home in the nineteenth century, and the twentieth century finished the job of shrinking the household. The postwar suburb, with its single-family houses set on their own lots, was a real achievement in comfort and privacy, and it came with a cost nobody itemized at the time. David Brooks made the case in *The Atlantic* in 2020, under the title "The Nuclear Family Was a Mistake," that the detached two-parent household of the 1950s was a historical anomaly, sustainable only for a brief period when extended kin still lived nearby and churches and neighborhoods were thick with ordinary help. When those supports thinned, the nuclear family was left carrying loads it was never built to carry alone. You don't have to accept every part of Brooks's argument to recognize the kitchen at three in the morning in it. Two people, often hundreds of miles from their parents, often new to their town, are now expected to do what used to be done by a village, and to do it while both of them hold down jobs.

The church, if we are honest, has not been much better at this than the culture. We are good at the beginning. We throw the baby shower, we bring meals for a week or two, and we celebrate the dedication or the baptism with real joy. Then, in most congregations, the new parents quietly disappear from our field of vision for about two years. They miss services because the baby is sick. They drop out of the small group because it meets at bedtime. When they do come, they spend the sermon in the hallway or the nursery. We tend to read their absence as a lapse in commitment, and they tend to read our silence as proof that nobody noticed. Both readings are partly right. The early church was remembered by its pagan neighbors for how it cared for the vulnerable, and it would be hard to find anyone more vulnerable in a modern congregation than a couple three months into their first child, running on no sleep and too proud or too tired to ask. We who have already come through that year are the ones who forgot it fastest.

So when you feel alone, you are not imagining it. You are living at the end of a long historical process that stripped away the help a newborn's parents used to have, inside churches that often haven't yet noticed what was stripped. That doesn't fix anything tonight. It does change what you should be ashamed of. The loneliness is not a personal failure. It is a structural one, and it has a structural answer, which we'll come to.

## The faith that falls asleep in the chair

The quietest grief of the first year is often spiritual. Many Christians enter parenthood with some kind of devotional rhythm, a morning quiet time, a prayer list, a Bible reading plan, and within weeks it is gone. The morning belongs to the baby now. The Bible sits on the nightstand under a burp cloth. When you do try to pray, you fall asleep in the chair halfway through the first sentence, and when you wake up you feel guilty about that too.

It is worth asking where the guilt comes from. Much of the devotional practice evangelicals inherited assumes a particular kind of life: an adult with control over the first hour of the day, a quiet room, and enough mental margin to read and reflect. That practice has done real good, and nothing here is an argument against it. But it is not the only form of Christian devotion, and it was never available to most Christians who have ever lived, including most mothers of infants in every century. When the only picture of a faithful life you have is one that requires a quiet hour, the loss of the quiet hour feels like the loss of God.

Martin Luther took aim at exactly this confusion in *The Estate of Marriage* (1522). Against the reason that looks at a household with a baby and sees only sleepless drudgery, he insisted that the same despised chores, done in faith, please God as truly as any prayer said in a cloister. That was a radical claim in a church that had taught for centuries that the vowed celibate stood higher on the ladder of holiness than the parent. It remains a radical claim in churches that have quietly transferred the ladder from the monastery to the morning quiet time.

A century and a half later, a Carmelite lay brother in Paris named Lawrence, who spent years working in his monastery's kitchen, left behind conversations and letters that were gathered after his death and published as *The Practice of the Presence of God* (1692). His whole teaching comes down to the claim that the time of work need not differ from the time of prayer, that a man could be as near to God among the pots and the clatter of the kitchen as on his knees before the sacrament. He did not mean that set prayer is unnecessary. He meant that God is not waiting for you to get free of your duties before he will be present to you. He is already in the kitchen at three in the morning.

Scripture says the same thing more plainly. "It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep" (Psalm 127:2). That is the verse just before the one about children being a heritage from the Lord, and it is not an accident that they sit together. The psalm is telling parents that the household does not finally rest on their exertion. When the prophet Elijah, running for his life, sat down in the wilderness and asked God to take it, the first thing God did was not to correct his theology. "And he lay down and slept under a broom tree. And behold, an angel touched him and said to him, 'Arise and eat'" (1 Kings 19:5). There was bread and water by his head. He ate and slept again, and the angel woke him a second time and fed him again, because the road ahead was more than he could manage on his own strength. God's first answer to his exhausted servant was food and rest. Only later, on the mountain, did the conversation turn to what Elijah believed.

None of this means the first year is a spiritual vacation. It means the shape of faithfulness changes. It may look like a single psalm read aloud to a baby who understands none of it and a parent who needs every word. It may look like the Lord's Prayer said once, slowly, in the middle of the night, because it's the only prayer you can remember when you're this tired. It may look like the discipline of getting to church even when you'll spend the sermon in the hallway, because the point of being there was never only to hear the sermon. And it may look like letting the psalmist's confidence carry you when your own is gone: "Behold, he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Someone is keeping watch over this house tonight, and it isn't you. That is why you are allowed to close your eyes.

## Two people who used to be a couple

The marriage question is the one most couples find hardest to talk about, because it feels disloyal to the baby. You're supposed to be happy. You are happy, in a way. You are also lonelier in your marriage than you've ever been, and more irritated with the person you promised to love than you ever expected to be.

Some of the irritation is chemistry and sleep, and it will pass. Some of it is the scorekeeping that the Cowans found in their couples: two people, each quietly convinced they are carrying more, each keeping a ledger the other can't see. And some of it is deeper. A child changes both of you, and it changes you in different directions at different speeds. The woman who gave birth is recovering from a physical ordeal her husband can sympathize with but never share. The man who is trying to be a good father may feel, without being able to say it, that he has been demoted in his own home. Each of you is meeting a new version of the other, and neither of you asked for the introduction.

Stanley Hauerwas made the point, in a 1978 essay on sex and marriage that Tim and Kathy Keller later made famous in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011), that we always marry the wrong person. He didn't mean that everyone chooses badly. He meant that we never really know whom we're marrying, and that even if we did, marriage itself would change them into someone else. The first year of parenthood is that principle compressed into twelve months. You are not married to the person you married. Neither is your spouse. The Kellers' answer, drawn from Hauerwas and from Scripture, is that the task of marriage is not to find a person who stays the same but to learn to love the stranger you are married to, again and again.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer said something that belongs next to this. In May 1943, sitting in a Nazi prison, he wrote a sermon for the wedding of his friend Eberhard Bethge and his niece Renate Schleicher, which he could not attend. In it he wrote, "It is not your love that sustains the marriage, but from now on, the marriage that sustains your love." The line is usually read at weddings. It belongs, if anywhere, in the kitchen at three in the morning. There will be weeks this year when your feelings for your spouse are thin, when affection is buried under fatigue and resentment and the plain fact that neither of you has had a real conversation since the baby came home. Bonhoeffer's point is that the marriage is not a feeling that must be kept alive by your effort. It is a covenant, made before God, and the covenant holds you up when your feelings can't. The promise you made on your wedding day was made for exactly this season.

That doesn't mean you do nothing. The couples in Gottman's study whose marriages held steady give a clue to what faithfulness looks like here, and it is less dramatic than you might expect. It looks like a husband who says out loud what he admires in his wife, not once but repeatedly, even when both of them are too tired to make much of it. It looks like two people asking each other, in the ten minutes they have, what the day was actually like, and listening to the answer. It looks like confessing the ledger. "I've been keeping score, and I'm sorry" is one of the most healing sentences a new parent can say, precisely because it's so hard to say. It looks like asking for help from outside the marriage before the resentment calcifies, which means admitting to your parents or your small group or your pastor that you are not managing as well as your photos suggest.

Paul told the Galatians, "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). In the first year, the first person whose burden you're asked to bear is the one lying next to you, who is carrying a load you can't fully see. The second thing Paul's sentence implies is harder for proud people: you have to let someone see your burden before they can bear it.

## When it is more than tired

There is a line between the ordinary exhaustion and sadness of the first year and something that needs more than rest and a kind word, and it is important to say plainly where that line is.

Postpartum depression is not a spiritual failure and it is not rare. Nor is it only a mother's struggle. A meta-analysis by James Paulson and Sharnail Bazemore, published in *JAMA* in 2010, found that about one in ten fathers showed signs of depression in the period around a child's birth, and that a father's depression was linked to the mother's. If you or your spouse feel a heaviness that doesn't lift for more than a couple of weeks, if you can't sleep even when the baby sleeps, if you feel nothing toward the child or feel that the child would be better off without you, if you're having frightening thoughts that won't leave, or if you're thinking about harming yourself or anyone else, please don't wait for it to pass and don't try to pray it away alone. Tell your doctor or your midwife or your child's pediatrician. In the United States, the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline is available at any hour at 1-833-852-6262, by call or text, and Postpartum Support International's helpline, a support line rather than a crisis line, is 1-800-944-4773. If you are thinking of ending your life, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, now.

This is not medical advice, and no essay can diagnose you. But the church has sometimes taught, by implication if not in words, that a Christian who is struggling with depression after a baby is failing to trust God. That teaching has hurt people. The Scriptures are full of faithful people who were at the end of themselves, and God's answer was not to shame them but to send help. Elijah got bread and sleep. Hagar, alone in the wilderness with a child she thought would die, was met by a God who opened her eyes to a well. Seeking a doctor is not a lack of faith. It is one of the ways faith receives the bread it is given.

## The lambs in his arms

Here is the wider thing the first year can teach you, if you let it. You are learning, in your body, what it means to be responsible for someone who can give you nothing back. The baby doesn't thank you for the feedings. It doesn't notice the sacrifice of your sleep or your career plans or your figure or your freedom. It simply needs, and you simply give, and on some nights you give resentfully and on others with a love so strong it frightens you. This is the nearest most of us will come, in this life, to seeing how God has always related to us. "Can a woman forget her nursing child, that she should have no compassion on the son of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isaiah 49:15). The prophet chose the image of a nursing mother because he knew it was the strongest human picture of a love that doesn't depend on being repaid, and he said God's love was stronger still.

The same prophet gave an image that belongs to the parents as much as to the child. "He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms; he will carry them in his bosom, and gently lead those that are with young" (Isaiah 40:11). Notice the last line. The shepherd doesn't only carry the lambs. He adjusts his pace for the ewes who are nursing. He knows they can't keep up with the rest of the flock this season, and he doesn't drive them. He leads them gently. If you are a new parent who can't keep up with your old spiritual life, your old friendships, your old marriage, this is the verse for you. God is not disappointed in your pace. He has slowed down to match it.

That leaves a charge for the rest of us, and I include myself in it. If God leads the nursing ewes gently, his church should too, which means our care for new parents has to outlast the casserole rotation. It means older couples who remember the first year offering to take the baby for an afternoon so the young couple can sleep, or talk, or simply sit in a quiet house. It means small groups rearranged so a nursing mother isn't forced to choose between her child and her friends. It means that when we stand in the front of the church and promise to help raise this child, whether at a baptism or a dedication, we understand that promise to begin now, in the year when the parents most need it, and not later, when the child is old enough for Sunday school. Every Christian in that room made a vow to that family. Most of us forget it by the parking lot.

And for the couple in the kitchen, there is a promise that has nothing to do with how well you are handling this. The child in your arms was made for more than this year, and so were you. The sleeplessness will end, though it will not feel like it. The marriage will change, and it can come out of this year deeper than it went in, though it won't happen automatically. And the faith that seems to have gone quiet has not gone anywhere, because it never rested on your ability to keep it awake. It rests on a God who keeps watch through the night, who does not slumber or sleep, and who once took on the helplessness of a newborn himself, carried by a tired young mother who treasured up these things and pondered them in her heart (Luke 2:19). He knows what the first year is like from the inside. He was the baby in it. Tonight, when you get up for the third time, you're doing something he has done before you, and you're doing it under his eyes.
