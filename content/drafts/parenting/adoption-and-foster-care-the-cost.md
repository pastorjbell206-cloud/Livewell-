Picture a couple driving home after a Sunday service that ended with a video. A family stood on a courthouse step holding a small girl in a white dress, the judge's signature fresh on the decree, and the pastor read James 1:27 over the final frame. In the car, the wife says what both of them are thinking. They have two healthy children, a spare bedroom and a steady income. They have love to give. Within a year they're licensed, and within two they have a seven-year-old boy who hoards granola bars under his mattress, screams for an hour at bedtime, and one night, when his new mother tries to hold him, spits out the sentence she had braced herself for and still can't answer: "You're not my real mom." Their nine-year-old daughter asks, quietly, why they did this to her.

They love the boy. They'd do it again, most days. But they feel something nobody warned them about, a mixture of grief, exhaustion and shame, and they can't say it at church, because at church they're the family in the video. They are supposed to be the proof that the gospel works.

This essay is for them, and for the couple still in the car deciding. It asks the question people type into a search box, whether Christians should adopt or become foster parents, and it answers slowly. The theology of adoption is real and beautiful, and it has been misread in a way that places the wrong people at the center of the story. The children are real, and so is what happened to them before they arrived. And the cost is real, and it deserves to be counted before anyone says yes. The broader questions about the American foster care system and the adoption movement's critics are taken up in [What Does the Bible Say About Foster Care and Adoption?](/writing/foster-care-unfinished-mandate). This one stays inside the house.

## What Paul meant by adoption

The verse most often quoted is Romans 8:15: "For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" It is usually read as an invitation to adopt, and it may become one. But it doesn't start there, and the context matters.

Romans 8 begins with freedom: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). Paul explains that the law couldn't do what it demanded, so God did it himself, "sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin" (Romans 8:3). By the time he reaches verse 15, the contrast is between two households. One is slavery, where fear governs everything and belonging depends on performance. The other is sonship, where the Spirit himself teaches the child to use the family's most intimate word for the father. The next verse makes the point sharper: "The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God" (Romans 8:16). The adopted child doesn't produce the cry. It is produced in him.

Then Paul adds something the adoption video never includes. Children are heirs, he says, heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, "provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him" (Romans 8:17). The adoption leads through suffering, not around it. A few verses later he says that the adoption itself is still being completed: "we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23). In Paul's theology, adoption is secure and unfinished at once. The decree is signed, the child is still learning to believe it, and there is groaning in between.

Ephesians 1:5 widens the frame. It comes in the middle of one long sentence of praise that runs, in the Greek, from verse 3 to verse 14. "In love he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will, to the praise of his glorious grace, with which he has blessed us in the Beloved" (Ephesians 1:4–6). The adoption began before the foundation of the world. It was God's initiative from start to finish, and its purpose is the praise of his grace. Then comes the price: "In him we have redemption through his blood" (Ephesians 1:7). In the next chapter Paul explains whom he means. The Gentile readers had been "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12). "But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Ephesians 2:13). Adoption in Ephesians is how outsiders became members of a household that had once been closed to them, and it was paid for with a death.

Read in context, these passages settle who plays which part. The adopter in Paul is God, and only God. The cost is borne by the Son. The Spirit does the work of teaching trust. Every Christian stands in the story as the one adopted. That includes the couple in the car. When they take a child into their home, they don't step into the role of the Father. They stand beside the child as fellow adoptees, older brothers and sisters in the same household, who were once far off themselves and still haven't finished learning to call God Father without fear.

Russell Moore's *Adopted for Life* (2009) did the church a real service by insisting that adoption belongs near the heart of the gospel and that churches should support the families who take children in. He was careful to say that not every family is called to adopt. What went wrong in parts of the movement that followed was a subtle reversal of the analogy. Adoptive parents began to be cast as God in the story, the child as the sinner being rescued, and the child's first family as the slavery the child was being saved from. The analogy runs the other direction. God adopts us. We don't adopt anyone the way God does, and we have no business playing him.

## The savior complex, and where it came from

The savior complex is easier to recognize than to confess. One sign is the expectation that the child will be grateful. Another is a family's social media account that narrates a child's trauma for an audience, with photographs, in a story where the parents are the heroes. A third is the language of "God's plan," used in a way that makes the child's loss of a mother the means God used to give a couple what they wanted. And it appears most painfully when the child's first family is erased, spoken of as if it never mattered, or as a danger the child was lucky to escape.

This didn't start with the adoption movement of the 2000s. In the decades after the Second World War, American adoption was built on what might be called the clean-break theory. Ann Fessler's *The Girls Who Went Away* (2006) collects the testimony of women who, as unmarried pregnant girls in the years before 1973, were sent to maternity homes, some of them church-run, and pressed to surrender their babies. They were told to forget and to move on. The children were placed with married couples, often with new birth certificates and sealed records, as though they'd been born to the adoptive parents. The theory was that a child could be moved from an unworthy mother to a worthy couple with no loss to anyone. Many of those mothers spent the rest of their lives grieving, and many of those children grew up with a question no one would let them ask.

The sociologist H. David Kirk, himself an adoptive father, saw the problem as early as 1964. In *Shared Fate* he distinguished between families that rejected the difference between adoptive and biological parenthood, pretending it didn't exist, and families that acknowledged it. He found that the families who acknowledged the difference could talk more honestly with their children, and that the pretense, however loving its intention, cut children off from the truth of their own lives. From the 1970s onward, adult adoptees began pressing for access to their records, and open adoption, in which birth and adoptive families stay in some form of contact, became far more common. The shift was hard-won, and much of it came from adoptees themselves.

Adoptees have been saying what the clean-break theory denied. Nancy Verrier's *The Primal Wound* (1993) argued that separation from the birth mother leaves a lasting wound even on a newborn who remembers nothing. The thesis is contested; some clinicians consider it overstated, and some adoptees find it too fatalistic about their lives. But many others have said that it put words to something they had always felt. Nicole Chung's memoir *All You Can Ever Know* (2018) tells of growing up as a Korean American adoptee with white parents in Oregon, raised on a comforting, tidy family story of her adoption, and of what she found when, as an adult, she searched for her birth family. Her book isn't an attack on her parents. It's an account of how much more complicated the truth was than the story she'd been given, and how much she needed the truth.

We should hear that, and we in the church should hear it first. It has been easy for us to celebrate adoption in a way that serves our narrative more than the children. We made a picture of the gospel out of their losses, and then we didn't want to hear from them when they grew up and told us the losses were real.

## The trauma is real

The boy with the granola bars under his mattress isn't being ungrateful. Food hoarding is one of the most common behaviors in children who have known hunger or neglect, and it can persist for years in a house where the pantry is always full. The child's body learned something before his mind could put words to it, and a full pantry doesn't unteach it.

The child psychiatrist Bruce Perry, writing with Maia Szalavitz in *The Boy Who Was Raised as a Dog* (2006), describes how early neglect and trauma shape a developing child, and what helps. His cases are hard to read. His conclusion is hopeful and demanding at once: children heal through relationships, through many small, patterned, predictable experiences of safety with caring adults, repeated over a long time. There is no shortcut. Karyn Purvis, whose *The Connected Child* (2007) has become a standard guide for adoptive and foster parents, put it in a phrase: children from hard places need felt safety, the experience of being safe, and not only the fact of it. A child can live in a safe house for months before his body believes it.

This has direct consequences for how Christian parents discipline. Many come to adoption with a strong theology of obedience and a set of methods that worked with their biological children. With a child who has been abused or neglected, those methods can backfire badly. A child who responds to correction with rage or collapse is often responding with fear, and punishment that increases fear usually makes the behavior worse. Purvis's approach, like Perry's, puts connection before correction. That isn't permissiveness. It's recognizing that the child can't yet receive correction from someone he doesn't trust.

There are other realities the video leaves out. Many children in foster care and in international adoption were exposed to alcohol or drugs before birth, and fetal alcohol spectrum disorders are more common among them than in the general population, often undiagnosed. Children may carry grief for parents, siblings, foster families and places they lost, and it may surface at birthdays, holidays and adolescence. Parents may find that they don't feel the love they expected, at least not at first, and then feel guilty about it. Biological children may resent the time and attention the new child requires, and they may be frightened of him. Marriages come under strain. None of this means the adoption was a mistake. It means that love is going to be practiced here as a discipline long before it's felt as a delight.

The worst outcome is the one that shows most clearly why preparation matters. In 2013 Reuters published an investigation by Megan Twohey called "The Child Exchange," reporting that some American parents who had adopted children, many of them internationally, had passed them to strangers through online forums when the children proved harder than expected, with no court or agency involved. Some of the children ended up with dangerous adults. Several states later changed their laws. The story is an extreme one, but it follows from the savior complex: parents who adopted to rescue a child, found that the child didn't want rescuing on their terms, and had no one to help them.

If you're a parent in crisis with an adopted or foster child, the answer is not to go it alone and not to act in desperation. Your agency and post-adoption support services exist for this. A therapist trained in trauma and attachment is worth traveling for. If you suspect a child is being abused, call the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline at 1-800-422-4453. If a child or anyone in your home is talking about wanting to die, call or text 988. This essay is not medical, psychological or legal advice, and the specifics of your situation need people qualified to address them.

## Counting the cost before you say yes

Proverbs has a warning that fits the couple in the car: "It is a snare to say rashly, 'It is holy,' and to reflect only after making vows" (Proverbs 20:25). A sense of calling is not the same thing as readiness, and the holiness of a cause doesn't make a rash commitment wise. The families who do this well are almost always families who counted the cost first, and who had people around them who helped them count it honestly.

Some of what needs counting is motive. A couple grieving infertility may come to adoption with the grief unresolved, hoping the adopted child will fill the place of the biological child they couldn't have. That hope is understandable and it asks too much of a child. No child should be adopted to replace another. The grief needs to be grieved on its own terms first, so that the child who comes is loved as himself rather than as a substitute. The same is true of the desire to rescue. Love that needs the child to be rescued, grateful and transformed is love with a condition attached.

Some of it is capacity. Do you have the time? Is your marriage strong enough to carry a child who may try to divide you? Are your biological children old enough and secure enough to share you with a child in pain? Can you pay for the therapy the child may need for years? (This is not financial advice, and many states provide support for adopted children from foster care, which an agency can explain.) Do you have a church and a circle of friends who will bring meals, provide respite and still be there in year three?

Some of it is purpose. Foster care and adoption are different callings, and confusing them hurts children. In most cases the goal of foster care is to reunify the child with his parents, and foster parents are asked to love a child fully while working toward his going home. That is one of the hardest things anyone can be asked to do. A family that becomes foster parents because they want to adopt, and treats the birth parents as obstacles, is setting itself against the most likely outcome for the child and against the child's own deepest wish. Families who can't bear to love a child and give him back may be called to adoption rather than to foster care, or to supporting foster families rather than becoming one.

And some of it is a genuinely contested question on which faithful Christians disagree. In 1972 the National Association of Black Social Workers issued a statement opposing the placement of Black children with white families, arguing that such placements cut children off from their people and their culture. In 1994 Congress passed the Multiethnic Placement Act, strengthened in 1996, which bars agencies receiving federal money from delaying or denying placements on the basis of race, on the argument that children waiting in care need families now. The strongest case against transracial adoption is that a child's identity is formed in a community, and that love inside a family cannot substitute for belonging among people who share and understand his experience. The strongest case for it is that the child waiting today needs a family today, and that love across difference is possible and good. This is a second-order question, and it has no single Christian answer. What can be said is that parents who adopt across race or culture take on obligations they can't meet by love alone: learning the child's history, living and worshiping where he isn't the only one who looks like him, and listening without defensiveness when he tells them what it's like. The same holds, with differences, for international adoption, where the questions about how a child became available deserve careful answers before anyone signs anything.

The church itself needs to count the cost of its own rhetoric. Those of us who preach have often handled James 1:27 as though every family should take in a child. Not every family should, and a church that shames people into adoption will produce the fragile placements that end in disruption. But every church can make it possible for the families who do say yes to keep saying yes. That means meals, respite, rides to therapy, patience with a child who disrupts the service, and a willingness to hear the family say it's hard without treating that as a failure of faith.

## Loving a child who belongs to more than you

The hardest discipline for many adoptive and foster parents is honoring the child's first family. It can feel like a threat. It is the child's history, and a child who is not allowed to love where he came from is being asked to split himself in two.

Scripture has a small picture of this that is easy to overlook. Hannah, after years of barrenness, gave her son Samuel into the care of Eli the priest at Shiloh, as she had vowed. It wasn't an adoption in the modern sense, and the analogy shouldn't be pressed. But the text records a detail that every adoptive family should notice: "And his mother used to make for him a little robe and take it to him each year when she went up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice" (1 Samuel 2:19). Samuel grew up in another household, under another adult's care, and his mother kept coming, every year, with a robe she had made. Eli did not send her away. The child knew where he came from, and the one who raised him let him know it.

In practice that means telling the child the truth about his story in words suited to his age, from the beginning, so that there's never a day he discovers a secret. It means speaking of birth parents with respect, even when their failures were serious, because the child is half made of them. In foster care it means treating visits as the child's right and supporting reunification where it's possible. In open adoption it means keeping the promises you made about contact, even when it's inconvenient. It means not publishing the child's history online, because it's his, and he'll grow up and read what you said. And it means expecting the grief to return at certain seasons, at birthdays and holidays and especially in adolescence, and not taking it as a verdict on your love.

Kirk's distinction still holds. The family that acknowledges difference is not a lesser family. It is a truthful one, and the child raised in the truth has something solid to stand on when the questions come.

## The Spirit who teaches the cry

The seven-year-old with the granola bars may take years to trust his new parents. Some children never fully do. That's the part of this calling that most requires faith, because it asks parents to keep loving without the response that love usually receives.

Romans 8 has already told them they're in good company. God did not require his adopted children to feel at home before he kept them. The cry of "Abba" is produced in them by the Spirit, slowly, over a lifetime, and even the apostle admits he is still groaning, still waiting for the adoption to be complete. Adoptive parents are learning, from the other side, what God has always known about us. We are the children who kept hoarding, who couldn't believe the table would still be set tomorrow, who raged at the one who held us. He kept us anyway. The cost of keeping us was his Son, who suffered so that we could be heirs, and who calls those who suffer with him fellow heirs of what's coming.

That doesn't make the nights shorter. The mother sitting outside a bedroom door at two in the morning, listening to a boy scream at a fear he can't name, doesn't need a theology lecture. She needs sleep, and a friend, and a therapist who understands. But she can also know that she's not the savior in this story and doesn't need to be. Paul wrote one more sentence in that chapter for her: "Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). The same Spirit who is teaching the boy to trust is praying for his mother when she has no words left.

The couple in the car should still go home and talk about it. Some of them should say yes. They should say it knowing that the child who comes will carry a loss they can't repair, a family they must honor, and a fear that will outlast their patience more than once. They should say it knowing they aren't the rescuers but fellow members of a household that took them in when they were far off. And they should know that whether the boy ever calls them what they hope to be called is not finally up to them. That word has to be taught by someone else, and he has been teaching it to them for longer than they've been parents.
