The drive home from church is the most honest sermon a pastor preaches. The congregation gets the prepared one. The children in the back seat get the other one — the tone in the car when the elders pushed back, the silence when the offering came up short, the thing said about the family in the third pew that was not said from the pulpit. They are listening. They have always been listening. And the theology they are building is built from the gap between what you say at the front and what you become on the way home.

I have watched this in other men's families. I have watched it in my own attention, in the moments I caught myself performing a patience I did not feel. The pastor's child is not raised on doctrine. The pastor's child is raised on the integrity, or the absence of it, between the doctrine and the man.

This is not a parenting tip. It is a verdict about formation.

## What the research already told us

Christian Smith's National Study of Youth and Religion, published across *Soul Searching* (2005) and *Souls in Transition* (2009), found that the single strongest predictor of a child's faith in adulthood was not church attendance, not youth group, not the Christian school. It was the practiced faith of the parents. Not the professed faith. The practiced one. The faith the child watched, not the faith the child was told.

Now apply that to the pastor's home. The pastor professes faith for a living. The professing is the job. Which means the pastor's child grows up with the widest possible field on which to measure the distance between profession and practice — fifty-two Sundays a year of hearing the father describe a God he then has to live in front of them on Monday. The child becomes an expert in one thing. The child becomes an expert in the gap.

Some pastor's kids inherit a faith of unusual depth, because the gap was small and the practice was real. Some inherit a permanent cynicism, because the gap was a canyon and they fell into it. The same vocation produces both. The difference is not the eloquence of the sermons. The difference is what happened in the car.

## The performance the family absorbs

The danger is not hypocrisy in the cartoon sense — the secretly faithless preacher. Most pastors believe what they preach. The danger is subtler and more common. It is the slow conscription of the family into the image the ministry requires.

The pastor's wife learns which feelings are permitted in the foyer. The pastor's children learn that their behavior is a referendum on their father's fitness for the office. They learn to be a credit. They learn that the family is, in part, an exhibit. And a child raised as an exhibit does not learn the gospel. A child raised as an exhibit learns public relations.

Eugene Peterson spent his pastoral life warning about exactly this — the reduction of the pastor to a religious professional, a manager of religious goods and services. He was naming a vocational corruption. But the corruption does not stop at the pastor. It travels down the table. The children eat it. They are formed by a father who has, without meaning to, started treating the household as a branch office of the church's reputation.

Bonhoeffer, in *Life Together*, said the man who loves his dream of community more than the community itself destroys it, however well-intentioned. The pastor who loves his ministry's reputation more than his actual children will, however unintentionally, sacrifice the second to the first. The sermon Sunday morning. The sons absorbing the cost Sunday afternoon.

## The two things they are actually watching

They watch how you treat their mother. Not how you describe marriage from the pulpit — how you speak to her when the sermon went badly. Malachi says God hates the breaking of faith in marriage, that the covenant was made so that he might seek godly offspring. The text ties the marriage and the children together on purpose. The children are not separate from the marriage. They are watching it for their picture of what God's faithfulness costs and whether it is real.

And they watch what you do when you are wrong. The pastor who never repents in front of his children raises children who think the Christian life is a performance of never being wrong. Then they leave home and discover they are wrong constantly, and they conclude — reasonably — that the faith was for people who could keep up the act. But the pastor who says *I was harsh with you and I was wrong and I am sorry* teaches the one thing the sermons cannot. He teaches that grace is for the people in this house too. That the father is not exempt from the gospel he preaches. That confession is not weakness but the actual shape of the thing.

## The cost nobody put in the job description

There is a particular loneliness here, and it deserves naming. The pastor is on display, so the family is on display, and there are few places where the strain can be set down. The man who counsels the whole town on their marriages has nowhere to take his own. The children sense the strain even when it is hidden, because children always sense the strain that is hidden. Concealment does not protect them. Concealment only removes their ability to name what they are already feeling.

The faithful pastor's home is not the home with no strain. It is the home where the strain is allowed to be true. Where the father can come home and not be the office. Where the children see a man who believes the gospel enough to stop performing it.

## The history we inherited and rarely examine

This did not start with you, and naming where it started should lower the shame. The professional Protestant minister as we know him — salaried, credentialed, the public face of an institution that must be kept respectable — is a relatively recent invention, hardened in American Christianity across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as the church organized itself like a corporation with the pastor as its brand. Robert Bellah, in *Habits of the Heart*, traced how American life dissolved the older communal forms into a culture of individual performance and image. The pastor did not escape that solvent. He got a building, a board, a budget, and a reputation to protect, and the reputation began to require a family that looked the part. The pastor's kid became, without anyone deciding it on purpose, part of the institution's public relations.

So the pressure you feel to present a flawless family is not a private failing of yours. It is the water the whole American pastorate swims in. But understanding where the current comes from does not excuse drowning your children in it. It only tells you what you are fighting. You are fighting an entire vocational form that wants to turn your home into an exhibit. Lesslie Newbigin, returning to a secularized England after decades as a missionary in India, said the only real apologetic the church had left was a community whose life was unaccountable apart from the gospel. The pastor's family is supposed to be a small instance of exactly that — not a flawless one, an honest one. A household where grace is so obviously operating that it cannot be explained any other way. That is not the same as a household with no problems. It is a household where the problems are met by the gospel in front of the children, instead of hidden from them to protect the brand.

## The verdict

Your children are not your congregation. They do not need your sermon. They need to watch you receive the same mercy you offer everyone from the platform. They are building their picture of God out of you, in the car, on the bad days, when the door is closed and the audience is gone.

They are watching. They have seen more than you think. The question was never whether they are forming a theology from you. The question is which one. The faith goes down with the man, or it does not go down at all. They will inherit what you were on the drive home. Not what you said at the front.
