Picture a family on a Sunday afternoon. They came home from church at half past twelve, and by one o'clock the day had dissolved. One parent is doing the week's laundry because Sunday is the only day there's time. The other is answering email at the kitchen counter, telling himself it will make Monday easier. The children are scattered to their screens, each in a separate room, and by four o'clock someone is crying because homework that was due tomorrow has just been remembered. By evening the whole household is more tired than it was on Friday. They had a day off. They did not rest.

This is the ordinary Sunday of a great many Christian families, and most of them would not describe it as a spiritual problem. It feels like a logistics problem. There is too much to do and too little time, and the weekend is where the overflow goes. When parents search for how to keep the Sabbath as a family with young kids, they are usually not looking for a theology. They're looking for a way to make one day in seven feel different from the other six, and they suspect, rightly, that it will take more than good intentions.

Some of the hesitation is honest and should be granted. Many adults remember Sundays from childhood that felt like a punishment: no games, no friends, good clothes all day, and a long afternoon of silence under an adult's disapproving eye. Charles Dickens gave that Sunday its most famous portrait in *Little Dorrit* (1857), where Arthur Clennam, returning to London on a gloomy Sunday evening with the church bells jangling, remembers the dreaded Sundays of his childhood, spent in enforced piety under a severe mother, as a weekly sentence to be served. Parents who carry memories like that are not wrong to fear giving their children the same thing. A Sabbath that children experience as a prison teaches them that God is a jailer.

But the Sabbath as Scripture gives it is something else, and the distinction this essay turns on is the one that family in the kitchen had lost. A day off and a Sabbath are different things. A day off is rest as private consumption. It is earned by the week's work, spent by each person according to his own preference, and very often paid for by someone else's labor: the parent who cooks while the others relax, the worker who staffs the store so the family can shop. A Sabbath is rest as a gift received. It is given, not earned; it is kept together, not scattered; and it is shared by the whole household, especially by the ones who ordinarily serve. A family can take a hundred days off and never keep a Sabbath. And a family that keeps even a modest Sabbath, imperfectly, will discover that it is teaching its children something the rest of the week cannot.

## A palace in time, built in a house

Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Sabbath* (1951), taught a generation of Christians that the first thing Scripture calls holy is not a place but a day, and he called the Sabbath "a palace in time," a structure built not of stone but of hours, which a people enters every week. The theology behind that image, from Genesis through the ways Christians have kept the day, is laid out in [What Is the Sabbath and Should Christians Keep It Today?](/writing/what-the-sabbath-is-and-why-you-need-it). This essay is about the house: what it looks like when a family with children actually walks into that palace, week after week.

What Heschel described was not only an idea. It was a household practice, and Christians who want to keep a Sabbath at home have much to learn from how observant Jewish families keep it, as long as we learn with respect and don't pretend their practices are ours. The Sabbath in a Jewish home begins at sundown on Friday. The preparations are done before it: the food cooked, the house set in order, the work put away. Candles are lit, traditionally by the woman of the house. A blessing is said over wine and over bread. In many homes the parents lay their hands on their children's heads and bless them by name. A sixteenth-century hymn from Safed, the *Lekha Dodi* attributed to Shlomo Halevi Alkabetz, welcomes the Sabbath as a bride arriving at the door, and Heschel loved that image: the day is not a gap in the schedule but a guest who has come to stay. At nightfall on Saturday, a short ceremony called Havdalah, with wine, sweet spices and a braided candle, marks the end of the day and the return to ordinary time.

Notice what that pattern does for a household with children. It makes the day physical. It has a beginning a child can see, a flame and a meal and a hand on the head, and an end a child can smell. It is not an abstraction explained by adults but an event the whole family walks into together. And it requires preparation. The Sabbath is kept on Friday afternoon as much as on Saturday: the cooking, the cleaning and the finishing of tasks all happen beforehand, so that the day itself can be free.

This is not a rabbinic invention. It is written into the manna. When Israel gathered bread in the wilderness, they were told to gather a double portion on the sixth day, because none would fall on the seventh. Moses said, "Tomorrow is a day of solemn rest, a holy Sabbath to the LORD; bake what you will bake and boil what you will boil" (Exodus 16:23). And then: "See! The LORD has given you the Sabbath; therefore on the sixth day he gives you bread for two days" (Exodus 16:29). God doesn't command rest and leave the family to scramble. He gives two days of bread on the sixth day.

The later rabbis defined the work forbidden on the Sabbath by listing thirty-nine categories of labor, recorded in the Mishnah, and rabbinic tradition linked those categories to the work of building the tabernacle, because the Torah sets the command to rest right beside the instructions for the sanctuary. Whatever one makes of the details, the insight is striking. The work that stops on the Sabbath is the work of building, and the day itself becomes the sanctuary. A family doesn't need a cathedral to keep holy time. It needs a kitchen table and the willingness to stop building for a day.

## Pharaoh's quota in the family calendar

If Heschel shows what the Sabbath is, Walter Brueggemann shows what it resists. In *Sabbath as Resistance* (2014) he reads the fourth commandment against Pharaoh's brickyards, where the quotas only rose and, when Moses asked for days to worship, the straw was withheld and the count of bricks kept the same (Exodus 5). He organizes the book around what the Sabbath refuses: anxiety, coercion, exclusivism and multitasking. A people who rest one day in seven are declaring that they are no longer Pharaoh's property.

That can sound like the language of politics, and in part it is. But it lands hardest in a household, because the same quota now runs the family calendar. The children's activities, the parents' work, the house that must be cleaned and the lawn that must be mowed: each has a quota that only rises, and the family lives inside a system whose logic is that nothing is ever finished. The Sabbath does not solve that by making the family more efficient. It solves it by refusing, one day in seven, to play by the system's rules at all. The laundry will not be finished. The email will not be answered. The lawn will stay long for another day. And the world will not end.

When Moses repeats the Sabbath command in Deuteronomy, he names everyone in the house and then adds a clause that matters enormously for families: "On it you shall not do any work, you or your son or your daughter or your male servant or your female servant, or your ox or your donkey or any of your livestock, or the sojourner who is within your gates, that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (Deuteronomy 5:14).

That your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you. The commandment is aimed at the head of the household, and it says the rest is not his private privilege. It belongs to everyone under his roof, and especially to those whose work makes his rest possible. That is the clause that exposes the family in the kitchen. In most homes, the one who cooks the Sunday dinner, clears the table and does the dishes is the one who never rests, and in many homes that person is the mother. A family Sabbath in which one parent labors so the others can relax is not a Sabbath. It is the brickyard with a better menu.

This is where Heschel's Friday and Brueggemann's Pharaoh meet. The only way a whole household can rest is if the work is done beforehand, shared across the family, or simply left undone. That means the children help prepare on Saturday. It means the Sunday meal might be simple, made the day before or ordered in, or eaten as leftovers. It means the parent who ordinarily carries the domestic load has the day as fully as anyone else. A child who grows up watching his father clear the table on the Sabbath so his mother can sit learns something about the Fourth Commandment that no sermon could teach him.

The Deuteronomy clause also raises a harder question, one Nehemiah faced when he returned to Jerusalem and found merchants selling at the gates on the Sabbath. He ordered the gates shut at dusk on the eve of the day and kept them shut until it was over (Nehemiah 13:15–22). A family that makes Sunday its shopping day should at least notice who is working the register. Christians disagree about how much of this binds us, and a Sabbath kept in order to accuse the cashier would miss the point. But a household that wants to rest while keeping someone else perpetually at work has not yet heard the whole commandment.

## The Lord's Day and the honest objections

Christians have never agreed about exactly how the Sabbath command carries into the new covenant. Some traditions, with the Westminster Confession of 1646, hold that the Sabbath moved to the first day of the week with the resurrection and still binds, the whole day given to worship and to works of necessity and mercy, with the household's affairs arranged beforehand so it can be kept. Others read Paul's warning that no one should pass judgment "with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath" (Colossians 2:16) and conclude that the day is fulfilled in Christ and left to conscience, though the pattern of rest and the gathering of the church remain wise and good. Still others keep the seventh day itself. This is a second-order question, one on which faithful Christians differ, and this essay doesn't settle it. What every serious position shares is that rest is a gift God gives and that his people are poorer when they refuse it.

The objections a family raises are usually more practical than theological, and they deserve their strongest voice. The first is the parent who says: we can't. A nurse works Sundays. A single mother has one day to do everything that didn't get done in six. A family with a child who has a disability may have no day on which care stops. For households like these, a day free of all work is a fantasy, and a sermon that assumes otherwise lands as one more burden. That objection is right as far as it goes. The Sabbath was never meant to be a luxury for families with margin. It was given to slaves. But that also means it can be adapted. The day can be a different day. It can be half a day. It can begin at dinner on Saturday and end at lunch on Sunday. The rabbis themselves made room for saving a life and caring for the sick, and Jesus healed on the Sabbath and defended it: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). A family that keeps some rest faithfully is keeping the commandment's spirit better than a family that keeps a rigid day resentfully.

The second objection comes from the parent who remembers the Dickensian Sunday. A day of rules, the fear goes, will teach children to dread God. That is a real danger, and the answer is not to abandon the day but to recover its joy. Isaiah described a people who "call the Sabbath a delight and the holy day of the LORD honorable" (Isaiah 58:13), and promised that those who did so would "take delight in the LORD" (58:14). Psalm 92 is titled "A Song for the Sabbath," and it doesn't sound like a jail sentence: "For you, O LORD, have made me glad by your work; at the works of your hands I sing for joy" (Psalm 92:4). The rabbis spoke of *oneg Shabbat*, the delight of the Sabbath, and treated feasting, rest, marital love and good company as ways of keeping the day. A Sabbath children remember as the best day of the week, the day with the special meal, the long walk, the board game with both parents actually present, is a Sabbath that has understood the command. A Sabbath they remember as the day they couldn't do anything has not.

The third objection is that all of this sounds like one more thing to add to an already impossible week. That is the strongest objection and the most revealing, because it assumes the Sabbath is one more task. It is exactly the reverse. It is the removal of tasks. Anyone who has tried to keep it knows that the difficulty is not doing something new. It is stopping something old, and discovering how afraid we are of what happens if we stop.

## What a household Sabbath looks like

No one should try to adopt a complete Sabbath practice in a single week, and there is no correct template. But the church has thought carefully about what the day contains, and a family can build slowly.

Marva Dawn, a Lutheran theologian, organized her book *Keeping the Sabbath Wholly* (1989) around four movements that remain the most useful map for a household: ceasing, resting, embracing and feasting. Ceasing means stopping work, and also stopping the striving, the worry and the buying that fill the week. Resting means rest of body and mind, and it may include sleep. Embracing means taking hold of the values the day represents: time with God, with family and with neighbor, and a deliberate trust that the world is in God's hands and not ours. Feasting means celebration: good food, music, beauty, laughter, the day as a small foretaste of the kingdom. A family that thinks of its Sabbath in those four movements has a way to begin, and a way to judge whether what it is doing is actually rest.

Dorothy Bass, in *Receiving the Day* (2000), puts the same thing in terms of time itself. The week, she argues, is a gift to be received and not a resource to be managed, and the Sabbath is the practice that trains a household to receive it. Her language is useful for parents, because it turns the question from what we must do to what we are being given.

In practice, many households begin with an evening. On Saturday night, or whatever evening begins their day of rest, the family eats a meal together that is better than the ordinary one, lights a candle, reads a psalm and prays, and then puts away the phones. Andy Crouch, in *The Tech-Wise Family* (2017), suggested a rule of thumb that many families have found workable: one hour a day, one day a week and one week a year with devices turned off. A household that simply sets aside its screens from Saturday dinner to Sunday dinner will be shocked at how the day changes, and how hard the first few weeks are. The children will be bored. So will the adults. The boredom is the sound of the quota system withdrawing, and it passes.

Then the day itself. Worship with the church is at its center, not as one obligation competing with others but as the reason for the day. After worship, the day is for things done for their own sake. A long walk. A nap. Reading aloud. Visiting a grandparent or an elderly neighbor. Playing outside. Cooking something together if cooking is a joy rather than a chore. Wendell Berry, the Kentucky farmer and writer, has published a whole body of poems written over many years on Sunday walks through the woods and fields near his farm; they record a man paying attention to a world he did not make and cannot own, which is close to the heart of what the Sabbath asks. Children are often better at this than their parents. They know how to stare at a creek for an hour. What they need is parents who will stop long enough to stare with them.

The day should also have an ending, something that marks the return to ordinary time: a prayer over the coming week, a blessing, a candle blown out. Children need to know the day has edges. A Sabbath that fades into Sunday-night homework and anxious preparation for Monday is a Sabbath that has lost its shape.

And families should expect it to fail, often. A child will get sick. A work emergency will intrude. A teenager will refuse. The household will fall back into the old pattern for weeks at a time. None of that means the practice has failed. The Sabbath comes around again every seven days. That is part of its mercy: no one keeps it perfectly, and everyone is invited to begin again.

## The rest that remains

Jesus' invitation is the plainest word Scripture has for a tired household: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls" (Matthew 11:28–29). It is spoken to people, not to schedules. The rest the Sabbath points toward is finally found in a person, and a family's day of rest is one small, weekly way of taking him at his word.

Remember the hands laid on the children's heads at the Friday table. Christian households can borrow words for that moment which the synagogue also uses, the blessing God gave Aaron to speak over Israel: "The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you; the LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace" (Numbers 6:24–26). A parent who says those words over a child at the start or the close of a day of rest isn't performing a ritual for its own sake. The parent is telling the child, on the one day of the week built for it, that his life is held under a face that shines on him before he has done anything at all.

That is what a household Sabbath rehearses, and it is why it matters so much for children. Every week, a family that stops is telling its children with their bodies that the world is held together by God and not by them, that their worth doesn't depend on how much they produce, that they are loved before they achieve anything, and that the anxious quota running the rest of the week is not the truth about reality. They are learning to trust. And a child who has learned in his body, every seven days for eighteen years, that it is safe to stop has been given something he will need for the rest of his life, in the hospital waiting room and at the graveside and on the night when everything he has built has fallen down.

The family in the kitchen on that Sunday afternoon isn't failing because they're lazy or faithless. They're failing because nobody taught them that the day was a gift, and a gift has to be received before it can be kept. The children are watching to see whether their parents believe it. The question is not whether the family can afford to stop. It's whether they can afford to teach their children that stopping is impossible.
