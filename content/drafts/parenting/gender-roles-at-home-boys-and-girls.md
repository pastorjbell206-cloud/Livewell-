Picture a family with a ten-year-old son and a twelve-year-old daughter, at a holiday dinner at the grandparents' house. When the meal ends, the women rise and clear the table while the men drift to the living room and the game. The daughter follows her mother into the kitchen because that's what the girls do here. Later, in the car, she asks why her brother didn't have to help. The parents look at each other. One of them says something about how it's just the way Grandma's house works. The other stays quiet, because they don't agree, and they've never quite talked about it.

Questions like that come up in every Christian home, and they arrive in ordinary forms. Should the chore chart be the same for sons and daughters? What do we say when our son wants to be a nurse, or our daughter wants to be a pastor, or an engineer, or a stay-at-home mother? When she asks whether she has to obey her husband someday, or he asks whether he's supposed to be in charge, what's the answer? Parents who search for what the Bible says about gender roles in the home often find themselves caught between a church culture that says one thing and a school culture that says another, and they aren't sure which parts of what they grew up with are Scripture and which are just Grandma's house.

This is a subject on which Christians disagree in earnest, and on which the disagreement has become bitter. It's worth saying at the start that faithful believers who love Scripture, confess the creeds and honor women and men alike come out in different places. They read the same texts and reach different conclusions about headship in marriage and office in the church. This essay will not pretend that disagreement away, and it won't settle it. It will try to state each side at its strongest, name what each gets right, and then ask what both kinds of households owe their sons and daughters, because on that question the agreement is wider than the argument suggests.

## Three questions wearing one name

Much of the confusion comes from the fact that the phrase "gender roles" is used for at least three different questions, which carry very different weight. Separating them is the most useful thing a parent can do.

The first question is whether being male and female is real, meaningful and good. On that question the church's witness is broad and ancient. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27). Both are made in God's image, equally and fully. Sexual difference isn't an accident or an illusion; it is part of the goodness of creation, and the Son of God took on a real human body when he became man. Christians of every tradition have confessed that men and women share one nature, one dignity and one redemption, and that the difference between them is a gift, not a defect. Nearly everything else in this essay rests on that shared ground.

The second question is whether Scripture assigns the husband a distinct responsibility of headship in marriage, and whether some offices in the church are reserved for qualified men. This is the real dispute between complementarians, who answer yes, and egalitarians, who answer no. It is a second-order question: it divides faithful churches and denominations, but it does not divide the faith. The creeds don't settle it. Christians on both sides are brothers and sisters, and should treat one another that way. The marriage texts are read closely in [What Does the Bible Say About Submission in Marriage?](/writing/what-the-bible-says-about-submission), and the question of church office in [Can Women Be Pastors? What the Bible Says About Women in Ministry](/writing/women-in-ministry); this essay stays with what parents teach their children.

The third question is who does what in a particular household. Who earns the income, who cooks, who handles the finances, who does the yard, who stays home with a sick child, whether a mother works outside the home. This is the question families argue about most, and it is the one Scripture addresses least. Neither complementarian nor egalitarian theology, rightly understood, requires that the wife do the dishes or that the husband mow the lawn. These are matters of wisdom, circumstance and the gifts of particular people, and they are largely open. Some complementarians read Paul's instruction that older women train the younger to be "working at home" (Titus 2:5) as giving mothers a primary calling to the household, and that conviction deserves to be named; but even they rarely claim Scripture assigns every chore, and most treat the division of daily work as wisdom rather than command. A complementarian household can have a mother who runs a business and a father who cooks every night. An egalitarian household can have a mother who stays home full-time with small children.

Most of the fights at holiday dinners are about the third question, conducted as though they were about the first. The daughter in the car isn't really asking about the doctrine of headship. She's asking why girls clear the table, and that is a question neither side's theology answers. Parents who can tell the three questions apart will be able to give their children honest answers: this we are sure of, this faithful Christians disagree about, and this is simply how our family has chosen to do it.

## How the traditional family was made

Before looking at the two sides, it helps to know that the arrangement many Christians call traditional is younger than we think.

For most of history, the household was a place of production, and husbands and wives both worked in it. Martin Luther married Katharina von Bora in 1525, and she ran the household in the former Augustinian monastery in Wittenberg where they lived: she managed the gardens and livestock, brewed beer, took in student boarders and kept the accounts. Luther teasingly called her "Herr Käthe," my lord Katie. Proverbs 31 describes a similar world. The excellent wife "considers a field and buys it; with the fruit of her hands she plants a vineyard" (Proverbs 31:16), trades in cloth and "looks well to the ways of her household" (31:27), while her husband "is known in the gates when he sits among the elders of the land" (31:23). She is not confined to domestic tasks in the modern sense. She is running an enterprise.

The industrial revolution split that world in two. Work moved into factories and offices; the home became a private refuge. Men went out to earn, and women stayed in to keep the home. In 1966 the historian Barbara Welter described the ideal that took shape in America between 1820 and 1860 in an influential essay, "The Cult of True Womanhood." The true woman, according to the magazines, sermons and advice books of the period, was defined by four virtues: piety, purity, submissiveness and domesticity. Her sphere was the home, and it was a sacred one, but it was a sphere. That picture, sharpened in the suburban 1950s, is what many people now mean by the traditional Christian family.

Christians on both sides have noticed that this is not simply the biblical pattern. Nancy Pearcey, who writes as a conservative evangelical, argued in *Total Truth* (2004) that industrialization drew fathers out of the home and left them with a thinner role than the older Christian tradition had given them. In the pre-industrial household, fathers were present, teaching and working alongside their children. After it, fatherhood shrank to earning, while the moral and spiritual life of the home was handed to mothers. Her point cuts against both sides' assumptions: the modern arrangement diminished fathers as much as it confined mothers.

The late twentieth century produced two organized responses. In 1987 the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood issued the Danvers Statement, affirming male headship in the home and the church while insisting on the equal dignity of women and men. In 1989 Christians for Biblical Equality issued "Men, Women and Biblical Equality," affirming the mutual submission of husbands and wives and the exercise of women's gifts in every ministry and office of the church. Both groups claimed to be recovering Scripture from culture. Both, in fact, were responding to the same modern upheaval, and both carried some of their century with them.

That history should humble every household. None of us is simply living out the biblical pattern. We are all living in the aftermath of an economic revolution that reorganized the home, and our instincts about who does what owe as much to 1850 or 1955 or 1975 as to the apostles.

## The complementarian case at its strongest

At its best, complementarianism is not a defense of Grandma's house. It is a claim about the meaning of creation.

It begins in Genesis 2, where the man is formed first and the woman is made as "a helper fit for him" (Genesis 2:18). Complementarians note that the New Testament appeals to this order not as a cultural custom but as a creation pattern. Paul grounds his instructions in it: "For man was not made from woman, but woman from man. Neither was man created for woman, but woman for man" (1 Corinthians 11:8–9). He grounds church order in it too: "For Adam was formed first, then Eve" (1 Timothy 2:13). And he describes an ordered relation: "the head of every man is Christ, the head of a wife is her husband, and the head of Christ is God" (1 Corinthians 11:3). To complementarians, these texts show that the distinction between husband and wife is not a product of the fall or of ancient patriarchy. It belongs to God's good design, and it will not be erased by the gospel but redeemed.

The strongest complementarians insist that headship is not domination. The husband is commanded to love his wife "as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25). His authority, if it is Christian at all, is the authority to go first in sacrifice, to bear responsibility, to die for his family. Elisabeth Elliot, writing to her daughter before her marriage in *Let Me Be a Woman* (1976), presented the difference between men and women as a gift to be received gladly, and a wife's submission as a free act of faith, not a surrender of her personhood. Tim and Kathy Keller, in *The Meaning of Marriage* (2011), described headship as servant leadership and the distinct callings of husband and wife as a gift, a way of honoring difference in which neither partner is worth more than the other.

At its best, complementarianism offers children something valuable: a picture of sexual difference as meaningful, of a father who takes responsibility rather than abdicating it, and of a marriage in which each partner gives themselves in a distinct way. The sociologist W. Bradford Wilcox found, in *Soft Patriarchs, New Men* (2004), that churchgoing evangelical fathers who held traditional views on paper were, on the whole, more engaged with their children and more emotionally attentive to their wives than many of their peers. That doesn't prove the theology, but it complicates the caricature.

The strongest objection this view hasn't fully answered is its fruit. If male headship is a creation ordinance, why has it so often been indistinguishable from the culture of 1955, and why has the language of headship so often been used to cover control, silence and abuse? Complementarians rightly condemn abuse, but critics ask why a structure meant to be sacrificial has been so easily turned into a license. Aimee Byrd, writing from within a confessional Reformed church, argued in *Recovering from Biblical Manhood and Womanhood* (2020) that parts of the movement had hardened cultural stereotypes into doctrine, and had taught women to see themselves chiefly through their difference from men rather than their shared calling as disciples. There is also a theological warning. In 2016 a sharp debate broke out among Reformed theologians when some complementarian scholars grounded human gender roles in an eternal subordination of the Son to the Father, and critics including Liam Goligher and Carl Trueman argued that this departed from Nicene orthodoxy. Several prominent complementarians sided with the critics. The episode showed that the view is strongest when it rests on the texts about marriage and weakest when it reaches for a doctrine of God to prop them up.

## The egalitarian case at its strongest

At its best, egalitarianism is not the adoption of secular feminism. It is a claim about the direction of redemption.

It begins in Genesis 1, where the man and the woman are created together in God's image and given dominion together: "And God blessed them. And God said to them, 'Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion'" (Genesis 1:28). No hierarchy appears in the first chapter; the rule is shared. In Genesis 2, the woman is a "helper," but the Hebrew word, *'ezer*, is used most often in the Old Testament for God himself. "My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth" (Psalm 121:2). Carolyn Custis James, in *Half the Church* (2011), argued that the word carries the sense of a strong ally, even a warrior, and egalitarians press that reading: the woman was made not as a subordinate but as an indispensable partner. The first mention of a husband ruling over his wife comes after the fall, as part of the curse: "Your desire shall be contrary to your husband, but he shall rule over you" (Genesis 3:16). To egalitarians, that rule is a description of sin's damage, not God's design, and redemption undoes the curse.

The New Testament, they argue, points that way. Paul writes that in Christ "there is no male and female" (Galatians 3:28). He tells husbands and wives that each has authority over the other's body (1 Corinthians 7:4), a startling mutuality in the ancient world. He introduces the household code in Ephesians with a command to all: "submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21). And even in the passage complementarians cite about creation order, Paul adds: "Nevertheless, in the Lord woman is not independent of man nor man of woman; for as woman was made from man, so man is now born of woman" (1 Corinthians 11:11–12). Egalitarians argue that the restrictions in Paul's letters addressed specific situations and that the trajectory of the gospel, like its trajectory on slavery, moves toward full partnership.

Rebecca Merrill Groothuis, in *Good News for Women* (1997), pressed a philosophical point. Complementarians say women are equal in being but different in role. But if a woman's subordinate role is permanent, universal and grounded in her nature as a woman, and not in her gifts or circumstances, then it is hard to see how it differs from being lesser in being. A role that no woman can ever step out of, simply because she is a woman, is not merely a role.

At its best, egalitarianism offers children something valuable too: a picture of a marriage in which husband and wife defer to each other, decide together and honor each other's gifts, and a vision of daughters as fully called to whatever God gives them to do.

The strongest objection this view hasn't fully answered is Paul's appeal to creation. When Paul grounds his instructions in the order of Adam and Eve, he seems to reach beyond his own culture to the beginning, and egalitarians must explain why that appeal doesn't mean what it appears to. Critics also ask whether egalitarianism can hold on to the meaning of sexual difference at all. If husbands and wives have no distinct callings, what is the difference between them for, beyond biology? Some egalitarians have answered well; others have drifted toward treating male and female as interchangeable, and that drift, once begun, has sometimes kept going. The best egalitarians insist that equality doesn't mean sameness. Their critics want to know what the difference means, and the most careful egalitarian writers have taken that question up in earnest.

## What both kinds of homes owe their sons and daughters

Here the sociologists have something humbling to say. In 1999, Sally Gallagher and Christian Smith published a study of evangelical families under a title that has become a phrase: "Symbolic Traditionalism and Pragmatic Egalitarianism." They found that evangelical couples often affirmed male headship in principle but made most daily decisions jointly and shared a great deal of the work in practice. Their headship was real in their language and modest in their kitchens. Meanwhile, Arlie Hochschild, in *The Second Shift* (1989), studying dual-earner couples in the San Francisco Bay Area, found that even many who professed equality still left the bulk of housework and child care to the wife when she came home from her paid job. The traditionalists were living more equally than they said, and many professed egalitarians less equally.

That's worth sitting with. Our children don't learn gender from our doctrines. They learn it from our kitchens. A son who hears his father speak of servant leadership and then watches him sit while his mother clears the table has learned a different lesson from the one intended. A daughter who hears her parents speak of equality and then watches her mother carry every invisible task in the household has also learned something. Whatever a family believes about the second question, it will be judged by its children on the third.

On that, both kinds of Christian homes can agree on more than they realize.

Both can teach sons to serve. The Son of God, who "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:6–7), knelt with a towel and washed his disciples' feet, and then said, "If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet" (John 13:14). No reading of headship exempts a boy from the dishes, the diapers or the care of a sick sibling. A complementarian father who believes he will answer to God for his household should be the first to take the towel. An egalitarian father has no excuse for leaving it to anyone else.

Both can teach daughters that their lives are not defined only by marriage. Many will marry and many will not, and Paul honored singleness as a calling (1 Corinthians 7:32–35). The Proverbs 31 woman buys fields. Priscilla, with her husband, taught Apollos the way of God more accurately. Paul commended Phoebe to the Roman church as a servant of the church at Cenchreae and a patron of many, including himself. A daughter raised to believe that her only purpose is to be someone's wife has been given a smaller vision than Scripture's, on any reading of headship.

Both can teach sons and daughters that the other sex is not a rival or a threat, but a partner in the image of God. "In the Lord woman is not independent of man nor man of woman" (1 Corinthians 11:11). Children raised in homes where men and women respect and need each other are less likely to be recruited into the contempt that runs through so much of the culture, whether it's the online rage aimed at women or the dismissiveness aimed at men.

Both can teach children to tell the three questions apart. When the daughter asks why girls clear the table at Grandma's, a family on either side can answer honestly: that's a custom, not a command, and in our house everyone clears. When she asks whether she'll have to obey a husband someday, a complementarian family can explain its conviction about headship as the call to love and sacrifice it is meant to be, and an egalitarian family can explain its conviction about mutual submission, and both can tell her that faithful Christians disagree about it.

And both must teach children that no reading of Scripture ever requires anyone to endure abuse. Headship, whatever it means, is never permission to control, frighten, isolate or harm a wife or a child, and submission, whatever it means, is never a duty to remain in danger. Any teaching that uses Scripture to keep a woman or a child in a violent home has betrayed the Scripture it quotes. If anyone in your home is being hurt or threatened, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233 (or text START to 88788), and in immediate danger, call 911.

This essay doesn't tell you where to land on the second question, and that is deliberate. It is a question on which faithful households differ, and the aim here is to help both kinds of homes raise children well. A thoughtful complementarian and a thoughtful egalitarian should both be able to finish it feeling understood, and both have work to do in their own kitchens.

## A basin and a towel

On the night before his death, Jesus did something that neither side of this debate can claim as its own and both must answer to. He rose from supper, laid aside his outer garments, tied a towel around his waist, poured water into a basin and began to wash his disciples' feet. It was a servant's task. Peter protested; Jesus insisted. And when he was finished, he told them that he had given them an example.

Every Christian vision of men and women has to pass through that room. Whatever authority a husband has, it looks like a man kneeling with a towel. Whatever equality a wife has, it is the equality of a disciple who has been washed by her Lord and sent to wash others. The argument about headship matters, and it will go on. But no child ever learned what it means to be a man or a woman from a position paper. They learn it from watching who in their house picks up the towel.

The daughter in the car is still waiting for an answer. She deserves to hear what her parents are sure of, what they are unsure of and what they have simply chosen. More than that, she deserves to grow up in a home where the question of who serves is answered every day by both her father and her mother, and where she and her brother learn, by the time they leave, that in the kingdom of God the greatest is the one who kneels.
