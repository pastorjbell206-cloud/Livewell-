The most dangerous thing you can teach a child is the right answer. Not because the right answer is wrong. Because the child can learn to produce it without ever holding it, and the producing feels, to everyone watching, exactly like belief. Raising kids who think for themselves begins the day you stop being satisfied with the performance and start asking whether anything underneath it is real.

I have sat across from young adults who could recite the catechism cold and did not believe a word of it. They learned the recitation the way they learned the multiplication tables — as a performance that bought peace, that earned the approving face across the table, that ended the uncomfortable conversation faster than honesty ever could. The faith was a transaction. They gave the answer; they received the approval; they kept the answer in a drawer they never opened. Then they left for college, opened the drawer, found the answer had no roots, and threw it out. We called it deconstruction. It was never constructed in the first place. You cannot tear down a house that was only ever a façade propped against the weather.

This is not about whether the kids leave the church or stay. It is about whether anything we handed them was ever theirs. **A faith that has never been chosen will be discarded the first time choosing it costs something.**

## We built the economy the performing child performs in

We did not invent the performing child. We built the economy the child performs in, and then we acted surprised when the child became fluent in it. The school grades performance. The team rewards performance. The phone in the pocket runs on the metrics of performance — the count under the photo, the speed of the reply, the public ledger of approval updated by the hour.

The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt has spent years documenting what this does to the developing mind, most pointedly in *The Anxious Generation* in 2024, and the part that should arrest the Christian parent is not the anxiety statistics, alarming as they are. It is the underlying reformation of the self into a thing that is always being evaluated, always managing an audience, always producing the response the metric will reward. The child raised inside that machine does not experience the self as an interior to be cultivated. The child experiences the self as a product to be optimized for whoever is watching.

Into that economy we introduced God, and we made the catastrophic mistake of letting God look like one more audience. Be good and he is pleased. Doubt and he is disappointed. Ask the wrong question and the room goes quiet, which the child reads instantly as the room going wrong. So the child learns to manage God the way the child manages every other evaluator — by performing the expected output and hiding the actual interior. The interior, unattended, goes feral. The performance, well-fed, grows confident. And the parents, watching the confident performance, conclude that the formation is working.

It is not working. It is the precise opposite of working. We have produced a faith that cannot survive the first question it is not allowed to ask.

## Faith was never the absence of the question

The biblical witness does not reward the child who never doubts. It rewards the one who wrestles and refuses to let go. Jacob wrestles the stranger at Peniel through the whole night and walks away limping and blessed and renamed. He does not walk away because he was polite. He walks away because he would not release his grip until something real was given (Genesis 32). The Psalms are full of accusation flung directly at God — how long, O Lord; why have you forgotten me; will you forget me forever (Psalm 13). Job demands an audience with the Almighty and gets one, and the friends who defended God with tidy answers are the ones rebuked at the end, not Job who shouted at the whirlwind. The book is brutal to the people who gave the right answer.

The pastor and translator Eugene Peterson spent a lifetime insisting that the language of biblical faith is not the polished language of the press release. It is the raw, contending, often furious language of people who took God seriously enough to argue with him. A child who has only been given the press release has not been given the faith. The child has been given the marketing department — and a smart eighteen-year-old learns to distrust the marketing department faster than anything else in the building.

To raise a child who thinks is to raise a child who is permitted to wrestle. Permitted in your kitchen. Permitted before the stakes are existential, before the doubt has to be smuggled out of the house and detonated somewhere you cannot see. The question that gets asked at your table at fourteen is a gift. It is the doubt arriving while you can still be present for it. The parent who flinches at it, who corrects it, who answers it too fast and too cleanly, has just taught the child to take the next question somewhere else — to a roommate, a forum, a stranger who at least did not look afraid.

## The cost of answering too well

There is a particular failure I am guilty of, and most thoughtful Christian parents are guilty of, and it is the failure of the good answer delivered too soon. The child asks something hard. We know something true about it. So we say the true thing, completely, immediately, and we feel the warm relief of having handled it. We have not handled it. We have ended it. We have communicated that the question was a problem to be closed rather than a door to be entered, and the child has learned that wondering aloud in this house gets you a lecture, not a companion.

Tim Keller was relentless on this point in the way he handled skeptics across decades of ministry in Manhattan. He never rushed the answer. He sat inside the question long enough to honor it, long enough that the questioner felt understood rather than processed, and only then did he offer the harder, deeper thing. The honoring was not a technique to make the answer go down easier. The honoring was the recognition that a question taken seriously is the beginning of thought, and thought is the only soil real faith grows in. A faith that has never thought is a faith that has never been chosen.

**Stop rewarding the right answer. The approving face you give the correct recitation is teaching your child to perform for you instead of believe with you.**

## Thinking children require a parent who is not afraid

They require, more than anything, a parent who is not threatened by the not-knowing. This is the hard part, and there is no way around it. A child detects the parent's fear the way an animal detects it — in the body, before a word is spoken. If the parent is secretly terrified that the question, fully entertained, might dismantle the parent's own faith, the child will read that terror as the real answer: that the faith cannot bear examination, that it survives only by not being looked at directly. No words can override that signal. The child believes the fear, not the doctrine.

So the work is not, finally, in the child. It is in us. We have to have done enough of our own thinking, asked enough of our own questions, gone far enough into our own doubt and come back, that the child's hardest question lands in a parent who is genuinely unafraid of it. That parent can say I don't know, let's find out, and mean it as an invitation rather than a retreat. That parent can say I have wondered that too, and here is where the wondering took me. That parent is not modeling certainty. That parent is modeling the kind of trust that has been tested and held — which is the only kind worth handing down. The other kind, the untested kind, was never faith. It was inheritance waiting for an excuse to lapse.

There is an older, sturdier confidence underneath this than the brittle certainty we often mistake for strong faith. Augustine, writing in the late fourth century, described faith as something that seeks understanding rather than fears it — *fides quaerens intellectum*, the formula a later tradition would carry forward. Faith is not the wall that keeps the questions out. Faith is what does the questioning, because it trusts there is something true at the bottom of the asking. A parent who has internalized that is free in a way the anxious parent is not. The anxious parent guards the answer. The free parent walks the child toward it.

## Why the performed faith collapses the moment it costs something

There is a reason the performance ends exactly when it does. The performance is calibrated to an audience, and the audience changes. At home the audience was the parent, and the reward was the approving face, and the cost of the recitation was nearly nothing — a few memorized lines exchanged for peace. Then the child leaves, and the audience becomes a roommate who finds the recitation strange, a professor who treats it as a curiosity from a backward town, a culture that prices belonging in the currency of doubt. The metrics flip overnight. The same performance that bought approval at the kitchen table now costs status in the dorm. And a performance is, by definition, the thing you do for the reward. Remove the reward, add a cost, and the performance stops. It was always going to stop. We built it on the wrong foundation and called the collapse a crisis of faith.

The contrast in scripture is stark. The faith the Bible commends is the faith that holds when it costs everything — the three young men in Daniel who tell the king that even if God does not rescue them they will not bow (Daniel 3:18), a faith with no audience left to please and every incentive to fold. That faith was not produced by performance. It was produced by conviction that had become their own, down past the reach of reward and punishment. You cannot drill that into a child by rewarding correct answers. Reward-shaped faith dies the moment the reward is withdrawn, which is precisely the moment faith is supposed to begin doing its real work. The thing that survives the withdrawal of reward is the only thing that was ever faith. Everything else was rehearsal for an audience that has now left the building.

## What we owe the generation we taught to perform

We raised a generation to perform faith and then grieved when the performance ended. The performance was always going to end. Performances do. What we never gave them was the thing underneath the performance — permission to doubt out loud, in the house, while we were still in the room and still unafraid.

This is not a softening of the faith. It is the opposite. The cheap version, the recitation that wins the approving face, asks nothing of anyone and forms no one. The costly version asks the parent to have actually wrestled, to carry a faith with scars on it, to be the kind of person whose belief was tested in the open rather than protected in the dark. A child can tell the difference between a parent quoting a conclusion and a parent who arrived somewhere. The first is a relay. The second is a witness. Only the second transmits.

The goal was never the right answer produced on cue. The goal is a person who has touched the thing themselves, who holds it because they have tested it, who can lose the answer for a season and find their way back because the roots went down past the recitation into something real. That person will not be assembled by better curriculum. That person is grown, slowly, in the one place willing to let the questions in.

## The verdict

The kids who think will scare you. They will ask the questions you have been avoiding in yourself. They will not take the answer simply because the room expects it. They will make you go back and find out whether you actually believe what you have been telling them, or whether you, too, have been reciting.

That is not the faith failing. Read it again. That is the only evidence you will ever get that it has a chance of becoming theirs.

A child who never questions has given you nothing to trust. A child who questions and stays, who pushes and returns, who tests the floor and finds it holds — that child is building something you cannot build for them and could never hand them ready-made. The wrestling is not the threat to their faith. The wrestling is the faith, arriving for the first time as their own. The only real danger was the silence we mistook for belief.
