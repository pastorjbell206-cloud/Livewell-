The most dangerous thing you can teach a child is the right answer. Not because the right answer is wrong. Because the child can learn to produce it without ever holding it, and the producing feels, to everyone watching, exactly like belief.

I have sat across from young adults who could recite the catechism and did not believe a word of it. They learned the recitation the way they learned the multiplication tables — as a performance that bought peace, that earned the approving face across the table, that ended the conversation faster than honesty would have. The faith was a transaction. They gave the answer; they received the approval; they kept the answer in a drawer they never opened. Then they left for college, opened the drawer, found the answer had no roots, and threw it out. We called it deconstruction. It was never constructed in the first place.

This is not about politics. It is not about whether the kids leave the church or stay. It is about whether anything we handed them was ever theirs.

## The performance economy

We did not invent the performing child. We built the economy the child performs in. The school grades performance. The team rewards performance. The phone in the pocket runs on the metrics of performance — the count under the photo, the speed of the reply. Jonathan Haidt has spent years documenting what this does to the developing mind, and the part that should arrest the Christian parent is not the anxiety statistics, alarming as they are. It is the underlying reformation of the self into a thing that is always being evaluated, always managing an audience, always producing the response the metric will reward.

Into that economy we introduced God, and we made the mistake of letting God look like one more audience. Be good and he is pleased. Doubt and he is disappointed. Ask the wrong question and the room goes quiet, which the child reads instantly as the room going wrong. So the child learns to manage God the way the child manages every other evaluator — by performing the expected output and hiding the actual interior. The interior, unattended, goes feral. The performance, well-fed, grows confident. And the parents, watching the confident performance, conclude that the formation is working.

It is not working. It is the precise opposite of working. We have produced a faith that cannot survive the first question it is not allowed to ask.

## Faith is not the absence of the question

The biblical witness does not reward the child who never doubts. It rewards the one who wrestles and refuses to let go. Jacob wrestles the angel at Peniel and walks away limping and blessed and renamed. He does not walk away because he was polite. He walks away because he would not release his grip until something real was given. The Psalms are full of accusation flung at God — how long, why have you forgotten me, will you forget me forever. Job demands an audience with the Almighty and gets one, and the friends who defended God with tidy answers are the ones rebuked at the end, not Job who shouted at the whirlwind. The book is brutal to the people who gave the right answer.

Eugene Peterson spent a pastoral lifetime insisting that the language of faith is not the polished language of the press release. It is the raw, contending, often furious language of people who took God seriously enough to argue with him. A child who has only been given the press release has not been given the faith. The child has been given the marketing department, and marketing departments are the first thing a smart eighteen-year-old learns to distrust.

To raise a child who thinks is to raise a child who is permitted to wrestle. Permitted in your kitchen. Permitted before the stakes are existential, before the doubt has to be smuggled out of the house and detonated somewhere you cannot see. The question that gets asked at your table at fourteen is a gift. It is the doubt arriving while you can still be present for it. The parent who flinches at it, who corrects it, who answers it too fast and too cleanly, has just taught the child to take the next question somewhere else.

## The cost of answering too well

There is a particular failure I am guilty of, and most thoughtful Christian parents are guilty of, and it is the failure of the good answer delivered too soon. The child asks something hard. We know something true about it. So we say the true thing, completely, immediately, and we feel the relief of having handled it. We have not handled it. We have ended it. We have communicated that the question was a problem to be closed rather than a door to be entered, and the child has learned that wondering aloud in this house gets you a lecture, not a companion.

Keller was relentless on this point in the way he handled skeptics. He never rushed the answer. He sat inside the question long enough to honor it, long enough that the questioner felt understood rather than processed, and only then did he offer the harder, deeper thing. The honoring was not a technique to make the answer go down easier. The honoring was the recognition that a question taken seriously is the beginning of thought, and thought is the only soil real faith grows in. A faith that has never thought is a faith that has never been chosen, and a faith that has never been chosen will be discarded the first time choosing it costs something.

## What thinking children require

They require a parent who is not threatened by the not-knowing. This is the hard part, and there is no way around it. A child detects the parent's fear the way a dog detects it. If the parent is secretly terrified that the question, fully entertained, might dismantle the parent's own faith, the child will read that terror as the real answer — that the faith cannot bear examination, that it survives only by not being looked at directly. No words can override that signal. The child believes the fear, not the doctrine.

So the work is not, finally, in the child. It is in us. We have to have done enough of our own thinking, asked enough of our own questions, gone far enough into our own doubt and come back, that the child's hardest question lands in a parent who is genuinely unafraid of it. That parent can say I don't know, let's find out, and mean it as an invitation rather than a retreat. That parent can say I have wondered that too, and here is where the wondering took me. That parent is not modeling certainty. That parent is modeling the kind of trust that has been tested and held — which is the only kind worth handing down.

## The verdict

Stop rewarding the right answer. The right answer, produced on cue to win the approving face, is not the goal and never was. The goal is a person who has touched the thing themselves, who holds it because they have tested it, who can lose the answer for a season and find their way back because the roots went down past the recitation into something real.

We raised a generation to perform faith and then grieved when the performance ended. The performance was always going to end. Performances do. What we never gave them was the thing underneath the performance — permission to doubt out loud, in the house, while we were still in the room.

The kids who think will scare you. They will ask the questions you have been avoiding in yourself. They will not take the answer because the room expects it. They will make you go back and find out whether you believe what you have been telling them. That is not the faith failing. That is the only evidence you will ever get that it has a chance of becoming theirs.
