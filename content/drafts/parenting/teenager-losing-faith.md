It usually comes out flat. Not a slammed door, not a manifesto. Just a sentence across the kitchen — *I don't think I believe this anymore* — delivered with the same weight as a comment about dinner. And the floor drops out from under you, and your whole body wants to do one of two things: argue them back, or grieve them gone. I want to talk to the parent standing in that kitchen, in the three seconds before they say the wrong thing.

Both instincts come from love and both of them will fail. The argument treats the announcement as a debate to be won. The grief treats it as a death to be mourned. It is neither. It is a test. And the thing being tested is not the case for the resurrection.

It is you.

## What they are actually saying

When a teenager says *I don't believe anymore*, they are rarely reporting the conclusion of a careful philosophical inquiry. They are doing several things at once, and most of them are not about God's existence.

They are individuating — discovering they are a separate person from you, which is developmentally exactly what they are supposed to be doing. They are testing whether your love is conditional on agreement. They are reacting to something — a hypocrisy they spotted, a wound the church handed them, a question nobody would let them ask out loud. And sometimes, underneath all of it, they are in genuine pain and have noticed that the faith as it was sold to them did not account for the pain.

The sentence is a door, not a verdict. How you walk through it determines whether they ever open it again.

## Why the argument loses even when it wins

Your instinct is to reach for evidence. The historical case for the resurrection, the fine-tuning of the universe, the moral argument, the manuscript count. You may even be right. You will still lose.

Because the teenager did not present a thesis. They presented a relational test, and you answered with a debate, and the answer they heard was: *your doubt is a problem to be fixed, and I would rather be correct than close to you.* You won the point and lost the room. They will not bring you the next question. They will take it to the internet, where the loudest voices are not interested in their flourishing.

There is a deeper problem. If you make their staying conditional on accepting your arguments, you have taught them that the relationship runs on agreement. Then the gospel itself becomes a transaction — believe the right things and you are in, doubt them and you are out. That is precisely the works-righteousness the gospel came to end. The prodigal's father in Luke 15 does not meet the returning son with a doctrine exam. He runs. He runs while the son is still a long way off and still smells of the pigpen. He runs before a word of repentance is spoken. The running is the theology.

## Hold the door, not the line

Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age*, described the world your teenager actually lives in — a world where belief is no longer the default, where faith is one option among many, contested, optional, easy to set down. James K. A. Smith has spent his career translating what that means for formation. Your child is not losing faith in a vacuum. They are coming of age in a culture engineered to make belief feel like a quaint choice they are too smart to keep making. The pressure is real. The pressure is not their fault.

Which means your job is not to remove their doubt. Your job is to remain the person they can bring it to. To keep the door open longer than the doubt lasts. The faith they were handed as a child has to die for the faith of an adult to be born — and that dying looks, from the outside, exactly like loss. Bonhoeffer in a Nazi prison wrote letters wrestling with God in ways that would have alarmed his Sunday school teachers. The wrestling was not the end of his faith. It was the deepening of it. Do not confuse the labor with the death.

## What to actually say

Say the thing that keeps the door open. *Thank you for telling me. That took guts. I am not going anywhere, and I am not going to love you less for an inch.* Then ask, and mean it: *What happened? What stopped making sense?* And then the hardest part. Listen long enough that they finish. Do not load the response while they are still talking. Most teenagers have never been listened to all the way to the end on this. The listening itself preaches.

You are playing a longer game than tonight. Augustine's mother Monica prayed for him across years of his running, and her great act was not winning the argument — it was refusing to disappear from his life while he ran. You may be in for years. The parent who stays reachable across those years is the parent the child eventually circles back to.

## The grief you are not allowed to weaponize

There is a real grief here, and you are allowed to feel it. You imagined a faith handed cleanly down a generation, the whole family at the same table believing the same things, and that picture is being taken from you. Grieve it. But you cannot make your child carry it. The parent who responds to a teenager's doubt with visible devastation — the tears, the wounded silence, the *do you know what this does to me* — has just taught the child that their honesty is an act of violence against the family. So the child does the only kind thing left: they hide. They perform belief at the dinner table to spare you, and they take the real self underground, and now you have a stranger who smiles at grace and means nothing by it. Your grief, set on their shoulders, manufactures the very distance you were afraid of.

This is the temptation the prophets kept naming in Israel — the urge to manage God's people by fear and guilt rather than to love them toward the truth. Brueggemann called the prophetic alternative an imagination that refuses despair, that can hold grief and hope in the same hand without collapsing one into the other. That is the parental task exactly. Hold your grief in one hand and your unshaken hope for this child in the other, and do not let the grief become a bill you hand them. The God you are trusting them to is not anxious. Borrow his composure. You will need more of it than you think.

## What the long obedience requires

Eugene Peterson borrowed a phrase from Nietzsche, of all people, and titled a book *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction*. Faith, he argued, is not a series of dramatic moments but a steady walking that outlasts the moods. Apply it to parenting a doubting child. Your task is not the dramatic intervention, the perfect conversation, the night you finally say the thing that brings them back. Your task is the long obedience — the thousand ordinary days of staying warm, staying interested, staying unafraid, refusing to make the relationship a referendum on their beliefs. Most returns, when they come, do not come because of an argument. They come because, after years of running, the child remembers there is a place where they were loved without conditions, and they are tired, and they go home. You cannot script that. You can only make sure the home is still there.

## The verdict

You cannot argue your child into the kingdom. You were never able to. What you can do is be the unanxious, unconditional, undefeated presence that makes the faith look like something worth returning to. Keep the table set. Keep the porch light on. Keep loving them past the point where it makes you feel effective.

The doubt may pass. It may not pass on your schedule. But a child who knows the door never locked has somewhere to come home to. Leave it open. Stay where they can find you. The running is not the end of the story. It was not the end of mine.
