Picture a mother pulling into a church parking lot at 10:52 on a Sunday morning, eight minutes late, with a six-year-old who lost a shoe somewhere between the front door and the car and a twelve-year-old who didn't want to come. She finds three seats near the back. The sermon series is called "Building a Strong Family," and the slide behind the pastor shows a husband and wife on a porch swing. The announcements mention a couples' retreat, a men's breakfast at 6:30 on Saturday, and small groups that meet on weeknights at seven, childcare not provided. Afterward, someone kind asks whether her husband works Sundays.

Nobody did anything wrong, exactly. The pastor wasn't thinking about her when he chose the slide, and that's the problem. By the time she buckles the children back into the car she has concluded, without anyone saying so, that this church is built for other families. She may keep coming. Many single parents do, out of faithfulness or for the children's sake. But she'll come as a guest in a house arranged for someone else. The single father who brings his daughters every other weekend feels it too, often more sharply, because the church has even fewer categories for him.

That is the question behind the searches: why do single parents feel invisible at church, and is there a place for a household like mine? This essay argues that two things are true at once and that the church has mostly managed to hold only one of them. Children generally do better with two committed parents, and the church is right to say so. And a church that builds its whole common life around the two-parent household has forgotten what Scripture says the church is. The single parent's household is not a defective version of the Christian family. It is a household inside the family, which is the church, and the church has been failing to act like one.

I was raised without a father. I won't make this essay about that, and the father wound is its own subject, taken up in [What Does the Bible Say About Fathers and the Father Wound?](/writing/what-fatherhood-requires). But it's why I can't read the statistics about fatherless homes as abstractions.

## What the data says, and what it doesn't

The honest place to begin is with the evidence the church usually cites, because it is real. In 2019 the Pew Research Center, comparing 130 countries, found that the United States had the highest share of children living with a single parent and no other adults, about 23 percent, against roughly 7 percent worldwide. The sociologists Sara McLanahan and Gary Sandefur, in *Growing Up with a Single Parent* (1994), found that children raised without one of their parents did worse on average in school completion, early childbearing and employment. They also found that lower income was the largest single factor, accounting for as much as half of that gap, which means money matters a great deal and that money isn't the whole story. More recently the economist Melissa Kearney, making the case on economic rather than religious grounds, argued in *The Two-Parent Privilege* (2023) that the decline of two-parent homes has become one of the quiet engines of American inequality, and that it's no kindness to children to pretend otherwise.

The argument has a long and bruising history. In 1965 Daniel Patrick Moynihan, then an official in the Johnson administration, wrote a report on the rising number of fatherless households among Black Americans, and it was denounced for blaming families for what segregation and poverty had done to them. In 1992 Vice President Dan Quayle criticized a television character for having a child alone, and was mocked across the country. A year later Barbara Dafoe Whitehead published an essay in *The Atlantic* titled "Dan Quayle Was Right," laying out the research on children and divorce. Each time, a claim about children's welfare was heard as an attack on the parents raising them, and each time both sides dug in.

The church should neither retreat from the evidence nor use it as a weapon. Scripture's picture of marriage and family was not drawn from sociology, and the sociology has largely confirmed it: a child is served when a mother and father are bound to each other and to the child for life. To stop saying that would be both unfaithful and unkind to the next generation. But the same evidence says something the church often skips. It describes averages, not destinies. Most children raised by one parent grow up to be stable, capable, faithful adults. And the evidence has nothing whatever to say about the worth, the faithfulness or the belonging of the parent raising them.

This is the distinction the whole question turns on. There's a difference between a norm the church teaches and a condition of belonging it imposes. The church can teach that God's design for children includes a mother and father in covenant, and it must. It cannot make that design the price of admission to its common life, because the people who fall short of the design are not mostly people who rejected it. Many single parents are widowed. Many were abandoned. Many fled a home where they or their children were in danger, and if that's where you are, the National Domestic Violence Hotline answers at 1-800-799-7233. Some made choices they now regret, as every parent in every pew has done in some other part of life. A church that treats all of them as illustrations of what goes wrong when people don't follow the plan has confused a teaching about children with a verdict about adults.

## How the church came to be built for two parents

The early church did not have this problem in the same form, because it did not understand itself as a collection of nuclear families. It understood itself as a family. Joseph Hellerman argues in *When the Church Was a Family* (2009) that the dominant image the New Testament uses for the church is a group of brothers and sisters, and that in the Mediterranean world this was not a sentimental metaphor. Siblings in that culture were the most loyal of relations, bound to share resources and defend one another. When Paul calls Christians brothers and sisters, he's describing obligations as concrete as money and food.

You can see it in the church's first administrative crisis. "Now in these days when the disciples were increasing in number, a complaint by the Hellenists arose against the Hebrews because their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution" (Acts 6:1). The church in Jerusalem was already feeding widows every day, and it was already forgetting some of them, the ones who spoke Greek and sat a little outside the center. The apostles didn't answer the complaint with a sermon. They had the congregation choose seven men and put the daily distribution in their hands. The church's first structural reform was organized around a household without a husband.

By the time Paul wrote to Timothy, the congregation kept a list of widows it supported and had rules for who belonged on it (1 Timothy 5:3–16). The same chapter holds families responsible first: "But if anyone does not provide for his relatives, and especially for members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever" (1 Timothy 5:8). The church was not replacing the family. It was standing behind it and filling in where it had failed. Rodney Stark argued in *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) that this kind of care was one reason the faith grew, and he cited the Emperor Julian, who around 362 complained in a letter to a pagan priest that the Christians supported not only their own poor but the pagan poor as well. The church's enemies noticed who it fed.

The modern American church was built on a different pattern. After the Second World War, congregations followed families to the suburbs and organized themselves around the households they found there: a husband who worked, a wife who kept the home, and children who were sorted by age into Sunday school classes. The couples' class, the men's breakfast, the women's circle meeting on a weekday morning and the family night at church all assumed that arrangement. David Brooks argued in a much-discussed 2020 essay in *The Atlantic*, "The Nuclear Family Was a Mistake," that the detached household of two parents and their children, living apart from extended kin, was a brief mid-century arrangement that depended on unusual economic conditions and collapsed when those conditions changed. One doesn't have to accept all of his argument to see its force for the church. Congregations organized themselves around a particular kind of household at the moment it was most common, and kept the organization after the households changed.

Then, from the late 1970s, American evangelicalism made the family a central public cause. There were good reasons. Divorce had risen sharply through the 1970s as no-fault laws spread from state to state, and pastors watched children absorb the damage. But a movement built to defend the family could easily make families the center of church life, and the sociologist Robert Wuthnow observed in *After the Baby Boomers* (2007) that congregations had become organized around married couples with children, and were poorly equipped for the growing number of adults whose lives did not fit that pattern. The single parent was caught in the middle. Her household was the thing the family movement warned about, and the church's life was arranged for the households she didn't have.

We did this. Pastors, and I am one, plan church calendars around the families who arrive on time and have the energy to volunteer. We preach on marriage without a word to the person in the third row who was left. None of it was malicious. It was easier, and the people it left out were the least likely to complain.

## The single parent in Scripture

Scripture does not share the church's silence. The first single mother in the Bible is Hagar, an Egyptian slave used by Abraham and Sarah to produce an heir and then driven out when she was no longer convenient. The first time she flees into the wilderness, pregnant and alone, the angel of the Lord finds her by a spring. "So she called the name of the LORD who spoke to her, 'You are a God of seeing,' for she said, 'Truly here I have seen him who looks after me'" (Genesis 16:13). She is the first person in the Bible to give God a name. Years later, sent away for good with her son and a skin of water, she puts the boy under a bush and walks off so she won't have to watch him die. "And God heard the voice of the boy, and the angel of God called to Hagar from heaven and said to her, 'What troubles you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the boy where he is'" (Genesis 21:17).

The story is not tidy. Hagar's suffering was caused by the patriarch of the faith, and the text doesn't excuse him. But it does show where God's attention goes. The household that Abraham's family expelled is the one God follows into the desert.

The widow of Zarephath meets Elijah while gathering sticks for a last meal. "I have nothing baked, only a handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug. And now I am gathering a couple of sticks that I may go in and prepare it for myself and my son, that we may eat it and die" (1 Kings 17:12). Every single parent who has done the arithmetic at the end of a month knows that sentence. In Luke's Gospel Jesus meets a funeral procession at the gate of Nain: "a man who had died was being carried out, the only son of his mother, and she was a widow" (Luke 7:12). Luke says that "when the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her" (Luke 7:13). His compassion is directed at the mother. In that world her son was her only protection, and his death left her with nothing. Jesus gave him back to her.

The Psalms put this at the center of who God is. "The LORD watches over the sojourners; he upholds the widow and the fatherless, but the way of the wicked he brings to ruin" (Psalm 146:9). And one of the New Testament's most important young leaders was raised in a divided home. Timothy was "the son of a Jewish woman who was a believer, but his father was a Greek" (Acts 16:1), and Paul credits the faith Timothy carried to his mother and grandmother. Whatever his father's presence, the faith came through the women who raised him.

Then there is the passage that changes how the whole question should be framed. When Jesus's mother and brothers came to take him home, he looked around at the people seated with him and said, "Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:34–35). Later, when Peter protested that the disciples had left everything, Jesus promised that no one who had left "house or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or lands" for his sake would fail to receive "a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:29–30). Look carefully at the second list. Fathers are missing. The new family has brothers and sisters and mothers and children in abundance, and one Father.

That is the theological ground under everything else. In the church, no household is complete on its own and none is meant to be. The married couple with three children is not the church's standard unit, with the single parent as a broken version of it. Both are households within a larger family whose members owe each other the loyalty of siblings. The single mother's household lacks a husband. The married couple's household lacks the grandmothers, uncles, older friends and younger mentors that every child needs and no two parents can supply alone. The church is supposed to be the family that fills in what every household lacks.

## What forgetting looks like, and what remembering would take

It helps to be concrete, because the forgetting is mostly practical. It looks like a weeknight small group with no childcare, which a single parent can't attend without paying a sitter. It looks like the volunteer rotation that assumes two adults, so that one can serve while the other watches the children. It looks like Mother's Day and Father's Day services where the parent raising children alone is honored in neither role, or in both without anyone noticing. It looks like a men's ministry that has nothing to offer a boy without a father, and a women's ministry that meets at ten on a Wednesday morning. It looks like the pastor's warmth toward the widow and a slight coolness toward the divorced mother, as though the church had to decide how much sympathy each had earned.

The single father has his own version. Churches often have no category for him. Women in the congregation may be unsure how to offer help without seeming forward, and men may assume he has it handled. He often doesn't. He's learning to braid hair from videos and wondering whether his daughters need something he can't give them.

Remembering would take less than it seems. It would mean childcare at the evening events. It would mean a family in the congregation who has the single mother's children over every other Thursday so she can get groceries alone. It would mean a retired mechanic who looks at her car before she takes it to the shop. It would mean men in the church who take a fatherless boy fishing, teach him to use a drill and come to his games, and women who do the same for a girl being raised by her father. Paul told Timothy to encourage an older man "as you would a father, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters, in all purity" (1 Timothy 5:1–2). He was describing a church in which the roles a household lacks are filled by the congregation.

The phrase "in all purity" is not decoration. Predators seek out single-parent families because an overwhelmed parent may welcome any adult who offers to help. A church that takes up this work has to take up protection with it: background checks for anyone working with children, two adults present rather than one, transparency with the parent about every outing, and a willingness to act on any concern. The church's history in the last several decades makes this non-negotiable. Care for the fatherless that leaves them less safe is not care.

Remembering would also take a change in how we speak. It would mean a pastor who, when he preaches on marriage, pauses to address the person raising children alone, not with pity but with honor. It would mean prayers on Mother's Day for the mothers who are also doing a father's work. It would mean a church where a divorced parent's story is not the first thing anyone wants to know. Much of this costs almost nothing. It only requires that someone notice.

## To the parent carrying it alone

If you are that parent, a few things should be said to you directly.

Your household is not a lesser household in the eyes of God. The Scriptures that speak of the widow and the fatherless do not speak of them as problems. They speak of them as people God watches over personally. You are carrying the load of two people with the resources of one, and some of what you're failing at is not failure. It's arithmetic. There are only so many hours, and you can't be at the recital and the second job at once.

The statistics are about averages, and your children are not averages. They will be shaped by what you do, by the adults you bring into their lives and by the grace of God that doesn't track the census. Scripture tells us almost nothing about Timothy's father except that he was a Greek. His mother and grandmother shaped a man who led churches.

There are temptations particular to your situation, and naming them is a kindness. One is to make an older child into a confidant or partner, leaning on a twelve-year-old for the emotional support another adult should give. Children in this position often become remarkably capable, and they often pay for it later. Another is bitterness toward the other parent, which children absorb whether or not you voice it. Still another is pride that refuses help because accepting it feels like admitting defeat. Every one of these is understandable. Every one of them can be brought to God and to a trusted friend in the church. And if you've reached a place where the weight feels unbearable, where you wonder whether your children would be better off without you, please call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, today. The feeling is real, and it is lying to you.

On practical matters, child support, custody and money especially, get advice from people qualified to give it. This essay is not legal or financial advice. Many churches can connect you with members who are lawyers, accountants or financial counselors willing to help without charge. Ask. The worst thing a church can do is fail to offer. The second worst is for you not to ask when it would have.

And the church is yours. Not as a guest, and not as a ministry project. You belong to it as fully as the couple on the porch swing, because you were brought into it the same way they were, by grace and through a covenant made on your behalf. If the people around you have forgotten that, they need reminding, and you have more standing to remind them than you think.

## Behold your mother

There is a scene at the cross that the church reads on Good Friday and then mostly forgets. Jesus, dying, looks down and sees his mother. By then she seems to have been a widow; Joseph is never mentioned during the ministry years. Her firstborn son is being executed. In that culture she was about to be left without the person responsible for her care. "When Jesus saw his mother and the disciple whom he loved standing nearby, he said to his mother, 'Woman, behold, your son!' Then he said to the disciple, 'Behold, your mother!' And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home" (John 19:26–27).

In the middle of bearing the sin of the world, Jesus arranged a household for a woman who was about to be alone. He didn't do it by restoring her husband or her son. He did it by joining her to a disciple, making the two of them family by his word, and the disciple took her home that same hour. That is the church's first family ministry, performed from the cross, and it has the shape the church has been forgetting ever since. The household God builds for the person left alone is not a program or a sympathy card. It is another member of the family who takes her in.

The mother in the parking lot will be back next Sunday, eight minutes late again, with the shoe found or not. Whether the church she walks into knows it is her family, whether anyone has noticed she comes alone and done something about it, is not a question she can answer. It belongs to the rest of us. Jesus answered it for his own mother with his last strength. The disciple he spoke to didn't form a committee. He took her home.
