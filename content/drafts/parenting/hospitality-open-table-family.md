Most families mean to have people over. They talk about it on the drive home from church, when they see a family they'd like to know better, or a widow sitting alone, or a college student far from home. Then the week happens. The house is a mess. The children are tired and short-tempered by six o'clock. There's no time to cook anything worth serving, and the thought of a guest seeing the pile of shoes by the door and the dishes in the sink is enough to end the idea before it starts. Months pass. The only people who come through the front door are relatives at holidays and the children's friends for play dates, and the family doesn't notice that its home has quietly closed.

Parents who search for how to practice hospitality as a family usually aren't looking for a theology of the stranger. They're looking for permission and a plan: some way to open a home that feels full and chaotic to people outside it, without making life harder for children who already need more of their parents than they get. That's a fair thing to want, and it deserves a real answer. But it's worth asking first why an open door feels so difficult, because the difficulty isn't only about time. It has a history, and the history shaped what we think a home is for.

The distinction this essay turns on is between a haven and a fortress. A haven is a place of shelter, and every child needs one: a home that is safe, warm and set apart from the demands of the world, where he is known and loved. A fortress is a haven that has forgotten why it shelters. It protects in order to exclude. The walls that were meant to keep a family safe become walls that keep everyone else out, and the children inside grow up believing that love is something a family keeps for itself. The difference between them isn't the size of the house or the number of guests. It's whether the door can open.

I'll grant at once what is true in the fortress, because it's a great deal. Children need their parents' attention, and a household constantly full of guests can starve them of it. Families need privacy to fight and reconcile, to grieve, to be tired in front of one another. Some homes are fragile: a marriage in trouble, a child who is struggling, a parent who is ill. For them, a protected space is not selfishness but mercy. And there are real dangers in an open door, especially for children. None of that is wrong. The question is whether a home built to protect can also be a home that welcomes, and whether children raised in it will learn both.

## How the home became a haven

For most of human history, the household was not private in our sense. It was an economy, a place of work where parents, children, servants, apprentices, boarders, aging relatives and passing strangers all lived and labored under one roof. It was crowded and porous. The line between family and guest was blurry because the household itself was a mixed company.

The industrial revolution changed that. As work moved out of the home and into the factory and office, the household lost its economic function and gained an emotional one. The Victorians gave the new home its ideal. John Ruskin, in his lecture "Of Queens' Gardens," published in *Sesame and Lilies* (1865), described the true home as a place of peace, a shelter from the injury, terror and division of the outer world, a sacred place set apart. Home was where the husband retreated from the harsh competition of the market, and where the wife kept a sanctuary for him and the children. The idea was beautiful and, in its way, a real good. It also sealed the household off from the world it had once been part of.

The historian Christopher Lasch named the result in the title of his book *Haven in a Heartless World* (1977). The modern family, he argued, had been asked to be a refuge from a market society, the one place of warmth and loyalty in a world of contracts and competition. But the haven was besieged. The very experts and institutions that promised to support the family also invaded it, and a household expected to supply all the love its members needed was carrying a weight no family could bear.

Postwar America built the haven in concrete. The suburban house with its private backyard, the attached garage that let a family drive in and out without ever greeting a neighbor, the television in the living room: each was a small improvement in comfort, and together they turned the home inward. David Brooks, writing in *The Atlantic* in 2020 under the provocative title "The Nuclear Family Was a Mistake," argued that the detached nuclear family, two parents and their children living alone, was a brief historical anomaly, flourishing roughly from 1950 to 1965 before it began to crack. For most of history, he pointed out, families were larger and more porous, woven into extended kin and neighbors who shared the burden of raising children and caring for the old. Brooks didn't argue against marriage or parents. He argued that the isolated household asks too much of too few people.

That history explains the feeling that stops the family on the drive home from church. We inherited a picture of the home as a private sanctuary for the nuclear family, and a guest feels like an intrusion into it. We also inherited Ruskin's standard of what a home should look like, which makes the pile of shoes by the door feel like an exposure. The feeling isn't laziness. It's the residue of an idea about home that has little to do with Scripture.

## What the gospel asks of a house

The biblical case for hospitality is made at length in [What Does the Bible Say About Hospitality? More Than Hosting](/writing/hospitality-not-optional): the Greek word *philoxenia*, the love of the stranger; Paul's command to pursue it (Romans 12:13); Abraham running from his tent in the heat of the day; the tables of Jesus and the house churches that followed. It's enough here to hold on to two things that bear directly on a family with children.

The first is that Scripture never pretended hospitality was easy. "Show hospitality to one another without grumbling," Peter wrote (1 Peter 4:9), which means he expected there would be something to grumble about: guests who stay too long, the lost evening, the mess left behind. He didn't treat the cost as an exemption. An open home isn't a home that never feels the cost. It's a home that pays it, and children who watch their parents pay it without resentment learn what love costs better than any lesson could teach them.

The second is that the gospel is itself an act of hospitality. The complaint against Jesus was that "this man receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2), and it was true. Those of us who were once strangers to God and were welcomed at his table aren't free to keep our own tables closed. A child raised in a Christian home will hear, again and again, that God welcomed him. The question is whether he ever sees his family do for anyone else what God has done for him.

## A room on the roof

There is a story in 2 Kings that shows what hospitality can mean for a household, including its children, and it is rarely told for that reason. The prophet Elisha regularly passed through the town of Shunem, and a wealthy woman there urged him to stop and eat whenever he came through. Then she went further.

> And she said to her husband, "Behold now, I perceive that this is a holy man of God who is continually passing our way. Let us make a small room on the roof with walls and put there for him a bed, a table, a chair, and a lamp, so that whenever he comes to us, he can go in there." (2 Kings 4:9–10)

It's a small thing, a room with four pieces of furniture. But notice what it is. It isn't a grand gesture or a single dinner. It's a permanent change to the house, made by a husband and wife together, to make room for someone outside the family. The Shunammite woman didn't wait for Elisha to need something. She built the room before he asked.

The story goes on. Elisha, wanting to repay her, promises that she will have a son, though she has none and her husband is old. The child is born. Years later the boy collapses in the field and dies in his mother's lap, and she carries him up to the prophet's room and lays him on the prophet's bed. She rides to find Elisha, and he comes back to the house and raises the boy to life in that same room (2 Kings 4:18–37).

The details matter. The child's life came through the household's hospitality, and when the child died, his mother laid him on the bed she had built for a stranger. The room made for the guest became the place where her son was given back to her. The story doesn't promise that every act of hospitality will be repaid, and it would be a cruel reading that turned it into a formula. But it does show a household in which the welcome of the outsider and the life of the child are bound together, and in which the family's openness to a stranger became, in the end, the thing that carried its own child through death.

Christians have seen the same pattern in the new family Jesus creates. When his mother and brothers came looking for him, he looked at the people sitting around him and said, "Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:34–35). That isn't a rejection of his family. It's a widening of it. And he promised his disciples that no one who left house and family for his sake would fail to receive "a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions" (Mark 10:30). The only way that promise is kept is through Christian households that open their doors. The widow's new family is someone's dinner table. The student's new home is someone's guest room. A family that keeps its door closed is withholding the hundredfold Jesus promised to someone else.

## What the guest teaches the child

Children raised in an open home learn things that are hard to teach any other way.

They learn, first, that they are not the center of the universe. A child who regularly gives up his seat at the table, or his room for a night, or his parents' undivided attention for an evening, learns that love makes room for others. That is not a lesson that can be delivered in words. It has to be practiced, and practiced often enough that it becomes part of how the child sees the world.

They learn, second, that the body of Christ is larger and stranger than their own family. Around an open table, a child meets the old man who fought in a war, the refugee family, the single mother, the recovering addict, the international student, the missionary on furlough. Each guest brings a story the child would never otherwise hear. A child who has sat at dinner with people of different ages, nations and circumstances is harder to recruit into the contempt that divides so much of our common life. He has eaten with the people he's told to despise.

Rosaria Butterfield, a former professor at Syracuse University, has written about both sides of this. In *The Secret Thoughts of an Unlikely Convert* (2012) she described how her own conversion, from a life she had built far from Christian faith, began at the dinner table of a pastor named Ken Smith and his wife, Floy, who welcomed her into their home again and again without pressure or agenda. Years later, as a pastor's wife and a mother, she wrote *The Gospel Comes with a House Key* (2018), whose subtitle names what she calls "radically ordinary hospitality." Her argument is that welcome does its deepest work when it is daily and unremarkable, woven into ordinary days rather than staged for special ones. For a family, that is the useful part. Children raised in such a house don't experience guests as an interruption of family life. They experience them as part of it.

There is a cautionary witness too, and it should be heard. In 1955, Francis and Edith Schaeffer opened their home in the Swiss village of Huémoz to young people with questions about God. Edith told the story in *L'Abri* (1969), and the community that grew there became famous: thousands of seekers passed through, and many came to faith. Their son, Frank Schaeffer, grew up in the middle of it, and his later memoir, *Crazy for God* (2007), describes that childhood, lived in the middle of his parents' guests and their mission, with a mixture of affection and grievance. Whatever one makes of his wider account, parents who open their homes should take the warning seriously: a child raised inside a mission can come to feel that he is secondary to it. Hospitality that consumes the family's own children has lost its balance. A child should experience the guest as someone the family welcomes together, not as a rival for his parents' love.

Robert Farrar Capon, an Episcopal priest and cook, wrote *The Supper of the Lamb* (1969), a meditation on food as a gift of God to be received with delight and attention. His point, pressed with humor across the whole book, is that a meal is not just fuel but a small act of worship, and that setting a table for others is one of the most human things we do. Children understand this instinctively. They love helping to cook for a guest, choosing the dessert, lighting the candle. The work of hospitality is one of the few forms of service that small children can genuinely share in.

And the table has to be protected from our devices if it is going to do its work. Sherry Turkle, in *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015), reported research suggesting that even a silent phone on the table changes what people are willing to talk about, keeping conversation shallow. A family that opens its table to a guest and then lets everyone drift into their screens has opened its door and closed its attention. The guest notices, and so do the children.

## The objections at the door

The objections to an open home deserve their strongest statement, because many of them are wise.

The first is exhaustion. Parents of young children are often at the edge of what they can manage, and the thought of adding guests is overwhelming. That's true, and the answer is not to add a burden but to lower the standard. Hospitality isn't entertaining. It doesn't require a clean house, a special meal or a free evening. It requires a willingness to let someone into the life the family is already living. A guest who eats macaroni with the children at a table covered in crayons has received hospitality. A lonely guest is rarely grading the kitchen.

The second is temperament. Some people are introverts who find company draining, and some homes are small. That's real, and hospitality can take many forms. It can be a regular meal with one person rather than a party. It can be a walk, a shared picnic, a standing invitation for one lonely neighbor. What matters is the direction of the heart, not the scale of the event.

The third is safety, and it must be taken with full seriousness. An open home is not a home without boundaries. Parents have every right, and every duty, to protect their children: to know whom they invite, to keep children's bedrooms and bathrooms private, to avoid leaving children alone with adults they do not know well, and to be especially careful with overnight guests. The church's own history of abuse has taught painful lessons about misplaced trust, and a family that ignores them in the name of hospitality has confused openness with carelessness. If you are ever worried that a child may be at risk, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline is at 1-800-422-4453 (call or text), and if a child is in immediate danger, call 911. Wisdom and welcome are not enemies. The Shunammite woman built a separate room.

The fourth is the season of trouble. A family in grief, in illness, in a marital crisis, or caring for a child with great needs may simply not have anything left to give. That's not a failure of hospitality. It is a season to receive it. The church exists partly so that families in those seasons can be carried by others, and a family that has been fed by others in its hard season will be the most generous host when the season turns.

The fifth is the spouse who isn't willing. Hospitality that one partner imposes on the other breeds resentment and damages the home it was meant to open. The Shunammite woman spoke to her husband first. A couple that disagrees about how open their home should be needs to talk honestly and move at the pace of the more hesitant partner, because a welcoming home begins with a united one.

Beneath all these objections is a real truth: the haven matters. Children need a safe home, and parents need rest. The answer is not to tear down the walls of the haven but to put a door in them, a door the family opens together, on purpose, often enough that the children learn that the shelter they enjoy is meant to be shared.

## A door the children remember

There is a moment in Mark's Gospel when the disciples have been arguing on the road about which of them is the greatest. Jesus answers them with a child. "And he took a child and put him in the midst of them, and taking him in his arms, he said to them, 'Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me, and whoever receives me, receives not me but him who sent me'" (Mark 9:36–37). The child in that scene isn't the host. He is the guest, the one with nothing to offer, and Jesus makes him the measure of welcome. Every child in a Christian home was first received that way, small and unable to repay, and an open door teaches him to receive others as he was received.

The Christian story ends at a table. The marriage supper of the Lamb is the final picture of what God is doing, and the guest list is people from every nation, tribe and language, none of whom earned their place. Every Christian meal is a small rehearsal of that feast, and a family that sets an extra plate for someone who has nowhere else to eat is practicing, in its own kitchen, the hospitality of the kingdom.

Children who grow up at an open table carry it with them. Long after they've forgotten the lessons their parents taught them, they remember who sat at dinner, the stories they heard and the way their parents treated people who had nothing to give back. They learn that love is not something a family hoards. It's something a family gives away, and finds that it has more.

The house will not be clean enough this week, and the children will be tired, and the dinner will be ordinary. That was always going to be true. The question is whether someone who needs a table will sit at yours anyway, and whether your children will grow up believing that the door of a Christian home is meant to open.
