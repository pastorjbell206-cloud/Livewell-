Picture a father standing in a church lobby at five minutes to ten on a Sunday morning. His nine-year-old son has autism and doesn't speak. He's wearing noise-canceling headphones, and his father carries a bag with the only crackers the boy will eat and a tablet loaded with the same three videos. Last Sunday the boy began to hum loudly during the pastoral prayer, and heads turned, and afterward an usher gently mentioned the room at the back with the window and the speaker. The usher meant well. The father stands in the lobby deciding whether to try the sanctuary again or go home.

His week has already been long. On Tuesday there was the meeting at school about his son's Individualized Education Program, where he and his wife sat across from six professionals and argued for two more hours of speech therapy. On Wednesday there was an insurance denial to appeal. Most nights the boy sleeps four or five hours, and one of his parents sleeps near him. The father hasn't been to a men's group in three years. His marriage runs mostly on logistics. His daughter, eleven, is wonderful with her brother and quieter than she used to be.

He has questions he doesn't say out loud. Why did God allow this? Does his exhaustion mean he doesn't love his son enough? Will the church ever really have room for them? And the one that comes at three in the morning: what happens to his boy when he and his wife are gone?

A word about words. Many disabled adults dislike the phrase "special needs," which they hear as a euphemism for needs that are simply human, and prefer to be called disabled. Many parents use "special needs" because it's the phrase schools and churches use. This essay uses both, and means by them the whole range of conditions, intellectual, developmental, physical and medical, that change what raising a child will ask. The broader argument about disability in Scripture and in the church's history is made in [What Does the Bible Say About Disability?](/writing/disability-dignity-and-the-image-of-god). This essay is for the parents.

## The child you were given

Almost every parent of a disabled child is eventually handed a short piece called "Welcome to Holland." Emily Perl Kingsley, a writer for *Sesame Street* whose son Jason was born with Down syndrome, wrote it in 1987. She compares expecting a baby to planning a trip to Italy, with guidebooks and a phrasebook, and learning a child has a disability to landing in Holland instead. Holland isn't a terrible place, she writes. It's different: slower, with windmills and tulips, and you'll meet people you would never have met. But everyone you know is coming back from Italy, and the pain of that doesn't go away.

Many parents find it a lifeline. Others find it too gentle. Some parents say their child's condition is less like Holland than like a war zone, with self-injury, seizures, violent meltdowns and hospital stays, and that the essay's tulips feel like a reproach. Both reactions are honest. The strength of Kingsley's piece is that it gives parents permission to grieve the trip they planned without that grief becoming a rejection of the place they've landed. That distinction is worth holding onto. Grieving the child you expected is not the same as rejecting the child you have. Scripture gives parents the language of lament for exactly this: "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). The psalmist didn't stop believing in God to say that. He said it to God.

The writer Andrew Solomon offers a sharper frame in *Far From the Tree* (2012), a book built from interviews with hundreds of families. He distinguishes vertical identities, which pass from parent to child, such as ethnicity or language, from horizontal identities, which a child doesn't share with his parents, such as deafness, autism or Down syndrome. Parents, he observes, usually assume their children will be like them, and a child with a horizontal identity forces them to learn to love someone who is, in important ways, unlike them. Solomon found that many parents described the experience as both loss and gift, and he refused either to romanticize their lives or to reduce them to tragedy.

Stanley Hauerwas pressed the theological point years earlier. In *A Community of Character* (1981) he argued that Christians should understand children not as projects we design but as gifts we receive, and in an important sense as strangers. Every parent receives a child who is not the child they imagined, with a temperament, a will and a future of his own. Parents of typical children can go on pretending for years that they chose what they got. Parents of disabled children can't. They know from the beginning what every parent eventually learns: the child was given, not made. In *Suffering Presence* (1986) he went further, warning that the drive to prevent suffering can quietly become a drive to prevent the people who suffer.

This is the first thing a parent in the lobby might be told, and seldom is. You are not in a lesser version of parenthood. You are in the version where the pretense has been stripped away, where you've had to learn from the start that love receives rather than designs. The rest of us are in the same situation. We just haven't noticed.

## How we got here: from the institution to the IEP

The father's battles on Tuesday and Wednesday are the fruit of a history, and it's worth knowing, because it explains both what he's owed and why the weight lands so heavily on him.

For much of the twentieth century, American parents of children with significant intellectual disabilities were routinely told by doctors to place them in institutions and try to forget them. Many did. In 1950 the novelist Pearl Buck, already a Nobel laureate, published *The Child Who Never Grew*, about her daughter Carol, whose condition was later identified as phenylketonuria, and whom Buck had placed at the Vineland Training School in New Jersey. It was an act of courage to write it at all. Families hid such children, and Buck's book told thousands of parents they weren't alone. Three years later Dale Evans Rogers, the wife of the cowboy star Roy Rogers, published *Angel Unaware*, written in the voice of their daughter Robin, who had Down syndrome and died before her second birthday. The Rogerses had been advised to institutionalize her and had kept her at home. The book was a bestseller, and it brought the subject into churches across the country.

Both books did great good. *Angel Unaware* also started a way of speaking about disabled children that still echoes. Robin in the book is an angel sent from heaven to teach her parents. The comfort is real, and so is the problem. A child who is an angel is not quite a child, and a child sent to teach her parents has become, however lovingly, a means to someone else's growth. Disabled children are not angels. They are children, with tempers and preferences and sins of their own, and they deserve to be loved as themselves.

The institutions were not what their defenders claimed. In 1972 a television reporter named Geraldo Rivera took cameras into Willowbrook State School on Staten Island, and the images of naked, neglected children in overcrowded wards shocked the country. A lawsuit followed, and so did reform. In 1975 Congress passed the Education for All Handicapped Children Act, which for the first time guaranteed disabled children a free, appropriate public education. It was renamed the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act in 1990. That is the law behind the IEP meeting. Congress passed the Americans with Disabilities Act the same year.

This was a moral victory, and it came with a cost that isn't often named. When the institutions closed and children came home, the weight that had been carried, badly, by the state was transferred to families, and within families mostly to mothers. Parents became case managers, advocates, therapists, nurses and lawyers for their children, while still being parents. The law guarantees services. It doesn't guarantee that a parent will have the strength to fight for them every year.

Where was the church? In some places it was ahead of everyone. Christians founded many early schools and homes for disabled people, and the ministry Joni Eareckson Tada founded in 1979 has served families for decades. But many congregations were simply absent. Children who couldn't sit still or stay quiet weren't welcome in Sunday school, and families learned that church was for other people. Some of us who lead churches still don't know how many families in our own towns stopped trying.

## The father who said "help us"

There is a father in the Gospels whom parents of disabled children often recognize. Mark tells of a man who brought his son to Jesus's disciples, and they couldn't help him. Luke adds that he told Jesus, "he is my only child" (Luke 9:38). The boy's condition is described as a spirit that threw him into convulsions, and it has to be said plainly that the passage is not a diagnosis of any modern condition. Christians have done real harm by treating disabilities as demonic, and nothing here should be read that way. What matters for this essay is not the boy's condition but how Jesus treats his father.

Jesus asks the father a question: "How long has this been happening to him?" And he said, "From childhood" (Mark 9:21). It's a question a doctor might ask, or a friend. It invites the father to tell his story. The father tells it: the fire and the water, the years of danger. Then he makes his request: "But if you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us" (Mark 9:22). Notice the pronoun. Not "help him," but "help us." The father understood what parents of disabled children know: the condition belongs to the child, and the burden belongs to the whole family.

Jesus presses him on the "if," and the father's answer has become one of the most honest prayers in Scripture: "Immediately the father of the child cried out and said, 'I believe; help my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:24). Jesus accepts that prayer. He doesn't require the father to purify his faith first. The father brings belief and unbelief tangled together, and it's enough. Then, Mark says, "Jesus took him by the hand and lifted him up, and he arose" (Mark 9:27).

Two things in this story deserve attention. The first is that the disciples failed before Jesus acted. The father had already brought his son to the people who represented Jesus, and they couldn't help. Many parents of disabled children have had that experience in church. The second is that Jesus took the father's burden as seriously as the son's condition. He asked about the history. He answered the prayer that said "us."

Not every prayer for a disabled child will be answered with a cure, and parents who have been told that more faith would bring healing have been handed a cruelty dressed as theology. The father in Mark 9 is a model not because his son was healed but because he told the truth about his faith and his need. Parents are permitted to pray the same way. The psalmist says of God, "Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them" (Psalm 139:16). The child was known before the diagnosis and will be known after it. That doesn't answer the question of why. It does mean that the child's life is not an accident God is scrambling to fix.

## What it costs a marriage, and the other children

Parents of children with autism are often told that 80 percent of such marriages end in divorce. The figure has no reliable source. When researchers led by Sigan Hartley studied the question and published their findings in 2010, they found that parents of children with autism did divorce at a higher rate than a comparison group, and that the elevated risk lasted well into the child's adolescence and early adulthood, when the risk for other parents usually declines. The strain is real, and it's long. But most of these marriages survive, and the myth of near-certain collapse does its own damage by persuading couples that failure is inevitable.

The strain takes predictable forms. One parent, often the mother, becomes the expert on the child, attending every appointment and learning every term, and the other feels shut out or guilty. Spouses grieve differently, and each can read the other's way of grieving as not caring. Sleep deprivation erodes patience. The conversation narrows to schedules and medications. Intimacy disappears, and nobody has time to notice. None of this means the marriage is failing. It means the marriage is carrying a weight most marriages don't, and it needs deliberate care. A few hours away together is not an indulgence. It's maintenance. So is a counselor who understands families like yours.

The siblings carry their own weight. Brothers and sisters of disabled children often grow up with remarkable compassion and maturity. They also grow up with less of their parents' attention than their friends get, with embarrassment they feel guilty about, with responsibilities that come early, and with the quiet knowledge that someday their brother or sister may depend on them. They need their own time with each parent, their own permission to be frustrated, and their own place to talk. Sibling support groups exist in many places, and a church can offer something like them. The eleven-year-old who has become quieter is not a problem to fix. She's a child who needs to be seen.

And the parents themselves need someone to watch over them. Caregiving on this scale can lead to depression and, in some cases, to despair. If you've reached the point where you can't see a way forward, or where thoughts of harming yourself come, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, at any hour. That's not a failure of faith. The psalms of lament were written by people who reached the end of themselves.

## The church that can hold them

Churches often fail these families in ways that are easy to name. There is the well-meant comment that God gives special children to special parents, which makes a parent's exhaustion feel like ingratitude. There is the stranger who prays for healing over a child without asking, as if the child weren't a person present in the room. There is the children's ministry that can't manage a child's behavior and calls the parents out of the service every week until they stop coming. There is the quiet suggestion to use the room at the back. None of it is cruel. All of it tells the family that the church was built for someone else.

What helps is usually simple. Some churches assign a trained volunteer, often called a buddy, to sit with a child during children's ministry or in the service so the parents can worship. Some create a quiet room that is a place of welcome, not exile, with the service on a screen and permission to move and make noise. Some learn a child's name, how he communicates and what calms him. Some provide respite nights so parents can have an evening alone. Some simply keep a pew for a family and don't turn around when the boy hums.

There are deeper questions too. How does a church baptize, receive into membership or give the Lord's Supper to someone who can't articulate a profession of faith? Traditions answer differently, and those differences are real. But across traditions the church has held that God's grace is not bound to intellectual capacity, and that the Spirit can be at work in a person who can't explain it. The theologian Brian Brock, whose son has Down syndrome and autism, argues in *Wondrously Wounded* (2019) that the church must stop thinking of its disabled members as people to be served and start receiving them as members through whom Christ is present to the rest of the body. That changes the question from what the church can do for a family to what the church is missing without them. Paul put it bluntly: "If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honored, all rejoice together" (1 Corinthians 12:26).

Hauerwas's thinking about disability was shaped partly by L'Arche, the communities in which people with and without intellectual disabilities share life, and he wrote with its founder, Jean Vanier. In February 2020 L'Arche International published the findings of an independent inquiry it had commissioned. It received credible and consistent testimony from six adult women, none of them disabled, that between 1970 and 2005 Vanier had initiated sexual relationships with them, usually in the context of spiritual accompaniment, and it judged those relationships manipulative and emotionally abusive. That has to be said plainly, and the other essay on disability treats it at length. The women he harmed come before any movement's reputation, and the disabled members of those communities bear none of the shadow.

Parents themselves have written some of the most important witness. Amy Julia Becker's *A Good and Perfect Gift* (2011), about her daughter Penny, who has Down syndrome, describes how she came to see that her own ideas of what makes a life good were what most needed to change. Ian Brown, a Canadian journalist and not a religious writer, wrote *The Boy in the Moon* (2009) about his son Walker, born with a rare genetic condition that left him unable to speak and prone to hurting himself. Brown is honest about the exhaustion, the nights of little sleep and the questions about what Walker's life is worth. He comes to believe that Walker's life has meaning apart from any usefulness, and that the question of its value says more about the questioner than about his son. A Christian reader can hear in that an echo of something older. Worth is not earned. It's given.

We should confess that churches have often been the last to learn this. Those of us who lead congregations plan buildings, services and programs around people who can sit still and follow along. We welcome disabled families with warmth and then arrange everything so that they can't really take part. The father in the lobby isn't asking for a special program. He's asking whether there's room.

## Who will carry him when we're gone

The fear that wakes parents at three in the morning is about the future. A child with significant disabilities may need care for life, and parents know they won't live forever. Who will make his decisions? Where will he live? Who will know that he likes the blue cup and hates loud rooms? Who will love him?

Some of the answers are practical, and they matter. At eighteen, a disabled child becomes a legal adult, and parents may need to consider guardianship or less restrictive alternatives such as supported decision-making. Special needs trusts can hold money for a disabled person without disqualifying him from public benefits. Since the ABLE Act of 2014, many disabled people can save in dedicated accounts without losing eligibility for certain programs. A letter of intent, describing the person's routines, preferences and history for whoever cares for him next, is among the most loving documents a parent can write. This is not legal or financial advice, and the rules differ by state. A lawyer who specializes in special needs planning is worth finding early.

But the deeper answer is not a document. It's a people. The question of who will carry a disabled adult when his parents are gone is one the church is meant to answer, because the church is the only family that doesn't die with a generation. Congregations can be the place where a disabled adult is known by name over decades, where someone visits the group home and brings him to worship, where he has friends who aren't paid to be there. Few churches are this yet. Some are learning.

Scripture puts God himself at the center of this fear. "Listen to me, O house of Jacob, all the remnant of the house of Israel, who have been borne by me from before your birth, carried from the womb; even to your old age I am he, and to gray hairs I will carry you. I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save" (Isaiah 46:3–4). The prophet was speaking to a nation, contrasting the living God with idols that had to be carried on the backs of animals. But the promise holds for every person in that nation, including the one who can't carry himself. God carries from the womb to the grave, and beyond it.

Paul's great chapter on the resurrection says: "It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power" (1 Corinthians 15:43). What the resurrection body of a disabled person will be like is a question Christians answer differently, and the other essay leaves it open. What isn't open is that the body will be raised, and that the one raised will be the same person God knew before he was born.

The father in the lobby may go into the sanctuary this morning or he may go home. Whichever he does, the question he's carrying is one the church can't answer by explaining things to him. The father in Mark 9 asked Jesus to help "us," and Jesus took the boy by the hand. The church is the body through which Christ still takes people by the hand, and whether that father finds a hand held out, a pew kept for his family, a buddy for his son, a friend for his daughter and someone who promises to know his boy's name when he's gone, depends on the rest of us. His son is not waiting to become a person worth making room for. He already is one, and the church that learns to receive him will discover how much it was missing.
