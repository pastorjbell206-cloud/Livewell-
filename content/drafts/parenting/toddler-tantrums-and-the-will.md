Think of a grocery store on a weekday afternoon. A two-year-old wants the cereal with the cartoon on the box, and her mother has said no, and the child has gone down in the aisle like a building being demolished. She is rigid, then limp, then kicking. The noise she makes is not quite crying. It is something older and louder than crying, the sound of a whole person refusing a whole world. Other shoppers are pretending not to look, which is worse than looking. And the mother, who has read the books and knows she is supposed to stay calm, feels something rising in her own chest that is not so different from what is happening on the floor. She wants what she wants too. She wants to leave this store with her dignity and her groceries, and she wants this small tyrant to stop, and for a moment she is not sure which of them is the adult.

Every parent of a toddler has stood in some version of that aisle. The advice for it is plentiful, and most of it is about technique: get down on the child's level, name the feeling, offer two choices, ignore the performance, carry her out without a word. Some of those techniques are wise, and a few are indispensable. But the question underneath the tantrum is not really a question of technique. It is a question about what a child is, and what a will is, and what a parent is for. The answers we give to those questions, usually without knowing we're giving them, decide what we do in the aisle. They also decide who the child becomes twenty years later, long after the cereal is forgotten.

## Innocent or fallen: the oldest argument about small children

The Christian tradition has argued about small children for sixteen hundred years, and the argument begins with one of the most unsentimental passages ever written about a baby.

Augustine, writing his *Confessions* around the year 400, turned in the first book to the question of whether he had been innocent as an infant. He couldn't remember his infancy, so he did what any honest observer would do and watched other babies. What he reported reads, sixteen centuries later, like a description of the grocery aisle. A baby, he wrote, slowly becomes aware of what it wants and of the adults who could give it, but its wishes are inside and the adults are outside, and it has no way to get the one across to the other. So it flails its limbs and makes what noises it can. And when it doesn't get its way, either because no one understands it or because what it wants would hurt it, it grows indignant with its elders for not obeying, as though they were its servants, and takes its revenge in tears. Both halves of the modern argument about tantrums are already in that passage. There is the child who cannot make herself understood, and there is the child who will not accept a no. Augustine saw no need to choose between them. Then he added an observation that is easy to pass over and may matter more than the first. Adults, he noticed, put up with these things in infants, not because they are harmless, but because everyone assumes they will disappear with age. A little later he described learning to speak, and his account of why is just as blunt: he learned words so that he could make his wishes known and get what he wanted.

Nearly fourteen centuries later, Jean-Jacques Rousseau opened *Emile* (1762), his enormously influential treatise on education, with a sentence that amounted to a direct reply. Everything is good as it comes from the hands of the Creator, he wrote, and everything degenerates in the hands of man. The child is born good. Society corrupts him. The educator's task is mostly to protect the child's natural goodness from interference. Rousseau's picture, softened and secularized, is the ancestor of a great deal of modern parenting advice, including much of what circulates among Christians, and it has real wisdom in it. Children are corrupted by adult cruelty and hypocrisy. Many of their outbursts are provoked by adults who ask too much of them, too fast, in environments designed for adult convenience.

It would be easy to set the two men against each other and choose a side, and many Christian parenting books have done exactly that, usually choosing Augustine and then treating the child as an adversary to be subdued. But the Gospels complicate the choice. When the disciples tried to keep children away from Jesus, he was indignant, and he said, "Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God" (Mark 10:14). He took them in his arms and laid his hands on them. On another occasion he told grown men, "Truly, I say to you, unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:3). Whatever Jesus thought of children, he did not think they were small criminals.

The way through is a distinction that Rousseau lost entirely and many of Augustine's heirs lost in the other direction. A child is not innocent, and a child is not an enemy. A child is a creature made in God's image, bearing the same inclination toward self that every human being bears, and also possessing something adults tend to lose: the capacity to receive, to trust, to depend without shame. When Jesus held up children as models, he was pointing to that dependence, not to their moral purity. When Augustine watched the indignant infant, he was pointing to the self-will, not denying the dependence. Both are there in the grocery aisle. The child on the floor is a small sinner and a small image-bearer at the same time, and she needs the adult to treat her as both.

Augustine's quieter observation matters most here. The adults he watched assumed that the infant's self-will would simply fade with age, and he thought they were wrong. It doesn't fade. It grows up. The toddler who wants the cereal becomes the teenager who wants the car and the adult who wants the promotion, the house, the spouse's agreement, the last word. What changes is not the will but its sophistication. A parent who waits for age to fix the will is waiting for something that never comes on its own.

## Can't and won't

If the old argument is about what a child is, the modern argument is about what a tantrum is, and it has two camps that mirror Augustine and Rousseau more closely than either would admit.

James Dobson's *The Strong-Willed Child* (1978) spoke for the first camp and shaped a generation of evangelical parents. Dobson argued that some children are born with an unusually determined will, that they will test every boundary a parent sets, and that the parent who fails to win the early contests will lose the later ones. It is worth steelmanning him, because his critics rarely do. Dobson was writing against a permissive culture that treated every limit as repression, and he saw clearly that a child who never meets a firm no is not free but abandoned. He also drew a distinction his critics often forget. He separated willful defiance, where a child knowingly challenges a parent's authority, from childish irresponsibility, where a child forgets, spills, or fails because he is a child, and he insisted that the second should not be punished as though it were the first. That is a good distinction, and many parents who have read the book have forgotten it. The larger quarrel over punishment, including the rod and spanking, belongs to a different question and is taken up in this site's essay on [discipline without anger](/writing/discipline-without-domination); this one stays with the tantrum itself.

Ross Greene's *The Explosive Child* (1998) spoke for the second camp and gave it a slogan: kids do well if they can. Greene, a clinical psychologist, argued that most children who melt down are not choosing to misbehave. They are lacking skills, such as flexibility, frustration tolerance and problem-solving, that the situation demands, and punishment cannot teach a skill a child doesn't have. His work has helped countless families whose children were being treated as rebels when they were actually overwhelmed. Daniel Siegel and Tina Payne Bryson, in *The Whole-Brain Child* (2011), gave parents a simple picture of the same idea: the parts of the brain that manage impulse and plan ahead are the last to mature, so a two-year-old in the grip of a strong feeling has, quite literally, not yet built the equipment to calm herself down. They suggested that naming the feeling for the child helps her begin to bring it under control.

Developmental research adds a humbling footnote to both camps. Walter Mischel's famous marshmallow experiments, run at Stanford in the late 1960s and early 1970s, found that preschoolers who could wait for a second treat tended to do better years later. For a generation that was read as proof that self-control is a fixed trait, visible early and predictive for life. But Mischel's own later book, *The Marshmallow Test* (2014), emphasized that waiting is a skill children can be taught, and a 2018 replication by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan found that the link to later outcomes was considerably smaller than the famous studies suggested, and that much of it was explained by a child's family background and early abilities. Most striking is a 2013 study by Celeste Kidd and her colleagues. Before the marshmallow test, children met an adult who either kept or broke a small promise. The children whose adult had proven reliable waited far longer than those whose adult had not. Waiting, it turns out, depends partly on whether the child has reason to believe the promise.

So which is it, can't or won't? For most toddler tantrums, the honest answer is that it's both, tangled together in a way the child can't separate and the parent often can't either. A two-year-old really does lack the capacity to manage a strong feeling. And a two-year-old really does want her own way, with a force that has nothing to do with lacking skills. The Christian account of the person, which holds that we are both fragile and willful, creatures who are limited and creatures who are fallen, is better equipped for this tangle than either camp alone. The parent who sees only won't will punish what the child cannot help. The parent who sees only can't will never ask the child to do the one thing she is capable of learning, which is to want something other than what she wants right now.

## The will is formed by what it loves

If the will is not to be broken and not to be left alone, what does forming it actually mean? Here the older tradition has more to say than the modern debate, because it never thought of the will as a muscle to be overpowered. It thought of it as a love to be ordered.

Aristotle argued in the second book of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, written in the fourth century before Christ, that virtue is not produced by instruction but by habit. We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave ones. Character is the residue of repeated action, which is why he thought the habits formed in youth make not a small difference but a very great one, "or rather all the difference," in W. D. Ross's translation. A toddler does not learn patience by being told that patience is a virtue. She learns it by waiting, over and over, for things that are worth waiting for, in the company of someone who makes the waiting bearable.

Augustine gave Christians a deeper version of the same idea. In *The City of God* he offered a short definition of virtue: the right ordering of love. The problem with the human will, in his account, is not that it loves too much but that it loves in the wrong order, putting lesser goods above greater ones, the self above God and neighbor. Formation, then, is not the suppression of desire but its reordering. C. S. Lewis took up this line in *The Abolition of Man* (1943), arguing that the whole aim of education in the older Western and Christian traditions was to train a child's feelings, so that she would come to like and dislike what she ought, to feel delight at what is good and revulsion at what is shameful. A child who has only learned rules will obey while someone is watching. A child whose loves have been trained will do the right thing because she has come to want it.

Jonathan Edwards made the same point about faith itself in *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* (1746): "True religion, in great part, consists in holy affections." Edwards was writing about adult converts in the aftermath of the Great Awakening, but the principle reaches all the way down to the grocery aisle. The will does not move because it is commanded. It moves toward what it loves. The deepest work of parenting a toddler is not winning the contest over the cereal. It is slowly, patiently, over thousands of small moments, helping a child to love something more than the cereal: the parent's delight, the pleasure of a shared meal later, the goodness of being someone who can wait.

This is also where the Kidd study becomes theological. A child learns to wait when she trusts the one who promised. That is not only a fact about toddlers. It is the structure of faith. Abraham waited for a son because he trusted the God who promised him. Israel waited for a Messiah across centuries of disappointment. The church waits for the return of Christ. In every case the capacity to wait rests on the reliability of the promiser. A parent who keeps small promises, who says "after lunch" and means after lunch, who says "I'll come back" and comes back, is building in the child the very capacity for trust that will one day be asked to rest on God. The psalmist has a picture of the result: "But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me" (Psalm 131:2). The weaned child is no longer screaming for the breast. She has learned that her mother is good even when she doesn't get what she wants, and she rests against her.

## The temper in the room is also mine

It would be dishonest to write about toddler tantrums without writing about the other temper in the aisle. For many parents, the hardest part of the toddler years is not the child's anger but their own.

The toddler has a way of finding the exact spot where a parent's patience ends. She says no to the thing you most need her to do, at the moment you have the least margin to deal with it. She does it again after you've asked six times. She screams in public, which exposes you to the judgment of strangers, and in private, where there is no one to see what you do next. Many parents discover in these years a capacity for rage that they did not know they possessed. It frightens them, and it should, because the gap between a parent's rage and a child's harm is not as wide as we like to think.

The letter of James has a sentence for this that is harder to live than to quote: "Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:19-20). A few chapters later James asks where our fights come from, and his answer is uncomfortably close to Augustine's indignant infant: "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you?" (James 4:1). The parent standing over the screaming child is not a neutral party enforcing the law. He is another will, with his own passions at war, wanting his own way. The toddler didn't create his temper. She revealed it.

That is humbling, and it is also a gift, because it puts the parent and the child on the same side of the problem. The toddler is learning to rule her spirit, and so is her father. Proverbs puts the achievement in military terms: "Whoever is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city" (Proverbs 16:32). A child who watches her parent struggle with anger and win, or lose and then apologize, learns more about self-control than any lecture could teach. A child who watches her parent explode and then act as though nothing happened learns that the adult's will is the only one that matters, and that power is the answer to frustration. She will practice that lesson on her younger siblings long before she practices it on her own children.

There's a theological point underneath this. When God revealed his name to Moses on Sinai, the first thing he said about himself was this: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6). The God who made the child and the parent is slow to anger. That doesn't mean he never gets angry. It means his anger is always in the service of his love, never a discharge of his frustration. When the prophet Jonah sulked outside Nineveh, furious that God had spared the city, God didn't shout at him. He asked a question: "Do you do well to be angry?" (Jonah 4:4). He asked it again when Jonah raged over a withered plant (Jonah 4:9). It is the question every parent needs to hear in the grocery aisle, asked not with contempt but with the kind of patience that gives the angry person room to see himself.

## What a formed will looks like

No essay can tell a parent exactly what to do in the aisle, because every child and every moment is different. But the account we have been building suggests the shape of a faithful response, and it is less dramatic than either camp in the modern debate would like.

It begins with the parent's own spirit. Before the parent can help the child regulate, he has to be regulated himself, which often means a breath, a silent prayer, a decision not to speak until the first wave of heat has passed. It continues with holding the limit without escalating the conflict. The cereal is still no. It doesn't become yes because the child is screaming, and it doesn't become a lecture or a threat either. It simply remains no, held by an adult who is not frightened by the child's anger and not provoked into matching it. It includes naming what the child is feeling, not to excuse it but to give her words for what she can't yet describe, so that someday she can say "I'm so angry" instead of throwing herself on the floor. It includes staying close, because a child learns to calm down first by being calmed by someone else. And it ends, crucially, with repair. When the storm has passed, the parent comes back, holds the child, and restores the relationship. If the parent lost his own temper, he confesses it. The child learns that the relationship survives the conflict, that love is not withdrawn when she fails, and that adults are also sinners who say they're sorry.

None of this happens once. It happens hundreds of times over the toddler years, and most of the time it happens imperfectly. The formation of a will is slow work. Aristotle knew that, and so did every grandmother who ever lived. What the Christian adds is the knowledge that self-control is finally not a technique but a fruit. "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control; against such things there is no law" (Galatians 5:22-23). Fruit grows. It can't be manufactured by effort, though it can be tended. The parent is not the source of the child's self-control any more than the gardener is the source of the apple. The parent is the one who waters and prunes and waits, trusting that God is at work, "for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13).

Here, finally, is the thing worth carrying out of the grocery store. The goal of forming a child's will is not a child who has no will. The broken will, the child who has learned that her wants don't matter and her voice will be crushed, is not a Christian achievement. It is a wound, and it often produces adults who are either endlessly compliant or explosively rebellious. The goal is a child whose will has been trained to love rightly and who can, freely and out of love, set her own desire aside for something better.

There was one human being whose will was perfectly formed, and the Gospels show it most clearly at the moment it cost him everything. In the garden of Gethsemane, facing the cross, Jesus prayed, "Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). Notice that he had a will. He did not want the cup, and he said so. His will was not broken. It was given, freely, in trust, to a Father he knew to be good. That is what the toddler on the grocery store floor is being formed toward, a long way off, and what her parent is still being formed toward too. Neither of them will get there by force. Both of them will get there, if they do, by learning to trust the One who keeps his promises, and by being loved through every tantrum by the One who is slow to anger and who does not give up on either of them.
