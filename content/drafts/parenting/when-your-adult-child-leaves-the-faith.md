Picture a couple in their late fifties sitting at a kitchen table after their son has driven home. He's twenty-nine. He grew up in the church, went to youth camp every summer, once led worship for the middle schoolers. He hasn't attended anywhere in years, and they had suspected, and tonight over coffee he told them plainly. He doesn't believe any of it anymore. He was gentle. He said he wasn't angry with them and that he loves them. He said he'd been trying to find the right time to tell them for a long while.

After he leaves, the mother starts to cry and can't stop. She's going back over twenty-nine years, the family devotions they kept and the ones they skipped, the church they left when he was fourteen, the argument about the youth pastor, the college they let him attend. The father has gone quiet. He says it was the professors. She says it was them. By midnight they're arguing, not about their son but about whose fault it is, and neither of them can sleep.

The questions parents bring to a search box at that hour come in a few forms. What did we do wrong? How do we get him back? What does the Bible say about this? There's an essay on this site for parents of a teenager who says they don't believe anymore, [When Your Teenager Says They Don't Believe Anymore](/writing/teenager-losing-faith), and some of what it says holds here. But an adult child is a different matter. He doesn't live under your roof or your rules. He has thought about this for years, not months. You have no authority over what he believes, and you shouldn't want any. What's left to you is harder to name and, in the end, more important. This essay tries to name it.

## Why it's different now, and why it's so common

It helps first to know you are not alone. The authors of *The Great Dechurching* (2023), Jim Davis and Michael Graham, working with the political scientist Ryan Burge, estimated that about forty million American adults stopped attending church over the preceding quarter century, one of the largest religious shifts in the nation's history. Burge's *The Nones* (2021) traces the rise of Americans who claim no religion at all, a group that stood in the single digits as recently as the early 1990s and grew to something like a quarter of the country or more. Many of the people in those numbers were raised in Christian homes. Their parents are sitting at kitchen tables all over the country.

The reasons they give vary more than parents tend to think. David Kinnaman's *You Lost Me* (2011), based on research among young adults who had grown up in church, found that many experienced the church as shallow, overprotective, hostile to science, simplistic about sexuality, exclusive and unwilling to let them doubt out loud. Some left over intellectual questions they couldn't get answered. Some left because of what the church had become in public, whether its alliance with partisan politics or its silence in the face of abuse. Some were hurt by the church directly. Some simply drifted, and one day noticed they had stopped believing. And some left because they wanted to live in ways the faith forbids, which is not a reason parents should pretend never happens. A parent who wants to understand his child needs to find out which of these, or which mixture, is true, rather than assuming.

Behind all of it is a change in the world our children grew up in. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), describes a society in which belief in God is no longer the default but one option among many, contested on every side, so that even believers know that thoughtful people around them see the world completely differently. Earlier generations could inherit their faith without ever having to choose it against serious alternatives. Our children couldn't. That doesn't excuse every departure, and it doesn't mean the faith is false. It means that the faith of a grown child was always going to be tested in ways the faith of his parents may never have been.

I came to faith from atheism. From the inside, unbelief rarely feels like rebellion. It usually feels like honesty, and anyone who hopes to speak into it has to reckon with that.

## Guilt, responsibility and the verse that haunts parents

Almost every Christian parent in this situation eventually runs into Proverbs 22:6: "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it." Read as a promise, it's a verdict. If he departed, you didn't train him right. Many parents have lain awake with that verse for years.

But Proverbs is not a book of promises. It's a book of proverbs, and a proverb states what is generally true about how life works under God, not a guarantee that applies in every case. The book itself makes this plain. "Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest you be like him yourself. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:4–5). The two verses sit side by side and give opposite counsel, because each is wise in some situations and not others. Proverbs expects its readers to know the difference between a general truth and a mechanical law. Proverbs 22:6 describes the ordinary fruit of faithful parenting. It does not say that every child faithfully raised will believe, and it doesn't say that every child who doesn't believe was raised badly.

The prophets make the point with even more force. Ezekiel was speaking to exiles who believed they were suffering for their parents' sins, and he answered them: "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (Ezekiel 18:20). Each generation stands before God on its own account. And God himself is presented in Scripture as a parent whose children turned away. Isaiah opens with it: "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth; for the LORD has spoken: 'Children have I reared and brought up, but they have rebelled against me'" (Isaiah 1:2). Hosea describes God teaching Israel to walk, holding him by the arms, and Israel walking away: "The more they were called, the more they went away" (Hosea 11:2). If the perfect Father had children who left, the fact that your child left doesn't prove you failed.

Here a distinction matters. There's a difference between responsibility and causation. You are responsible for how you raised your child, and you are responsible for how you love him now. You are not the cause of his beliefs. He is an adult, a moral agent, created in the image of God with a will of his own, and treating his departure as nothing more than the result of your mistakes is, strangely, a way of not taking him seriously. It makes him a product of your parenting rather than a person who has made his own choices, for his own reasons.

That distinction cuts both ways. It lifts a weight that isn't yours to carry, and it leaves the one that is. Some parents did real harm. A home that was harsh, hypocritical or frightening, where faith was used as a weapon or a mask, may have taught a child to associate God with things God hates. Where that's true, the way forward is not vague self-blame, which is often another way of keeping the focus on ourselves. It's specific repentance. "I was too harsh with you about your questions when you were sixteen, and I'm sorry." "I talked about grace and didn't show it at home." A parent's honest confession may do more for a grown child's view of the faith than any argument, because it shows the faith working.

Those of us who were parenting in the last several decades should also admit that we were sold something. A generation of Christian parenting books and programs promised, or seemed to promise, that the right method would produce believing adults: the right schooling, the right curriculum, the right rules about dating and media. Many of those books had good things in them. But the promise underneath was not the gospel. It was a formula, and formulas don't account for the freedom God gives each person. Some of our children left the formula and took the faith with it, because we had never shown them the difference.

## The prodigal, read carefully

Parents in this situation almost always turn to the parable of the prodigal son in Luke 15, and they're right to. But it has to be read carefully, because it's easy to take the wrong lesson from it.

Start with why Jesus told it. "Now the tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear him. And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them'" (Luke 15:1–2). The parable is the third of three stories told to those grumblers. In the first, a shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep to search for the one that's lost. In the second, a woman sweeps the house until she finds a lost coin. In the third, something different happens. The father does not go looking.

"There was a man who had two sons. And the younger of them said to his father, 'Father, give me the share of property that is coming to me.' And he divided his property between them" (Luke 15:11–12). The scholar Kenneth Bailey, who spent decades in the Middle East and wrote about the parables in books including *Poet and Peasant* (1976), argued that in the village world the story assumes, a son asking for his inheritance while his father was alive was close to wishing him dead. Some scholars think Bailey pressed the point too far. Either way, the request was an outrage, and the father granted it. He let the son go, with the means to go far. He didn't send servants after him. He didn't follow him to the far country to argue.

The change, when it comes, happens in the far country. After the money is gone and a famine has come and the son is feeding pigs, "when he came to himself, he said, 'How many of my father's hired servants have more than enough bread, but I perish here with hunger!'" (Luke 15:17). No one argued him home. He came to himself.

Then the verse every waiting parent knows: "And he arose and came to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). The father saw him a long way off, which means he was looking. Bailey pointed out that a man of the father's standing running in public would have been undignified, and that the father took the shame on himself to reach his son before the village could. The son had prepared a speech ending with a request to be made a hired servant. The father doesn't let him finish it. He calls for a robe, a ring, shoes and a feast. "For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found" (Luke 15:24).

Helmut Thielicke, the German preacher who preached through the parables in Stuttgart after the Second World War, called his collection of those sermons *The Waiting Father*, because he believed the real center of the story was not the son but the father. Parents in this situation can learn from the father's posture. He let the son go. He kept watching. He ran when he saw him. He restored him without making him grovel.

But three warnings belong beside that lesson. The first is that the father in the parable stands for God, and a parent is not God. We can imitate the father's posture. We can't do what only God does, which is to bring a person to himself in the far country. That work isn't ours, and trying to do it usually drives our children further away.

The second warning is about the son. Many parents instinctively cast their adult child as the prodigal, wasting everything in reckless living. Some are. Many aren't. A good number of adults who leave the faith are thoughtful, honest, morally serious people who have concluded, for reasons they can articulate, that it isn't true. To treat them as if they were feeding pigs, when they're paying their taxes and caring for their neighbors and trying to be truthful, is to insult them, and they'll know it. The parable is about how God receives sinners. It isn't a template for describing your child.

The third warning is the one Jesus was aiming at his hearers. The parable doesn't end with the feast. It ends with the older brother, who had stayed home and worked and never disobeyed, standing outside in the dark, angry. "But he was angry and refused to go in. His father came out and entreated him" (Luke 15:28). The older brother lists his faithfulness and his father's unfairness. "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command" (Luke 15:29). Timothy Keller's *The Prodigal God* (2008) argues that both sons were lost, the younger through open rebellion and the older through a righteousness that used obedience to get what he wanted from his father, and that the older son's lostness is harder to see and harder to cure. Jesus told the parable to older brothers. The Pharisees were the ones outside.

Parents of adult children who have left should hear that. It's possible to love the faith in a way that is closer to the older brother than to the father. It sounds like "after everything we did for you," or like a quiet resentment that the child who left gets more attention than the children who stayed. And the brothers and sisters who stayed may feel exactly what the older brother felt. The father in the parable went out to both sons. He pleaded with the one who never left. Parents may have to do the same.

Henri Nouwen, in *The Return of the Prodigal Son* (1992), described sitting for hours in front of Rembrandt's painting of the scene, completed near the end of the painter's life. He first saw himself in the younger son, then, more painfully, in the older one. He finally came to believe the call on his life was to become the father, whose hands on the son's back hold him without asking anything in return. That's not a technique. It's a lifetime's growth, and most of us are still somewhere in the first two figures.

The parable ends without telling us whether the older brother went in. Jesus left it open, for his hearers and for us.

## Grief for someone who is still here

The mother at the kitchen table is grieving, and she may feel she's not allowed to. Her son is alive, healthy and kind. He's still coming for Thanksgiving. And yet something has died, or seems to have: the future she imagined, a family that worshiped together, the hope of seeing him baptize his own children, the assurance, for her, about where he'll spend eternity. That last one is the weight Christian parents carry that others don't, and it's not a small one.

Scripture doesn't tell her to stop grieving. When Saul turned from God, the text says, "And Samuel did not see Saul again until the day of his death, but Samuel grieved over Saul" (1 Samuel 15:35). When David learned his son Absalom was dead, after a rebellion that nearly cost David his throne, he went up to the room over the gate and wept: "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son!" (2 Samuel 18:33). Paul, speaking of his own people who had not believed, wrote of "great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" (Romans 9:2). Jesus wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41). Grief for people we love who don't believe is not a failure of faith. It's what love feels like when it can't do anything.

Grief also needs watching, because it can go wrong in two ways. It can become a weapon. "You're breaking your mother's heart" is a sentence that has driven many adult children further away, because it turns the parent's pain into a demand. And grief can become despair. If you find that the weight of this has pushed you to a place where you can't see a way forward, where thoughts of harming yourself come, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. That's not an overreaction. Grief this deep deserves real help.

The marriage needs watching too. Spouses often grieve differently. One wants to talk about it constantly, and the other wants to go quiet. One wants to fight for the child, and the other wants to let go. Like the couple at the kitchen table, they can turn their grief into an argument about blame. The same child left both of them, and they need each other. Praying together, even in silence, is one of the most important things they can do. So is finding a pastor or counselor who won't hand them a formula.

## What love looks like now

What does a parent actually do? The answer is less dramatic than most parents want, and it takes far longer.

Keep the relationship. It's the one thing you can still do, and it's the thing that matters most. Invite your son to dinner. Call on his birthday. Ask about his work, his friends, what he's reading. Don't make your welcome conditional on his beliefs, and don't let every conversation become a conversation about God. An adult child who knows he'll be met with an argument every time he comes home will come home less.

Stop arguing unless you're invited. There may be a time for a serious conversation about the faith, and if your son wants one, have it, with humility and without needing to win. But most parents who try to argue an adult child back into belief succeed only in confirming his sense that faith is about winning. Augustine records in his *Confessions* that when he was a young man caught up in a sect his mother, Monica, found heretical, she begged a bishop to talk him out of it. The bishop, who had once belonged to the same sect himself, refused. The young man wasn't ready to be taught, he said, and he would find his own way out by reading. Leave him alone, he told her, and pray. When she kept pressing him with tears, the bishop sent her away with the assurance that the son of such tears could not be lost. Augustine did come back, years later, and became one of the church's greatest teachers. The bishop's counsel is not always the right one, but it's wiser than most of what parents are tempted to do.

Ask, and listen. Most parents never really learn why their child left, because they're too busy defending themselves or the faith. Ask your son what happened, what he believes now, what he found in the church that he couldn't accept. Listen without correcting. You may learn things about the church, or about yourself, that you need to hear. And listening to him fully, without rebuttal, is itself a form of love he'll remember.

Tell the truth about your own faith, including the hard parts. If you've had doubts, say so. If there are questions you can't answer, admit it. Many adult children who left did so partly because the faith they were shown seemed brittle, unable to bear a question. A parent who can say "I don't know, and I still believe, and here's why" may show his child a faith he never saw growing up.

Some decisions will be hard, and on some of them faithful Christians disagree. An adult child may marry outside the faith, or live in ways his parents believe are wrong, or ask them to attend a ceremony they can't celebrate in good conscience. Whether to attend such an event is a genuinely contested question among Christians. Some believe that attending shows love and keeps the door open, and that presence isn't endorsement. Others believe that attending would say something they can't say, and that love can be shown in other ways. Both positions are held by people who love their children and fear God. This essay doesn't settle it. It only urges that whatever you decide, you tell your child early, with love and without a lecture, and that you make clear the relationship isn't ending.

And pray, persistently, specifically, for years if necessary. Pray for your child's good, not only his return. Pray for the people in his life. Pray for your own heart, that you'll be the father who runs and not the brother who sulks. I have five sons, and I know that no parent can see into the years ahead of a child. What we can do is keep praying through them.

## The long watch

The father in the parable saw his son "while he was still a long way off." That detail has comforted parents for centuries, because it means he was watching the road. The waiting wasn't passive. It was the posture of someone who had let go of control and not of hope.

Scripture shows that God himself waits this way. Hosea's prophecy, after describing a son who walked away from the Father who taught him to walk, reaches one of the most tender passages in the Old Testament: "How can I give you up, O Ephraim? How can I hand you over, O Israel? ... My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender" (Hosea 11:8). God's heart toward his wandering children is not cold. And he did more than wait. The whole story of Scripture ends in a cross, where the Son went into the far country of death and abandonment so that the Father's lost children could come home. The parent who grieves a child who left the faith is feeling, in a small way, something God has felt for his people since Eden, and God's answer was not to give up but to give himself.

The resurrection is the ground under all of this. It means no one is beyond the reach of God while he lives, and that the story of any person is not finished until God finishes it. That's not a guarantee that every child will return. Some don't, in this life, and parents who have prayed for decades know it. The parable of the prodigal itself ends without resolution, the older brother still outside, the father still pleading. Jesus didn't tie it up, and we shouldn't pretend to.

The couple at the kitchen table will eventually go to bed. Tomorrow they'll have to decide how to answer their son's text asking whether he's still welcome at Christmas. They should tell him yes, before anything else. Then they'll have to decide something harder, which is what kind of parents they'll be for however many years this takes: whether they'll be the father watching the road, or the brother outside in the dark, keeping a list of everything they did right. The son may come home or he may not. That part was never theirs to decide. What they can decide is whether, if he ever does come up the road, there will be someone watching who sees him while he is still a long way off.
