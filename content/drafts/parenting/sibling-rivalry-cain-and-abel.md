Consider a minivan on the way home from church. Before the car has left the parking lot, the two in the back row have started. He's touching my side. She's looking at me. He took the good seat last time. It isn't fair. The parent in the driver's seat, who has just heard a sermon about love and was hoping to hold onto it for at least the length of the drive, issues a warning, then another, then a threat about canceling lunch, and by the time the van is in the driveway everyone is angry, including the parent, who can't remember who started it and suspects it doesn't matter.

Most parents of more than one child know that van. Sibling conflict is among the most common and most exhausting parts of family life, and it's easy to treat it as mere noise: an irritation to be managed, a phase to be outlasted, a set of disputes to be refereed as fairly as possible until the children are old enough to move out. Some of the advice parents receive assumes exactly that. Separate them. Ignore the small stuff. Don't take sides. Wait for them to grow out of it.

But underneath the noise, many parents carry a quieter fear they rarely say out loud. They are afraid their children won't love each other. They picture a funeral, years from now, their own, and they wonder whether the siblings standing at the graveside will be friends or strangers or enemies. Some of them are afraid because they know how their own sibling relationships turned out. The Bible, it turns out, shares that fear. The first story it tells after the garden is about two brothers, and it ends in blood.

## The first brothers

Genesis 4 is short and almost unbearably spare. Adam and Eve have two sons. Cain works the ground and Abel keeps sheep. In the course of time each brings an offering to the Lord, Cain from the fruit of the ground and Abel from the firstborn of his flock and their fat portions. The Lord has regard for Abel and his offering, but not for Cain and his. The text doesn't explain why in any detail. The letter to the Hebrews later says that Abel offered "by faith" a more acceptable sacrifice (Hebrews 11:4), and many interpreters have noted that Abel's gift is described as the firstborn and the best, while Cain's is described only as some of the fruit. But Genesis doesn't dwell on the reason. It dwells on what happens inside Cain.

"So Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (Genesis 4:5). Then God speaks to him, and it is one of the most tender warnings in Scripture: "If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin is crouching at the door. Its desire is contrary to you, but you must rule over it" (Genesis 4:7). God does not reject Cain. He comes to him in his anger, names what is happening, and calls him to master it. Sin is pictured as an animal crouching at the threshold, waiting. The door is still Cain's to hold.

He doesn't hold it. He speaks to his brother, and when they're in the field he rises up against Abel and kills him. Then God comes again with a question, the way he came to Adam in the garden: "Where is Abel your brother?" And Cain answers with a line that has echoed through every family since: "I do not know; am I my brother's keeper?" (Genesis 4:9). God's reply is terrible: "What have you done? The voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground" (Genesis 4:10).

Genesis is telling us something by putting this story here. The first sin, in the garden, is against God. The second, just outside it, is against a brother. The fracture between human beings and God shows up immediately as a fracture between siblings, and the specific form it takes is envy: the pain of seeing someone close to you receive what you wanted. That's why the New Testament, when it reaches back to this story, puts it in those terms. "We should not be like Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous" (1 John 3:12). Cain's problem was not that Abel had wronged him. It was that Abel's goodness exposed him.

Augustine, in the fifteenth book of *The City of God*, noticed something else about the story. After the murder, Cain went away and built a city. Abel, the shepherd, a pilgrim on the earth, built none. For Augustine this was the beginning of the two cities that run through all of history, the earthly city founded on the love of self and the heavenly city founded on the love of God. The first human civilization in the Bible is founded by a man who killed his brother. That is not a comfortable thing to read as the parent of children who fight in the back of a van. But it tells us the stakes are larger than they look.

## The pattern Genesis will not let go

If the story of Cain and Abel were the only sibling story in Genesis, we might read it as an extreme case. But Genesis keeps returning to the same wound, generation after generation, as though it cannot stop probing it.

Isaac and Rebekah have twins who struggle in the womb, and the struggle never stops. Esau is born first, Jacob grabbing his heel. And then Genesis adds a sentence that every parent should read slowly: "Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob" (Genesis 25:28). Each parent has a favorite, and the favoritism shapes everything that follows: the stolen birthright, the stolen blessing, Esau's murderous rage, Jacob's flight into exile for twenty years. The rivalry between the brothers is not only theirs. It's been fed by their parents.

Jacob, who suffered from his parents' favoritism, becomes a father who does exactly the same thing. "Now Israel loved Joseph more than any other of his sons, because he was the son of his old age. And he made him a robe of many colors. But when his brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, they hated him and could not speak peacefully to him" (Genesis 37:3-4). Joseph doesn't help matters. He reports his brothers' misdeeds to their father and tells them his dreams, in which they all bow down to him. The brothers' envy grows, and when they find him alone in the fields, far from their father, they plot to kill him, then sell him to traders headed for Egypt. They dip his robe in goat's blood and send it to their father with a message: "This we have found; please identify whether it is your son's robe or not" (Genesis 37:32).

Robert Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Narrative* (1981), noticed that the phrase "please identify" returns in the very next chapter, when Judah, one of the brothers who sold Joseph, is confronted by his daughter-in-law Tamar with evidence of his own wrongdoing: "please identify whose these are, the signet and the cord and the staff" (Genesis 38:25). The man who used a piece of clothing to deceive his father is now confronted with his own belongings and forced to recognize his own guilt. The echo is deliberate. Genesis is showing us that the sin of the brothers doesn't stay buried. It comes back to be named.

Jon Levenson, in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993), traces a pattern running through these stories: God repeatedly chooses the younger son over the elder, Abel over Cain, Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph over his older brothers. Levenson argues that this chosenness is never simply a privilege. The beloved son is again and again brought near to death, and his chosenness provokes the bitter resentment of those who were not chosen. Election, in Genesis, is a costly gift, and the envy it provokes is one of the great themes of the book. Levenson's point matters for parents because it means the Bible does not pretend that love can be distributed in such a way that no one ever feels passed over. It knows that the feeling of being unfavored is one of the deepest sources of human violence. Why God chose to work through this one quarreling family at all is a question this site takes up in its essay on [why God chose Abraham](/writing/why-god-started-with-one-family).

And yet Genesis does not end in blood. Esau, twenty years after vowing to kill his brother, "ran to meet him and embraced him and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept" (Genesis 33:4). Judah, the brother who proposed selling Joseph, is the one who, years later in Egypt, offers himself as a slave in place of his youngest brother Benjamin, so that their father will not lose another son: "Now therefore, please let your servant remain instead of the boy as a servant to my lord, and let the boy go back with his brothers" (Genesis 44:33). The man who once sold a brother now offers himself for one. And Joseph, weeping, reveals himself to his brothers, and at the end of the book, when they fear his revenge, says to them, "Do not fear, for am I in the place of God? As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today" (Genesis 50:19-20). The book that begins with a brother's murder ends with a brother's forgiveness.

## Why they fight over the same toy

Genesis gives us the depth of the problem. Some of the modern thinkers who have studied it help us see its mechanism, because they explain things that happen every day in ordinary homes.

The French literary critic and anthropologist René Girard spent a long career developing a single idea, first set out in *Deceit, Desire, and the Novel* (1961): that human desire is largely imitative. We don't simply want things because of their inherent value. We want them because we see others wanting them. Girard called this mimetic desire, and he argued that it inevitably produces rivalry, because when two people imitate each other's desires, they end up wanting the same thing, and the closer and more alike they are, the more intense the rivalry becomes. Anyone who has watched two children fight over a toy that neither cared about until the other picked it up has seen Girard's theory in action. Siblings are the closest possible models for one another. They share parents, a house, a table, often a room. They are perfectly placed to want what the other wants. In *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning* (1999), Girard read Genesis 4 as a founding murder, the kind of violence on which human cultures are built. But he argued that the Bible does something the myths of other cultures do not: it tells the story from the side of the victim. Abel's blood cries out, and God hears it. The murder is not justified or hidden. It is exposed.

The developmental psychologist Judy Dunn, who spent years observing young siblings in their homes, gives a different angle on the same phenomenon. In *Sisters and Brothers* (1985) and her related studies, she found that sibling relationships are marked by conflict, but also that those very conflicts are one of the places where young children first show that they understand what another person feels. A toddler who knows exactly what will make her older brother angry, or exactly what will comfort him, is demonstrating an understanding of other minds that develops in the rough-and-tumble of family life. Sibling conflict is not only a problem. It's also a school, where children learn what hurts and what heals the people closest to them.

Adele Faber and Elaine Mazlish, in their widely read *Siblings Without Rivalry* (1987), turned these insights into practical counsel. They warned parents against comparing children, even favorably, because every comparison sets siblings against each other. They urged parents not to lock children into roles, the smart one, the athletic one, the difficult one, because children often live up to or rebel against the labels we give them. And they suggested that children need to be loved not equally but uniquely: each child needs to know that he or she is loved for who they are, not measured against a sibling. It's worth adding one caution about a popular explanation for sibling conflict. Many parents assume birth order determines personality, that the firstborn will be bossy and the youngest spoiled. But a large 2015 study by Julia Rohrer, Boris Egloff and Stefan Schmukle, drawing on data from tens of thousands of people in several countries, found no meaningful effect of birth order on the major personality traits. Whatever writes the roles our children play at home, the order in which they arrived is a poor excuse, which leaves more of the script in our hands than we might like.

## Fairness, favor and the father

"It's not fair" may be the most common sentence in any home with more than one child. It's worth taking seriously, because it's both a genuine moral protest and a disguise for something else.

On the surface, the child who says it's not fair is appealing to justice, and sometimes he's right. Parents do play favorites, sometimes without knowing it. We find one child easier, or more like us, or more rewarding to parent, and it shows in a thousand small ways. Genesis doesn't let Isaac and Rebekah and Jacob off the hook for their favoritism, and it doesn't let us off either. An honest parent will ask whether the child's complaint has something to it. None of us stands outside this charge. Many of us were shaped by our own parents' preferences, and we carry those old wounds into our parenting without seeing them. The child who feels passed over in our home may be reliving something we felt in our parents' home. Cain's question comes back to us too. Where is your brother? Where is the child you find harder to love?

But underneath the appeal to fairness there is often something else, which is the fear that love is a fixed quantity, and that whatever my sibling gets is taken from me. This is the logic of envy. It assumes there isn't enough to go around. And it is precisely this logic that Jesus confronts in the parable we usually call the prodigal son, which is really a story about two sons. When the younger son comes home after wasting his inheritance, his father runs to meet him and throws a feast. The elder son, coming in from the fields and hearing the music, is furious. "But he was angry and refused to go in. His father came out and entreated him" (Luke 15:28). The elder brother pours out his grievance: he has served faithfully for years and never received even a young goat to celebrate with his friends, and now this son who wasted everything gets the fattened calf. It isn't fair.

The father's answer doesn't deny the elder son's faithfulness. It denies his arithmetic. "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. It was fitting to celebrate and be glad, for this your brother was dead, and is alive; he was lost, and is found" (Luke 15:31-32). The father's love for the younger son subtracts nothing from the elder. Notice, too, that the father goes out to both sons. He runs to the younger one on the road, and he goes out to the elder one in the yard. Neither son can earn his place, and neither is left outside by the father's choice. The parable ends without telling us whether the elder brother goes in. Jesus leaves that question hanging over the religious people who were listening, and over us.

This is the deepest answer to "it's not fair." Love is not fair in the sense of being measured out in equal portions. It is better than fair. It is abundant, particular and unconditional, and a parent who reflects even a little of the heavenly Father's love will not try to love each child identically. He will try to love each one fully and uniquely, and to help each one see that a sibling's gain is not his loss. That is hard for a child to believe. It is hard for adults to believe. The whole economy of envy depends on not believing it.

## Am I my brother's keeper?

What does this mean for the parent in the minivan? It doesn't mean every squabble is a spiritual crisis, and it doesn't mean children should be lectured on Genesis every time they fight over a seat. Much sibling conflict really is small, and some of it resolves best when parents stay out of it. But it does mean the home is doing something more important than keeping the peace. It is teaching children how to live with the people they didn't choose, whom God has placed beside them, and whom they will know longer than almost anyone else in their lives.

That changes the parent's role from referee to something more like a teacher of reconciliation. A referee decides who's right and assigns penalties. A teacher helps both children learn to repair what's broken. Jesus taught that reconciliation with a brother takes precedence even over worship: "So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:23-24). A family that takes that seriously will treat the repair of sibling relationships as holy work. That means helping children name what happened and how it made them feel, helping the one who did wrong to say he's sorry without being humiliated, and helping the one who was wronged to forgive without pretending it didn't matter. Paul's instruction applies to children as well as adults: "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger" (Ephesians 4:26). Bedtime in a family with siblings is a good time to practice it.

It means answering Cain's question the opposite way. Am I my brother's keeper? In a Christian home the answer is yes. Children can be given real responsibility for one another's welfare, the older helping the younger, the stronger protecting the weaker, each learning to notice when the other is sad or left out. This is not about making older children into substitute parents, which can breed its own resentment. It's about teaching every child that the people God put beside them are theirs to care for.

It means guarding against the sources of rivalry that parents create. We can stop comparing. We can resist labeling. We can pay attention to the child who gets less of our attention because he makes less noise. We can examine our own hearts for favoritism and repent of it when we find it, including, where it helps, saying so to the child who was hurt.

And it means knowing where ordinary conflict ends and something else begins. Not all sibling conflict is ordinary rivalry. When one child persistently dominates, intimidates or physically harms another, when there's a significant difference in age or power that is being exploited, or when there is any kind of sexual contact between siblings, parents must intervene decisively, protect the child who is being harmed, and seek help from a pediatrician, counselor or child protection professional. The Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline is available at 1-800-422-4453, and if anyone in the home is thinking of harming themselves, call or text 988. No teaching about sibling reconciliation should ever be used to keep a vulnerable child in harm's way. This is not legal or medical advice, but it is a line no Christian parent should blur.

The minivan will probably still be loud next Sunday. The children in the back row will still want what the other has and still insist that it isn't fair. But a parent who has read Genesis knows the stakes are higher than the noise suggests, and also that the story doesn't end where it began. It ends with Esau running to embrace the brother who cheated him, with Judah offering himself as a slave in place of the brother he might once have sold, with Joseph weeping and forgiving. And the whole biblical story ends with another brother. The letter to the Hebrews says of Jesus that "he is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Hebrews 2:11), and it speaks of "the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Hebrews 12:24). Abel's blood cried out from the ground for justice. Christ's blood speaks a better word, the word of forgiveness, spoken by an elder brother who did not envy his siblings but gave himself for them. He is the answer to Cain's question. He is his brothers' keeper. The children in the back of the van, and the parent in the front, have a better word spoken over them than the one Cain heard, and a home where siblings learn to forgive each other is one of the places where that word is heard again.
