The American church has a history. It contains real courage and real faithfulness — abolitionists who read their Bibles toward freedom, believers who hid the hunted, congregations that fed cities through plagues. That history is true and it should be told. But it is not the whole history, and a church that tells only the flattering half of its story has not been honest; it has been doing public relations.

Because there is another half. There were the pulpits that defended slavery from the text, the theologians who built careful arguments for an institution that bought and sold the image of God. [cite — a representative pro-slavery clergyman/text if used.] There were the white churches that watched their Black brothers and sisters be terrorized and said nothing, or said *not now, not like this, be patient* — which from a safe pew is just *no* with better manners. There were the comfortable congregations during the civil rights years whose great sin was not cruelty but quiet — the ones Dr. King named from a jail cell, the white moderates more devoted to order than to justice. [cite — Letter from Birmingham Jail, for accuracy if quoted directly.]

I want to be careful here, because this kind of reckoning gets used badly in both directions. Some wield the history to indict the faith itself, as though the failures of the church disprove the gospel — when in fact the gospel is the standard by which we can even name them as failures. Others refuse the reckoning entirely, hear any honest account of the past as an attack, and retreat into the flattering half of the story. Both are evasions. The faithful path is the hardest one: to own the history as ours, neither weaponized nor denied.

And it is *ours*. This is the part the comfortable Christian wants to skip. It is easy to condemn the slaveholding preacher across the distance of a century — it costs nothing, and it lets me feel I would have been the brave exception. I would almost certainly not have been. The people who defended the indefensible were not monsters; they were ordinary believers who let their culture, their comfort, and their economic interest read the Bible for them. That is not a nineteenth-century disease. It is the permanent temptation of the church in every age, including mine, and the only people who escape it are the ones honest enough to fear it.

That is why this reckoning is not mainly about the past. A church that cannot tell the truth about where it was silent before will not recognize the next thing it is being silent about now. The mechanism never changes: the issue is contested, taking a side is costly, the comfortable position is to call faithfulness *prudence* and wait. Every generation has its version. Every generation's faithful remnant looks, to the comfortable majority, like people making the church political — until enough time passes that the verdict is obvious and everyone claims they were on the right side all along.

Scripture does not flinch from this pattern. The prophets spent most of their breath not on the pagans outside but on the people of God inside — on a worshiping community whose songs God could not stand because their hands were full of blood (Isaiah 1:15). The harshest words in the Bible are aimed at the religious, not the irreligious. We should sit with that longer than we do.

The reckoning is not self-flagellation, and it is not despair. It is repentance, which is a Christian word and a hopeful one — the turning that becomes possible only after the truth is told. A church that owns its history of silence is not weakened by the confession. It is freed by it — freed to be, this time, the remnant that speaks rather than the majority that waited.

We would rather not own this. That preference is exactly the thing the prophets were sent to break. The history is ours. The silence was ours. And the only way it does not repeat is if we are willing to say so.
