I have spent twenty years pastoring people who lost their faith in the Bible — not because they encountered a hard question, but because they encountered the Bible itself, and the Bible did not match the marketing they had been sold. The marketing was wrong. The marketing was always wrong. The Bible the church has actually read for two thousand years is a different book, and it is, in every way that matters, a better one.

I want to write this carefully because it touches the core anxiety of the contemporary skeptic and the contemporary deconstructing evangelical, both of whom often arrive at the same intellectual position from opposite directions. The skeptic has read enough of the Bible to know that the marketing version cannot be true. The deconstructor has read enough of the Bible to know that the version her childhood church taught cannot be true. Both are right. Both are, in important ways, wrong about what the alternatives are. The actual Bible is a stranger and more honest book than either side has generally acknowledged. I want to describe it.

## What the marketing said

The marketing version of the Bible, which has dominated American evangelical popular discourse for roughly a hundred years, says something like this. The Bible is a single book. It was written by God. It is perfect in every detail, including scientific and historical detail. It is internally consistent in every respect. It settles every important question if you read it the right way. Disagreements about its meaning are the result of either bad faith or bad reading. The whole thing was inspired by God in such a way that the human authors are essentially scribes, taking divine dictation.

This is the marketing. The marketing is not the historic Christian view. The marketing is roughly the strict inerrantist position developed in late-nineteenth-century American Protestantism in response to the rise of higher criticism, codified at Old Princeton, popularized through the Scofield Reference Bible (1909) and the early fundamentalist movement, and inherited by twentieth-century American evangelicalism without much serious examination. The marketing is about a hundred and fifty years old. The position has its serious defenders, and the strict inerrantist view can be held intellectually by people who do the careful work. The position is not, however, the position of the historic church. The position is not the position of Augustine, or Calvin, or Luther, or any major figure in church history before the late nineteenth century. The position is a recent development, presented to evangelical laypeople as if it were the only Christian option, and the presentation has produced extraordinary damage when those laypeople actually opened the book.

## What the Bible actually is

The Bible is a library. It contains sixty-six books in the Protestant canon (more in the Catholic and Orthodox), composed over more than a millennium, by multiple authors, in three languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek), across a wide variety of genres. It contains narrative, law, prophecy, wisdom literature, poetry, apocalyptic, gospel, epistle. The genres are not interchangeable. The Bible is not a uniform document, and it does not pretend to be. The marketing's claim that the Bible is a single book of essentially one type was always a category mistake.

The Bible was composed and transmitted through human processes. The Pentateuch's composition history is complex and is the subject of substantial scholarly conversation; the older view that Moses wrote it all in one sitting in the wilderness is not the view of any careful historical reader. The historical books of the Old Testament show evidence of redaction and compilation over centuries. The prophets are collections of oracular material, often arranged thematically rather than chronologically. The wisdom literature borrows from and engages with ancient Near Eastern wisdom traditions, in ways that are obvious once you read the relevant comparative material. The New Testament gospels are not stenographic records of Jesus's life. They are theologically shaped narratives, each with distinctive emphases, drawing on earlier sources and oral traditions. The Pauline letters are occasional documents, addressing specific congregational situations, with all the contextual complications that entails.

None of this is news to careful Christians. All of it is news to most lay Christians, and the news is alarming when it arrives because the marketing pitch has lied about what the book actually is. The deconstruction crisis in American evangelicalism over the past two decades is, in significant part, the consequence of evangelicals discovering that the Bible is not the book they were told it was, and concluding — wrongly — that the church has been concealing the truth about the Bible from them. The church has not, on the whole, been concealing the truth. The church has been concealing the limits of its marketing. The two are different.

## What the historic church held

The historic Christian view, before the late nineteenth century, was a high view of scripture that did not require the marketing's strict claims. Augustine, in *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, argues that the creation account requires interpretive care because the text does not always mean what its literal surface suggests, and that what looks like a contradiction in scripture is generally a problem with the reader's interpretation rather than the text. He grants the apparent difficulties of the text honestly. He does not require himself to defend every detail at face value.

Calvin, in his commentaries, recognizes that scripture accommodates the limited understanding of its human audiences — what theologians call the doctrine of accommodation. The Bible speaks of the sun rising and setting because that is how the human authors and their audiences experienced the sun; the doctrine of accommodation lets the Christian read these passages without committing to a flat earth or a geocentric cosmology. The doctrine of accommodation is older than Calvin — it goes back at least to Origen and Augustine — and it provides interpretive resources that the marketing version of the Bible has lost.

The Reformers held a high view of scripture in a different sense than the strict inerrantists held it. The Reformation slogan *sola scriptura* — scripture alone as the final authority in matters of faith and life — did not require strict inerrancy. It required scripture to be the highest authority among the various authorities Christians appeal to. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), the most precise Reformed statement on scripture, says scripture is *infallible* in matters of faith and life. It does not say scripture is technically inerrant on every scientific and historical detail. The distinction matters. The Westminster Divines knew what they were doing. They were trying to articulate a view of scripture that could survive honest reading.

The Christian tradition has, on the whole, recognized that scripture is the word of God in human words, that the human words are real human words with real cultural and linguistic contexts, that the divine inspiration works through the human authors rather than around them, and that the careful reader has to do interpretive work to get to the meaning. This is the view I hold, and the view most careful Christian scholars hold, and the view that has supported the church's reading of scripture for nineteen of its twenty centuries. It is not the marketing view. It is a more honest view.

## How the skeptic's objections land differently

When you stop treating the Bible as a uniform document of strict inerrancy, the skeptical objections that have driven much of the secular case against the Bible lose most of their force.

The apparent contradictions in the gospels are no longer existential threats. They are the kind of variations you would expect in independent witness accounts of overlapping events, written for different audiences with different emphases. The gospel writers were not stenographers. They were authors, doing what authors do — selecting, arranging, emphasizing for their particular purposes. The variations among them are not refutations of the Christian story; they are evidence of multiple independent traditions, which is precisely what historians look for when assessing the reliability of ancient sources.

The scientific objections — that the Bible's cosmology is incompatible with modern science, that the creation accounts contradict the evolutionary record — are no longer existential threats. They are the result of a category error in the marketing's reading. Genesis 1 and 2 are not scientific accounts. They are theological accounts, doing the kind of work that the ancient Near Eastern creation narratives all did — telling a community where it came from, who its God was, and what its place in the cosmos was. John Walton's *The Lost World of Genesis One* (2009) is the best contemporary treatment. The creation accounts are doing theological work, not scientific work, and reading them as scientific is what the marketing taught us to do. The historic church did not, in general, read them this way.

The moral objections — that the Bible contains passages that are morally troubling — are real but do not function as the deconstructors often think they function. The conquest narratives in Joshua. The imprecatory psalms. The instructions for slavery. These passages are difficult, and the careful Christian tradition has been working on them for centuries. The honest reader has to recognize that scripture contains material that even the canonical writers acknowledge as difficult — the imprecatory psalms are honest expressions of human emotion that the Bible includes precisely because the Bible is willing to let humans be human in its presence — and that the larger arc of scripture moves toward the explicit ethics of the Sermon on the Mount, which judges the earlier material in ways the careful Christian reader has to respect.

The historical-critical work on the composition of biblical texts is, on the whole, careful scholarship that the Christian tradition has been engaging seriously for over a hundred years. The deconstruction movement's recent presentation of higher criticism as revelation is, in many cases, the rediscovery of work that thoughtful Christian scholars have been doing in conversation with the same material for generations. Brevard Childs's canonical criticism. The Catholic biblical scholarship of Raymond Brown and Joseph Fitzmyer. The recent evangelical work of Peter Enns and Michael Bird and Craig Keener. The serious Christian engagement with the historical-critical material is robust, and the marketing version of the Bible was, in significant respects, an attempt to insulate evangelical laypeople from work the careful tradition was actually doing.

## What to do with this

Read the Bible as the library it is. Read it slowly, attentively, in conversation with the church's two-thousand-year tradition of reading. Read it without the marketing's expectations. Read it without the deconstructors' expectations either. Let it be what it is.

The Bible read this way is more interesting, more rewarding, and more demanding than the marketing version was. The Bible read this way is also more resilient against skeptical objections, because the objections are usually aimed at the marketing version, and the marketing version is not what serious Christians have been holding to all along.

I want to be honest. The reading of scripture I am describing requires more work than the marketing's promised. The reading requires familiarity with the historic interpretive tradition, attention to genre, willingness to grapple with passages that are difficult, and the discipline of slow reading over years. The marketing promised that you could pick up the Bible cold and find clear answers to every question. The marketing was lying. The historic Christian reading promises that, if you do the work, scripture will speak to you in ways that will shape your life, your character, your understanding of God, and your sense of vocation. That promise has been honored across two millennia, in countless lives. The marketing version, by contrast, has produced a generation of disillusioned evangelicals who picked up the Bible cold, found contradictions, were told there are none, and concluded that the whole project was a fraud. The project was not a fraud. The marketing was.

## The verdict

The Bible is not the book the marketing said it was. The Bible the church has actually read for nineteen centuries is a stranger, deeper, more honest book — a library composed by many hands over more than a millennium, with the divine word speaking through real human authors with real human contexts, requiring the patient interpretive work that all serious literature requires. The skeptical objections that target the marketing version do not, on the whole, target the actual Bible. The skeptic who has dismissed Christianity on the basis of the marketing's Bible has dismissed something that was not, in fact, the church's Bible to begin with.

I am writing this for the skeptic and for the deconstructor, both of whom have generally been treated with insufficient honesty by Christian institutions. I am writing this also for the lay Christian who has been carrying the marketing version of the Bible into the deconstruction conversations of her own generation and finding it inadequate. The Bible is fine. The reading we have been taught was incomplete. The recovery of the historic reading is part of the work of the post-Christendom church, and the work is, in my experience, one of the more hopeful pieces of the recovery — because the actual Bible is, in every way that matters, a better book than the marketing pretended.
