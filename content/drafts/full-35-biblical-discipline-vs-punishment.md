American evangelical parenting has conflated discipline with punishment for two generations. The conflation has produced anxious children and exhausted parents, and it has produced a generation of young adults who left the church partly because they associated Christianity with the punishment they received from their well-intentioned but poorly-informed parents. The recovery of the distinction is one of the most important things Christian parents can do in our generation.

I want to write this essay carefully because the topic touches one of the deepest convictions of evangelical parenting culture, and I am going to be saying things that many of my readers will instinctively disagree with. I want to ask for the slow reading, because the disagreement that emerges from the slow reading is the productive kind, and the disagreement that emerges from the fast reading usually misses what I am actually saying.

## What discipline meant in scripture

The biblical Hebrew word for discipline is *musar*, and the Greek word is *paideia*. Both terms have a meaning much broader than the contemporary English word *discipline* in its usual American Christian usage.

*Paideia* in the Greek world referred to the whole process of forming a citizen. It included instruction, character formation, the cultivation of virtue, the slow shaping of a person into the kind of person who could participate well in the life of the polis. *Paideia* was not primarily about behavioral correction. *Paideia* was about formation. The Latin equivalent — *educatio* — gives us the modern word *education*, but *educatio* in the classical world was much closer to the original sense of *paideia*: the whole work of forming a person rather than the narrow work of transmitting information.

When the New Testament uses *paideia*, the New Testament is using it in this fuller sense. Hebrews 12:5–11 is the locus classicus. The author of Hebrews quotes Proverbs 3:11–12 — *do not regard lightly the discipline of the Lord, nor be weary when reproved by him; for the Lord disciplines the one he loves* — and then expands the citation into a sustained argument about how God's *paideia* operates in the Christian life. The Father disciplines his children. The discipline is what fathers do because they love their children. The discipline produces, over time, *the peaceful fruit of righteousness*. The discipline is formation, not punishment.

Ephesians 6:4 is the parental application. *Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction (paideia and nouthesia) of the Lord.* The instruction Paul gives includes a negative — do not provoke to anger — and a positive — form them in the way of the Lord. The negative matters. Most American evangelical parenting writing on this passage has emphasized the positive without giving adequate weight to the negative. The negative is what most distinguishes the biblical practice of discipline from the American evangelical practice that has been confused with it.

The biblical word *musar*, in the Old Testament, has similar range. Proverbs uses *musar* frequently, and the use covers instruction, formation, the inculcation of wisdom, and yes, sometimes corrective measures. But the corrective measures are part of a larger formative project, not the substance of it. The Proverbs father who disciplines his son is the Proverbs father who has been teaching his son the way of wisdom over years. The discipline makes sense within that larger frame, and is unintelligible outside it.

## What punishment is, and how it became confused with discipline

Punishment, in the technical sense I am using it here, is the imposition of negative consequences in response to wrongdoing, primarily for the purpose of correcting behavior. Punishment can be a part of discipline, but punishment is not the substance of discipline, and punishment alone — without the larger formative context — is not what scripture calls discipline.

The American evangelical conflation of these categories happened gradually over the twentieth century, through a combination of cultural factors and a particular reading of certain Proverbs passages on the rod. The reading isolated certain verses, abstracted them from the larger Proverbs frame, and produced a parenting paradigm in which physical punishment for behavioral infractions became the center of what was called *biblical discipline*. The paradigm was promoted through influential parenting books from the 1970s through the 1990s, was institutionalized in Christian schools and homeschool curricula, and shaped two generations of evangelical parents.

The paradigm has had costs. The most documented cost is the psychological one. The children raised under behavioral-control parenting — what the developmental literature calls authoritarian parenting — show, in repeated studies, higher rates of anxiety, lower self-regulation skills, more difficulty with internal motivation, and lower long-term adherence to the values their parents tried to instill. The findings are not a progressive bias. The findings are consistent across multiple research traditions. The parenting style that has been called *biblical* by the dominant American evangelical writing has been failing to produce the long-term outcomes it promised.

The second cost is theological. The behavioral-control paradigm makes God's love conditional on the child's behavior in the parental modeling. *Behave, and you will be loved.* The model is not the gospel. The gospel is that we are loved while we are still sinners (Romans 5:8). The parental modeling that contradicts the gospel is, in some real sense, teaching the children a false gospel. The children grow up with an internalized framework in which divine love is performance-based, and many of them leave the framework in adulthood when they discover it cannot bear the weight of their actual moral failures.

The third cost is the visible breakdown of family relationships in many evangelical households. The relationship between parents and adult children in many evangelical families is more distant than the parents had been led to expect by the parenting books they followed. The breakdown is not always traceable to specific parenting failures; many factors contribute. But the behavioral-control paradigm, with its emphasis on compliance over character and on punishment over formation, has produced relational damage that the paradigm did not predict and has not, in most cases, repaired.

## What biblical formation actually looks like

I want to be concrete about what the recovery of *paideia* — biblical formation rather than behavioral control — looks like in actual parenting.

It looks like presence. The most consistent finding in the developmental literature on parenting is that the quality of the parent-child relationship is the primary predictor of long-term outcomes. The relationship is built through presence, through sustained time together, through the parent's attention to the child as a particular person with particular interests and struggles. The relationship is what makes the rest of the formation work possible. Without the relationship, the formation work has no traction. With the relationship, the formation work has substantial traction even when specific practices are imperfect.

It looks like conversation. The biblical model of formation in Deuteronomy 6 — *talk of them when you sit in your house and when you walk by the way, when you lie down and when you rise* — assumes ongoing conversation about the meaning of scripture, the meaning of life, the meaning of the various situations the family encounters. The conversation is the substance of the formation. The catechism without the conversation is not biblical formation; it is rote memorization, which has some value but does not produce what the conversation produces.

It looks like modeling. Children are forming, all the time, by watching the parents. What the parents actually do — how they handle conflict, how they handle money, how they treat their spouses, how they treat the surrounding community, how they respond to suffering, how they pray — is the formation that the children are receiving. The modeling cannot be faked. The modeling is what the children will eventually do themselves, in the absence of explicit instruction to do otherwise.

It looks like repair after rupture. The parent who fails his child — and every parent does, in various ways, throughout the parenting years — has the opportunity to do something formative when the failure happens. The repair work — the acknowledgment of the failure, the genuine apology, the demonstration of changed behavior, the willingness to talk through what happened — is some of the most formative work parents do. The children whose parents repair after rupture grow up with a framework in which mistakes are normal and repair is possible. The children whose parents do not repair grow up with a framework in which mistakes are catastrophic and pretending nothing happened is the only available response.

It looks like the integration of faith into the household rather than the outsourcing of faith formation to the church. The household is the smallest unit of the church. The household that prays together, reads scripture together, serves together, worships together, repents together is the household in which the children are receiving primary catechesis. The household that outsources all this to the youth program is the household whose children's faith is at the mercy of factors the household does not control.

It looks like discipline that is genuinely corrective, used sparingly, integrated into the larger formative work, and explicitly framed as part of love rather than as punishment for the parent's frustration. The historic Christian tradition has consistently included corrective discipline as one tool among many in the larger work of formation. The corrective discipline has not been the substance of formation; it has been a particular function within it. The recovery of the proportion is what the contemporary Christian parent most needs.

## The verdict

The discipline your children remember at thirty-five is not the discipline you delivered in anger. It is the slow, patient, repeated work of being formed in a household where character was honored, where rupture was repaired, where mistakes were occasions for formation rather than occasions for shame. That work is most of what parenting is. The behavioral pieces are the smallest part. Get the formation right. The behavior follows.

I am writing this for the Christian parent who has been doing the behavioral-control paradigm and is exhausted by it and is watching it not produce what the books promised. The paradigm was an aberration in the history of Christian parenting. The biblical concept of *paideia* is older, deeper, and more sustainable. The recovery is available. The recovery starts with the recognition that the goal is formation, not compliance — that the goal is the long shaping of a person who will love God and love neighbor at forty, not the short-term management of a four-year-old's behavior.

The four-year-old's behavior matters. The forty-year-old's character matters more. The strategies that produce the second are different from the strategies that produce the first. The Christian parent has to choose. The choice is being made now, in your household, with the strategies you are currently running. Reconsider them. The recovery is worth the discomfort.

The practice has been lost in most contemporary evangelical households. The recovery is part of what this writing project will keep returning to. The next essay names it.
