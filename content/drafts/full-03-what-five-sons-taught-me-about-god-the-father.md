I did not believe in God when I was twenty. I do now. The change happened in a library at first, and over a long time in conversation, and finally — finally — in the room where my first son was born, and then four more times after, and I am still finding out what I learned in those rooms.

I came to faith from atheism. I was raised without a father. I have five sons. Those last two facts are connected, and they are connected to the God I write about, and they are connected to almost everything I have to say. The Father in the Trinity is not the father I had. The Father in the Trinity is the father I have had to learn how to be. And every son has taught me something different about him.

## What it costs to grow up without one

The father-absent boy does not know what he has lost until he is in his thirties at the earliest, sometimes never. The loss is structural. It is not the absence of a particular man. It is the absence of the category that man was supposed to fill — the category that says, *here is what a man does, and here is what a man holds, and here is what a man bears the weight of without flinching.* The boy without a father has to invent these things, often badly, from the films he watches and the men he half-watches at a distance.

I invented mine from movies. I invented them from the older boys at the apartment complex. I invented them from books I read in the library where I also found, eventually, the books that ended my atheism. The inventing was incomplete. There was a kind of masculinity-shaped hole in me that nothing in my twenties could fill, and most of what I tried to fill it with — the things young men try — left the hole worse.

I do not want to overstate this. There are men without fathers who have grown up into magnificent men. There are men with fathers who never recovered from their fathers. But there is a particular vacancy that the father-absent boy carries, and the church has not been generally interested in that vacancy except as a problem to be solved by ministry programming. The vacancy is older than ministry programming. The vacancy is what made the Old Testament so insistent that the God of Israel is a *Father* — the father to the fatherless, the defender of widows. Most of the people the Bible was written for would have heard this and wept.

## What it cost to become one

Becoming a father did not heal the vacancy. It deepened it.

The night my first son was born, Susanna handed him to me, and I held him, and the room was quiet, and the first thing I thought was: *I have no idea what I am doing.* I did not know how to be the thing he needed me to be. I had never seen it done. I knew, in the way you know a doctrine before you know it, that the father he needed was the father I had not had, and I would have to become it from scratch, and I would fail at it more often than I would succeed.

Most of what I have learned about God the Father in the twenty-two years since I have learned in the failing.

The first son taught me that fathering is not primarily about doing. It is about being there. He did not need me to know what I was doing. He needed me to keep showing up while I did not know what I was doing. The Father in the Trinity does not give Israel a set of instructions and step away. He stays. The staying is most of the fathering.

The second son taught me that the father is the one who carries weight the children should not yet have to carry. There is a difference between *the work you do for your family* and *the work you let your family see you carry.* The second is the formative one. He has watched me carry weight. He watched the way I held my own composure during a season of conflict in the church, and he was nine, and I did not know he was watching, and he told me eight years later that he had been watching. The Father in the Trinity carries the weight no one else can carry. The Son sees it. The Son becomes the man who can carry that weight, because he saw it carried first.

The third son taught me about repentance. He was the one who, at three, made me realize how often I was angry at things that were not really him. He had been disobeying. I was not yelling about his disobedience. I was yelling about the rest of the week. He stood there, three years old, and looked at me, and I sat down on the floor and I told him I was sorry, and I meant it, and he forgave me before I had finished saying it. The Father in the Trinity is not always angry. The Father in the Trinity does not lash out from the rest of the week. The Father in the Trinity disciplines us, but he disciplines us out of love and toward our formation, not out of his own exhaustion. I did not know this in my body until my three-year-old taught it to me.

The fourth son taught me about delight. He is the one whose temperament makes him impossible not to enjoy. He has been delighted with the world since he was a baby. He laughs about everything. He still laughs. Watching him, I had to revise my picture of God — a picture I had inherited from atheist parents and assembled from religious magazines — toward a God who actually *delights* in his children. Not a God who tolerates us. Not a God who corrects us. A God who looks at us and lights up. The Father in the Trinity, in Isaiah 62, *exults over* his people with singing. I did not believe this could be true until I had a son whose face did the same thing every morning when I walked into his room.

The fifth son taught me about waiting. He came later than the others. There was a long stretch in which we thought he would not come at all. The waiting was hard, and it formed something in Susanna and me that the easier seasons had not, and when he came, we knew what we were holding because we had spent so long not holding him. The Father in the Trinity has been waiting longer than any of us can imagine. The waiting is part of the fathering. The waiting is part of how he loves us. I did not understand this when I was a younger man. The fifth son taught it to me.

## What I would tell a father starting out

If I could write a letter to the man I was the night my first son was born, the letter would be short. It would say four things.

You do not have to know what you are doing. You have to keep showing up while you do not. The showing up is most of it.

The man you are now is not the father your son needs. He cannot be. He is too young, too unfinished, too false. The fathering will form you. You will be a different man at forty-six than you are at twenty-four, and the difference will be because of the boys in the next room.

When you fail him, repent. Quickly. Without defensiveness. He is watching how you handle being wrong more than he is watching how you handle being right. He will become the kind of man he sees you become.

The God you are coming to believe in is the Father you have never had. He is not the father I had either. None of us has had him, in the way the doctrine describes him. He is the Father we are all becoming and the Father we are all being made by — and the work of being a father is, mostly, the work of letting him be the Father he is.

## The verdict

I did not know God was a Father until I became one. I am still finding out. Every son has been a chapter in the catechism. I will not write the catechism completely; I am not finished being fathered, and they are not finished being mine. The God who came to me through philosophy and through atheism and through a long library season is the same God I now meet, most reliably, in the room where my sons are. He is the Father the fatherless boy needed. He is the Father the new father needed. He is the Father I am still becoming.
