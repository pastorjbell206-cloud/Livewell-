Psalm 88 ends without resolution. That is unusual. Most lament psalms move through darkness toward some form of trust — they end with a declaration of confidence, a memory of God's past faithfulness, a choice to keep worshipping in spite of the darkness. Psalm 88 does none of that. It begins with a cry to God and ends with the words "darkness is my closest friend." There is no turn. There is no uplift. The darkness is the last word.

The church has generally not known what to do with Psalm 88. We quote Psalm 23 at funerals. We sing Psalm 100. We return to Psalm 51 for confession. Psalm 88 sits in the collection, rarely preached, rarely sung, rarely prayed — because it does not go where we expect the Psalms to go, and its refusal to go there makes us uncomfortable.

But the Psalter is not a collection of devotional verses arranged for our comfort. It is the prayer book of the people of God, assembled over centuries, including the full range of what happens when human beings try to talk to God honestly. The fact that Psalm 88 is in the canon is a theological statement: this is also what prayer looks like. This is also what faith sounds like when it has nowhere left to go.

## What the Psalms actually are

Walter Brueggemann's distinction between psalms of orientation, disorientation, and new orientation [cite — confirm] is the most useful framework I know for understanding why the Psalter is structured the way it is. Psalms of orientation celebrate the goodness of God in a world where things are working. Psalms of disorientation — the laments, the complaints, the expressions of abandonment — name what it is like when the world has fallen apart and God seems absent. Psalms of new orientation describe what it is like to have come through darkness to a different kind of trust, not the naive trust of orientation but the hard-won trust of someone who has been in the darkness and survived it.

The church has been very comfortable with orientation psalms and new orientation psalms. It has been systematically uncomfortable with disorientation psalms — the ones where God is accused of sleeping, of forgetting, of turning his face away. We have treated those psalms as embarrassing ancestors of a more mature faith, the raw expressions of people who had not yet learned to trust properly.

This is exactly backwards. The laments are not evidence of immature faith. They are evidence of honest faith — of people who believed in God enough to bring their grief and their accusation to him rather than collapsing into silence or turning away. The person who cannot bring anger to God has not matured beyond the lament psalms. She has lost access to part of her own interior life that God already knows is there.

## What we have lost by aestheticizing them

The King James Bible's translation of the Psalms is genuinely beautiful. That beauty has been, in certain respects, a problem. When Psalm 22 becomes a text we admire for its poetry — "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" — we have placed it at aesthetic distance from the person in the hospital room who is praying the same prayer without the archaic diction and without any sense that this is a psalm that belongs to her.

She should know it belongs to her. That is precisely what it is for.

Eugene Peterson spent three years rendering the Psalms into contemporary English for what became The Message, [cite — confirm] not because the original translations were inaccurate but because the original translations had, through familiarity and liturgical distance, become texts people admired rather than prayers people prayed. He wanted to give people the Psalms as working documents — as tools for the interior life rather than exhibits in the museum of religious literature.

The imprecatory psalms are where this becomes most acute. Psalm 137, which ends with a brutal image of infant slaughter as an expression of the Babylonian exiles' grief and rage, is genuinely difficult. We do not sing it. We do not pray it. We explain it in footnotes. But it is in the canon, and its presence there is the canon's acknowledgment that the grief of people who have lost everything — who have watched their city burned and their families destroyed and their children taken — can produce a darkness that Scripture does not sanitize. The psalm does not endorse the image it contains. It names what grief at that depth sounds like. The naming is itself an act of pastoral care.

## How to pray them again

The most important thing is to read the whole collection, including the parts you have skipped. Particularly the parts you have skipped.

The next most important thing is to understand that the Psalms are permission. Permission to be angry at God. Permission to accuse. Permission to say that you cannot find him, that the prayers are not being answered, that the darkness is lasting longer than you were told it would. The permission is not the end of the prayer. It is the beginning of the kind of prayer that goes somewhere real, because it starts somewhere real rather than starting where we think prayer is supposed to start.

Thomas Merton wrote that the Psalms are the prayers of Christ himself — that when we pray the Psalms, we are praying in the voice of the one who prayed them before us. [cite — confirm] The cry of dereliction from the cross is Psalm 22. That is not incidental. Jesus, at the moment of maximum suffering, prayed the lament psalms. He had learned them in the synagogue. He prayed them when nothing else was left.

The Psalms are not poetry, though they are also poetry. They are not history, though they are also history. They are the voice of people in extremity, talking to God, and they are the record that God accepted that conversation.

Pray them. All of them. Especially the ones that end in darkness.
