The word *sabbatical* makes a lot of churches nervous. It sounds like a long vacation, a perk, a reward handed to a favored pastor while the congregation keeps the lights on. That misunderstanding is why so few churches have one, and why so many lose good pastors to an exhaustion a sabbatical might have healed. So it is worth saying plainly what a sabbatical actually is, and then saying the harder thing — that the healthiest churches do not merely offer it. They require it.

A sabbatical is the biblical rhythm of rest, scaled up. The same God who built a weekly Sabbath into creation also built rest into the years — the seventh year in which the land itself lay fallow (Leviticus 25). The principle is consistent and it is not sentimental: things that are worked without rest are eventually worked to ruin, and the remedy is not a softer pace but a real stop. A sabbatical applies that ancient wisdom to the long arc of a ministry — an extended season, every several years, in which the pastor genuinely steps back to rest, to be restored, often to study and pray and recover the soul that the relentless weekly grind wears thin.

It is not a vacation. A vacation interrupts the work for a week and then returns to it; the pastor is still, on some level, on call, still bracing for Monday. A sabbatical is long enough and complete enough to do what a vacation cannot — to let the well actually refill, to let prayer become something other than preparation again, to let a man remember who he is apart from his function. The research on this is consistent enough to act on: pastors return from real sabbaticals renewed, more creative, more durable, and they stay in ministry longer. Now the harder claim. A sabbatical the pastor must request is a sabbatical most pastors will never take. The same instincts that drive a man to exhaustion — the fear of seeming weak, the belief that the church cannot run without him, the guilt of resting while others work — will keep him from asking. He will let the policy sit unused and grind himself down rather than be the one who appears to need a break. So the wise church does not leave it to his request. It builds the sabbatical into the structure and *requires* it, the way it requires anything it considers essential, so that taking it is obedience rather than indulgence and refusing it is not an option.

Requiring it also protects the church from itself. A congregation that depends entirely on one man's unbroken output has built something fragile and called it strength. The sabbatical forces the healthy thing: the raising up of others to lead in the pastor's absence, the discovery that the church belongs to Christ and not to its pastor, the dismantling of the quiet idol that says the whole enterprise rests on one set of shoulders. A church that has learned to function while its pastor rests is a stronger church, not a weaker one — and it usually finds, to its own surprise, that the weeks went fine.

I know the objections, because I have made them. We cannot afford it. We are too small. The timing is never right. But the church cannot afford the alternative, which it pays for constantly: the burned-out pastor, the forced resignation, the years of fruitfulness lost because no one made the man stop in time. A sabbatical is not a luxury a church indulges in good years. It is preventive care a church cannot afford to skip, and the bill for skipping it always comes due.

Build it in. Make it required. Plan for it before the pastor needs it, because by the time he obviously needs it, the thing that would have prevented the crisis has already been deferred too long.

The God who rested on the seventh day was not setting an example for the weak. He was telling the truth about how his creatures are made. The wise church believes him — and writes it into the bylaws.
