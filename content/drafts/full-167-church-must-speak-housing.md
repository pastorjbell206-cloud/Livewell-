In most American cities right now, the cost of housing is consuming a proportion of working-class income that previous generations would have considered a crisis. It is a crisis. The median rent in most major metros has outpaced wage growth for two decades. [cite — confirm] Teachers, nurses, sanitation workers, the people the city requires to function, cannot afford to live in the city they sustain. They commute. They double up. They are one unexpected expense from losing the roof they have.

The church has largely treated this as someone else's problem.

That cannot continue. Not because housing is a political issue that happens to be urgent, but because housing is a biblical issue that the church has been slow to name as such.

## What a home is, theologically

The Hebrew concept of shalom — the comprehensive flourishing that God intends for human life — has a spatial dimension. To dwell, in the Hebrew imagination, is to be placed. The promise to Abraham is a land. The promise to Israel in Deuteronomy is a land where each family will sit under its own vine and fig tree (Micah 4:4). The return from exile in Ezekiel is described as a resettlement, a rehousing, a restoration of each person to their place. The eschatological vision of Revelation is the New Jerusalem, the city, the dwelling of God with his people. Homelessness in the Scripture is not merely physical deprivation. It is a condition that violates the deep structure of what it means to be a creature made to inhabit a world God called good.

The book of Nehemiah begins with a man weeping over broken walls. Walls and gates — the structures that make a city habitable, that provide protection and community — are the substance of his grief. When the walls are rebuilt, when the city is reorganized and the people are settled, it is described as an act of faithfulness to the covenant. The work is practical and theological at the same time. The physical and the spiritual are not separate categories in the biblical imagination.

## What zoning does

The modern housing crisis is not primarily a failure of individual ambition or personal responsibility. It is the downstream consequence of deliberate policy choices made over decades. Single-family zoning, established in American cities largely during the mid-twentieth century, restricted the density of housing in desirable areas, limiting supply and driving prices up. These policies were in many cases explicitly designed to keep certain populations out of certain neighborhoods. [cite — confirm] The language has changed. The effect persists.

I am aware that this sounds like policy analysis, and the objection will come quickly: the church should not be in the business of zoning advocacy. But the prophets of Israel did not refrain from addressing the economic and legal structures that produced poverty among God's people. Micah condemned those who "covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away; they oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance" (Micah 2:2). The specific legal and economic mechanism by which the powerful appropriated housing and land from the vulnerable was precisely the prophetic concern.

The mechanism changes. The concern does not.

## What the congregation already knows

There are people in most congregations who know this from the inside. The single mother who is working two jobs and still cannot make rent. The young couple who grew up in the community and cannot afford to stay in it. The senior member who bought a home in 1978 on a single income and watches her children unable to replicate what she had. The recently arrived family navigating the rental market without the credit history that most landlords require.

These people are not abstractions. They are in the pews. Their housing precarity shapes their mental health, their marital stress, their children's school performance, their capacity to participate in the life of the congregation. When we refuse to name the system that produced their precarity, we are asking them to bring their suffering into a space that has quietly agreed not to discuss its cause.

That is a form of pastoral failure. It is also a failure of prophetic nerve.

## What the church can actually do

Speaking is not sufficient. Speaking is where faithfulness begins.

The church has assets — land, buildings, institutional relationships, organized volunteer capacity — that make it one of the few actors positioned to respond to the housing crisis at a local level with both prophetic voice and practical action. Congregations have built affordable housing on surplus land. Church coalitions have engaged city councils on zoning reform. Congregations have organized to prevent the displacement of long-term residents from gentrifying neighborhoods.

This work is not the whole gospel. But the gospel that does not touch the housing status of the family sleeping in their car in the parking lot is not the whole gospel either.

Isaiah 58:7 asks: "Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house?" The house in that verse is not a metaphor. It is a dwelling. A roof. A place where a person is safe.

The church knows how to talk about homelessness as a spiritual crisis. It has been slower to talk about housing as a justice crisis. The two are the same crisis. We do not get to treat one without the other.
