The homeless man sleeping under the interstate overpass three blocks from my church does not come to our Sunday services. This is not because he has not been invited. It is because the social distance between where he is and where we gather on Sunday morning is too great to be bridged by an invitation.

I have known this for years. I have mostly not said it from the pulpit.

The people Jesus spent the most time with — the tax collectors, the prostitutes, the lepers, the demoniacs, the Samaritan woman at the well, the man in the tombs among the Gerasenes who cut himself with stones — these were not the kind of people who showed up at synagogue. They were excluded from the formal religious community. Some of them were legally impure and could not enter. Some of them were socially marked in ways that made their presence unwelcome. And Jesus went to them. He did not wait for them to figure out how to cross the threshold into the respectable gathering. He crossed the threshold into their world.

The church has built many programs to reach vulnerable people. Food banks, clothes closets, holiday toy drives, addiction recovery groups — these are genuine goods, and I am not dismissing them. But they are all structured around an assumption: that the vulnerable need to come to where the church is. They need to make the journey to our building, to enter our space, to participate in our system. The model is almost entirely attractional, even when the programs are labeled outreach.

Jesus did not operate on an attractional model. The Sermon on the Mount was delivered to a crowd that had followed him out into a field because there was something in him that drew people who had nothing else to try. His ministry was characterized by going — into the synagogues, yes, but also into the countryside, into the cemetery, into the home of a tax collector, into the social space of a Samaritan village where no self-respecting rabbi would have stopped. He went where the need was, and the need was not primarily inside the religious gathering.

Why are the most vulnerable people in your city not in your pews? Several reasons.

The building is inaccessible — architecturally, socially, culturally. The gathered congregation is, in most cases, economically and culturally homogeneous in ways that signal to the poor and the marginalized that this is not a room built for them. The service is structured around the assumptions and capacities of middle-class participants. The social rituals of hospitality — the coffee hour, the greeting, the small talk — require a kind of social fluency that people in crisis do not have on Sunday morning.

These are design choices. Every design choice is a theological statement about who the church is for.

I am not arguing that the church should become a social service agency. The church's primary gathering is for worship — the proclamation of the word, the celebration of the sacraments, the formation of people in the gospel. But the question is whether the gospel we are proclaiming is the gospel of the one who left the ninety-nine to find the one, or a gospel that assumes the ninety-nine can all find their way to the same building at the same time on the same terms.

Luke 14 describes a dinner where the host, after the invited guests make excuses, sends his servant to bring in "the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame" from the streets and alleys (Luke 14:21). When there is still room, he sends the servant out again to the roads and country lanes to compel people to come in, "so that my house will be full." This is not a parable about church growth strategy. It is a description of what the kingdom feast looks like — and it requires the host to go where the guests are, rather than waiting for the guests to arrive.

The most vulnerable people in your city are not in your pews. They are on the streets and in the shelters and in the county jail and in the apartments where the heat has been cut off. The church that takes Luke 14 seriously knows where to find them.

The question is whether it will go.
