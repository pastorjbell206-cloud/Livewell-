There is a story, attributed to various sources over the years [cite — confirm], about people standing on a riverbank pulling drowning bodies from the water. They work heroically, exhausting themselves, saving every person they can reach. Then someone turns and walks upstream. The others call out: where are you going? We need you here. And the one walking says: I'm going to find out who's throwing people in.

The story is not an argument against rescuing the drowning. It is an argument about the limits of rescue when throwing continues uninterrupted.

The church in America is very good at the riverbank. We run food pantries, emergency assistance funds, coats for kids drives, and Christmas toy collections. These things are not nothing. They are genuine expressions of love and genuine responses to genuine need. I have been part of them. I am grateful for them. The people who received a bag of groceries from our pantry were helped by that bag of groceries. The help was real.

But the bag of groceries did not change why they needed it. And if the same person returns to the pantry for the fourth consecutive month, we are managing a symptom. The cause — whatever arrangement of income and cost and lack of savings and medical debt and housing instability produced the need — is still operating. The throwing continues. We are on the riverbank.

This distinction has a theological lineage. The Hebrew prophets were not opposed to generosity. They were opposed to generosity that functioned as a substitute for justice. Amos is the sharpest voice: "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies. Even though you offer me your burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them" (Amos 5:21–22). The community was performing religious obligation while leaving the structural arrangements of economic exploitation untouched. God called their worship noise. Not because the offerings were insincere but because they were incomplete — they addressed the individual's religious duty without addressing the social conditions the same individuals were creating and benefiting from.

The structural alternative to symptom treatment is the disruption of the system that produces the symptoms. This is what Amos calls for: "Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24). Not a trickle of charitable distribution. A torrent — an ongoing, structural, self-sustaining current of right ordering. The image is hydrological, not transactional. Water rolling down is not something you hand out. It is something that changes the shape of the land.

I have been part of churches that had flourishing benevolence ministries and no public voice on the policies that produced the need those ministries were serving. We helped people survive the consequences of arrangements we never named. That is not full faithfulness to the prophetic tradition. It is the acceptable half — the half that does not cost relationships with wealthy donors or require public positions on contested policy questions or put the pastor on the wrong side of the congregation's political preferences.

The objection deserves honest engagement: policy change is slow, contested, uncertain. Direct charity helps people now, in this week, in this particular emergency. You cannot feed a child with a policy paper. True. The choice between charity and justice is not a real choice — both are necessary, and the church should be doing both. The problem is the church that has chosen only charity and told itself this is equivalent to justice. It is not. It is the easier choice. It is the choice that keeps the uncomfortable questions unasked.

The harder question is always: why? Why does this family need emergency food assistance three months in a row? Why does this neighborhood have no grocery store within two miles? Why does this child's school have a forty-year-old textbook? Why does this worker earn less in real terms than a worker doing the same job did thirty years ago? [cite — confirm] Those questions lead to systems, to policies, to arrangements that benefit some people at the expense of others. The people benefiting are often in our pews. The people paying the cost are often in our pantry lines.

Charity without justice is not neutral. It functions as a pressure valve — enough relief to prevent the suffering from becoming politically destabilizing, not enough change to alter the conditions that produce it. That is a service to the arrangement, not a challenge to it.

The church is called to both. To pull people from the river, yes. And to walk upstream and name what is happening.

Those who are throwing need to be named. Not managed.
