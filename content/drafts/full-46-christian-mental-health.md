American evangelicalism has, until recently, had a long and largely unhealed history of treating mental illness as a spiritual failure. The history has cost lives. The recovery requires confessing what the church has been wrong about, recovering what the historic tradition has known about the body's role in the soul's life, and building structures of pastoral care that include rather than reject professional mental health care. I want to write about all three.

This essay is for the Christian reader who has been struggling with depression or anxiety or some other diagnosable mental health condition, and who has been carrying additional shame because the church has been treating his condition as a sign of spiritual deficiency. The shame is unjustified. The shame has been one of the church's failures, and the failure is being slowly addressed in some quarters but is still widespread enough that I want to address it directly.

## What the church has been getting wrong

I want to name the specific patterns of evangelical mishandling of mental health, because the naming is the necessary work and the naming has been delayed for too long.

The first pattern has been the treatment of depression as a sign of weak faith. The implicit and sometimes explicit framing has been that the genuine Christian, in possession of the Holy Spirit and oriented toward Christ, should not be experiencing sustained depression. The Christian who is depressed has therefore been doing something wrong — sin he has not confessed, unbelief he has not addressed, gratitude he has not practiced. The framing has produced a generation of depressed Christians who concluded that their condition was their own fault and who therefore did not seek the help that would have addressed it. Many of them have died from the untreated condition. The pattern is well-documented and has been confessed by some evangelical leaders, but not as widely or as honestly as the cost has required.

The second pattern has been the treatment of anxiety as a failure of trust. The Christian who experiences anxiety has been told, often from pulpits, that anxiety reflects insufficient trust in God's provision. The teaching cites Philippians 4:6 — *do not be anxious about anything* — as a direct command that the anxious Christian is violating. The teaching ignores the broader scriptural witness on the body, on the various conditions that produce anxiety, and on the difference between sinful worry and clinical anxiety. The teaching has produced Christians who interpret their anxiety as evidence of their own failure to trust God, when the anxiety is often a medical condition with a medical etiology that has nothing to do with the person's spiritual state.

The third pattern has been the suspicion of professional mental health care. The pattern has had several forms. The crude form has been the explicit rejection of psychotherapy and psychiatric medication as worldly substitutes for spiritual disciplines. The more sophisticated form has been the institutional preference for biblical counseling that explicitly denies the legitimacy of professional psychology. The most sophisticated form has been the public acceptance of professional care combined with the private suspicion of it, the assumption that the truly spiritual Christian should be able to address mental health concerns through scripture and prayer rather than through medication or therapy. The pattern has been particularly damaging because it has prevented many Christians from accessing the care that would have helped them.

The fourth pattern has been the failure to address mental health pastorally with any seriousness. Most American evangelical pastors have not been trained in mental health basics. Many do not know the signs of clinical depression. Many do not know what suicidal ideation looks like. Many have not learned the appropriate response when a congregant discloses a mental health condition. The undertraining has produced, in many congregations, the situation in which the person most likely to be approached for help — the pastor — is the person least equipped to provide it. The undertraining has not been addressed adequately in seminary curricula or in continuing education for pastors, and the inadequate addressing is part of the broader pastoral education failure I have written about elsewhere.

## What scripture actually says

I want to recover what scripture actually says about the body, the soul, and the conditions we now call mental illness, because the recovery is necessary for the pastoral response to be theologically grounded rather than therapeutically minimal.

Scripture takes the body seriously. The Hebrew tradition, more strongly than the later Hellenized tradition, treats the human person as a body-soul unity rather than as a soul that happens to inhabit a body. The Hebrew word *nephesh* — usually translated *soul* — actually refers to the living person in his bodily wholeness. The Hebrew word *basar* — usually translated *flesh* — refers to the bodily aspect of the same wholeness. The two are not separable in the way later platonized Christian theology often treated them. The mental and emotional conditions of the person are conditions of the bodily-soul unity, and they have bodily as well as spiritual dimensions.

This has implications for how we read the experiences of biblical figures who, on contemporary clinical assessment, would meet diagnostic criteria for various mental health conditions. Elijah, after his confrontation with the prophets of Baal, fled into the wilderness and asked God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4). His behavior is consistent with what we would now call major depression with suicidal ideation. God's response was not rebuke. God's response was sustained pastoral care that included food, rest, the appearance of God in the still small voice, and the assignment of a new task. God treated the body's exhaustion and the soul's despair together, in ways that integrate what modern psychiatry has distinguished.

David's psalms of lament are among the most psychologically acute documents in ancient literature. Psalms 42 and 43 describe what we would now call depression with substantial clinical accuracy. *Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?* The psalm is canonical. The condition described is part of normal Christian experience, and the psalm models the practice of addressing the condition through both honest naming and disciplined return to God.

Hannah, in 1 Samuel 1, is described as in deep distress over her infertility — she wept, she could not eat, her face was downcast for years. The priest Eli initially misread her condition; he thought she was drunk because of the unusual quality of her prayer. Hannah corrected him. *I am a woman troubled in spirit; I have not been drinking wine.* The narrative honors Hannah's grief and treats her response — sustained prayer in the temple — as appropriate to her condition rather than as a failure to trust God.

Paul, in 2 Corinthians 1, describes his own experience: *we were so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself.* Paul, the apostle who wrote about thirteen letters of the New Testament, was at one point overwhelmed to the point of despair. The despair was not a failure of his faith. The despair was a feature of his actual experience that he described honestly in scripture. The despair gave way, in the narrative, to a renewed sense of God's faithfulness — but the despair was real and was not minimized.

The scriptural witness, taken honestly, includes the recognition that what we now call mental health conditions are part of normal human and Christian experience, that they have bodily as well as spiritual dimensions, and that the appropriate response involves both spiritual care and the practical attention to the body's needs. The witness has been suppressed in some traditions of evangelical preaching, and the recovery of the witness is part of the broader recovery of healthier pastoral practice.

## What the church should be doing

I want to be concrete about what healthy pastoral response to mental health conditions looks like, because the abstract conversation about how the church has gotten it wrong does not produce the practical changes the church needs to make.

The church should affirm the legitimacy of professional mental health care. The Christian who is struggling with depression should be encouraged to see a therapist and, if appropriate, a psychiatrist for medication evaluation. The Christian who is struggling with anxiety should be encouraged to seek the same kinds of help. The church's role is not to replace professional care; the church's role is to provide the spiritual care that complements the professional care. The dichotomy between spiritual care and professional care is false, and the false dichotomy has cost lives.

The pastor should be trained in mental health basics. The training should include the recognition of common conditions, the appropriate response when a congregant discloses, the basics of suicide risk assessment, and the network of professional resources the pastor can refer to. The training does not make the pastor a therapist; the training makes the pastor a competent first responder who can connect the congregant to the right level of care. Most American evangelical pastors have not received this training, and the absence is one of the most consequential gaps in pastoral education.

The congregation should be a place where mental health conditions can be disclosed without shame. The disclosure should be received with care rather than with the implicit suggestion that the person should have been able to manage the condition through more prayer or more scripture. The small groups should be safe places for disclosure. The pulpit should explicitly address mental health in ways that honor the reality of the conditions and the appropriate responses.

The household should include mental health in the categories of care it provides. The Christian household that has been doing family worship, mutual care, sustained relational presence — the household that does this well includes attention to the mental health of its members. The household notices when a member is struggling. The household supports the member's pursuit of appropriate care. The household does not require the member to manage the condition in isolation.

The broader culture of the church should reflect the recovery. The books in the church library should include sound resources on Christian engagement with mental health. The recommended therapists in the area should be ones the church has vetted as professionally competent and theologically respectful. The sermon series should occasionally address mental health directly. The cultural recovery happens through many small choices over years rather than through one announcement.

## What helps if you are in the condition

I want to address Christian readers who are currently struggling with depression, anxiety, or some other mental health condition directly, because the abstract pastoral conversation does not help the person in the condition.

Seek professional help. The Christian therapist or psychiatrist who is competent and theologically respectful is widely available in most areas. The cost has often dropped substantially in recent years through telehealth options and through churches that subsidize mental health care for members. The willingness to seek help is not a sign of weak faith. The willingness to seek help is the appropriate response to a medical condition that has a medical etiology and medical treatments.

Take the medication if it is prescribed. The Christian who needs an antidepressant or an anti-anxiety medication is not failing spiritually by taking it. The medication is a gift of God's common grace through the medical profession, and it allows the brain to function in ways that support the spiritual disciplines the person wants to practice. The historic Christian tradition has not, on the whole, treated medical treatment of bodily conditions as spiritually problematic, and the contemporary suspicion of psychiatric medication has been an aberration that should be confessed and refused.

Maintain the spiritual disciplines you can. The depressed Christian often cannot pray, cannot read scripture with attention, cannot worship with the feeling that he had before the depression. The historic tradition has been clear that the maintenance of the practices, even when they produce no felt response, is part of the appropriate Christian response to spiritual dryness. The practices are not productive of the condition's resolution; the practices are the appropriate response to the condition while it lasts.

Get pastoral care alongside the professional care. The pastor who is mental-health-competent can offer the spiritual care that complements the professional treatment. The spiritual care addresses dimensions of the experience that the professional care does not address — the theological questions, the relationship with God, the integration of the experience into the Christian life. The combination of pastoral and professional care is generally more effective than either alone.

Be honest with your household and with trusted friends. The condition is harder to bear in isolation. The household and the trusted friends who know about the condition can provide the relational support that contributes to recovery. The disclosure can be embarrassing in cultures where mental health has been treated as a shameful condition. The disclosure is still worth doing.

## The verdict

The church has been wrong about mental health for too long. The wrongness has cost lives. The recovery has been beginning in some institutions and is still incomplete in most. The recovery requires confession of what the church has been wrong about, recovery of what scripture actually says about the body and the soul, and the building of pastoral practices that include rather than reject professional mental health care.

I am writing this for the Christian reader who has been carrying a mental health condition along with the additional shame the church has imposed. The shame is not yours to carry. The condition is medical. The treatment is available. The Christian life is fully consistent with seeking the treatment. The historic tradition has known this. The contemporary American evangelical tradition has, on the whole, failed to transmit it. The transmission is being recovered, slowly, and the recovery is part of the broader work of the church becoming healthier than it has been.

Get the help you need. Take the medication if it is prescribed. Maintain the spiritual disciplines. Find a pastor who understands. Be honest with your household. The condition you are carrying is not a sign of your spiritual failure. The condition is a feature of the body's life, the body that God made and that God redeems and that God will eventually transform. The redemption is in process. The treatment is part of how the redemption works in time. Begin the treatment. The healing is on offer, often through means more practical than the previous generation of evangelical teaching allowed for.
