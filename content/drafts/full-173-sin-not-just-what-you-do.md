The therapeutic version of sin works like this: sin is the things you do that hurt yourself or others. The solution is awareness, accountability, and better habits. The gospel, in this reading, is primarily information about what constitutes sin and motivation to do better. Jesus shows us what the right behavior looks like and provides inspiration and community support to pursue it.

This is not wrong exactly. It is so inadequate that it produces a Christianity that cannot explain why people who are aware of their failures, who are accountable to communities, who are genuinely trying — still cannot change the things that matter most.

Paul knew the problem from the inside. Romans 7 is his autobiographical account of it: "I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate." He is not describing a person who lacks information about the right behavior. He is describing a person who knows precisely what the right behavior is and finds himself unable to do it — not out of laziness or lack of effort, but because there is something wrong at a level deeper than behavior. Something at the level of the will itself.

## The theological category

The Hebrew word for sin — chatta't — can mean a missing of the mark, but the broader canonical picture of sin is not reducible to that image. Genesis 3 does not present the first sin as a simple behavioral failure. It presents it as an act of disordering — of placing the self in the position of God, of reaching for the knowledge of good and evil, of deciding that the creature's judgment should override the Creator's word. The disorder is ontological before it is behavioral. The wrong action is the expression of a disordered self, not merely a bad choice made by a healthy self.

This is why the prophets can say that the human heart is deceitful above all things (Jeremiah 17:9) without exception and without exemption. This is why Jesus, in Mark 7:21-22, locates the source of all defilement inside the person — in the heart — rather than in external contact with the unclean. "From within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts, sexual immorality, theft, murder, adultery, coveting, wickedness, deceit, sensuality, envy, slander, pride, foolishness." He is not describing a list of behaviors that certain people practice. He is describing the interior from which all human beings, without intervention, generate their lives.

## The Augustinian account

Augustine articulated this with a precision that shaped Western theology for more than a thousand years, and whose insight remains the most honest account I know of the actual human condition. His concept of the will's bondage — non posse non peccare, the inability not to sin — described not a person who lacks freedom in the ordinary sense but a person whose will has been so disordered by the fall that even its best efforts at righteousness are directed by something less than the love of God. [cite — confirm]

The steel-man case against this position is Pelagius's: if sin is a condition of nature rather than a behavior, then it is not our fault, and if it is not our fault, then God's judgment of it is unjust. If we cannot do otherwise, we cannot be held accountable for what we do. This is a serious objection and it requires a serious response. The response — worked out by Augustine, affirmed by the councils that followed, refined by the Reformers — is that the bondage of the will is itself the consequence of a choice freely made. The condition is not imposed from outside. It is the result of what Adam, and in Adam all of us, did with the freedom that was given. We are accountable for the condition because we produced it.

But more importantly: the Pelagian account does not fit the experience of people who have tried to change. I have tried to change. I have watched people try to change who were more disciplined than I am, more aware than I am, more accountable than I am. The things that need changing most deeply — the pride, the fear, the desire to be seen that shapes even my most apparently generous acts — do not yield to effort and awareness. They yield to something that comes from outside. That is not a theory. That is what I have witnessed in thirty years of trying to follow Christ and pastoring people who are trying to do the same.

## Why this matters for salvation

The shape of the problem determines the shape of the solution. If sin is primarily behavioral, the solution is primarily behavioral — education, motivation, spiritual disciplines, community accountability, sustained effort. These are not worthless. But they address the symptoms while the condition remains.

If sin is ontological — a condition of the will and the self, not merely of behavior — then the solution must address the self at its root. Paul calls this new creation (2 Corinthians 5:17). John calls it being born again, born from above, the generation of a new life that the flesh cannot produce from within itself (John 3:3-8). The language is deliberately biological and ontological. It is not language about improved behavior. It is language about a different kind of being.

This is what the gospel actually announces. Not that we have been shown the right behavior and given resources to pursue it. That Jesus has accomplished something — in his death and resurrection — that makes possible a different kind of human existence. The Spirit applies that accomplishment by producing in us the capacity that we did not have before.

Sin is what we are. Salvation is what God does about what we are. And what God does is not primarily tell us to do better. It is make us, slowly and not without suffering, into something different.

That is harder to believe. It is also the only account I have found that is honest about the problem.
