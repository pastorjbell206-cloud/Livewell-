Look at how Paul planted a church and you will notice what he did before he left.

He appointed elders. In every city, in every congregation, he and Barnabas appointed elders and committed those congregations to the Lord with prayer and fasting (Acts 14:23). Not because Paul was finished with those churches — the letters prove he was not. But because a church that exists only as long as the planter stays is not yet a church. It is a gathering dependent on one man's presence, and Paul understood that dependency as a failure of formation rather than a sign of success.

This is the theology of leadership team that most of us have not fully received.

The pastor who leads alone — who is the primary decision-maker, the sole vision-holder, the only one with sufficient insight to direct the congregation's future — has built a structure that is by design fragile. When he travels, it stalls. When he is sick, it drifts. When he eventually leaves or dies or cannot continue, it collapses into a search committee trying to find another single person to carry what should have been distributed from the beginning.

I have built ministries that way. I know what they feel like from the inside — there is a particular satisfaction in being needed, in being the person through whom everything flows. I would not have called it pride at the time. I would have called it dedication. But the satisfaction of being indispensable is not the same thing as healthy leadership, and the difference becomes visible at the moments when healthy leadership is most required.

What the New Testament images of leadership consistently picture is not a single leader with followers but a plurality of leaders with shared responsibility. The elders in Acts 20 are addressed collectively. The leadership structures Paul describes in Titus and 1 Timothy are designed for multiplicity — multiple elders, multiple deacons, a distributed rather than centralized set of functions. This is not accident or accident of cultural form. It is theological conviction about how the Spirit distributes gifts and how the church is designed to function.

Building a leadership team is not primarily a management problem. It is a discernment problem. Who in the congregation has the gifts, the character, and the capacity to bear genuine responsibility? The answer is almost never the person who asks for the responsibility most loudly, and almost always includes people who have been quietly faithful over a long stretch of unglamorous work. You are looking for the kind of character that 1 Timothy 3 describes — not brilliance or charisma or the ability to attract followers, but proven trustworthiness, household management, self-control, the capacity to teach and to be taught.

Those people are in your congregation. Finding them requires looking rather than waiting for them to appear fully formed and self-nominated.

Once found, they must be developed rather than just deployed. The pastor who adds people to a leadership team without investing in their formation is creating committee members, not leaders. The formation is the work — the long, patient work of shared discernment, honest conversation, accountability, and the kind of trust that only accumulates through time and repeated decisions made together. You cannot shortcut it and get the real thing.

What you get when you do the work is something worth protecting: a room of people who care about the mission as much as you do, who know the congregation's real condition, who can speak honestly to you when you are wrong, and who can carry the work when you cannot. That room is not a threat to your leadership. It is what makes your leadership worth having.

The pastor who fears the leadership team fears being known too well. That fear is worth examining. The leaders who outlast their churches are almost always men who gathered people around them who knew their limitations and believed in their calling anyway — and who were empowered to fill what the pastor could not.

Build the team. Not because you cannot lead alone. Because the church was never yours to carry alone.
