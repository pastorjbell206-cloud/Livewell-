The American evangelical church has built almost everything for the nuclear family. The single Christian — younger or older, never married, widowed, divorced — has often been treated as an unfinished category waiting for marriage. The treatment is theological malpractice. Singleness is a vocation in scripture, and the recovery of that vocation is among the most pressing pastoral works of the moment.

I want to write this essay for the single Christian who has been navigating the church's awkward treatment of his or her situation for years, and for the married Christian who has not yet thought carefully about what the church owes single members of the body. The conversation has been overdue in evangelical institutions for a long time. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions have done it better, partly because their institutional structures retained the vocational space for celibate religious life that Protestantism largely abolished. The Protestant recovery has to happen on Protestant ground, with Protestant theological resources, and the recovery has barely begun in most American evangelical institutions.

## What scripture actually says

The biblical witness on singleness is more positive than the contemporary evangelical reader has often been taught. I want to lay out the witness, because the witness is the necessary ground for the conversation.

Paul, the most prolific writer in the New Testament, was single. He wrote about singleness as a vocation in 1 Corinthians 7. *I wish that all were as I myself am* — Paul's plain statement that he viewed his own singleness as something he hoped others might share. The passage as a whole grants that marriage is good and that those who cannot exercise self-control should marry, but the passage also presents singleness as a positive vocation rather than as a transitional state. Paul lived this himself, and his Pauline letters are the product of a single Christian whose unmarried life enabled the apostolic work he did.

Jesus was single. The Gospels do not, on careful reading, support the various speculative theories that have tried to argue otherwise. The singleness of Jesus is one of the most consequential facts of his historical life, and it has theological implications the church has not always honored. The redemption that Christ accomplished was accomplished by a man who lived a complete and fulfilled human life without marriage and without sexual activity. The historic Christian tradition has consistently honored this as significant. The contemporary American evangelical tradition has often treated it as an exception that does not generalize, in ways the historic tradition would not have endorsed.

Many of the women in the early church were single. Mary Magdalene, in the accounts after the resurrection, appears as a single woman. Many of the early Christian women whose names appear in Acts and in the Pauline letters — Phoebe, Lydia, Priscilla (though married, working in ministry), the various widows who became central to the work of the early church — were either unmarried, widowed, or operating in vocational capacities that the surrounding culture did not provide space for. The early Christian community honored these women in ways that the surrounding pagan culture did not, and the early Christian honoring of single women is one of the historically distinctive features of the early church.

The historic Christian tradition included whole orders of single women and men — the monastic traditions — who took singleness as their vocation and were among the most theologically formed Christians of their eras. Macrina the Younger, Hilda of Whitby, Hildegard of Bingen, Catherine of Siena, Teresa of Ávila — these are some of the most significant single Christian women in the church's history, and their work shaped the church's theology and practice across centuries. The Protestant Reformation, in its abolition of the monastic orders, recovered the dignity of marriage as a vocation but also lost the institutional honor of celibate religious life that the historic tradition had provided.

Five hundred years later, Protestantism has still not built an institutional replacement. The single Protestant Christian — particularly the single Protestant woman — often experiences the church as having no clear vocational space for her, and the absence is the institutional shape of the theological malpractice I named at the start of this essay.

## What the contemporary church gets wrong

I want to be specific about what the contemporary evangelical church has been doing that has not honored single members of the body.

The church structures its programming around the nuclear family. The classes, the small groups, the events, the messaging are aimed at married couples with children. The single member often experiences the church as an institution designed for someone else, in which her presence is welcomed but in which the basic activities of church life assume a family configuration she does not have.

The church treats singleness as transitional. The implicit and sometimes explicit messaging is that the single member is waiting for marriage, and that her current state is not her actual life but a preliminary phase. The messaging produces, over years, the sense that the single Christian's life does not have its own substance, that she is incomplete until the marriage that may or may not arrive, and that the church's interest in her is partly the interest in seeing her transition to the married state that the church understands better.

The church gives the single member the volunteer roles no one else wants. The pattern is recognizable. The single members of many congregations are the ones doing the childcare for the married members' Bible study, the ones staffing the kitchen at the church event, the ones available for the tasks the married members cannot do because of family commitments. The pattern is not always sinister; it sometimes reflects actual availability. The pattern produces, over time, the sense that the single member's value to the congregation is largely instrumental — she is useful for what she can do, rather than honored for who she is.

The church does not welcome the single member into the household life of married members in sustained ways. The single Christian who eats dinner alone several nights a week, who sleeps alone, who handles medical emergencies alone, often does not have the access to married households that the historic church's practice of hospitality would have provided. The married members are inside their own households with their own children, and the single member is outside, looking in, with limited access to the household life that scripture treats as the basic site of Christian community.

The church does not pastorally address the specific pastoral situations of single Christians. The loneliness, the grief over unrealized hopes, the financial precariousness, the medical and care challenges, the question of where the single Christian will be in old age — these are pastoral situations the church can address with specificity, and most evangelical congregations are addressing them poorly or not at all. The poor addressing is part of why many single Christians, particularly single Christian women, eventually leave the church.

## What the recovery looks like

I want to be concrete about what the recovery of the vocation of singleness would look like in practice, because the abstract conversation has been going on for decades and has produced limited change.

The church teaches singleness as a vocation. The catechetical work the church does with young adults includes the explicit teaching that singleness is one of two Christian vocations — alongside marriage — and that both are honorable, both have specific gifts and challenges, and the discernment between them is part of the Christian life. The teaching has to be specific and sustained rather than the occasional sermon mention. The teaching shapes the imagination of young Christians who are considering both possibilities, and it shapes the way the broader congregation thinks about the single members in their midst.

The church integrates single members into household life. The married Christian household that practices hospitality to the single Christians in its life — regular meals, holiday inclusion, ongoing involvement in the family's daily rhythm — is doing one of the most concrete things the church can do to honor the single member. The hospitality is not occasional or symbolic. The hospitality is sustained and substantial, in ways that recognize the single Christian as a permanent member of the household's extended community rather than as a temporary guest. The historic Christian household assumed this. The contemporary nuclear household generally does not. The recovery requires the household to expand its sense of who belongs.

The church creates structures of community among single members. The historic monastic orders provided community for single Christians at the deepest possible level — shared life, shared work, shared worship, shared property. The Protestant tradition cannot replicate the monastic structure, but it can provide intermediate forms — sustained small groups specifically for single members, shared housing arrangements where single Christians live together for periods of their lives, intentional friendship structures that the church helps to facilitate. The structures should be substantial rather than token. They should address the real needs single members have for community, in ways that the standard congregational small-group structure often does not.

The church creates pastoral care that addresses the specific situations of single members. The loneliness, the grief, the financial questions, the care planning for the older single Christian — these deserve explicit pastoral attention. The pastor who can speak honestly with single members of his congregation about these specific situations is doing the pastoral work that most congregations have been failing to do.

The church honors the work single members can do. Single Christians often have time and energy that married Christians with young children do not. Single Christians can serve in ways married members cannot. The church that honors this work — sermons that recognize the contributions of single members, leadership opportunities that do not assume family configurations, the genuine receiving of the gifts single Christians bring — is doing the recognition that scripture requires and that the contemporary church has often failed to do.

## What the single Christian can do

I want to address single Christian readers directly, because the recovery of the vocation is partly the work of single Christians themselves making claims on the church's pastoral and institutional resources.

Name the vocation as a vocation. The single Christian who has been treating his own singleness as a problem to be solved, as a waiting period, as the prelude to the real life he expects to begin when marriage arrives — this Christian has, in important ways, accepted the church's bad framing. The recovery begins with the single Christian's own theological reframing. Read Paige Benton Brown's classic essay *Singled Out for Good*. Read Sam Allberry's *7 Myths About Singleness*. Read the historic vocational literature on singleness. Treat your own singleness as one of two Christian vocations rather than as the absence of marriage. The reframing is for your own formation as much as for the church's.

Make claims on the church's pastoral resources. The pastor who has not been giving you specific pastoral attention as a single member should be asked for it. The married households that have not been welcoming you into their household life should be asked. The structures of community for single members that do not yet exist in your congregation should be advocated for. The advocacy is part of the recovery, and the recovery requires single Christians who are willing to make claims rather than accepting the institutional invisibility that has been the standard treatment.

Live the vocation with substance. The single Christian's life is not provisional. It is the actual life God has given. The substance — the work, the friendships, the household practices, the engagement with scripture and prayer, the service to the church and the surrounding community — is real, is honorable, and is the substance of the vocation. The living of it with substance is itself part of the church's recovery, because the living of it shows the church what the vocation actually looks like.

## The verdict

The single Christian in your church is not a project. She is a sister, a colleague, a saint, a vocation. The church that treats her as transitional has misunderstood the gospel — which has, since Paul, recognized singleness as one of the two vocations of Christian life and refused to rank them.

I am writing this for the single Christian reader who has been carrying the church's inadequate framing for years, and for the married Christian reader who has been participating in the inadequate framing without examining it. The recovery is overdue. The recovery has resources in the historic Christian tradition and in scripture itself. The recovery requires both single and married Christians to make the recovery concrete in particular congregations.

Single Christians: make claims on your church's pastoral resources. Married Christians: open your households. Pastors: catechize the vocation explicitly. The recovery is the work the next generation of faithful churches will be built on, and the work begins this week, in particular conversations and particular households and particular pastoral choices. Begin. The vocation has been honored in the church for two thousand years. The contemporary American evangelical context has, on the whole, dishonored it. The dishonoring stops here, in particular congregations that decide to honor the vocation again.
