## 37. Your Children Will Inherit Your Doubts Before They Inherit Your Certainty

**Slug:** `your-children-will-inherit-your-doubts-before-they-inherit-your-certainty`
**Track:** `parenting` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** The faith you actually hold — the parts you actually believe in your body, not the parts you say in catechism — is the faith your children will inherit. Your doubts go down with the rest. Pretending otherwise has cost two generations of evangelical kids their faith.

**Argument beats:**
1. The Christian Smith research (*Soul Searching*; *Souls in Transition*): the strongest predictor of a young adult's faith is the *practiced* faith of their parents — not the verbal faith, the practiced one.
2. The category error most evangelical parenting makes: teach the answers, drill the catechism, attend the church, and the faith will transmit. This works if the practiced faith of the parents matches the catechism. It does not work if the practiced faith and the catechism diverge.
3. What the practiced faith is: the things you actually rely on, the things you actually fear, the things you actually love, the way you actually treat your spouse and your money and your enemies. Your children are watching this, not your sermons.
4. The doubts get inherited too — but with a twist. Inherited doubt that is acknowledged becomes the child's own honest faith. Inherited doubt that is hidden becomes the child's secret crisis.
5. The constructive move: tell your kids what you actually doubt. Tell them what you actually believe. Tell them the reasons you stay in the faith despite the doubt. This is the formation, not the formation that lives in the curriculum.
6. Self-implication: I have doubted publicly to my sons. The public doubting has been formative for them in ways the curriculum was not.
7. The verdict: stop pretending. Your kids already know. The kids who lose faith mostly lose faith because the family pretended to a certainty no one was actually holding.

**Closing weight:** Your children will inherit your faith if your faith is real. Your children will inherit your doubts if your doubts are honored. Your children will inherit your pretense if you choose pretense, and they will not return to faith until the pretense is dismantled — usually in their own children's lives, two generations later. Choose honesty. The honesty is the formation. The pretense is the loss.

**Sources:** Christian Smith (*Soul Searching*; *Souls in Transition*); Vern Bengtson (*Families and Faith*); James K.A. Smith (*Desiring the Kingdom*); the work of Sharon Galgay Ketcham.

## 38. The Discipline That Is Not Punishment

**Slug:** `the-discipline-that-is-not-punishment`
**Track:** `parenting` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~10 min

**Positioning sentence:** American evangelical parenting has conflated discipline with punishment for two generations. The conflation has produced anxious children and exhausted parents. The recovery of the distinction is one of the most important things parents can do.

**Argument beats:**
1. The biblical word for discipline (*paideia*) is the same word ancient Greeks used for the long formation of a citizen. It means formation. It does not mean punishment.
2. Hebrews 12: discipline is what fathers do to their sons because they love them. The discipline in view is not behavioral correction. It is the long formation of character.
3. The American evangelical pattern: behavioral control mistaken for character formation. The spanked child who learned not to hit a sibling has not become a non-violent person. They have become an externally-controlled person.
4. What actual formation looks like: presence, conversation, modeling, repair after rupture, naming the deeper thing under the behavior, refusing to make compliance the metric.
5. Concrete: the difference between *"Stop hitting your brother"* and *"You hit your brother because you were angry that he took your toy. Anger is a real thing. Hitting is not how we handle it. Here is what we do instead. Let me show you."* Both correct the behavior. Only one forms the person.
6. The cost of the punishment-as-formation model: a generation of evangelical kids who left the church because they had been managed into compliance and had never been formed in love.
7. The verdict: discipline is formation. Punishment is sometimes a small part of discipline. It is not the whole. Treating it as the whole has cost too much already.

**Closing weight:** The discipline your children remember at thirty-five is not the discipline you delivered in anger. It is the slow, patient, repeated work of being formed in a household where character was honored, where rupture was repaired, where mistakes were occasions for formation rather than occasions for shame. That work is most of what parenting is. The behavioral pieces are the smallest part. Get the formation right. The behavior follows.

**Sources:** Tim Kimmel (*Grace-Based Parenting*); Paul Tripp (*Parenting*); Karyn Purvis (*The Connected Child*); Daniel Siegel (*The Whole-Brain Child*); the work of L.R. Knost.

## 39. The Family Worship That Survives Adolescence

**Slug:** `the-family-worship-that-survives-adolescence`
**Track:** `parenting` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~9 min

**Positioning sentence:** Family worship — the daily practice of prayer and scripture inside the household — is the practice that survives the rest. Most evangelical families have lost it. Recovering it is the highest-leverage parenting work available.

**Argument beats:**
1. The historical practice: the daily family altar. Father, mother, children. Scripture read. Prayer prayed. This was nearly universal in Protestant households for three hundred years. It has nearly disappeared in two generations.
2. Why it disappeared: the busyness story (insufficient; we have always been busy); the embarrassment story (more accurate — many parents stopped because they did not feel qualified to lead it); the privatization story (most accurate — devotion became individual rather than household).
3. The cost of losing it: the family with no liturgy of its own is the family whose only liturgies are the cultural ones. The kids absorb whichever liturgy is more consistent. Smartphones beat unprayed evenings every time.
4. The constructive: family worship does not have to be elaborate. Five minutes. A psalm. A short prayer. The Lord's Prayer with everyone. The children's evening prayer from the historic prayer books. The point is the *rhythm*, not the depth.
5. The adolescent challenge: the practice that began when kids were small has to survive when kids are sullen, busy, embarrassed. The way it survives is by being non-negotiable in the same way meals are non-negotiable — not "if everyone wants to," but "this is what we do in this household."
6. Self-implication: I lost family worship in our home for years. Recovering it has been one of the harder and more rewarding things I have done as a father.
7. The verdict: get the practice back. Five minutes a day. It will form your children in ways the church alone cannot.

**Closing weight:** The household is the smallest unit of the church and the most important. The household that prays is the household that forms. The household that does not pray is the household that is being formed by whatever it does instead — and what it does instead is usually screens. Recover the practice. Do not be embarrassed by simplicity. Five minutes a day, for eighteen years, is two hundred and seventy hours of liturgy. That is more formation than the church can offer in the same period. Use it.

**Sources:** Donald Whitney (*Family Worship*); Richard Baxter (*The Reformed Pastor*; *The Christian Directory*, sections on family); the Book of Common Prayer's family devotions; Tish Harrison Warren (*Liturgy of the Ordinary*); James K.A. Smith.

## 40. The Parenting Books That Will Make You Worse

**Slug:** `the-parenting-books-that-will-make-you-worse`
**Track:** `parenting` · **Audience:** `individuals` · **Reading time:** ~8 min

**Positioning sentence:** Most evangelical parenting books are based on a theology of parenting that is bad theology and bad parenting. Reading them is making you worse. Stop. Here is what to read instead.

**Argument beats:**
1. The genre. The "biblical parenting" book industry. The promise: follow these steps and produce godly children. The premise is wrong. Parenting is not a recipe. Children are not the products of techniques.
2. The theological problem: the books make God's love conditional in the parents' modeling. *Behave, and you will be loved.* This is not the gospel. Children raised on this theology of parenting are being given a false god.
3. The empirical problem: the books promise results — godly children — that the books cannot deliver. The kids who turned out faithful did so partly because of and partly despite their parents, and the variables are not what the books say they are.
4. What to read instead: the older Christian tradition on parenting. Augustine's *Confessions* on his mother Monica. Calvin's *Institutes* on the household. The historic Reformed catechisms designed for household use. Eugene Peterson on the family as a *theological practice*, not a *behavioral project*.
5. Modern reading that does not lie: Andy Crouch on technology and the family; Justin and Lindsey Holcomb on attachment; Karen Purvis; David Bayly on the Reformed family.
6. The hardest truth: there is no formula. Parenting is unrepeatable, unpredictable, ungeneralizable. The books that promise repeatability are lying.
7. The verdict: throw out the formula books. Read the older books. Pray. Stay. The formula was always going to fail you.

**Closing weight:** The good parenting book is the book that teaches you to parent the specific child in front of you. There is not a generic version of that child. There is not a generic parenting method that will form them. The faithful parent is the parent who is being formed by Christ alongside the child — both of them being made into the people they were not yet, in a household that has agreed to be a school of the gospel rather than a factory for compliant children. Throw out the recipe. Keep the gospel. Pray a lot.

**Sources:** Augustine (*Confessions*, Book I); Calvin (*Institutes* IV.16.32); Eugene Peterson (*The Pastor*, chapter on family); the Westminster Larger Catechism's exposition of the fifth commandment; Andy Crouch (*The Tech-Wise Family*); Karyn Purvis.
