I was raised without a father. I have spent twenty-two years as a Christian working out what the doctrine of God the Father means under that condition. I want to write this essay for the American men whose relationship with their fathers shaped, in ways most of them have never explicitly examined, their relationship with the God scripture calls Father. The shaping is real. The shaping is mostly invisible. The shaping is one of the most urgent pastoral conditions in the American church, and almost no contemporary pastoral writing addresses it at the level it needs to be addressed.

This essay is for the man whose father was absent, whose father was present but emotionally absent, whose father was harsh, whose father was complicit in family patterns the man has been recovering from for decades. The doctrine of God the Father has, for this man, a different texture than the doctrine has for the man whose father was warmly present. The texture matters. The pastoral work has to acknowledge the texture rather than pretend it does not exist.

## The data

The American father-absence crisis is real and is well-documented. Approximately one in four American children grows up in a household without their biological father present. The figure is higher among certain demographic groups and lower among others, but the overall pattern of significant father absence is consistent across the past several decades.

The father-absence figure does not capture the larger reality, though. The presence of a biological father in the household is not the same as the presence of an emotionally engaged, available, formative father. The category of *under-fathered* — children whose fathers were physically present but emotionally absent, or who were intermittently present, or who were present in ways that wounded rather than formed — is much larger than the strict father-absence category. The best estimates suggest that the majority of American men in adulthood report some significant form of father-related deficit in their developmental history. The deficit shapes how these men handle their own fatherhood, their work, their relationships, and — relevant to this essay — their religious imagination.

## What the wound does to the doctrine

The Christian doctrine of God the Father is foundational. The Trinity itself is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Lord's Prayer begins *Our Father*. The Christian baptism is in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The fatherhood of God is not optional vocabulary for the Christian tradition; it is the central language by which the tradition has understood God's relationship with God's people.

The doctrine has a particular pastoral problem in contemporary America. The doctrine assumes that the word *father* carries certain freight — the warm, present, protective, providing, loving father — and uses that freight to communicate something about God. The communication works to the extent that the freight is intact. The communication does not work, or works in distorted ways, to the extent that the freight has been damaged.

For the man whose father was absent, the word *father* may carry the freight of absence, of inadequacy, of longing for someone who was not there. When the doctrine says *God the Father*, this man hears the word through the lens of his own father absence, and the doctrine lands differently than it lands for the man whose father was warmly present. The doctrine may communicate, despite its intentions, *another absent father*, *another disappointment*, *another presence I cannot count on*.

For the man whose father was harsh, the word *father* may carry the freight of fear, of unpredictable anger, of impossible standards. When the doctrine says *God the Father*, this man hears the word through the lens of his own harsh father, and the doctrine may communicate *another harsh authority*, *another presence to be feared*, *another standard I cannot meet*.

For the man whose father was complicit in family patterns that wounded the family — absent through addiction, present but contemptuous, sexually unfaithful, abusive in various ways — the word *father* may carry the freight of betrayal. When the doctrine says *God the Father*, this man hears the word through the lens of his own betrayal, and the doctrine may communicate *another figure who promises and does not deliver*.

The pastoral problem is not abstract. The pastoral problem is that an enormous portion of the American male population has been hearing the central Christian doctrine through lenses that distort what the doctrine is trying to say. The distortion is invisible to the men themselves in most cases. The distortion is part of why the men who have wounded relationships with their fathers often have, despite their best intentions, wounded relationships with the God scripture calls Father — relationships marked by suspicion, distance, a sense that the God being described is too good to be true or too dangerous to approach, an inability to receive what the doctrine is actually offering.

## The Augustinian move

I want to introduce a move from Augustine that has been one of the most important pastoral resources I have found for this condition. Augustine begins his *Confessions* with an address to God, and the address is unmistakable. God is the one who has been more truly Father to Augustine than Augustine's actual father was. Augustine's father, Patricius, was not a Christian for most of Augustine's childhood. Patricius was unfaithful to Augustine's mother Monica. Patricius died when Augustine was a teenager. The father Augustine had was not the father the developmental literature would describe as adequate.

Augustine's *Confessions* makes a startling theological move. Augustine does not require the doctrine of God the Father to first be filtered through an intact human-father analogy. Augustine recognizes that the human-father analogy is broken in his own life, and lets the doctrine of God the Father correct and complete the broken analogy rather than depending on it. The God who is Father is, for Augustine, what the human father was supposed to be and failed to be. The doctrine is not waiting for the human-father analogy to land before it can do its work. The doctrine does its work directly, in the soul that receives it, and the receiving heals the analogy rather than depending on it.

This is the resource. The doctrine of God the Father is not waiting on the integrity of your relationship with your earthly father before it can address you. The doctrine is, in fact, what addresses the wound your relationship with your earthly father produced. The Father in the Trinity is not the father you had. The Father in the Trinity is the Father your father failed to be. The healing comes from the doctrine, not from any human relationship; the doctrine is, in some real sense, the means by which God provides the fathering that your earthly father did not.

I want to be careful about how I phrase this, because the move can sound dismissive of the real grief of father loss or father failure. The move is not dismissive. The grief is real and should be honored. The grief should be processed, in many cases with therapy and with spiritual direction and with sustained pastoral care. The doctrine is not a substitute for the grief work. The doctrine is what the grief work eventually opens up — the recognition that you have been fathered, in the deepest sense, by the God who is Father, and that the relationship with God the Father is the deeper fathering that the wounded human relationship was always supposed to point toward and never could.

## What this means pastorally

I want to give specific pastoral counsel, because the abstract theological move can be hard to apply in practice.

The first move is naming. The man who has not explicitly named the relationship between his father wound and his relationship with God the Father has been carrying the connection unconsciously for years. The naming makes the connection visible, which is the first step in addressing it. The naming sounds something like this. *My father was absent. I have been hearing God the Father through the lens of my father's absence. I have been suspicious of God's presence in ways I have not explicitly examined. I want to name the connection.*

The second move is grief. The grief work for the father wound is real work. It usually involves therapy. It involves the slow process of acknowledging what was lost, what should have been there and was not, what cannot be replaced and what can be repaired. The grief is not solved by Christian conversion; the grief continues to require attention long after the conversion. The men I have known who have done this work in the deepest ways have generally combined spiritual direction with professional therapy, in ways that addressed both the theological and the psychological dimensions of the wound.

The third move is the doctrinal recovery. The doctrine of God the Father has resources that the wounded man can access once he has begun the naming and the grief. The doctrine includes the warmth of the prodigal-son's father in Luke 15, who runs to meet the returning son while he is still far off. The doctrine includes the patience of the Father who has been forming Christ in his disciples for two thousand years. The doctrine includes the protection of the Father who, in Romans 8, has predestined his children to be conformed to the image of his Son and will see the conforming through to completion. The doctrine is rich. The wounded man often needs help receiving it, and the helping is part of what pastors and spiritual directors are for.

The fourth move is the participation. The man who has been wounded by his earthly father often becomes a father himself. The participation in fatherhood — the imperfect, ongoing, demanding work of being a father to his own children — is part of how the doctrine of God the Father becomes integrated rather than abstract. I have written elsewhere in this writing project about what five sons taught me about God the Father. The participation in fatherhood is one of the means by which the doctrine becomes real for the man who has been wounded. The participation also produces, over years, the kind of human father that the next generation will not have to recover from.

## The verdict

The Father who could not love you and the Father who can are two different beings. The first is the human father, with his failures and his finitude. The second is the eternal Father, who is what the human father was always supposed to point to and never could. Get to know the second. The first will be redeemed in the knowing. There is no other path.

I am writing this for the American man whose relationship with his father has shaped, in ways he has not yet examined, his relationship with God. The shaping is normal. The shaping is also addressable. The naming is the beginning. The grief is the middle. The doctrinal recovery is the path through. The participation in fatherhood, for the men who become fathers themselves, is part of how the integration happens over decades.

I came to faith from atheism partly through this work. The God I had been refusing was the God I had constructed from the inadequate father analogy I had inherited. The God who actually addressed me was the God of the doctrine — the Father who was what my earthly father had failed to be, who had been fathering me through my own absent years even when I did not know it, who was waiting for me to receive what he had been giving. The receiving was hard. The receiving was real. The receiving is what made the rest of the Christian life possible for me.

I am still finding out what the doctrine actually contains. The finding out is most of what the contemplative life is. The finding out is for you too, if you have been carrying the father wound and have not yet recognized how it has been shaping your relationship with God. Begin. The doctrine has been waiting. The Father has been there the whole time.
