The question rarely comes from the pulpit's enemies. It comes from the people who love you. A faithful elder, a deacon who has stood by you through two building campaigns, a member who tithes and never misses a Sunday and weeps in the prayer meeting — one of them catches you after the service, lowers his voice, and asks the question that is really a request: *Pastor, where do you stand on this?* He does not want analysis. He wants to know you are with him. And the warmth in his face when he asks, the trust, the assumption that of course a good and godly man sees it the way he sees it — that is the thing that makes the refusal so hard. To decline is to disappoint someone who came to you in good faith, looking for an ally and finding, instead, a pastor.

That is the cost, and it is worth naming plainly before we pretend the calling is easy. **To refuse a tribe in an age that has made tribe the meaning of life is to be, by turns, distrusted by everyone — and the people who distrust you will be the ones you love most.** The nonpartisan pastor is not the neutral pastor, and he is not the cowardly pastor, though he will be accused of both. He is something the age has almost no category for: a man whose deepest loyalty has already been spent, so that he has none left to sell to either side.

A caution belongs here, because there is a counterfeit of this calling that I have seen in my own heart. There is a nonpartisanship that is really just conflict-avoidance dressed in robes — a refusal to say anything because saying things is frightening and losing people is painful. That is not the calling. That is cowardice wearing the calling's clothes. The genuine article is louder and costlier than the counterfeit, because the genuine article does take sides — constantly, fiercely — on everything Scripture takes a side on. It simply refuses to let the world's two teams draw the map.

## Jesus prayed for the one thing the tribe destroys

On the night he was betrayed, with the cross hours away, Jesus prayed for us by name — not for our success, not for our comfort, but for our oneness. *I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me* (John 17:20–21). Read the last clause again, because it is the part we skip. The oneness of his people is not a nice internal arrangement. It is the evidence — *so that the world may believe.* Jesus stakes the credibility of his own mission on whether his people can be one across the lines that divide everyone else.

The partisan capture of the church is therefore not a political problem with spiritual side effects. It is a direct assault on the apologetic Jesus himself chose. When a congregation fractures along the same lines as the cable broadcast, the world looks on and concludes, reasonably, that the gospel is not strong enough to do what it claims — that it cannot, in fact, make one new people out of enemies, that it is just another flavor of the tribalism running everything else. Paul says the same thing to a Corinthian church already splintering into factions: *I appeal to you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you agree, and that there be no divisions among you* (1 Corinthians 1:10). *Is Christ divided?* (1 Corinthians 1:13). The factions in Corinth were not even political — they were loyalties to rival teachers, Paul and Apollos and Cephas. If Paul found *that* intolerable, the partisan sorting of the modern church would have undone him.

The pastor who will not pick a tribe is the one trying to keep visible the unity for which Christ prayed. He is not standing above the fray out of fastidiousness. He is guarding the one piece of evidence Jesus said the watching world would weigh.

## But unity is not the avoidance of confrontation

Now the correction, because the previous point is where the cowardice hides. If nonpartisanship meant never confronting anyone, we would have to throw out one of the strangest and most instructive episodes in the New Testament. Paul tells the Galatians, with no embarrassment, that he opposed Peter — Cephas, the chief apostle — to his face. *When Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned* (Galatians 2:11). Peter had been eating with Gentile believers, and then, when men from the circumcision party arrived, he drew back and separated himself out of fear (Galatians 2:12). It was, in our terms, a capture — Peter let the pressure of a faction override the truth of the gospel, and the others, even Barnabas, were carried along by it.

Paul did not preserve unity by staying quiet. He confronted the most important man in the church publicly, because the issue was not a disputable matter. It was *the truth of the gospel* (Galatians 2:14) — whether the table of Christ could be divided along the lines the world drew. That is the line the nonpartisan pastor must learn to see. There are matters on which silence is faithfulness, and there are matters on which silence is the betrayal. When a faction pressures the church to draw its table along tribal lines — when belonging starts to depend on the right vote rather than the one baptism — the pastor who stays quiet is not being unifying. He is being Peter, withdrawing out of fear of the circumcision party, and he needs a Paul to oppose him to his face.

The skill, and it is the central skill of this calling, is telling the two situations apart. The world wants you to treat every political question as gospel truth, because then it can conscript you. The coward wants to treat every question as a disputable matter, because then he never has to confront anyone. The faithful pastor does the slow, costly work of discernment — confronting like Paul where the gospel is genuinely at stake, and bearing with one another in love where it is not. He takes sides on the cross. He refuses to take sides on the tax code. And he will be hated by people who cannot tell the difference, which in this age is nearly everyone.

## The temptations come dressed as virtues

Watch how the pressure actually arrives, because it never presents itself as a request to compromise. It presents itself as a call to courage. The faction that wants you on its side does not say *abandon your impartiality*; it says *the times are too serious for fence-sitting, and silence is itself a side.* That sentence is half true, which is what makes it dangerous. There are matters on which silence is a side, and the faithful pastor must say so. But the faction extends that true principle over everything, until every contested policy becomes a hill the gospel supposedly dies on, and a man who declines to plant his flag on all of them is branded a coward or a compromiser or, the favorite word now, a wolf.

Both sides run the same play with the same vocabulary, which is the tell. To the captured progressive, the nonpartisan pastor is complicit, his neutrality a quiet endorsement of injustice. To the captured conservative, the same pastor is worldly, his restraint a failure of nerve, evidence that he fears man more than God. They cannot both be right about him, and in fact neither is, because both have made the identical error: they have assumed that the only reason a man would refuse their tribe is weakness, when the actual reason is that he answers to a louder voice than theirs. The pastor who can hold his ground under both accusations at once — coward to one camp, sellout to the other, in the same week, over the same sermon — is usually standing exactly where he should be. The crossfire is not proof he is wrong. It is frequently the only evidence available that he is not captured.

## The vision that makes the loneliness bearable

The reason a pastor can endure being the last nonpartisan in the room is that he has seen the room that is coming. John saw it. *After this I looked, and behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb* (Revelation 7:9). Every tribe. The word is *tribe*, and it is doing enormous work. The final gathering of the redeemed is the abolition of the tribe as the unit of belonging — not by erasing the nations, for they are named and present, but by subordinating every one of them to a loyalty that runs deeper than blood or border or party. The robes are white. The palm branches are in every hand. No one is checking jerseys at the throne.

**The pastor who will not pick a tribe is simply living early in the only world that finally lasts.** That is not a sentimental thought. It is a tactical one. The partisan church is betting on a kingdom that has an expiration date stamped on it — every party, every nation, every coalition that has ever organized human hope is going to dissolve before that throne. To pour your pastoral authority into one of them is to invest your life in a thing the book of Revelation has already buried. To keep the table open to every tribe, here and now, is to practice the posture of heaven against the grain of a culture that finds it incomprehensible. You will look, for a while, like a man with no loyalties. You are a man whose loyalty is simply spent somewhere the others cannot see yet.

## The counterfeit and the real thing, one more time

Because the cowardice counterfeit is so close to the genuine calling, it is worth a second look at how to tell them apart in your own chest, where it matters most. The two can produce identical behavior on the outside — the same refusal to name a party from the pulpit, the same measured silence when a member fishes for an ally. From the pew they look the same. From the inside they are opposites, and the difference is what you are protecting.

The coward is protecting himself. His silence guards his comfort, his attendance numbers, his freedom from the hard conversation, and if you press him on a genuine gospel matter where Scripture has clearly spoken, he will stay quiet there too, because his real loyalty is to the absence of conflict. The faithful nonpartisan is protecting the flock and the gospel, and the proof is that he is perfectly willing to bleed for them. He will absorb a confrontation Paul-style when the truth of the gospel is at stake, will lose the member, will take the accusation, will say the costly thing on the matters Scripture has settled — and only then refuse to be conscripted on the matters it has not. The coward avoids all conflict; the faithful man chooses his conflicts by a standard outside himself and accepts the cost of the ones he chooses. If your nonpartisanship has never once cost you anything, it is probably the counterfeit. The real thing bleeds, regularly, and from both sides.

## What it costs, named honestly

I will not pretend the vision dissolves the loneliness. It does not. Eugene Peterson wrote often about the isolation of the pastoral vocation — the way the work removes you from the easy belonging everyone else takes for granted, the way you are present at the center of the community and yet never quite of any of its factions. The nonpartisan stance deepens that isolation in a polarized age, because the one thing both sides offer is belonging, and you are declining both offers. You will sit at tables where the conversation assumes you agree, and your silence will be noticed, and the warmth will cool a degree. You will lose members who decide a pastor who will not say it must secretly be the enemy. You will be called weak by the brave and worldly by the pure, and some weeks you will not be sure they are wrong.

There is a particular grief reserved for the nonpartisan pastor that the partisan one never tastes, and it is the grief of being misunderstood by the people you have served the longest. The member who leaves over a single political stance you declined to take does not remember the decade of hospital visits, the funerals you preached for his family, the marriage you helped hold together. He remembers only that, in the moment he wanted you to be his, you would not be. That is a real loss, and pretending it does not wound is its own kind of dishonesty. Paul knew it; he asked the Galatians, who would once have torn out their own eyes to give him, *have I then become your enemy by telling you the truth* (Galatians 4:16). The answer, often, is yes. Telling the truth makes enemies of friends, and a pastor who refuses a tribe will collect those enemies one beloved face at a time.

Here is what holds. You did not take this stand to be liked by both sides; you took it because you answer to One. The flock is not finally yours to please or to lose. It is the Lamb's, bought at a price you did not pay, gathered from every tribe by a shepherd who himself was driven outside every camp and crucified between two thieves precisely so that no camp could claim him. To pastor like that — outside the camps, loyal past all of them, lonely in the particular way he was lonely — is not a failure of belonging. It is the shape his belonging took. The last nonpartisan in the room is not the man who belongs to no one. He is the man who already belongs, entirely, to the only one whose verdict will outlast the room. Everyone else is still auditioning for a tribe that will not be standing when the multitude no one can number finally lifts its palms.
