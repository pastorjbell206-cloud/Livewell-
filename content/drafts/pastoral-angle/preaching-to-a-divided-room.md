Somewhere around the third sentence of the sermon, the room sorts itself. You can feel it happen. A phrase lands and one section of the congregation leans in, recognizing an ally, while another section pulls back a half inch, bracing for betrayal. You have not said anything political yet. You said the word *justice*, or the word *order*, or you named the poor, or you named the unborn, and the room did the math before you finished the clause. They are not listening to your argument. They are listening for your jersey.

This is the condition of preaching now, and the temptations it produces are two, and they are opposite, and most of us oscillate between them. The first is to choose a side — to give the half of the room that already agrees the satisfaction of hearing you say it, because the warmth coming back at you feels, in the moment, like effectiveness. The second is to go silent — to preach only the safe, internal, spiritualized text, to wall off whole books of the Bible because they will be heard as a vote, until your preaching has nothing to say about anything anyone is actually afraid of. **One temptation makes you a chaplain to a tribe. The other makes you a chaplain to no one. Both are a dereliction, and both feel like wisdom from the inside.**

I have done both. I have preached the sermon that flattered my own instincts and called it courage, and I have ducked a text I should have preached and called it prudence. The work I am describing is not work I have mastered. It is work I am still learning to do without flinching toward either ditch.

## The whole counsel is not optional, and it was never safe

Paul, saying goodbye to the Ephesian elders, makes a claim about his own preaching that should unsettle every one of us who has trimmed a sermon to keep the peace: *I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God* (Acts 20:27). The whole counsel. Not the comfortable counsel. Not the half that plays well with the donors. The whole of it — which means the parts that comfort the afflicted and the parts that afflict the comfortable, the texts that wound the left and the texts that wound the right, the God who defends the widow and the orphan and the immigrant and the God who will by no means clear the guilty.

The shrinking Paul refused to do is the shrinking we have perfected. We have learned to preach a Bible with the political nerve removed — a private gospel about my heart and my anxiety and my personal walk, carefully sealed off from anything that might be overheard as a position. We tell ourselves this is keeping the main thing the main thing. It is not. It is editing God down to the size of a room that will not be offended, and a God who cannot offend the room is not the God of Amos or Jeremiah or the carpenter who turned over the tables.

The instruction to the young pastor is famous and we have domesticated it: *preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching* (2 Timothy 4:2). Read it slowly. *Reprove. Rebuke.* Those are not gentle words. They are the words of a man who expects his preaching to cut, who knows that *out of season* means the times when the cutting is unwelcome, which is exactly when the temptation to shrink is strongest. The verse that follows is the one about itching ears — about people accumulating teachers who tell them what they already want. Paul sets the whole counsel directly against the itching ear. To preach the whole counsel is, by definition, to tell the room something it did not come to hear.

## Brueggemann and the difference between a memo and a poem

Walter Brueggemann gave us the language for why so much of our political preaching fails even when it is brave. In *Finally Comes the Poet* (1989), he argued that the preacher's task is not to deliver prose to a settled world but to speak a poem into a world that has gone numb — to crack open the reduced, flattened imagination of people who have stopped expecting God to do anything. The prophets, he said, did not win by stating the obvious more loudly. They won by reframing reality so completely that the listener saw his own life inside a story he had been refusing to see.

This is the key the divided room hands us, if we will take it. The reason a congregation hears politics is that the politicians and the broadcasters got there first and supplied the only frame the people have. They handed your members a reduced map — a flat world of two teams, where every question has a red answer and a blue answer and nothing else. When you preach in prose against that map, naming positions and taking sides, you are playing on their board, and you will be scored by their rules. But when you preach as the poet Brueggemann describes — when you open the strange, larger world of the text until the familiar fight looks small inside it — you are not taking a side in their argument. You are dissolving the frame that made the argument seem like the whole of reality.

**The prophet does not win the room's argument. He changes what the room is able to see, until the argument it walked in with looks like a small thing said in a large house.** That is not evasion. It is the harder and more biblical thing. Amos did not endorse a party. He pulled the roof off the comfortable house and let his hearers see themselves trampling the poor in the dust while keeping their festivals scrupulously. He did not give them a position. He gave them a mirror, and the mirror was political precisely because it was not partisan.

## Nathan did not lead with the policy

The model for all of this is one of the most disciplined acts of preaching in Scripture, and it is barely four verses long. David has taken another man's wife and arranged another man's death, and he is sitting in unbothered certainty as the most powerful man in the kingdom. Nathan walks in. He has every right to lead with the indictment. He does not. *There were two men in a certain city, the one rich and the other poor* (2 Samuel 12:1). He tells a story — a poem, in Brueggemann's sense — about a rich man who slaughters a poor man's beloved lamb to feed a guest. And David, the captured king, the man whose own guilt has made him unable to see straight, renders judgment on the man in the story without realizing he is sentencing himself. *The man who has done this deserves to die.* And then the four words that every preacher to a divided room should have tattooed somewhere he can see them: *You are the man* (2 Samuel 12:7).

Study what Nathan did, because it is everything. He did not open with David's name. If he had, David's defenses would have closed like a gate, the way your congregation's defenses close the instant they think they know which team you are on. Instead Nathan got David to agree to a principle — to render a true verdict — while David still believed the verdict was about someone else. He let the king condemn himself before the king knew he was the defendant. By the time *you are the man* lands, David cannot retreat to his tribe, because he has already pronounced sentence with his own mouth.

There is a detail worth pausing on, because it guards against turning Nathan into a mere technique. Nathan could afford to be indirect because he was not indirect about the conclusion. Once David had condemned himself, Nathan did not soften it. He named the sin, named the man, and delivered the consequence without flinching — *you are the man.* The story was a way in, not a way out. We sometimes mistake the parabolic method for permission to never land the point, to leave the room comfortably wondering and call the ambiguity humility. That is not what Nathan did. The indirection bought him a hearing; the directness, once he had it, spent that hearing on the truth. A preacher who has learned the first half and skipped the second has learned to be interesting and forgotten to be faithful. The frame opens the door. Then someone has to walk through it carrying the verdict.

That is preaching to a divided room. You do not announce the application your hearers are braced to reject. You build the principle on ground they will gladly stand on, you let them agree to the standard while they still imagine it is meant for the other side, and only then do you turn the standard back on the agreeing heart. *You are the man.* Not them. You. The point of the whole maneuver is that the indictment must land on the listener and not on his enemy, because the moment it lands on his enemy, he is comforted instead of convicted, and you have preached him deeper into his capture rather than out of it.

## Self-implication is the only thing that buys you the right to wound

Here is the discipline that holds the whole thing together, and it is the one I most often fail. You may only wound the room from inside the wound. Nathan was a prophet, not a participant in David's sin — but the long tradition of prophetic preaching that he begins runs straight to a preacher who could say *we have all sinned*, who stood in the indictment alongside the indicted. The pulpit that points down at the people, or out at the absent enemy, has forfeited its authority before it opens its mouth. The pulpit that says *we did this, I did this, I am the man too* has earned the right to cut, because the knife is already in its own side.

Eugene Peterson warned against the preacher who becomes a performer, who learns to read the room and give it what produces the best response. The applause of the half that agrees with you is the most dangerous sound in ministry, because it feels exactly like fruitfulness and is usually its opposite. If your preaching reliably comforts one political tribe and reliably angers the other, you are not preaching the whole counsel. You are preaching a tribe's gospel with the tribe's God, and you have become, in Peterson's terms, a religious shopkeeper selling the customers what they came in wanting. The test of whole-counsel preaching is brutally simple: over a year, does every section of your divided room get wounded, and does every section get comforted, and can no one predict which Sunday will be which from your politics. If they can predict it, you have a tribe. If they cannot, you may have a pulpit.

## Preach to the fear, not the position

The last thing, and the most pastoral. The position your member holds is the surface. Underneath nearly every hardened political conviction in your congregation is a fear, and the fear is where the gospel actually does its work. The man who has gone rigid about the border is often a father terrified for his children's future. The woman who has gone rigid the other way is often a daughter who has seen real cruelty and cannot bear to see it ignored. Preach to the position and you get a fight. Preach to the fear and you get a hearing, because you have named the thing they could not say out loud, and you have offered it the only thing large enough to hold it — a God who is sovereign over the future they are frightened of, and who has already absorbed the cruelty they cannot abide, in his own body, on a Friday.

There is a practical discipline that protects all of this, and it is one of editing rather than eloquence. Before a sermon that touches anything contested, I have learned to ask one question of the manuscript: could a thoughtful member of either tribe walk out convinced I was secretly preaching for the other one. If the answer is no — if one side will clearly hear an ally and the other an opponent — I have not yet preached the text. I have preached a position and hung a verse on it. The whole counsel does not let either side feel safe, and it does not let either side feel attacked from outside; it implicates the whole room, because the Word implicates the whole room, and the preacher's job is to keep his own thumb off the scale long enough for the text to do its own dividing. The sword is sharp enough. It does not need my politics to give it an edge, and every time I lend it mine, I dull the edge that was already there.

This is also why the same passage must be allowed to cut different people on different Sundays. A congregation that has learned your pattern — that knows the prophetic texts are always aimed outward at their enemies and the comforting texts always inward at themselves — has learned that you are not preaching to them at all. You are arming them. The whole counsel refuses that service. It turns the indictment of injustice on the comfortable and the call to order on the rebellious and the warning against the love of money on the rich man in the third row who funds the building, and over a year no one in the room is allowed to be only a spectator at someone else's correction. That is the whole counsel doing what only the whole counsel can do. It does not tell the frightened father which way to vote. It tells him whose hands the future is in, and it does so without flinching from the parts of the text that would correct him, because a God edited down to comfort cannot finally comfort anyone. The room will keep sorting itself by jersey for as long as we let the politicians frame the world. The poet's task is to keep opening the larger house, week after week, until the people forget for an hour which team they came in wearing — and remember, instead, the one Lord whose table they are all about to share. That is the only unity worth preaching toward, and it will cost you the easy applause every single Sunday you reach for it.
