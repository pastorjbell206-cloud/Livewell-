You can hear it in the prayer requests. Not the ones spoken aloud — those are still about surgeries and prodigals and jobs. The other ones. The aside in the hallway, the comment dropped after the benediction, the thing a good man says with real fear in his voice about what is coming for the country if the wrong people win. I have learned to listen for the fear underneath the politics, because the fear is the tell. It tells me where my people actually live, and more often than I want to admit, it is not in the world the sermon described that morning. It is in the world a screen described to them the night before, and the night before that, and for three hours every evening for the last fifteen years.

That is the situation, and pretending otherwise is the first failure. **Your most committed members are being discipled forty hours a week by a medium that knows their fears better than you do, and you get them for forty minutes on a Sunday — if the kickoff is late.** This is not a metaphor. It is an arithmetic problem, and the arithmetic is not on our side.

I am not writing this as a man above the problem. I have caught my own attention pulled the same direction, felt the same pull toward the screen that promises to tell me what is really going on. The capture I am describing is not a sin other people commit. It is a current we are all standing in, and the pastor who imagines himself dry is usually the one already soaked.

## The formation we are competing with is better than ours

Eugene Peterson spent a career insisting that the pastor's work is slow, local, and unglamorous — the cure of souls, he called it, borrowing the old phrase, a craft more like gardening than like broadcasting. In *Working the Angles* he warned that pastors who abandon that slow work for something more visible become, in his blunt word, shopkeepers — managers of religious consumer demand. He wrote that before the smartphone. He was describing a competition for attention that has since become a rout.

Consider what the cable network actually does, because we should respect a rival before we name its lie. It is present every day. It speaks the same message at the same time across millions of homes, so your people are formed in unison with strangers they will never meet but feel they know. It tells a story with heroes and villains and a coming reckoning — a complete narrative of sin, judgment, and salvation, with everything except a savior who dies for his enemies. It asks nothing of the viewer but attention and rewards that attention with the warm certainty of being on the right side. It is, in the precise sense, a liturgy. It forms loves by repetition the way a liturgy forms loves by repetition, and it does so with a consistency most of our churches have never achieved.

We tend to think we are competing on the level of ideas. We are not. We are competing on the level of formation, and the screen has more hours, more repetition, and a tighter feedback loop than we will ever have. A man does not leave the faith over an argument he lost. He drifts, one evening at a time, into a different story about what he should fear and whom he should hate, until one Sunday the sermon sounds naive and the news sounds true. Paul saw the shape of it without the technology: *the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions, and will turn away from listening to the truth and wander off into myths* (2 Timothy 4:3–4). The genius of the modern version is that it lets a person accumulate those teachers while still calling himself orthodox. He keeps the doctrine and outsources the fear.

## Name the capture without naming the network

Here is where many of us go wrong, myself included on more than one Sunday. We sense the capture and we attack the symptom. We name the network from the pulpit, or worse, we name it sideways — a knowing aside, a little dig that the people who agree with us catch and enjoy. And in that moment we have stopped pastoring and started competing. We have entered the same arena, played by the same rules, and conceded that the question worth fighting over is which channel is right.

The deeper work is to name the capture itself — the condition, not the channel. You can stand before a room that is divided three ways politically and say, with no partisan fingerprint on it, that there is a kind of fear that is teaching us to see our brother as our enemy, and that this fear does not come from God. You can say that a steady diet of outrage will hollow out a soul as surely as a steady diet of pornography, and for the same reason: both train us to consume a human being as a means to a feeling. Nobody in the room can dismiss that as the other side talking, because it indicts the room. It indicts you. *For God gave us a spirit not of fear but of power and love and self-control* — Paul again, to the same young pastor (2 Timothy 1:7). The diagnosis is not that my people vote wrong. The diagnosis is that they are afraid, and their fear is being farmed.

**The enemy is not the channel your people watch. The enemy is what the watching is doing to their capacity to love the brother sitting three pews over.** That is a pastoral problem, and it is the one Scripture actually equips us to address.

## Romans 14 is the most underused chapter in a divided church

We reach for Romans 14 about food and holy days and then quietly file it under "first-century problems." That is a mistake. Paul is doing something there that we desperately need: he is teaching a congregation how to remain one body while holding genuinely different convictions about contested matters. *Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls* (Romans 14:4). *Why do you pass judgment on your brother? Or you, why do you despise your brother? For we will all stand before the judgment seat of God* (Romans 14:10).

Read that as a man whose congregation is divided over a contested election and it reorganizes everything. Paul does not flatten the convictions. He does not say the matters are unimportant or that everyone is equally right. He says something harder. He says the brother who disagrees with you is the servant of another master, and that master is able to make him stand, and your contempt for him is a usurpation — you have climbed onto a judgment seat that belongs to God. The political opponent in your congregation is not your project to fix. He is your brother to honor, and the day is coming when you both answer to the same Lord for how you treated each other while you waited.

The distinction we have to recover is the one Romans 14 assumes and our age has lost: the difference between the things worth dividing over and the things that are not. The first-century church divided over whether you could eat meat sacrificed to idols. Ours divides over tax policy and border policy and which strongman to fear most. I am not saying these questions do not matter. I am saying they are not the gospel, and a church that treats a policy disagreement as a test of fellowship has confused a disputable matter with a damnable one. That confusion is precisely what the capture produces. It takes a contested question and inflates it into a creed, so that to disagree is no longer to differ but to betray.

## Build the alternative liturgy or lose by default

Hauerwas and Willimon saw this coming in 1989. *Resident Aliens* argued that the church's first political act is not to take a position on the issues of the day but to be the church — a community whose common life is itself a witness, a colony of heaven in a world that has forgotten heaven exists. They were writing against the assumption that Christianity's job is to make America work better. Their claim was sharper: the church is a *polis* of its own, an alternative society, and its members are dual citizens whose deeper loyalty relativizes the shallower one. "The church doesn't have a social strategy," they wrote. "The church is a social strategy."

I resisted that book for years because it sounded like retreat. I have come to think it is the opposite. The reason cable news captures our people is that it offers them a community of meaning, a tribe with a story and an enemy and a sense of belonging — and too many of our churches offer them a service to attend and a building to maintain. We cannot out-shout the screen. But we can do the one thing the screen cannot do. We can put a person who voted one way at a table beside a person who voted the other way and have them pass the bread to each other and call each other brother, and mean it. That table is an argument no broadcast can answer. It is the demonstration that something binds these people deeper than the thing dividing everyone else.

This is why Ephesians 4 is not decoration. *I therefore, a prisoner for the Lord, urge you to walk in a manner worthy of the calling to which you have been called, with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace* (Ephesians 4:1–3). Note that Paul commands them to *maintain* a unity that already exists — *one body and one Spirit . . . one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all* (Ephesians 4:4–6). The unity is not something we manufacture by agreeing. It is something Christ already accomplished and we are charged to guard. The captured congregation has not lost a unity. It has stopped maintaining one it still possesses, because it has been taught by a more diligent teacher that the people across the aisle are the threat.

## The slow recovery, and why it has to be slow

So what do you actually do on Monday. Not much that will feel like enough, and that is the hardest part for those of us who want a strategy that works by Christmas.

You preach the whole text, including the parts that wound your own side, so that no one can predict your politics from your exposition. You catechize, relentlessly and unfashionably, because a people who know what they believe are harder to capture than a people who only know what they feel. You build the practices that form loves — the table, confession, the Psalms in the mouth, the long prayer for the very leaders your people fear — because James K. A. Smith is right that we are shaped more by what we repeatedly do than by what we occasionally think. You sit with the frightened man and you do not argue him out of his channel; you ask him what he is afraid of, and you stay long enough to hear that underneath the politics is usually a father's terror for his children's world, which is a fear the gospel actually has something to say to.

And you accept that you will not win the forty-hour war with forty minutes. You will win, if you win, the way Peterson said you win everything in this vocation: slowly, locally, by being present over years in a way no broadcast can be, until the relationship you have built outweighs the relationship the screen has built, and the man trusts your voice enough to question the other one. There is no shortcut. The shortcut is what the network sells, and selling shortcuts is how it captured him in the first place.

## Resist the capture in your own ministry first

There is a version of this essay I could have written that would have let me off the hook, and it is the version most of us prefer to read. It treats the captured congregation as a problem out there — a flock that has wandered while the shepherd stood faithful at the gate. That version is a lie, and the lie is comfortable, which is how I know to distrust it. The pastor is in the same current as the people, watching some of the same screens, feeling the same pull toward the certainty and the outrage and the warm sense of being among the right ones. The capture does not knock on the study door and ask permission. It seeps in through the same channels it uses on everyone, and it deforms the ministry in ways we are slow to name.

It shows in which sins we preach against and which we leave alone, and the pattern usually maps with embarrassing precision onto the sins of the other tribe. It shows in whose funerals we are warm at and whose we are merely correct at. It shows in the private relief we feel when a member who annoyed us politically leaves the church, a relief that should frighten any man who has read the parable of the ninety-nine. Hauerwas and Willimon insisted that the church is meant to be a community of character that produces a particular kind of person, and the pastor is the first product on the line. If outrage has formed me before it formed them, then every counter-liturgy I try to build will carry the contamination, because a shepherd cannot lead a flock somewhere he has not gone. The work of un-capturing the congregation begins, humiliatingly, with the un-capturing of the man who is supposed to be doing it — which is a work only the Spirit does, and only in a man honest enough to admit he needs it.

The congregation will not be saved by a better argument than the one on television. It will be saved, if it is saved, by a deeper belonging than the one on television — and that belonging is built one shared table, one honest conversation, one un-takeback prayer at a time, by a pastor patient enough to lose every news cycle while quietly winning the soul. The screen has the hours. We have the years. And we have a table the screen will never set, where enemies eat as brothers because a third party has made peace between them at his own expense. We have not yet begun to use it.
