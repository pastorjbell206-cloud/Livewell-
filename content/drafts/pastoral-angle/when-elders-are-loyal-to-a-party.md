The capture you can survive is the one in the pews. The capture that can end a church is the one on the board. A congregation can hold a hundred different political opinions and still be a church, because the people in the pews do not set the church's direction. The elders do. And when the men charged with guarding the church's life have quietly transferred their first loyalty to a party — when the lens through which they evaluate every decision has become red or blue before it is anything else — the church does not merely have divided members. It has a captured government, and a captured government will, in time, remake the church in the party's image, because that is what governments do.

This is the hardest of these essays to write, because it is about power and not just opinion, and because the men involved are usually not villains. They are often the most committed people in the church — the ones who are always there, who give, who have carried real weight for years. Their capture did not come from indifference. It came, frequently, from caring intensely about things that genuinely matter, and then letting the party become the frame through which that caring runs. **The most dangerous capture in a church is rarely held by careless men. It is held by faithful ones who have stopped being able to tell the difference between a conviction the Bible demands and a position their party requires.**

I have sat on the other side of this as well — have felt my own assessments running along partisan grooves before I had examined them, have caught myself in a leaders' meeting assuming a brother's spiritual seriousness from the way he votes. The capture is not a disease that infects elders and spares pastors. We are on the same board.

## The qualifications were always about character, never competence

Return to how the New Testament actually selects leaders, because we have drifted far from it. When the first crisis of distribution hit the Jerusalem church — the Hellenist widows being neglected — the apostles did not appoint the most influential men or the largest givers or the ones with the right connections. *Pick out from among you seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom* (Acts 6:3). Repute. Spirit. Wisdom. The qualifications were entirely about character and entirely silent about everything the world uses to choose leaders. And the result, Luke tells us, was that *the word of God continued to increase* (Acts 6:7) — the right kind of leaders did not just resolve the dispute, they unleashed the mission.

Paul's instructions to Titus run the same way. The elder must be *above reproach . . . not arrogant or quick-tempered . . . not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome* (Titus 1:7), and *he must hold firm to the trustworthy word as taught, so that he may be able to give instruction in sound doctrine and also to rebuke those who contradict it* (Titus 1:9). Read that list against the formation a partisan media diet produces. Quick-tempered. Quarrelsome. Combative toward the enemy, contemptuous of the other side, the disposition of a man trained by outrage. The qualifications for eldership are, almost point for point, the opposite of the character that political capture forms. A man can be doctrinally orthodox and still be disqualified by the temper the party gave him, because Paul lists temper alongside doctrine and treats both as load-bearing.

This is the first and most useful clarity for a pastor facing a captured board. The problem is not that your elders hold political views. The problem, when there is one, is a character problem that Titus 1 already named — quarrelsomeness, arrogance, a contentious spirit — and you are permitted, even required, to address it on those grounds without ever staging a fight about politics. You are not questioning a man's vote. You are holding an officer of the church to the qualifications of his office.

## Paul opposed Peter, and the church survived it

When the conflict is real — when a party loyalty is actively overriding the gospel in how the leadership governs — there comes a point where the pastor must do the thing every conflict-averse bone in his body resists. He must confront. The model, again, is Antioch. Peter, the senior man, had withdrawn from the Gentile table under pressure from a faction, and *the rest of the Jews acted hypocritically along with him, so that even Barnabas was led astray by their hypocrisy* (Galatians 2:13). Note that the capture spread *upward and outward* through the leadership — first Peter, then the others, then even Barnabas, a chain reaction running through the most respected men in the room. That is exactly how board capture works. One influential elder sets the frame and the others, not wanting to seem soft, follow, and soon the whole leadership is governing out of fear of a faction.

Paul's response is the template. *I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned* (Galatians 2:11). He did it publicly, because the offense was public and the table it threatened was public. He did it on the specific ground that *their conduct was not in step with the truth of the gospel* (Galatians 2:14) — not on the ground of personal preference or political disagreement, but on the ground that the gospel itself was being contradicted by how the leaders were acting. And the church survived it. Peter survived it. The relationship survived it. The confrontation was not the end of fellowship; it was the rescue of it.

The lesson for a pastor is twofold and both halves matter. First, confrontation of captured leadership is sometimes not optional — when the gospel itself is being deformed by a party loyalty, silence is complicity, and the pastor who keeps the peace at that cost has abdicated. Second, the ground of confrontation must always be the gospel and the qualifications, never the party. Paul did not say *Peter, your faction is wrong and mine is right.* He said *your conduct is out of step with the truth of the gospel.* The moment a pastor confronts a captured elder on partisan grounds, he has simply revealed his own capture and started a political fight he will lose. The only confrontation that holds is the one anchored where Paul anchored his — in the truth of the gospel and the character the office requires.

## Shepherds, not bosses, and not the party's men either

Peter himself — the same Peter who had to be opposed at Antioch — later wrote the charge that should govern every elder board, and the fact that he learned it the hard way gives it weight. *Shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you; not for shameful gain, but eagerly; not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock* (1 Peter 5:2–3). Three temptations, and each one is a doorway for capture. *Under compulsion* — leading out of pressure rather than calling, which is exactly what a faction applies. *Shameful gain* — and the gain need not be money; it can be the gain of standing with the tribe, the approval of the influential donors, the comfort of the party's regard. *Domineering* — governing by control rather than example, which is the management style outrage media trains.

The elder's job is to shepherd *the flock of God* — not the party's voters who happen to attend, not the faction's interests, but the flock, all of it, including the members whose politics the elder finds wrong. A shepherd who will only feed the sheep that vote his way is not a shepherd. He is a partisan with a title. And the antidote Peter offers is not a policy or a procedure but an image: *being examples to the flock.* The captured elder has made himself an example, all right — an example of the very contentiousness, fear, and tribalism the flock is drowning in. The faithful elder is an example of the opposite, a man whose evident security in Christ and charity toward opponents shows the congregation that another way is possible. You disciple a board the way you disciple a congregation: not primarily by argument, but by what you repeatedly model and require.

## The donor problem nobody wants to name

There is a complication under the surface of every captured board, and pastors discuss it in private and almost never from the pulpit. The most politically intense elders are frequently also among the most generous, and the church's budget knows it. This is where Peter's warning about *shameful gain* lands with particular force, because the gain that captures a leadership is rarely a bribe and almost always a dependence. A board that quietly organizes itself to avoid offending the men who keep the lights on has been captured as surely as one that fears a faction, and the capture is harder to see because it wears the respectable face of stewardship. We tell ourselves we are being prudent about the church's finances. We are sometimes being owned by them.

The pastors I have sat with who handle this well have all, at some point, made a costly decision to demonstrate that the pulpit is not for sale — to preach the text that wounds the major donor's politics, to hold the generous-but-quarrelsome elder to the same Titus 1 standard as everyone else, to risk the gift rather than trim the gospel to protect it. It is frightening, and the fear is the point: a church that cannot afford to obey God is a church that has already chosen a different master, and the congregation can smell it even when the budget looks healthy. The freedom of the pulpit is purchased, in part, by a willingness to lose money for the sake of the truth. Elders who understand that are guarding the church. Elders who do not are slowly converting it into a club that exists to keep its patrons comfortable, and the word of God does not increase in a club.

## The slow governance work, and the line you cannot cross for them

So what is the actual work. It is slower and less dramatic than the confrontation passages might suggest, because confrontation is the rare exception and formation is the daily rule, even on a board.

You form your elders the way you form your people. You open every meeting in the Word and in real prayer, not as a formality but long enough to remember whose church this is. You pray together, by name, for leaders both parties fear, because elders who intercede for their enemies cannot easily despise them. You teach the qualifications of Titus 1 plainly and regularly, so that when a man's temper needs addressing, the standard is already on the table and the conversation is about the office, not the party. You build a culture where it is normal for the board to disagree about politics and unthinkable for the board to divide over it, because you have made visible, in your own conduct, that the thing binding these men is deeper than the thing the world uses to sort them.

And you accept the limit. You cannot make a man's heart un-captured. **You can hold an elder to the qualifications of his office; you cannot reach in and re-order his loves — that work belongs to the Spirit, and the Spirit does not keep your timeline.** Some boards will resist the slow re-formation. Some elders, confronted on genuine gospel grounds, will choose the party over the pulpit, and a few of them will leave, and a very few may try to take the church with them. That is the cost, and Acts 6 is honest that leadership is established precisely *through* the resolution of conflict, not by its avoidance. The apostles did not appoint the seven to keep everyone comfortable. They appointed them so the word of God could increase, and increase it did, on the far side of a dispute they were willing to name.

## When the work fails, and a board has to be re-formed

Honesty requires naming the hardest case, because it is not rare. Sometimes the slow formation does not take. Sometimes you teach Titus 1 for years, pray with the men, model the charity, and a core of the board remains more loyal to the party than the pulpit, and begins to use its authority to bend the church toward the faction — vetting ministry decisions through a political lens, treating members of the other tribe as suspect, pressuring you to baptize the party's agenda from the platform. At that point the conversation is no longer about formation. It is about whether men who are governing out of a captured conscience should continue to govern, and that is a question of qualification, which is a question the church is permitted to ask.

Acts 6 is quietly instructive here, because the apostles' solution to a crisis was not to plead with the existing arrangement but to *appoint* — to put the right kind of men into office and let the structure itself carry the church forward. Leadership in the New Testament is not a tenure a man holds regardless of character; it is a stewardship contingent on the qualifications Paul lists, and a man who has lost the temper, the charity, and the loyalty to the gospel those qualifications require has, in a real sense, vacated the substance of the office while retaining its title. Re-forming a board is grievous work, and it should never be done in anger, never as a partisan purge, never with anything less than tears and a scrupulous insistence that the ground is the gospel and the qualifications and nothing else. But it is sometimes the faithful work, and a pastor who will not do it when it is genuinely required has chosen the comfort of the present board over the future of the flock. The apostles raised up new leaders so that the word of God could increase. A pastor who guards the qualifications of eldership, even at the cost of men he has loved, is doing the same thing for the same reason.

The church does not finally belong to its elders, captured or free. It belongs to the Chief Shepherd, and Peter ends his charge by reminding the elders that *when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory* (1 Peter 5:4). That is the verdict the captured elder has lost sight of — that he will answer not to his party for how well he advanced it, but to the Shepherd of the flock for how faithfully he fed it. Your job is not to win the board. Your job is to keep that appearing in front of it, to govern as a man who will give an account, and to trust that the Shepherd who is coming is also, even now, the one able to make a captured man stand. He has done it before. He did it with Peter, who became, in the end, the very voice that warned the rest of us not to domineer.
