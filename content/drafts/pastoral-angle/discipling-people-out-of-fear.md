The man across the desk is not angry, though it sounds like anger. Sit with him long enough and you find what is actually under there. He is afraid. He is afraid for his grandchildren, afraid of what the country is becoming, afraid that the people running things despise him and everything he loves, afraid in the specific, sleepless way of a person who has been told every night for years that the barbarians are at the gate and only vigilance can hold them off. The politics is the surface. The fear is the engine. And I have learned, slowly and against my own instinct to fix things with sentences, that you cannot argue a frightened man into peace. You can win the argument and lose the man, and send him home more afraid than when he came, now afraid of you too.

This is the thing about political capture that we keep misdiagnosing. We treat it as an information problem — if I can just show him the better facts, the truer frame, the correct theology of government, he will come around. But capture is not finally an information problem. **It is a formation problem, and the formation runs on fear, and fear does not yield to better information any more than a drowning man yields to a lecture on swimming.** The question for the pastor is not how to correct the frightened person's conclusions. It is how to disciple the fear itself — how to form a person whose loves and reflexes have been so reshaped by a steady diet of dread that the dread now feels like discernment.

I do not exempt myself. I have felt the pull of the dread, the way it offers a counterfeit of vigilance, the way being afraid can masquerade as being awake. The pastor who imagines he is above the fear is usually just better at hiding it, including from himself.

## Scripture names fear as the enemy, not a neutral fact

We tend to treat fear as morally neutral — an understandable response to a frightening world, regrettable but innocent. Scripture does not treat it that way. It treats fear as a power to be cast out. *There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear. For fear has to do with punishment, and whoever fears has not been perfected in love* (1 John 4:18). John sets fear and love in direct opposition. They cannot occupy the same space. To the degree a person is ruled by fear, he is not yet ruled by love, and the casting out of the one is the perfecting of the other. This is not a counseling insight John stumbled onto. It is the architecture of the Christian life.

Paul tells the same young pastor we keep returning to: *God gave us a spirit not of fear but of power and love and self-control* (2 Timothy 1:7). The spirit of fear is not from God. That sentence ought to reorganize how we hear the anxiety in our congregations. When a member's politics has become a vehicle for dread — for the conviction that everything depends on the next election, that the enemy is closing in, that vigilance and outrage are the highest virtues — that member is not being more discerning than his neighbor. He is operating under a spirit that Paul says God did not give. The kindest and truest thing a pastor can do is recognize that the fear is the captivity, and that freedom from it is not naivety but the very thing the gospel promises.

The Psalms model the alternative without ever pretending the threats are imaginary. *God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble. Therefore we will not fear though the earth gives way, though the mountains be moved into the heart of the sea* (Psalm 46:1–2). The psalmist is not denying that the earth gives way. He is locating his security somewhere the earthquake cannot reach. *Be still, and know that I am God. I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth* (Psalm 46:10). The frightened church needs to hear that God's exaltation among the nations does not depend on its anxiety, and that the command in the storm is not *fight harder* but *be still.* Isaiah says it to a people facing real exile: *fear not, for I am with you; be not dismayed, for I am your God; I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my righteous right hand* (Isaiah 41:10). Real danger. A larger God. That is the whole pastoral move, and you cannot make it with an argument.

## Why argument fails and formation works

James K. A. Smith spent three books — beginning with *Desiring the Kingdom* in 2009 — arguing a thesis the church had largely forgotten: that human beings are not primarily thinking things but loving things, and that our loves are shaped less by what we believe than by what we repeatedly do. We are, he argued, liturgical animals. Our hearts are calibrated by the rhythms and rituals we practice — and those rituals are running whether or not we choose them. The mall has a liturgy. The stadium has a liturgy. And the daily three hours of cable news, with its repetitions, its rhythms, its dependable cycle of threat and outrage and the warm relief of being on the right side, is among the most powerful liturgies our people undergo. It is forming their fears by repetition the way the Psalms once formed their peace by repetition.

This is why argument fails. You are trying to change with a single sentence what a thousand evenings of repetition have built. The frightened man's fear is not a conclusion he reasoned his way to and can reason his way out of. It is a disposition that has been trained into him, a reflex laid down by liturgy, and reflexes are not undone by debate. They are undone the way they were made — by a counter-liturgy, a competing set of repeated practices that train the heart toward peace as relentlessly as the screen trained it toward dread.

**You cannot lecture a person out of a fear that a liturgy formed. You can only out-form it, with a deeper and slower liturgy of your own.** This is enormously freeing for the pastor, because it relieves us of the impossible job of winning every argument and gives us the actual job — the one the church has always had — of forming people through Word, sacrament, prayer, confession, and the long rhythm of gathered worship. We were never supposed to be debaters. We were supposed to be the keepers of a counter-liturgy strong enough to reshape what people love and therefore what they fear.

## What the counter-liturgy actually trains

Consider what the gathered worship of the church does to a frightened person over years, if we let it do its work and stop interrupting it with our own anxieties. It puts the words of Psalm 46 in his mouth until *I will not fear* becomes a reflex rather than a struggle. It bends his knee in confession every week until he is reminded that he, too, is among the sinners and not only among the threatened — which is the single most decapacitating fact for the fear-based politics, because that politics requires a clean self and a guilty enemy. It places the Lord's Table in front of him and makes him receive grace he did not earn, in the company of people he disagrees with, until the reflex of receiving grace begins to compete with the reflex of dispensing blame.

It prays, week after week, for the very leaders the screen has taught him to fear and hate — *for kings and all who are in high positions* — and the act of intercession does something no argument can. You cannot hate a man fully while you are honestly praying for him. The liturgy of intercession is corrosive to the liturgy of contempt, and it works by repetition, not persuasion. It sits the man under preaching that names a God sovereign over the future he is frightened of, not occasionally but every Sunday, until the sovereignty of God over the nations stops being a doctrine he affirms and becomes a settledness he inhabits.

Eugene Peterson called the pastoral vocation a work that proceeds at the pace of grace — slow, unglamorous, resistant to the metrics that measure everything else. Discipling people out of fear is the purest form of that slow work. There is no Sunday on which you can announce it accomplished. There is only the patient setting of one liturgy against another, year over year, trusting that the counter-formation is real even when the screen seems to be winning, because the One whose Table it is has promised that perfect love, given time and repetition and the means of grace, casts out fear.

## Naming the fear is itself pastoral

There is a step before the long formation, and it is the one we most often skip because it feels too simple to matter. Before you can disciple a person out of a fear, you have to help him see that he is afraid. The captured man almost never describes his condition as fear. He describes it as realism, as vigilance, as finally being awake to what is actually happening while everyone else sleeps. The fear has disguised itself as clear sight, and the disguise is most of its power. A man who knows he is afraid can be comforted. A man who believes his fear is simply accurate perception cannot, because to comfort him looks, from inside the fear, like asking him to stop caring.

So part of the work is the patient, unhurried naming of the thing, and it cannot be done with a confrontation. You do not tell a frightened man he is frightened; you sit with him long enough, ask enough about what keeps him up at night, that he hears himself say it. The pastors I have sat with who do this best are not clever. They are present. They let the man talk past the politics until the fear surfaces on its own, and then they do not pounce on it or correct it. They name it gently and let it sit in the open, where the gospel can finally get at it. Because a fear that has been spoken out loud, in the presence of someone who did not flinch and did not argue, has already lost some of its grip. It has been moved from the place where it ruled him in the dark to the place where it can be brought, honestly, before the God who keeps saying *fear not* to people in far worse danger than ours.

## The pastor's own fear comes first

There is a hard turn here that I have to make on myself before I make it on anyone else. The reason so much of our preaching feeds the congregation's fear instead of healing it is that we are afraid too. We are afraid of losing members, afraid of the building's finances, afraid of the cultural ground shifting under the church, afraid in the particular pastoral way of a man who has staked his life on an institution that appears to be receding. And a frightened shepherd cannot disciple a flock out of fear. He will, without meaning to, transmit his own dread in the very act of trying to address theirs. The anxiety in the pulpit becomes the anxiety in the pew, multiplied.

So the first person who has to be discipled out of fear is the one holding the microphone. The pastor has to sit under Psalm 46 himself, has to be still and know, has to let the sovereignty of God over the nations become a thing he rests in rather than a thing he merely teaches. He has to stop measuring the kingdom by the metrics the fear uses — attendance, cultural position, electoral outcomes — and recover the older measure, the one Isaiah's God offers a people headed into exile: *I am with you.* Not *you will win.* Not *the danger is not real.* *I am with you.* That is the only ground from which a man can stand before frightened people and not merely amplify their fright.

## Fear is also good news about your people

There is a turn here that changes how a pastor feels walking into the hard conversation, and it is worth dwelling on. If the politics is a symptom and the fear is the engine, then the angry, rigid, exhausting member who corners you after the service is not primarily an opponent to be managed. He is a frightened person to be loved, and frightened people are not finally hard to love once you have seen the fear under the armor. The armor is loud and off-putting on purpose; that is what armor is for. But a man does not armor himself against a world he feels safe in. The very intensity that wears you out is the measure of how afraid he is, and the fear is the door the gospel walks through.

This reframe does not make the member easier to be around, but it makes him possible to pastor, because you are no longer trying to win. You are trying to reach the scared person inside the position, and that is a thing you actually know how to do. It is the oldest pastoral work there is — sitting with the frightened, the bereaved, the cornered, and speaking the *fear not* of Scripture into the specific dark they are in. The political shape of the fear is new; the fear is not. Pastors have been discipling frightened people out of fear since the church began, because the world has always given people plenty to be afraid of, and the answer has always been the same. *Fear not, for I am with you.* You have said it at a hundred bedsides. You can say it to the man whose dread has dressed itself as a politics, once you have learned to hear, under the noise, that it was dread all along.

The frightened in your congregation will not be argued into peace, and they will not be shamed out of their politics, and they will not be saved by the better cable network you wish they watched. They will be re-formed, slowly, by a community whose worship trains a deeper love than the one the screen is training — or they will not be re-formed at all. The work is unhurried because love is unhurried, and love is the thing that casts out fear. There is no faster way, and the search for one is usually just the fear, looking for a shortcut. Be still. The future the man is so afraid of is held in hands that were nailed open, and they have not let go yet.
