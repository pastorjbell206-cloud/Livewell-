For roughly three hundred years, Christianity was illegal somewhere in the Roman Empire most of the time. The Christians did not lobby for legality. They did not vote out the emperor — they could not. They did not, in any sense we would recognize, try to take political power. They did three things instead. They worshipped. They cared for the people the empire did not care for. They were willing to be killed for both. By the third century, the empire had noticed. By 313, Constantine had issued the Edict of Milan and Christianity was legal. By 380, under Theodosius, it was the state religion.

This is the part of church history American evangelicalism has spent the last forty years trying not to learn from. We look at the early church and we admire their faith, their courage, their willingness to die in the arenas. We do not, on the whole, study what they actually did between the dying. The dying was the dramatic part. The doing was most of it. And the doing is the part we need to recover.

## What they did not do

I want to start with what the early church did not do, because the absences are instructive.

They did not lobby. They did not organize voting blocs — they could not, the franchise did not exist for most of them. They did not run candidates. They did not write op-eds calling for legal protection. They did not, even in the periods when they could have, ask the surrounding pagan culture to honor their festivals or accommodate their scruples.

They did not try to take over the imperial cult. They refused to participate in it; they did not try to replace it. The distinction is important. When they refused to burn incense to Caesar, they were not refusing because they thought Caesar should be burning incense to Christ. They were refusing because they thought Christ was Lord and Caesar was Caesar, and the two were operating in different orders. The political order was the political order; the church was the church. The two had a relationship — a fraught one — but they were not the same thing, and the early Christians were not trying to make them the same thing.

They did not, in the end, run the playbook American evangelicals have run for forty years. They did not seek political power. They did not seek cultural privilege. They did not seek a return to a Christian past — there was no Christian past to return to. They were the first Christian present, building it in real time, in conditions that did not give them any of the political tools we have spent the past generation insisting were essential to faithful witness.

They built what they built anyway. The building took a different shape.

## What they did

They built three things. The three things are not complicated. They are slow.

They worshipped. The early church worshipped weekly, on the first day of the week, gathered in homes because they did not yet have buildings, around scripture and bread and wine. The worship was the center of everything. The worship was what they would not give up, even under threat of death. Polycarp, on the way to his martyrdom in 155, said the line that becomes the early Christian password — *eighty-six years have I served him, and he has done me no wrong; how then can I blaspheme my King who saved me?* The worship was the body of his ministry. The worship was what the empire wanted him to renounce. He would not.

The worship in the early centuries was richer than most of our worship is now. Justin Martyr's description in the *First Apology* (around 155, the same year as Polycarp's death) lays out a service that included extensive scripture reading, sermon, intercessory prayer, the kiss of peace, the eucharist, and care for the poor — explicit, structural, every Sunday. The poor were named. The widows and orphans were named. The collection was taken not for the building's mortgage but for the people who had nothing. The eucharist was understood as the central act, the participation in Christ's body and blood, the formation of the Christian into the body of Christ.

This is the first thing. They worshipped. They worshipped in a way that formed them into the kind of people who could do the next two things.

They cared for the people the empire did not care for. This is the part American evangelicalism finds easiest to admire and hardest to imitate at scale.

The Antonine Plague in the 160s and the Plague of Cyprian in the 250s tore through the Roman world, killing perhaps a quarter of the population in some areas. The pagan response, on the whole, was to flee — to leave the cities, to abandon the sick, to wait it out in the countryside. The Christian response was to stay. They nursed their own. They nursed their pagan neighbors who had been abandoned. Many of them died doing it. Rodney Stark's *The Rise of Christianity* (1996) calculates that ordinary nursing care during plague — water, food, warmth — could reduce mortality by as much as two-thirds. The Christians stayed and provided ordinary nursing care, and a much higher percentage of Christians than pagans survived the plagues, and Christians ended up nursing a substantial portion of the surviving pagan population back to health.

The pagans noticed. The pagans, in many cases, became Christians afterward. The plague years were the years of the church's most explosive growth, and the growth was not because the Christians had better arguments. It was because the Christians had better practices. They had a community that did not abandon its sick. They had a theology that made it possible to stay when staying meant dying. The theology and the practice formed each other.

They also took in abandoned infants. Roman practice permitted, and frequently encouraged, the exposure of unwanted infants — left in the open to die or to be picked up. The Christians, beginning very early and consistently, picked them up. They picked up especially the girls, who were exposed more often. The Christian gender imbalance over the early centuries — more women than men in many congregations — was partly a consequence of this practice. The infants picked up grew up. They were raised in Christian households. They became the next generation of the church.

They freed slaves at rates disproportionate to the surrounding culture. They educated women. They cared for the prisoners — visiting them, bringing food, often securing their release. They buried the dead the empire would not bury — the slaves, the unknown, the foreign. The Christian funeral practices were one of the most distinctive features of the early church, and one of the practices that drew converts.

This was not a program. This was practice. They did it because they thought scripture told them to and because their worship had formed them into the kind of community that could not look at the suffering of their neighbors and do nothing. The empire could not generate this kind of care. The empire had not been designed to. The church had been designed to, and the church did it, and the empire — slowly, over three centuries — noticed.

They were willing to be killed for both. This is the third thing, and it is the hardest to talk about honestly, because the language of martyrdom has become cheap in American discourse.

The early Christians who were killed by the Roman state were not killed because they had political views the state found troublesome. They were not killed because they were inconvenient to the regime. They were killed because they would not say *kyrios kaisar* — they would not acknowledge the divinity of Caesar, they would not burn the incense, they would not participate in the imperial cult that bound the empire together. They were killed because they refused, in a public and observable way, to acknowledge a god who was not Christ.

The dying was rare in absolute numbers. Modern estimates suggest perhaps three thousand to ten thousand martyrs total over three centuries — large for a small movement, but not the millions of later legend. The dying was strategically devastating, though. Each martyr's death was a sermon. Tertullian's line was *sanguis martyrum semen christianorum* — the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church. It was. The martyrs' deaths were attended by witnesses, talked about for years, often produced conversions, and shaped the courage of the next generation of Christians.

I want to be honest. I do not know if I would have died well. I do not know if any of us would. We have not been tested in the way the early Christians were tested. The training we would need to die well is the training of the worship and the care, accumulated over years, until the body knows what it would do before the mind has time to think.

## The verdict

The early church did not save Rome. The early church outlasted Rome. The empire fell. The church kept worshipping, kept caring, kept burying the dead, kept picking up the children. The empire that succeeded Rome inherited a Christianity that was older than the empire, and that had been the major force preserving literate culture, organized charity, and stable communities through the collapse.

This is the model. The model is not insurgent. The model is not political. The model is the slow construction of parallel institutions — institutions that do what the empire cannot do, in ways the empire cannot match, until the empire itself begins to notice and to depend on them. The model is, in short, the patient construction of an alternative city inside the city of empire, and the willingness to die rather than confess that the empire is what we have been worshipping all along.

This is the work in front of us. It is unflattering work. It does not produce the political wins American evangelicalism has spent forty years pursuing. It will not put a Christian on the Supreme Court. It will not pass the laws we wanted. It will not, on its own timeline, restore cultural Christianity to its previous position. It will do something else. It will, slowly and over decades, build the kind of church that the empire cannot kill — because the church has stopped depending on the empire, has built its own institutions, has formed its own people, and has remembered that the citizenship that finally matters is one the empire cannot revoke.

The early church figured this out under conditions worse than anything most of us will ever face. They did not have us as a model; they had only the scriptures and the apostolic tradition and the Holy Spirit. We have them. We have three hundred years of their practice, written down, studied, available. We have no excuse for not learning from it.

I am writing this for the pastor who is asking what to do, and for the layperson asking what to support. The answer is in the early church. Worship more deeply. Care for the people the empire cannot care for. Be willing to lose what the empire can take. The losing is the witness. The witness is the church. The church is what survives every empire, including the one we have grown up inside.

The next essay in this series takes up the remnant motif and the question of why minority status, far from being the disaster we have read it as, is actually the condition the church has been in for most of its history. The faithful have always been a minority. The early church knew that. We forgot. We are about to remember.
