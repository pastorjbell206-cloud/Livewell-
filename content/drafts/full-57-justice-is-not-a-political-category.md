Somewhere in the last several decades of American church life, a strange inversion happened. The word *justice* — which appears hundreds of times in Scripture, on the lips of prophets and the Son of God himself — became a word the faithful learned to distrust. It got filed under *politics*, and once it was filed there, half the church could safely ignore it and the other half could safely weaponize it, and almost no one had to deal with it as what it actually is: a category of God.

This is not a small mistake. It is a theological surrender disguised as discernment.

The Hebrew Scriptures do not treat justice as one issue among many. *Mishpat* — right judgment, the setting of things right — and *tsedaqah* — righteousness lived out in relationship — run through the Law and the Prophets like a load-bearing beam. "Let justice roll down like waters," Amos says, in the middle of God's furious rejection of worship offered by people who trampled the poor (Amos 5:24). "He has told you, O man, what is good… to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). This is not the political left talking. This is the text, centuries before there was a left or a right to take sides in.

Here is the inversion stated plainly. We took a category that belongs to God and handed it to Caesar. We let the political tribes define the word, and then we sorted ourselves by tribe and inherited the tribe's instinct — one side embracing *justice* as the whole gospel, the other treating it as a code word for compromise. Both tribes agreed on the one thing that should have been unthinkable: that justice is fundamentally a political question. It is not. It is a question about the character of God, and what it means to bear his image, and whether the worship we offer him is the kind he has said, repeatedly, that he hates when it floats above the suffering of the vulnerable.

I have been on both sides of the failure. I have used *justice* as a banner that let me feel righteous without being changed. I have also, in other seasons, flinched from the word because of the company it kept — let a political allergy talk me out of a biblical command. Both were the same error wearing different clothes. Both let the political map redraw the moral one. I am not writing this from above the problem. I am writing it from inside a church, and a heart, that did this.

The cost of the inversion is not mainly that we get policy wrong. The cost is to the witness. When the world hears the church treat justice as optional — a special interest, a wing of the congregation, a season's emphasis — it concludes, reasonably, that our God does not much care about the things that crush actual human beings. And a God who does not care about that is not the God of Amos, or Isaiah, or the Christ who opened his ministry by reading Isaiah's words about good news to the poor and release to the captives (Luke 4:18) and then said, *today this is fulfilled.*

To recover justice as a theological category is not to baptize a party platform. It is harder than that, and it cuts across every coalition. It refuses to let the right reduce the gospel to private morality, and it refuses to let the left reduce it to politics by other means. It asks, of every situation, not *which side is this* but *what does the God who loves mishpat require here* — and it is prepared for the answer to embarrass whichever tribe the asker came from.

The prophets were not activists with a religious vocabulary. They were people so possessed by the holiness of God that they could not stop seeing what injustice does to the ones he loves. That is the recovery. Not justice as politics. Justice as the unavoidable shape of taking God seriously.

We filed it under the wrong heading. It was never Caesar's word. It was always his.
