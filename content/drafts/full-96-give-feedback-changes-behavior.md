Most feedback conversations accomplish one thing: they make the person giving the feedback feel that they have discharged an obligation. The conversation happens. The words are spoken. The file is documented. And the person receiving the feedback leaves the meeting confused, defensive, or privately unconvinced — and changes nothing.

I have given feedback this way. I have sat across from a staff member or a volunteer leader and said the right things in the right sequence and watched the person nod in all the right places and known, even as it was happening, that nothing would change. Not because the person was resistant, but because I had said what I needed to say rather than what they needed to hear.

The first failure of ineffective feedback is that it is abstract. "Your communication needs to improve." "You are not as present in meetings as you should be." These statements may be accurate. They do not tell the person what to actually do differently on Tuesday morning. Specific behavior is the only addressable unit of change. The feedback that changes anything is feedback that is concrete enough to act on: "When you send the meeting agenda the night before, people come unprepared. Send it by Thursday afternoon and watch what changes."

The second failure is that it arrives too late. Feedback delivered six weeks after the event it is responding to is not feedback. It is a record. The person being addressed has no access to the emotional and contextual reality that the feedback is responding to — it has faded, reinterpreted itself, been processed in a dozen different directions. The feedback that has the best chance of changing behavior is feedback given close enough to the event that the person can re-enter it, can access the specific situation, can actually imagine doing it differently.

Proverbs has a great deal to say about the relationship between words and their timing. "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in a setting of silver" (Proverbs 25:11). The word fitly spoken is the right word in the right moment — not just accurate in content but appropriate in timing and delivery. The pastoral leader who has learned to speak fitly is a leader whose words actually penetrate.

The third failure is that it lacks genuine care for the person receiving it. This is the one that costs the most to correct. Feedback delivered as organizational maintenance — you are not meeting the job description, these are the requirements, this is being documented — communicates that the person is a function, not a person. Human beings do not change their behavior significantly in response to being treated as functions. They comply minimally and protect themselves. Behavior changes in response to someone who clearly cares about their actual growth and is willing to tell them hard things because they believe the hard things will help.

Paul's counsel in Galatians 6:1 — "Brothers and sisters, if someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently" — does not describe performance management. It describes pastoral intervention. The word *restore* is the word used of resetting a broken bone: painful, but aimed at making the person whole. The gentleness is not softness. It is the posture of someone who is in the work for the other person's sake, not for the sake of institutional compliance.

Feedback that changes behavior is specific, timely, and rooted in genuine investment in the person's growth. It does not protect the speaker from the discomfort of being direct. It also does not protect the speaker from the discomfort of actually caring.

Most of us find one of those easier than the other.

The hardest feedback I have ever had to give was to someone I genuinely loved and whose failure was going to cost both of us something. I gave it badly the first time. Too much care, not enough directness. I gave it again. It landed.

That is usually how it goes.
