The commission is specific. It begins in Ezekiel 34.

God speaks to the shepherds of Israel who have fed themselves on the flock rather than feeding it. Who have not strengthened the weak. Who have not healed the sick or bound up the injured or brought back the strayed or sought the lost. The scattered sheep, God says, have become prey — and God holds the shepherds accountable. Not sympathetically. With fury. "I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand" (Ezekiel 34:10).

The scandal of abuse in Christian institutions is not a modern problem with a modern cause. It is the ancient failure of shepherds who served themselves, and the ancient pattern of structures that protected the shepherd's position over the sheep's life. What is modern is the scale of documentation, the courage of survivors who refused to stay quiet, and the public accounting that has followed. The Reports on Sovereign Grace Ministries, the Southern Baptist Convention abuse investigation, the investigations across multiple Catholic dioceses — [cite — confirm] these are not peripheral events in the history of the church. They are the same event: institutionalized protection of abusers and systematic silencing of those they harmed.

I have to say something before I continue arguing.

I have served on committees. I have participated in conversations where the primary concern, before anyone named the harm done to a person, was what this would mean for the church's reputation and the ministry's continuation. I knew, in those rooms, that something was wrong with the priorities. I did not name it with the directness the moment required. I know what it is to choose institution over person while telling yourself you are managing the situation wisely. That was not wisdom. That was complicity with a pattern I am here naming as sin.

The structural argument deserves to be made plainly. Institutions protect themselves. This is not a characteristic peculiar to religious institutions — every organization develops self-preservation instincts that can override its stated purpose. But the church faces a particular corruption when it operates this way, because its stated purpose is the care of the vulnerable, and the one against whom its institutional instincts are activated is precisely the person its purpose requires it to protect. The abuser is a threat to the institution's reputation. The victim is also a threat to the institution's reputation. The institution's response — historically, repeatedly, documentably — has been to manage both threats, which in practice means removing the abuser quietly and pressuring the victim to silence.

That management is theological error of the most serious kind. It treats reputation as a value higher than the truth. It treats the institution's survival as a value higher than the person's dignity. It treats the abuser's continuity as a pastoral concern more urgent than the survivor's justice.

The gospel cannot survive that logic. A community organized around a God who took on flesh and suffered and was vindicated against the powers that killed him cannot simultaneously be an institution that sacrifices the suffering to protect the powerful. Not without losing the capacity to proclaim what it claims to believe.

What justice requires here is not complicated, though it is costly. Mandatory reporting. Independent accountability structures with genuine authority, not advisory bodies that report to the leaders they are supposed to oversee. Survivor-centered processes rather than institution-centered ones. Named accountability, not managed transitions to other ministry contexts. These are not radical demands. They are the minimum that basic institutional integrity would require, even apart from the theological claims the church makes about the God who hears the cry of the oppressed.

Psalm 82 pictures God standing in the divine council and indicting the rulers of the earth for failing to "defend the poor and the fatherless, do justice to the afflicted and the needy, rescue the weak and the needy" from the hand of the wicked (Psalm 82:3–4). The rulers of the earth. The ones with the power to protect. God holds them responsible for what they fail to prevent and what they actively enable.

The silence is not neutral. It never was.

What God requires of the church — and what the church has failed to provide — is the courage to say out loud what it already knows in private.
