The faith you actually hold — the parts you actually believe in your body, not the parts you say in catechism — is the faith your children will inherit. Your doubts go down with the rest. Pretending otherwise has cost two generations of evangelical kids their faith, and the pretending is one of the deepest unaddressed pastoral problems in American Christian parenting.

I am writing this for the Christian parent who has been doing the youth-group circuit and the Christian-school enrollment and the family-devotions checklist and is watching her teenagers drift in ways the strategy was supposed to prevent. The strategy was supposed to produce children who knew what they believed. The strategy has been producing children who, in many cases, are doing the opposite — leaving in larger numbers than any previous American Christian generation, leaving with apparent calm rather than dramatic crisis, leaving in ways that suggest the family formation work did not actually do what it was supposed to do. I want to address why.

## What Christian Smith found

Christian Smith's National Study of Youth and Religion is the most thorough recent research on the transmission of religious identity from parents to children. The study, published in *Soul Searching* (2005), *Souls in Transition* (2009), and several subsequent volumes, tracked thousands of American teenagers from adolescence into young adulthood, with detailed interviews about religious beliefs, practices, and family backgrounds.

The strongest finding, repeated across multiple analyses, is that the religious commitment of young adults is most strongly predicted by the *practiced* religion of their parents — not the verbal religion, not the catechetical exposure, not the church attendance frequency, but what their parents actually did and held as integrated commitments. The parents who held the faith as a deeply practiced reality produced children who, in significantly higher rates, held the faith into adulthood. The parents who held the faith as a verbal commitment that was not deeply practiced produced children who, in significantly higher rates, did not.

The finding is not surprising once you sit with it. Children are observant. They notice what their parents actually do, what their parents are actually afraid of, what their parents actually love, how their parents actually handle conflict, how their parents actually treat money, how their parents actually treat each other, how their parents actually handle the people the church says to care for. The catechism transmits if the catechism is being lived. The catechism does not transmit if the catechism is being said and not lived.

## What this means in practice

The practical implication is uncomfortable. The Christian parent who is doing the religious-formation checklist while not actually integrating the faith into the daily practice of the household is doing a kind of formation work that, on the strongest available evidence, does not produce long-term Christian commitment in children. The work has been the dominant pattern in American evangelical parenting for two generations. The pattern has been failing for at least one generation, and the failure has been widely visible in the millennial and Gen Z exit numbers.

The parents I have pastored who have produced children who held the faith into adulthood have generally not been the parents who did the most formal religious-formation work. They have been the parents who were, themselves, the most deeply formed by the faith — whose own marriages bore the marks of the gospel, whose own handling of money reflected scriptural commitments, whose own conversations with the children included theological questions handled honestly, whose own response to suffering looked like the response of someone who actually believed what the church said about suffering. The children watched. The children absorbed. The children grew up into adults whose faith was not a youth-group inheritance but a household formation.

The parents I have pastored who have lost their children to disaffiliation have, in many cases, done the formal religious-formation work conscientiously. They were not negligent parents. They were not insincere believers. They were, in many cases, parents whose household practice and verbal commitments were not as integrated as they thought they were, and whose children, watching, picked up on the gap and concluded — accurately, in some respects — that the faith being verbally professed was not the faith being actually lived.

## Why pretending makes it worse

The category I want to address most directly is the parental pretense that has been particularly destructive — the pretense of certainty that is not actually held.

Most Christian parents have doubts. The doubts are normal. The doubts include real intellectual uncertainties about specific Christian claims, real frustrations with the institutional church, real moments in which the felt presence of God has been absent, real disappointments with how prayers have been answered or unanswered. The doubts are part of what it means to be a Christian who has been doing the actual work of trying to hold the faith over decades.

The standard parental response, in American evangelicalism, has been to hide the doubts from the children. The hiding is well-intentioned. The hiding is supposed to give the children a stable framework for their own faith development. The hiding is supposed to model confident Christian commitment.

The hiding does the opposite of what it intends. The children notice the doubts even when the doubts are not named. The children pick up on the moments when the spoken commitment does not match the body language, when the prayer does not match the worried look, when the Sunday morning enthusiasm does not match the Monday morning tension. The children grow up with a confused sense of what the faith actually is — they have been told it is certainty, but they have been seeing something more ambivalent. The confusion produces, in many cases, a sense that the faith is fundamentally about performance rather than about honest engagement with God. The performance breaks down when the children leave home and no longer need to perform.

The alternative is the named-doubt approach. The parent who tells the children, at appropriate ages and in appropriate ways, *I have doubts about this; here are the doubts; here is why I still believe; here is how I am working through it* — this parent is doing several things. The parent is modeling honest engagement with faith rather than performed confidence. The parent is teaching the children that doubt is part of the Christian life rather than a failure of it. The parent is preparing the children to do their own doubt work without concluding that the doubt is a reason to leave. The parent is treating the children as intelligent observers whose noticing should be honored rather than suppressed.

The named-doubt approach is not the same as the casual-doubt approach that some progressive Christian parenting has tried, in which doubt is treated as an indifferent feature of religious identity and children are essentially raised without theological formation. The named-doubt approach is doubt held inside committed Christian practice, addressed honestly, worked through openly, and integrated into a faith that is both genuine and intellectually serious. The children raised this way are, in my pastoral experience, more likely to hold the faith into adulthood than the children raised on either parental certainty-performance or parental indifference.

## What this looks like in practice

Let me be concrete about what the named-doubt approach looks like in the actual household.

It looks like family conversations that include theological questions handled honestly. *I do not fully understand why God allowed that. I am trying to work it out. The Christian answers I have heard so far are insufficient. Here is the answer I have been working with. Here is what I am still uncertain about.* The conversations should be age-appropriate, but should not be condescending. Children handle more honesty than most parents give them credit for.

It looks like prayer that includes lament rather than only thanksgiving. The Psalms include the full range of human emotional engagement with God. The mid-century evangelical practice of teaching children prayer as a series of grateful affirmations cut them off from the lament tradition that scripture itself includes. Children should learn that prayer can include anger, complaint, grief, confusion. The model is in scripture. The model has been suppressed in many American Christian households. The recovery is healthy.

It looks like scripture reading that does not soften the hard passages. The conquest narratives in Joshua. The imprecatory psalms. The passages about slavery. The hard parts of Paul. The parts of Revelation that produce genuine moral discomfort. Children should encounter these as part of the actual Bible, not as edited-out features of a curated version. The encounter should be honest, the questions should be welcomed, and the parental response should be *this is hard, I am still working it out, here is what I think and why, what do you think.*

It looks like parental confession when the parent fails. The household in which the parent admits failure, apologizes specifically, and demonstrates the practical shape of repentance is the household in which children learn that the Christian life includes failure as a normal feature rather than as a disqualifying scandal. The children whose parents never admitted failure grew up with two equally bad options — to perform the same unfailing public posture, or to leave the faith because the failure they were experiencing in themselves could not be admitted to.

It looks like the integration of faith into how money is handled, how the marriage is lived, how the parents respond to suffering, how the parents treat the people the church says to care for. The integration is the formation. The integration cannot be faked. The integration produces children who, watching, grow up into adults whose faith is real because the faith they watched was real.

## The verdict

Your children will inherit your faith if your faith is real. Your children will inherit your doubts if your doubts are honored. Your children will inherit your pretense if you choose pretense, and they will not return to faith until the pretense is dismantled — usually in their own children's lives, two generations later. Choose honesty. The honesty is the formation. The pretense is the loss.

I am writing this for the Christian parent who has been doing the strategy and watching it not produce what the strategy promised. The strategy was incomplete. The deeper formation work is what produces children who hold the faith. The deeper work is harder. The deeper work cannot be outsourced to the youth pastor. The deeper work happens in the household, between parents and children, across the years that the catechism is being said and the integration is or is not happening.

Tell your children what you actually believe. Tell them what you doubt. Tell them how you are working it out. Let them see the faith integrated into the household, in concrete ways, over years. The seeing is the formation. The formation is what produces the next generation of faithful Christians. The next generation has been smaller than the previous one, and the shrinking has been substantially driven by the formation gap I have been describing. The recovery starts in particular households. The recovery starts in yours. The recovery starts now.
