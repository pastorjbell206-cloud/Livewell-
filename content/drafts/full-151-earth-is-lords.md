Psalm 24 opens with a statement so simple it can be missed: "The earth is the LORD's, and everything in it, the world, and all who live in it." This is not poetry about sunsets. It is a claim about property rights.

The earth belongs to God. We are tenants.

This is where the conversation about creation care should begin — not with climate data, not with policy debates, not with the question of what evangelicals think about environmentalism, but with who owns what. If the earth belongs to God, then the way we treat it is an act of stewardship, and stewardship is accountable. You do not get to trash what belongs to someone else and call it freedom. You are responsible for what you were given to tend.

The account in Genesis 2 uses a specific word for the human relationship to the garden: *abad* — to work, to serve. The same word is used for priestly service at the temple. Adam's vocation in the garden is priestly. He tends the place where God is present. When the church reads the dominion mandate of Genesis 1:28 as permission for extraction and consumption, it has badly misread what dominion means in its own cultural context. Ancient Near Eastern kingship was characterized by the king's responsibility to protect and sustain the land and its people — not to exploit them. Dominion was stewardship. It was never license.

I grew up in a tradition that treated creation care as a liberal distraction. Environmental concern was soft. It was political. The real issues were souls, not soil. I preached from that tradition without examining it, which means I preached a truncated gospel — one that cared for human beings while dismissing the world God made, the world God called good, the world God inhabits.

The vision of the renewed creation at the end of the biblical story is not escape from the earth. It is the earth restored. John's Revelation does not describe the saints escaping to heaven and leaving the world behind. It describes the New Jerusalem coming down to a renewed earth (Revelation 21:1-2). The trajectory of redemption is not away from matter. It is toward matter made whole. If that is where the story ends, it has implications for how we live in the middle of it.

Climate change is a justice issue as well as a creation issue, and these are not separable. The populations most affected by rising seas, extreme weather events, crop failures, and water scarcity are disproportionately the world's poor — those with the fewest resources to adapt and the least political power to demand protection [cite — confirm]. The church that cares about the widow, the orphan, and the stranger cannot be indifferent to a crisis that falls hardest on those same populations. To ignore climate justice is to ignore justice. The categories do not divide cleanly.

What does faithfulness look like here? It looks like the church refusing to treat creation care as a partisan position. It looks like congregations examining their own energy consumption, their own land use, their own patterns of waste, with the same seriousness they bring to examining their budget. It looks like preachers willing to say from the pulpit what Psalm 24 says: this is not our world to consume. It belongs to God. We are accountable for what we have done to it.

The early monastic communities understood this in a way the contemporary church has mostly lost. The Benedictine rule of *ora et labora* — pray and work — included working the land with care, treating the material world as a site of encounter with God rather than a resource to be extracted [cite — confirm]. That instinct was not environmentalism in the contemporary sense. It was theology. It was the recognition that the ground under your feet is holy because its owner is holy.

The earth belongs to God. We have treated it as if it belonged to us.

That is not stewardship. That is the old sin, dressed in modern machinery: the refusal to live within limits, the insistence that we are accountable to no one, the ancient hunger to be as God rather than to serve the God who made all of this and called it good.

The Psalmist does not ask whose it is. He states it. The claiming is already settled. What remains is whether we will live accordingly.
