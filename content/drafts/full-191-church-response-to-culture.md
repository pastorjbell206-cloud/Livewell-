H. Richard Niebuhr spent a book cataloguing the ways the church has answered the culture question — Christ against culture, Christ of culture, Christ above culture, Christ and culture in paradox, Christ transforming culture [cite — confirm]. The catalogue itself is instructive not because it provides an answer but because it reveals that the question has been live in every generation, and that every generation has reached for a different settlement.

We are living through a moment when the settlement our parents inherited is dissolving, and we do not yet know what will replace it.

The Constantinian arrangement — the broad alignment between Christian faith and Western cultural assumption that began in the fourth century and shaped Western civilization for more than a millennium — is not quite over, but its legitimacy is. The culture no longer defers to Christian claims. The institutions that once carried Christian assumption into public life without argument — schools, courts, civic ritual — have either abandoned those assumptions or are actively contested about them. We are not post-Christian in the sense that Christianity is gone. We are post-Christian in the sense that Christianity is no longer the default.

For many Christians, this is primarily experienced as loss. The cultural conditions that made the faith easy have been removed, and the faith now requires more active choice, more deliberate formation, more willingness to be countercultural in ways that are actually costly rather than merely nominal. This is disorienting. The instinct is to fight to restore the conditions — to recover the cultural establishment, to legislate the norms, to force the culture back into a shape where the faith is at home.

I understand that instinct. I have felt it. And I think it misreads both the moment and the tradition.

The church was not at its most prophetic when it was culturally established. The church at its most prophetic was the church under pressure — the early communities that held a different account of reality while the imperial account surrounded them, the Confessing Church in Nazi Germany, the Black church in America during the century of Jim Crow, the underground church in contexts where Christianity is genuinely illegal. What those communities share is not cultural comfort but theological clarity. They knew what they believed, because they had to. The pressure removed the option of comfortable ambiguity.

The pressure that post-Christendom creates is not without grace. It is clarifying. It forces a congregation to ask what it actually believes, as opposed to what it has inherited and never examined. It forces a distinction between the gospel and the cultural form the gospel wore in a particular time and place. The gospel is not Western. It is not American. It is not a product of any civilization, though every civilization has both received it and distorted it.

What the church is called to in this moment is not primarily a cultural strategy. It is faithfulness to a set of practices that form people into the image of Christ — practices that were always countercultural to some degree and are now more visibly so. Gathering, prayer, Scripture, table, hospitality, reconciliation, witness. These practices are not dependent on cultural establishment for their efficacy. They worked in the Roman Empire before Constantine. They work in the underground church today. They will work in post-Christendom America if we are willing to pursue them with the seriousness the moment requires.

The church that is desperate to remain culturally relevant will do almost anything to stay in the room. The church that is confident in its calling can afford to be strange — to practice the hospitality, the forgiveness, the economic solidarity that the surrounding culture finds implausible — and trust that the strangeness is its own kind of argument.

Not every generation gets to be culturally at home. The tradition is older than any single culture. It has survived arrangements far more hostile than the one we currently inhabit.

The question is not whether the culture will accommodate us. The question is whether we are willing to be the church anyway.

The answer to that question is not a cultural strategy. It is a decision about what we love.
