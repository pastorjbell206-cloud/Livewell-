The phrase about the arc of the moral universe bending toward justice was not invented in the twentieth century. Its modern form comes from [cite — confirm the attribution], and has been quoted in the decades since until it has lost most of its weight. Slogans do that. They start as conclusions of hard argument and end as decorations on coffee mugs.

The prophets believed something like it, but they were not optimists in the contemporary sense, and the distinction matters enormously.

Jeremiah believed that God was working toward something. He also watched Jerusalem fall. He dictated his laments into a scroll from the ruins. He did not survive to see the return from exile he announced. Ezekiel's vision of the valley of dry bones — an entire people reduced to skeletons in an open field, coming back to flesh and breath at the word of the Lord (Ezekiel 37) — was given to a man who was himself in exile, watching the destruction of everything he had believed would last. He was not watching from the winning side of history. He was a displaced man announcing an end he would not witness.

This is the difference between optimism and hope. Optimism is a temperamental assessment that things will probably improve. Hope is a theological conviction rooted not in the trajectory of present circumstances but in the character of the God who stands at the end of the arc. You can be a prophet and not be an optimist. You cannot be a prophet without hope.

## The long view in the prophetic tradition

Isaiah 40 is addressed to a community that has already lost. The northern kingdom has fallen. Judah is on the edge. The people are exhausted and afraid. Into that context, the word comes: "He gives strength to the weary and increases the power of the weak. Even youths grow tired and weary, and young men stumble and fall; but those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength" (Isaiah 40:29-31). This is not a promise that the situation will improve next week. It is a promise about the character of God sustaining people across time — time that may include more loss before the renewal comes.

The long arc, in the biblical imagination, runs through death. It does not bend around death. The Exodus narrative, which is the paradigmatic liberation story of the Old Testament, runs through forty years of wilderness before it reaches the land. The people who crossed the Red Sea do not, with a few exceptions, enter Canaan. Their children do. The liberation was real. The generations in the middle paid for it.

The resurrection is not an argument that things will work out fine. It is a statement that death is not the last word. That is different. That is actually harder to believe.

## What this means for the church's work

The church that does the work of justice must be prepared to not see the results. This is a pastoral reality that we do not address enough, and its absence produces a particular kind of burnout that is different from ordinary exhaustion.

When people engage seriously with justice — working against poverty, against incarceration, against the housing crisis, against the accumulated damage of racial inequality — they often do so with an implicit expectation that sustained effort will produce visible change on a human timescale. Sometimes it does. Often it does not. The systems that produce injustice are deeply rooted, powerfully defended, and extraordinarily patient. The person working against them can labor for decades and die without seeing the change she worked for.

Wilberforce spent twenty years in Parliament before the British slave trade was abolished in 1807. [cite — confirm] He spent the remaining decades of his life working against slavery itself in the empire, and died three days after the Slavery Abolition Act was secured in 1833, never having seen it pass. He knew what he was doing with his life. He did not stop.

The Confessing Church in Germany, which resisted the Nazi regime at considerable personal cost, did not stop the Holocaust. Bonhoeffer was executed at Flossenburg in April 1945, three weeks before Germany's surrender. The witness survived. The man did not.

I do not romanticize this. It is not something I counsel people to accept lightly. But the prophetic tradition does not offer a guarantee of visible success. It offers a guarantee of the faithfulness of the One who sent the prophets — and who, in the record we have, often asked them to speak into situations that did not change in their lifetimes.

## The verdict the prophets trusted

The arc bends. This is not naive. It is not a slogan. It is the considered conviction of people who watched it not bend for a very long time and kept working anyway, because they had been told something about the end that the present circumstances could not contradict.

The early church preached resurrection in an empire that crucified its founders. Within three centuries, the empire had converted. I am not claiming that was a pure victory or that Constantine was the fulfillment of the prophetic hope. I am claiming that the people who preached resurrection in Rome in the year 50 did not expect to see what had happened by the year 313, and they preached it anyway.

The faithfulness required of the church is not the faithfulness of those who see the end and work toward it. It is the faithfulness of those who trust the One who does, and work from that trust rather than from the visible evidence.

The evidence is always partial. The arc is always longer than one generation can see.

Work anyway. The verdict is not ours to deliver.
