In 1934, Karl Barth gathered with other German pastors at Barmen and produced the Barmen Declaration — a theological document that crossed Lutheran and Reformed lines to say a single clear thing: Jesus Christ alone is Lord, and the German church would not confess the Nazi state as a second source of revelation alongside Scripture.

The Confessing Church that emerged from Barmen was not uniform. The men who signed that declaration disagreed with each other on baptism, on church governance, on eucharistic theology. They had been separated by four centuries of institutional division. What they discovered at Barmen was that when the essential thing is under attack, the things that divide become secondary not by being dismissed but by being properly ordered. The confession that Jesus is Lord is prior to the disagreement about how baptism should be administered. Crisis clarified the hierarchy.

I want that clarity without requiring crisis to produce it.

We are in a post-Christendom moment in the West. The social conditions that made denominational distinctiveness feel like the primary issue have shifted significantly. When Christianity was the default cultural religion, the differences between denominations functioned like the differences between competing restaurants on the same block — you chose based on preference, and the competition was the relevant category. When Christianity becomes a minority position in a secular culture, the picture changes. The relevant category is no longer the differences between Christian communities. It is the shared claim that the risen Christ is lord of creation — a claim that the culture around us finds implausible.

Against that backdrop, the energy Christians spend fighting each other over secondary questions looks, from the outside, like a family arguing about the paint color while the house burns. I understand why the paint color matters. I am not dismissing the real theological differences between traditions. I am questioning whether the posture of competition and suspicion between Christian communities is the appropriate posture for this moment.

Paul's letter to the Ephesians insists that the church's unity is not merely organizational aspiration. It is theological reality: "one body and one Spirit — just as you were called to one hope when you were called — one Lord, one faith, one baptism; one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all" (Ephesians 4:4–6). The unity exists. The question is whether we are willing to practice it visibly rather than confining it to the level of doctrine while the actual communities remain in separate corners.

The practical form of collaboration does not require doctrinal capitulation. I do not need to pretend the differences between baptistic and paedobaptist traditions do not exist. I do not need to behave as if ecclesiology is irrelevant. What I can do is show up in the city where I serve alongside other pastors who hold Christ as Lord, work together on the needs of the community neither of us can address alone, and model to the watching city that the confession we share is more determinative than the practices that divide us.

I have done this, in fits and starts, in the communities where I have served. What I have found is that the collaboration does not flatten distinctiveness. It clarifies it. When I am working alongside a pastor whose tradition handles things differently than mine, I understand my own tradition more precisely — what I actually believe about it, what I hold firmly and what I hold loosely, and where the genuine disagreements lie. The encounter with difference is instructive rather than threatening, provided I am secure enough in my own convictions not to feel them challenged by contact with someone else's.

The church that is too afraid of other traditions to work alongside them has confused the tradition for the Lord of the tradition. It has made the container the content.

There is a city outside our buildings full of people who find the claim of the gospel implausible. The denominations' separate and competing presence does not help that implausibility. A unified witness — different expressions of the same fundamental confession, working together — is at least a more interesting argument.

Barth and his colleagues understood this. Crisis taught them.

We do not have to wait for crisis to learn what they learned.
