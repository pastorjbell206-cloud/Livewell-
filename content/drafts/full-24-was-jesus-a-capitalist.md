The American evangelical tradition has, for most of the past century, identified Christianity with capitalism. The identification has been mostly unconscious and almost entirely unexamined. It is also wrong. The scripture does not endorse capitalism, and it does not endorse capitalism's twentieth-century American form, and the church that has spent a century pretending otherwise has done so at the cost of its witness to the poor.

I want to write this essay carefully because the conversation has been bad on both sides for a long time, and the careful position is a third position that does not match either of the loud ones. The progressive Christian reading that wants to enlist Jesus as a socialist is just as wrong as the conservative reading that wants to enlist him as a capitalist — and I will write the companion essay on the socialist version in this same writing project. For now, I want to address the conservative side of the misreading, because the conservative misreading is the dominant one in the tradition I have spent my pastoral career inside, and because the misreading has done damage that has gone mostly unconfessed.

The honest reading of scripture on money is not what either side has been telling you.

## Did Jesus support capitalism?

The short answer is no. Jesus did not endorse capitalism. Capitalism as we understand it — an economic system characterized by private ownership of the means of production, market-based exchange, and the pursuit of profit as a primary organizing principle — did not exist in the first century, and any retrospective attempt to enlist Jesus as its advocate is anachronistic.

The longer answer requires us to read the gospels honestly on what Jesus actually said about money. He said a great deal. He said more about money than he said about almost any other topic, including the topics American evangelicalism has spent the past forty years arguing about most loudly. Roughly one out of every six verses in the synoptic gospels touches money in some way. Jesus told more parables about money than about prayer. He warned about wealth more often than he warned about sexual sin. The biblical record is consistent: money is one of the great spiritual dangers, accumulation is treated with sustained suspicion, and the wealthy are warned in ways the poor are not.

The conservative American reading has, on the whole, suppressed this. The suppression has happened gradually, through a combination of cultural identification with American capitalism, theological accommodation to the political coalitions of the past half-century, and the prosperity-adjacent theology that has crept into evangelical preaching even in churches that would explicitly reject the prosperity gospel. The result is a generation of American Christians who can quote *the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil* and have never seriously considered whether the surrounding economic culture they participate in is structured around exactly that love.

## What does the Bible say about money?

Let me lay out the witness, briefly, because the witness is what should ground the conversation rather than the partisan reading.

Old Testament: the Jubilee tradition in Leviticus 25 mandates a fifty-year reset of land ownership and debt. Land could be sold, but the land returned to its original family at the Jubilee year. Slaves were freed at the Jubilee. Debts were cancelled. The Jubilee tradition is not socialism in any modern sense, but it is a structural limit on the accumulation of wealth across generations that has no analog in modern American economics. The prophets repeatedly excoriate the wealthy for amassing land at the expense of the poor — Isaiah 5, Amos 8, Micah 2. The economic critique runs throughout the prophets, and it is consistent.

The Gospels: the rich young ruler in Matthew 19, Mark 10, and Luke 18. Jesus tells him to sell everything and give to the poor. The young man walks away because he had great possessions. Jesus then says it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God. The disciples are astonished. The American conservative tradition has spent a century explaining this passage in ways that soften it. The honest reading is that Jesus said what he said.

Lazarus and the rich man in Luke 16. The rich man is in torment specifically because he did not care for Lazarus, who was at his gate. The parable is not subtle. The rich man's failure was not extreme cruelty; it was indifference to the suffering at his door. The judgment was severe.

The early church in Acts 2 and 4: *all who believed were together and had all things in common; and they were selling their possessions and goods and distributing them to all, as anyone had need.* This is a community of voluntary economic sharing that the modern American evangelical reading has often dismissed as descriptive rather than prescriptive. The dismissal is convenient but is not, on careful reading, justified by the text.

Paul on giving in 2 Corinthians 8 and 9. Paul's economic theology is generous, voluntary, sacrificial, and explicitly cross-shaped — *though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that by his poverty you might become rich.* The cross-shaped economic life is what Paul calls the Corinthians to. It is not capitalism.

James 5 on the rich: *Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you. Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten. Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence against you and will eat your flesh like fire.* This is not a denunciation of all wealth. It is a denunciation of accumulated wealth that has been gathered at the expense of laborers — *the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you.* The economic critique in James is structural. The wealthy in view are wealthy because they have not paid the workers what the workers earned. The critique runs in a direction modern American evangelicalism is not generally comfortable engaging.

## What scripture does not say

Scripture does not, on the whole, address the question of which modern economic system is preferred. Scripture predates capitalism, socialism, and the various third-way models that have been argued for in modern political economy. To enlist scripture for any of these positions requires reading modern categories back into ancient texts, which is something every honest interpreter has to acknowledge as a methodological move with significant risks.

What scripture does is consistently warn about accumulation, consistently honor the poor, consistently call for generosity that costs the giver something real, consistently link the love of money with the gravest spiritual dangers, and consistently insist that the use of wealth is one of the central testing grounds for the disciple. These commitments are biblical commitments. They are not partisan commitments. They sit, in different ways, in tension with both the conservative and the progressive American economic frames.

The conservative American Christian who has been taught that the Bible endorses capitalism has been mistaught. The progressive American Christian who has been taught that the Bible endorses socialism has also been mistaught. The honest position requires the careful reading of texts the partisan readings have, in different ways, suppressed.

## What this means for how we live

I want to be concrete, because the abstract conversation about economic systems often functions as a distraction from the personal economic discipleship the gospel actually demands.

If you accept the biblical witness on money honestly, your financial life will change. You will give more than you currently give. You will hold your possessions more lightly than you currently hold them. You will be more generous to specific poor people whose names you know than the surrounding culture expects. You will likely make less money over your career than you could have made, because the optimization pressure of the surrounding economic culture will weigh less heavily on you. You will be poorer than you would have been.

The American conservative Christian reading of money has, in many cases, given American Christians permission to participate in the surrounding economic culture without the disciplines scripture calls for. The result is a generation of Christians whose financial lives are indistinguishable from the financial lives of their non-Christian neighbors, who give roughly 2.5 percent of income to all charitable causes combined, and who structure their financial decisions around accumulation in ways scripture explicitly warns against. This is not, in my reading, the Christianity scripture describes. This is the cultural Christianity I have written about elsewhere in this writing project, in its specifically economic form.

The discipleship I am calling for is not socialist. It is not the demand for state-imposed redistribution. It is the recovery of Christian economic practice — voluntary, costly, cross-shaped, oriented toward specific neighbors rather than abstract policy outcomes. The recovery is older than capitalism and older than socialism. The recovery is the church being the church, in its economic life, in a way that the surrounding culture cannot reproduce and the surrounding political coalitions cannot capture.

## The verdict

Jesus was not a capitalist. The honest reading of scripture does not allow it. The honest reading also does not endorse the modern American conservative economic frame that has, for half a century, been smuggled into evangelical pulpits under cover of biblical authority. The smuggling has been theologically dishonest, and the dishonesty has cost the church its witness on one of the topics scripture cares most about.

The conservative Christian reader who has been formed by the conservative economic frame may find this essay uncomfortable. I understand. I have been formed by the same frame, in significant ways, for most of my pastoral career. The discomfort is part of the honest reckoning the tradition I have served owes scripture. I am writing this as a man trying to do that reckoning, in writing, in public, before I have done it as fully as I should in my own household.

Jesus was not a capitalist. He was also not a socialist — and the companion essay in this writing project takes up the progressive misreading with the same care I have tried to bring to the conservative one. The third option is the gospel, applied to economic life, in a way that judges both modern systems by a standard neither system accepts. The third option is harder. The third option is right. The recovery of the third option is the economic work of the post-Christendom church, and the work has been delayed by partisan readings for too long. The next essay takes up the socialist misreading. Stay for it.
