We sat in a circle of folding chairs in a church basement in Fenton and disagreed about almost everything for three hours. A Reformed Baptist, two Pentecostals, an Assemblies of God pastor who had been in ministry for thirty years, a Presbyterian, and me. The coffee was bad. The conversation was one of the most clarifying I have had in fifteen years of ministry.

That is not what I expected. I expected politeness. I expected the careful avoidance of the live wires — cessationism, the ordo salutis, the question of whether what happened at Azusa Street in 1906 was the work of the Spirit or the work of human emotion or some complicated combination of both. I expected us to talk around the edges and call it unity.

We did not. And that is why it worked.

The Reformed pastor at the table did not believe tongues were for today. He said so, clearly, without apology, but without contempt. The Pentecostal pastor did not believe that the confessional tradition had fully accounted for the Spirit's ongoing activity in the life of the church. He said so with the same directness. What happened next was not resolution. It was something better than resolution. It was genuine engagement — the kind where you actually have to defend your position rather than assume the other person is simply less informed than you are.

The charismatic tradition has genuinely recovered something the Reformed tradition is sometimes too tidy to receive: the wildness of the Spirit, the expectation that God is actually present and active in the gathered community, not just ratifying the exegesis. That is worth something.

The Reformed tradition has genuinely preserved something the charismatic tradition sometimes loses in the fog of experience: the norming authority of Scripture over private revelation, the accountability of doctrine to the community across time, the understanding that enthusiasm without theology has historically gone off the rails very quickly. That is also worth something.

I came to faith from atheism. My first real theological encounters were with Reformed writers who took ideas seriously enough to argue about them. But some of the most formative moments in my pastoral development have been in rooms where prayer was not performative but desperate, where people actually expected God to show up in a way that exceeded the sermon outline. Both of those things are real. Both of them are insufficient alone.

What became clear at that table is that the division between these traditions is not always about the text. It is often about formation — what kind of Christian experience you were converted into, what community shaped your initial assumptions about how God works. A man raised in a Pentecostal church has a different operating assumption about corporate worship than a man raised in a Reformed congregation. Those assumptions are not always wrong. They are always partial.

The problem is not that we have different traditions. The problem is that we have allowed those traditions to become identities so thick that genuine engagement with the other becomes threatening. The Reformed pastor who cannot imagine that a Pentecostal might have something to teach him about prayer has confused his tradition with the whole of Christian truth. The Charismatic pastor who cannot imagine that the confessional tradition might have something to teach him about discipline and doctrinal accountability has made the same mistake in the other direction.

John 17:21 — "that they may all be one, as you, Father, are in me, and I in you" — is not a pious aspiration. It is the prayer of the Son to the Father on the night before his death. He thought it mattered. He thought it was worth praying for even knowing what was coming.

We sat in that basement and disagreed and listened and disagreed again. Nobody converted anyone. But something shifted. We left knowing that the pastor across the theological divide was not an obstacle to the mission. He was part of it.

That took three hours and bad coffee.

It was worth every minute.
