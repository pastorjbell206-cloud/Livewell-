I have spent five years watching the boys in my church drift toward the manosphere. They were not drifting because they wanted to be misogynists. They were drifting because the church had not offered them a vision of manhood that could compete. The manosphere offered them strength, agency, and identity. The church offered them moral correction. They chose the strength. We need to recover a theology of strength that is also theology.

I want to write this essay carefully because the topic is one where almost every existing position is exhausted or wrong, and the careful work has not been done at the level it needs to be done. The two main American Christian positions on manhood — the muscular-Christianity throwback associated with figures like Mark Driscoll and the more recent Wolfean nationalism, on the one side, and the progressive abandonment of distinctive masculine virtue on the other — have both failed. Each fails for related reasons. Each leaves a vacuum. The vacuum is being filled by Andrew Tate and the broader manosphere, and the filling has been catastrophic for a generation of young men. The church has to offer something better. The something better has to be older than either of the failed responses. I want to describe what it looks like.

## The crisis is real

I want to begin with the data, because the data is not in dispute and most Christian conversation about manhood proceeds as if it were.

Boys are falling behind girls in education at every level — from elementary reading scores through high school graduation rates through college admissions through completion rates. The gap has been widening for thirty years. Richard Reeves's *Of Boys and Men* (2022) is the most thorough recent documentation, and Reeves is not writing from a culturally conservative position; he is writing as a serious policy thinker who has seen the data and recognized that something is going badly wrong.

Boys are falling behind in employment. The labor-force participation rate for working-age men in the United States has declined for half a century and is now well below historical norms. Men without college degrees are particularly affected. The decline is not, on the whole, voluntary; it reflects the displacement of certain kinds of male-typical work by automation and outsourcing, the disappearance of intermediate-skill manufacturing, and the failure of the educational system to prepare boys for the work that is available.

Boys are falling behind in mental health. Suicide rates among young men are four times the rate among young women. Death-of-despair rates — overdose, alcoholism, suicide combined — are concentrated in working-class men in particular regions. Loneliness is endemic. The friendship gap, named in my earlier essay on the friendships men are supposed to have, is documented across multiple recent surveys.

Boys are falling behind in church participation. The exit of younger Americans from organized religion has been a male-led exodus in many evangelical churches. The pews are increasingly dominated by women. The leadership of evangelical churches is, of course, mostly male, but the church-going laity is increasingly female, and the generational trend is widening this gap rather than closing it.

These are not partisan claims. The data is broadly accepted across the political spectrum. The disagreement is about what to do.

## The two failed responses

The American Christian response to the crisis has, on the whole, taken two forms. Both have failed.

The first response is the muscular-Christianity throwback. This response treats the crisis of masculinity as a problem of insufficient masculine vigor in the church and the culture, and prescribes a recovery of authority, aggression, dominance, and traditional gender roles as the cure. The mid-2000s version was associated with Mark Driscoll's Mars Hill ministry and the broader neo-Reformed *young, restless, and Reformed* movement that had a complicated relationship with Driscoll. The current version is more politically explicit, with figures like Doug Wilson, Andrew Wilson (Doug's son), Joel Webbon, and a wider ecosystem of Reformed and politically reactionary pastors and influencers who have built large audiences among younger men.

The muscular-Christianity throwback has elements that are right. It correctly recognizes that the contemporary culture has lost confident teaching of distinctive masculine virtue. It correctly recognizes that young men are hungry for strong identity and clear vocation. It correctly recognizes that the progressive deconstruction of masculine virtue has not, on the whole, served young men.

The muscular-Christianity throwback also has serious problems. It prescribes the disease as cure. The aggression and dominance it celebrates are not, on careful Christian reading, the virtues the gospel calls men to. The masculinity of Christ — which is real, embodied, present in the gospels — is not the masculinity of the strongman. Christ's strength is the strength of meekness, of self-control, of voluntary suffering for the sake of others, of refusing to use his power for his own benefit. The muscular-Christianity tradition has often baptized the wrong virtues and called them masculine, and the baptism has produced, over a generation, a recognizable pattern of scandal, abuse, and pastoral failure that the movement itself has not seriously reckoned with.

The second response is the progressive abandonment of distinctive masculine virtue. This response treats the very category of *masculine virtue* as suspect, as gender essentialism, as a vestige of patriarchy. The progressive Christian tradition has largely refused, over the past forty years, to teach distinctively masculine virtues, on the grounds that any such teaching reinforces patriarchal structures. The result is that young men in progressive churches receive very little distinctive formation as men. They are taught not to be the worst version of themselves. They are not taught what the best version of themselves would look like.

The progressive abandonment is well-intentioned. It is responding to real harms that traditional gender teaching has produced. It is also, in my reading, catastrophically inadequate to the moment. Young men need formation as men. The progressive refusal to provide it leaves the formation work to the manosphere, which is delighted to do it on terms the church would not endorse. The progressive abandonment has, in many cases, produced the very results it was trying to prevent — a generation of young men radicalized by Tate and his successors, because the church had refused to compete for their formation.

Both responses have failed. Both have failed for related reasons. The failure has produced a generation of young men who are increasingly captured by frameworks that are not, in any serious sense, Christian. The church has to do better.

## What the older tradition offers

The recovery has to be older than both failed responses. The careful Christian tradition on masculine virtue is deeper, more demanding, and more interesting than either the muscular-Christianity throwback or the progressive abandonment.

The Catholic tradition's articulation of the cardinal virtues, going back through Aquinas to Augustine and the classical sources, identifies four foundational virtues — prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance — and locates the distinctively masculine in particular shapes of these virtues. Fortitude — the courage that sits between cowardice and recklessness — is a virtue of both men and women, but it has a particular masculine inflection in its protective and provider aspects. Temperance — the disciplined ordering of desire — has a particular masculine inflection in the discipline of sexual desire, the discipline of anger, and the discipline of ambition. The careful Christian tradition has been working on these virtues for centuries. The work is available. Most contemporary American Christian writing on masculinity has not engaged it at the level the work requires.

The Reformed tradition's articulation of vocation, recovered from Luther and developed in the post-Reformation Protestant pastoral literature, treats the distinctively masculine in terms of specific household and civic responsibilities. The husband, the father, the elder, the citizen — each has its own distinctive practice, formed by the larger Christian commitments but lived out in concrete relationships and obligations. The Reformed tradition has been clearer than most about the fact that vocational responsibilities are not optional, are not subject to negotiation when feelings change, and are part of how the Christian life is actually lived rather than abstractly considered.

The Eastern tradition's articulation of *theosis* — the participation of the Christian in the divine life — provides a framework in which the distinctively masculine is part of the larger picture of human persons being conformed to the image of Christ. The Eastern tradition has been less prone than the Western to either the muscular-Christianity excesses or the progressive abandonment, because the Eastern tradition has held more steadily to the central concern of formation in Christ.

These traditions, taken together, offer resources that the contemporary Christian conversation about manhood has not made full use of. The recovery does not require inventing something new. The recovery requires reading what the church has already written carefully, and applying it pastorally to the actual condition of young men in our time.

## What the practice looks like

I want to be concrete. What does the recovery of a theological account of manhood actually look like in the household, the congregation, and the surrounding culture?

In the household, it looks like fathers who carry weight. The work I described in the earlier essay on the man who carries weight is the heart of it — the father who bears economic, emotional, and vocational responsibility for his household without making the bearing visible to his children, and whose presence forms his children in ways the absence cannot. The household with such a father is a household that produces, over time, children who can themselves carry weight. The household without such a father has to do other work to compensate, and the compensation is harder and more partial than the original work would have been.

In the congregation, it looks like the explicit teaching of masculine virtue alongside the explicit teaching of feminine virtue. The catechesis of young men in the church has to include the kind of formation that the manosphere is currently providing — but in a Christian frame. The boys need to be told what strength is, what protection is, what discipline is, what responsibility is, in language that is concrete and demanding and honors the hunger they have for clear vocation. The teaching has to be embodied — by older men in the congregation who model what is being taught. The teaching has to be sustained over years. The teaching has to be honest about the failures of the men currently teaching it, including my own.

In the surrounding culture, it looks like the recovery of distinctive masculine work for the church. Young men need to be invited into specific work — leadership of small groups, mentorship of younger boys, deacon-type service to widows and the elderly, public-facing apologetic work in their own social spheres, the patient slow work of raising children well, the patient slow work of being a good husband across decades. The work has to be presented as actual masculine vocation, not as generic Christian service that happens to be done by men.

The teaching of distinctively masculine virtue does not require, and should not include, the diminishment of women. The recovery is not zero-sum. Women have their own distinctive vocations and virtues, and the parallel work for women is its own project. The two recoveries are different. Each requires what the other does not. The progressive instinct to treat any distinctive teaching for men as inherently patriarchal is wrong; the muscular-Christianity instinct to treat masculine virtue as superior to feminine is also wrong. The truth is that men and women have distinctive vocations within the larger Christian life, and both deserve careful formation in the particular shape of their callings.

## The verdict

The crisis of contemporary masculinity is real. The two failed Christian responses — the muscular-Christianity throwback and the progressive abandonment — have both failed for related reasons. The recovery has to be older than both. The recovery is available in the careful Christian tradition, in the cardinal virtues, in the vocational theology of the Reformation, in the participatory theology of the East, in the centuries of pastoral work on what it means to form a man who can carry weight.

I am writing this for the pastor reading it who has been watching his young men drift toward the manosphere. I am writing for the father who has been wondering how to form his sons in a culture that does not know what manhood means. I am writing for the young man himself, who has been offered two unsatisfactory accounts and has not yet been offered the third. The third option is available. The third option is older, more demanding, and more honest than either of the failures. The third option requires the church to do the work the church has been refusing to do, in different ways, from different sides, for forty years.

The boys leaving our churches are not leaving because they want to be cruel. They are leaving because we have not offered them a theology of manhood that is as muscular as their hunger and as kind as the gospel. The recovery is not nostalgia. The recovery is older than the muscular-Christianity tradition. The recovery is the gospel's account of strength under discipline, of protection without aggression, of the man who can be present without dominating and absent when his absence is the strongest thing he can offer. That man is rare. We have to start raising more of them. The work is the pastoral task of our generation. The work has not yet begun in earnest. We are out of time to put it off.
