Israel asked for a king because they wanted to be like other nations. Samuel told them what the king would cost.

The story is in 1 Samuel 8, and I want to spend some time inside it before I say what I want to say, because the story is the load-bearing argument of this essay and most American Christians have not read it slowly in a long time.

Samuel is old. His sons are corrupt. The elders of Israel come to him at Ramah and ask for a king. They give a reason. The reason is that they want to be like other nations. They want a king to judge them and to fight their battles for them. The reason is, on its face, modest. They are not asking for an empire. They are asking for institutional stability and military protection. They are asking for what every nation around them already has.

Samuel takes the request to the Lord, and the Lord says something extraordinary. *They have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them.* The Lord names the request as theological, not political. The Israelites have not asked for a better administrator. They have asked for a king who is not God. They have asked to be governed in the way the nations are governed, which means they have asked to stop being the people God was governing directly. The request is the rejection. The rejection is the request.

Samuel warns them. The warning is in verses 11 through 18, and the warning is precise. The king will take their sons and put them in his army. The king will take their daughters and put them in his court. The king will take a tenth of their grain and a tenth of their flocks. The king will take their best fields and give them to his favorites. The king will conscript their labor. And then, when the king has done all these things, the people will cry out to the Lord because of the king they have chosen, and the Lord will not answer them.

The people do not listen. They say *no, but we will have a king over us, that we also may be like all the nations, and that our king may judge us and go out before us and fight our battles.* They get the king. The king is Saul. Saul becomes everything Samuel warned about. The pattern repeats with David. The pattern repeats more terribly with Solomon. The pattern repeats most terribly with the divided monarchy that follows Solomon, in which the kings of Israel and Judah cost the people, by the prophets' reckoning, everything Samuel said they would cost.

## What we have asked for

I want to read this story slowly because the American church has been making the same request, in the same key, for some time now.

We have looked around at the loss of cultural Christianity, the rise of progressive moral frameworks we did not choose and do not endorse, the institutional capture of universities and corporations and media outlets, and we have grown afraid. The fear is not always irrational. There are real losses. There are real cultural shifts that will, over a generation, make Christian moral commitments more costly to hold publicly.

In our fear, we have asked for a king. We have asked for someone strong enough to push back against the losses, someone willing to break the rules the surrounding culture has been making against us, someone who promises to protect what we have left. We have, in many cases, found such a figure and supported him, and we have done so with explicit Christian language and explicit Christian institutional backing.

I want to be careful here. I am not naming a specific political figure, because the specific figure is not the deepest point. The deepest point is that the request itself is the rejection. The willingness to be governed in the way the nations are governed — by strongmen, by political power, by the imposition of one's will on the surrounding culture through political force — is itself the rejection of the King we already have. The strongman is not the answer to our problem. The strongman is the symptom of our problem. The problem is that we have stopped trusting that the King we have is sufficient, and have started asking for a king who looks like what the surrounding political imagination thinks a king should look like.

Samuel saw it. The Lord named it. The pattern is the pattern.

## What the strongman cannot do

The strongman cannot save the church. I want to say this slowly because it has to be said, and because most of the people in my circle who need to hear it have not yet had it said to them in a way they could receive.

The strongman cannot save the church because the strongman's power is political, and the church's life is not political in the strongman's sense. The strongman can defend the church's legal status, perhaps. The strongman can secure the church's tax exemptions, perhaps. The strongman can protect the church's cultural institutions from certain kinds of state pressure. The strongman cannot make people into disciples. The strongman cannot form character. The strongman cannot teach the catechism. The strongman cannot give the church the kind of theological coherence and pastoral depth that the church needs to survive a post-Christendom moment.

The strongman cannot save the church because the strongman's methods are themselves corrosive to the gospel. The cross is power-through-suffering. The strongman's politics is power-through-domination. The cross is the surrender of one's life. The strongman's politics is the imposition of one's will. The cross is the refusal of Satan's offer in the wilderness — *all the kingdoms of the world if you will fall down and worship me.* The strongman's politics is, structurally, the acceptance of that offer. The kingdoms of the world will be given. The kingdoms of the world will only be given if the church will agree to participate in the kind of politics the kingdoms of the world use.

Jesus refused. We can too.

The strongman cannot save the church because the strongman, when he has gotten what he wants from the church, will discard the church. This is the pattern of every regime that has used Christianity for its political ends. The Constantinian arrangement that protected Christianity also conscripted it. The German Christian movement that allied with Hitler in the 1930s was discarded once it had served its purpose; the Confessing Church survived because it had refused the alliance, while the German Christians were eventually disgraced and dissolved. Putin's Russian Orthodox Church under Patriarch Kirill is being conscripted right now, in real time, to bless an invasion that has produced sanctions and isolation; the Russian church will pay for the alliance for generations after Putin is gone. The strongman uses the church as a tool. The strongman is not loyal to the church. The strongman is loyal to himself. The church that has trusted in the strongman has, in every historical case I can think of, been betrayed.

The strongman cannot save the church because the strongman is not what scripture means by a savior. Scripture means by *savior* a specific person, killed by political authorities, raised from the dead, ascended to a throne that is not in any earthly capital, ruling over a kingdom that has not yet fully come. The church already has a savior. The willingness to look for a second one — even as a backup, even as a tactical convenience, even as a political ally — is the willingness to admit, in practice, that the first one is not sufficient. That admission is the heart of idolatry.

## What it cost Israel

Israel got the king they asked for. The king cost them what Samuel said he would cost them. Saul cost them his disordered jealousies and his suicide on Mount Gilboa. David cost them Bathsheba and Uriah and the long aftermath of Absalom's rebellion. Solomon cost them the temple's gold and his thousand wives and his hardening of the people under forced labor and his idolatry. The divided kingdom cost them civil war, foreign invasion, the exile of the northern tribes by Assyria in 722, and the exile of Judah by Babylon in 586. The whole monarchy was, in the prophets' reading, the long working-out of the rejection that began in 1 Samuel 8.

The prophets do not soften this. Hosea names the political alliances of the late northern kingdom as *zanah* — sexual unfaithfulness, treated as a covenantal betrayal. Isaiah names Assyria, the rod of God's anger, used to discipline God's faithless people. Jeremiah, on the eve of the Babylonian exile, names the king and the priests and the prophets as the agents of Judah's destruction. The prophetic verdict is consistent. The choice of a king was the rejection. The rejection produced everything that followed.

The Lord did not abandon the people through the monarchy. The Lord disciplined them through the consequences of their own choice, and the discipline produced the prophetic literature, and the prophetic literature produced the eventual recognition that the kingdom of God could not be a political project. The post-exilic community that came back under Cyrus did not rebuild the monarchy. They rebuilt the temple. They rebuilt the city. They did not rebuild the king. The prophetic verdict had landed.

The American church is, in many respects, asking for a king at the moment when the prophetic verdict on the monarchy is precisely the part of scripture we most need to read. The verdict is uncomfortable. The verdict is also the only honest reading of what political power has done to the church across the centuries.

## The verdict

The strongman cannot save the church. The strongman is the wrong category. The strongman represents the rejection of the King we already have. The willingness to ask for one is the symptom of the deeper problem, and the deeper problem is that we have stopped trusting that the King we have is sufficient.

I want to say this pastorally, because it has to be said pastorally as well as theologically. I understand the fear. The cultural losses are real. The progressive moral framework that is replacing the cultural Christianity many of us grew up inside is, in real ways, hostile to commitments we hold. The temptation to grab for political protection is a temptation born of fear, and the fear is not entirely irrational.

The temptation is also the wrong response. The right response is the response the early church gave under conditions far worse than ours — they worshipped, they cared for the people the empire could not care for, they were willing to lose the world to keep the gospel. The right response is the response the Confessing Church gave under Bonhoeffer in 1934 — they refused the conscription, they paid the cost, they preserved the gospel for the German church that came after the war. The right response is the response of the global persecuted church right now — pray for them, learn from them, recognize that the gospel does not require political power and may, in fact, be most clearly visible when political power is taken away.

The strongman cannot save the church. The King we have can. The King we have has been doing it for two thousand years, through every empire that promised to help and every empire that promised to destroy. He does not need the strongman. He does not need the empire. He does not need cultural Christianity. He needs disciples. He has always needed disciples. We can either be disciples or be the political subjects of someone we have agreed to call king instead of him. We cannot be both.

Samuel warned. The people did not listen. We have the warning. We have the rest of the Bible to show us what it cost when they did not listen. We have the option to listen now. The next essay in this series takes up the related question of voting and discipleship — the confusion of categories that has, more than any other, hollowed the church's witness in our generation. Vote however you want. Just stop calling it discipleship. The confusion is killing us, and naming it is the next thing.
