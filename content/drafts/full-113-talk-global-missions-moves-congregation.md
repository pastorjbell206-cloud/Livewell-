The missions presentation is a reliable feature of the American evangelical calendar. Somebody stands up with a PowerPoint that includes a map of the unreached world, a photograph of children in a village, a statistic about the number of people groups without a gospel witness, and an appeal to give. The congregation politely applauds and mostly does not give substantially more than they gave before.

The presentation was not wrong. It failed for a different reason.

It gave people information about a need rather than a story that could carry them into it. Information produces awareness. Story produces solidarity. And it is solidarity, not awareness, that moves people to give their money, their time, and eventually their children to a mission that is geographically distant and culturally foreign.

The distinction matters because of how the human imagination actually works. Paul understood this. When he wrote to the Romans about his mission to the Gentiles, he did not lead with statistics about the unreached population. He led with his own body — beaten, shipwrecked, hungry, in danger from rivers and from people and from false brothers (2 Corinthians 11:24-27). He led with the face of specific people: Lydia, the jailer at Philippi, the synagogue ruler in Corinth who believed with his whole household. The mission became concrete because the people were concrete.

The pastor who wants to actually move his congregation on global missions must become a storyteller first and a strategist second.

This means knowing your missionaries as people rather than as organizational representatives. The missionary presentation that says, "We support seventeen missionaries in fourteen countries" communicates institutional breadth. It moves almost no one. The presentation that says, "Let me tell you about David and Maria, who have spent the last six years in a city in Southeast Asia where the gospel has not gone before, who lost a child there, who built the first community of believers that city has ever had" — that presentation is working with entirely different material.

It means visiting. Not the short-term mission trip circuit, which has its own complicated relationship to the actual mission — that is addressed elsewhere. But the pastor who has stood in the city where his congregation's missionary works, who has met the local believers, who has seen the conditions of ministry and come home changed by what he saw, can preach missions from inside the experience rather than from a map. That difference is heard. The congregation knows the difference between the pastor who went and the pastor who read about it.

It means making the global local. The congregation member who has no visceral connection to the unreached world across the ocean may have a deep connection to the refugee family from that same region who lives three blocks from the church. When the pastor makes that connection explicitly — when he shows that the mission in Southeast Asia and the mission to the refugee family in your city are the same mission, drawing on the same theology, demanding the same posture of openness and service — the abstraction collapses. The congregation discovers that the unreached world is closer than they thought.

And it means being honest about cost. The American church has largely made missions into a comfortable transaction — you give money, someone else goes, and you remain as you are. That transaction is not wrong. But it is insufficient, and the congregation that has only ever been asked for that transaction will not be moved deeply enough to sustain the mission through the decades when the results are invisible and the news is discouraging. Genuine engagement with global mission eventually costs the congregation something more than money. It costs them people. It costs them their best young leaders who go and do not come back. It costs them the comfortable assumption that the church's primary mission field is its immediate neighborhood.

Being honest about that cost from the beginning — naming it as a cost and then making the theological case for why it is worth bearing — produces a different quality of giving than guilt or enthusiasm alone can generate.

The congregation that gives deeply to global mission is a congregation that understands its own story as part of that mission.

Telling them their story well is the pastor's job.
