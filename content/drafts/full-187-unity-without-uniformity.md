The night before he died, Jesus prayed for unity.

Not for agreement on every doctrinal question. Not for a single worship style. Not for political alignment or cultural consensus. He prayed that his followers would be one "as you, Father, are in me, and I in you" — and the purpose he named was not institutional efficiency but witness: "that the world may believe that you have sent me" (John 17:21). The unity is itself the argument. It is the thing that makes the claim credible in a world that has every reason to be skeptical.

We have, on the whole, not produced that thing. We have produced a different thing — a tendency to mistake sameness for unity, to create communities held together by shared cultural markers, shared political assumptions, shared worship aesthetics, shared levels of education or income, and call it the body of Christ. What we have created is a club. And clubs are not arguments. They are comfort.

The New Testament church was not a club. Acts 2 describes a community that would have been sociologically implausible in first-century Jerusalem — Parthians and Medes and Elamites and residents of Mesopotamia, Judea and Cappadocia, Pontus and Asia (Acts 2:9). These groups did not naturally gather. They had histories of conflict, mutual suspicion, economic competition, and ethnic distance. What gathered them was not shared background. What gathered them was a shared encounter with a risen Lord, and the claim that encounter made on every other organizing principle of their lives.

That community was not uniform. It was contentious. The disagreements in the letters of Paul are not hypothetical — they are pastoral responses to real fractures. Jews and Gentiles in Romans, Corinthians factionalized around apostles, the Galatians abandoning the gospel for a version that required cultural conformity. Paul's response to every fracture is the same at the theological level: the thing that unites us is prior to the things that divide us, and when we reverse that order we have misunderstood the gospel.

I have attended churches that were unified because everyone agreed on everything. They were also, without exception, churches in which the agreement was purchased at the price of someone's absence. The people who disagreed had left, or had learned to keep disagreement private. The uniformity was not unity. It was the appearance of unity made possible by the removal of everyone who complicated it.

Genuine unity costs more. It requires that you remain in relationship with the person whose conclusions you find difficult — that you share the table with someone whose politics or aesthetics or secondary theology sits wrong with you, because the thing you share is more fundamental than the things you dispute. It requires the theological confidence to say: the ground on which we stand together is not our agreement on peripheral things. It is our shared confession that Jesus is Lord, and that confession reaches further than my comfort with the people who share it.

The steel-man argument for uniformity is that doctrinal clarity requires some exclusion, and that a community with no center cannot hold. This is true. The unity I am describing is not doctrinal relativism — it is not the claim that all positions are equally valid and the church should affirm everything. It is the claim that the center of the gospel is capacious enough to hold real differences on the secondary questions, and that the church's calling is to demonstrate what that holding looks like rather than to narrow the circle until difference disappears.

That demonstration is the hardest thing the church does and the most necessary. A culture fracturing along every line of identity and conviction needs to see that fracture is not the final word — that there is a community where difference does not produce destruction.

We are not naturally that community. We become it through deliberate, costly, Spirit-dependent work.

The unity Jesus prayed for is not achieved once and then maintained. It is worked for, continuously, by people who have decided that the prayer matters more than the comfort.

That work is the witness. And the world is watching to see if we mean it.
