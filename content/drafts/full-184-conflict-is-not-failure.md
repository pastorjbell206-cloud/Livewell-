The New Testament church had conflict before it had a second generation.

In Acts 6, the Hellenistic widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution of food. There was a complaint, public and serious, about inequity in the earliest Christian community. The apostles did not pretend it wasn't happening. They did not reframe it as a misunderstanding. They gathered the disciples, named the problem directly, and restructured the community's leadership to address it (Acts 6:1–6). The seven deacons were appointed not as a gesture of pastoral care but as a structural response to a genuine failure.

The conflict did not threaten the church. The willingness to address it made the church stronger.

I spent years in pastoral ministry treating conflict as a diagnostic of inadequate leadership. If the congregation was in conflict, something had gone wrong on my watch, and my job was to smooth it over as quickly and quietly as possible before it spread. I have sat in more meetings than I want to count where the entire pastoral agenda was managing the temperature rather than addressing the underlying reality. The conflict was treated as the disease, when in almost every case it was the symptom — and the disease was something we were not willing to name.

Avoidance is not peace. It is pressure building behind a wall.

What I have learned — slowly, and not without cost — is that conflict in a congregation is almost always information. It tells you what the community actually values. It tells you where the unspoken agreements have been violated. It tells you which relationships have been maintained at the cost of honesty and which have accumulated enough trust to tell the truth. A congregation that never surfaces conflict has not achieved harmony. It has achieved a kind of managed suppression in which the real conversations happen in parking lots and living rooms rather than in the rooms where they might actually be resolved.

The pastor's job in conflict is not to be a peacemaker in the sense of eliminating disagreement. The biblical word for peacemaker in Matthew 5:9 comes from the Greek eirene, which carries the same weight as the Hebrew shalom — not the absence of tension but the presence of rightness, of whole relationships, of communities functioning in the truth about themselves. That kind of peace is not achieved by avoiding conflict. It is achieved by addressing it faithfully.

This is where Matthew 18 is actually practical rather than merely aspirational. "If your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone" (Matthew 18:15). The first move is direct, private, and relational. It does not route through the board or the prayer chain or the rumor network. It goes directly to the person. Jesus gives these instructions not because he expects them to always work but because the alternative — unaddressed grievance spreading through a community — is more destructive than the discomfort of the direct conversation.

The pastor who avoids conflict avoids something else without knowing it: the opportunity to model exactly the kind of courageous honesty that congregations need to see. The congregation learns how to handle conflict by watching how the pastor handles it. If the pastor smooths, they learn to smooth. If the pastor names things, they learn that naming things is something this community does.

I have had conversations I delayed for months because I did not want them to go badly, and in every case the delay made them worse. The thing I was avoiding did not get smaller while I avoided it. It grew, in the dark, into something that required twice the courage to address.

Conflict is not a sign that you have failed. It is a sign that real people with real convictions are in the same room together. The question is not whether it will appear. The question is whether you have the skill and the courage to address it in a way that makes the community more honest rather than more afraid.

That skill is learnable. The courage is a choice.

Make the choice before the wall gives way.
