A pastor who gives without receiving will eventually have nothing left to give. Every pastor agrees with that sentence. Almost none of us build a life around it.

We have made a quiet trade in American ministry. We took the word *faithful* and tied it to *tired*. The faithful pastor is the busy one. The serious pastor is the depleted one. The man who guards a day of rest is suspected, gently, of guarding himself.

It is a strange thing for the church to believe, given who we claim to follow. Jesus withdrew. Mark says it plainly and more than once — the crowds were still coming, the need was still there, and he left it to pray (Mark 1:35–38). He did not empty himself for us by running himself ragged. He emptied himself on a cross, once, having spent thirty years and three years of ministry refusing to be hurried by anyone's emergency but the Father's.

I have not always pastored that way. I have worn my exhaustion like a credential. I have let a full calendar stand in for a full soul, and I have called the substitution *commitment*. So this is not a warning I am issuing from a rested distance. It is a confession from a man who learned the hard way that a tank can run dry while the gauge still reads faithful.

Here is what the busyness hides. A pastor who never receives slowly forgets how. He becomes a closed system — pouring out, taking in nothing, and mistaking the dryness for depth. The sermons get thinner even as the hours get longer. The prayers become administrative. The love becomes technique. None of it shows up in the metrics the church watches, which is exactly why the church keeps rewarding the man right up until the morning he cannot get out of the car.

Rest is not the reward for sustainable ministry. Rest is the condition of it. The Sabbath was not given to Israel as a prize for productive weeks; it was given as a weekly insistence that the world holds together without them — that they are creatures, not engines, and that a God who rested on the seventh day will not think less of them for doing the same. A pastor who cannot keep a Sabbath does not have a scheduling problem. He has a theology problem. Somewhere underneath the full calendar is a quiet belief that the church is held up by his effort and would fall without it.

It would not fall. That is the freeing and humbling truth. The church belongs to Christ, was bought by Christ, and is kept by Christ. Your congregation will survive your day off. It will survive your vacation. It will, one day, survive you entirely. The question is not whether the work will get done without you. The question is whether you will still be a person by the time it does.

So receive. Not as a technique for lasting longer — though you will last longer — but because a pastor who will not be ministered to has stopped believing the gospel he preaches. Let someone counsel you. Let someone tell you the unflattering, true sentence and stay for the answer. Take the rest you cannot justify and did not earn, before the tank is empty rather than after.

The church does not need more tired pastors. It has plenty. It needs a few who are still alive in the soul by the time they reach sixty — who have something left to give because they spent thirty years letting themselves be filled.

Be one of those. Your busyness was never the gift. You were.
