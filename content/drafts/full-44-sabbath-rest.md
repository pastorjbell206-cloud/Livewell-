Most American Christians no longer keep Sabbath in any meaningful sense. The loss has been gradual, has been mostly unmourned, and has been catastrophic for the formation of Christian disciples who can hold the faith against the relentless pressure of the surrounding economy. The recovery of Sabbath practice is one of the most concrete things the church can do in our generation, and it is one of the most resisted, partly because the recovery requires the household to refuse the economic and cultural pressure that has organized its life.

I want to write this essay for the Christian reader who has known, for some time, that Sabbath practice was supposed to be part of Christian life and has not been able to figure out how to actually do it. The figuring-out is harder than the literature suggests, partly because the cultural environment is more hostile to Sabbath than most American Christians realize. The hostility can be addressed. The addressing requires deliberate counter-cultural practice, sustained over years, in a household that has decided to refuse the dominant rhythm of the surrounding economy. This essay is about what that looks like.

## What Sabbath actually is

The Christian conversation about Sabbath has, in my reading, suffered from two related errors. The first error has been treating Sabbath as primarily about personal rest. The second error has been treating Sabbath as primarily about religious observance. Both errors miss what Sabbath is theologically, and the missing of the theology has produced a contemporary practice that has, on the whole, not survived.

Walter Brueggemann's *Sabbath as Resistance* (2014) is the best contemporary treatment of what Sabbath actually is. Brueggemann's argument, briefly, is that Sabbath in the Old Testament is grounded in two distinct framings that together produce its theological substance. The first framing is creation. The Sabbath command in Exodus 20:8–11 is grounded in God's rest on the seventh day of creation. The rest is built into the structure of the cosmos itself. The created order includes rhythms of work and rest, and the Sabbath honors the rhythm God established.

The second framing is liberation. The Sabbath command in Deuteronomy 5:12–15 is grounded in Israel's liberation from slavery in Egypt. *You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out from there with a mighty hand.* Egypt was the empire of endless production. Pharaoh demanded bricks seven days a week, with no rest, no respite, no acknowledgment of the slaves' humanity. The Sabbath is the explicit refusal of Pharaoh's calendar. The Sabbath is the practice of remembering that the people of God have been liberated from the empire of endless production and have been given a rhythm that includes rest as a structural feature.

The two framings together make Sabbath a political practice as well as a religious one. Sabbath is the refusal to be ruled by the empire's time. Sabbath is the embodied participation in a different economy — God's economy, in which the worker is not reducible to his economic output, in which rest is a gift rather than a luxury, in which the rhythm of life includes time that does not have to produce anything. The political dimension of Sabbath has been the dimension most consistently lost in contemporary American Christian practice, and the losing has had consequences.

## Why we stopped resting

The decline of Sabbath practice in American evangelical life has happened gradually over the past century. The decline has had several causes, and the causes are worth naming because they continue to operate in contemporary American Christian life.

The first cause has been the displacement of Sabbath by the surrounding economic culture. The pre-industrial Sabbath was supported by a broader cultural rhythm in which most commerce stopped on Sunday and Christian Sabbath observance was supported by civic conventions. The industrial and post-industrial economy gradually eroded the civic support. By the mid-twentieth century, the surrounding economy was operating on Sundays as on other days, and the Christian household trying to keep Sabbath was doing so against the surrounding cultural rhythm rather than alongside it. The Christian household that had previously been able to lean on the surrounding culture to support its Sabbath practice now had to maintain the practice against the surrounding culture, and many households did not.

The second cause has been the rise of consumer culture. The surrounding economy has consistently moved toward seeing leisure as another form of consumption — sports leagues for children on Sundays, retail therapy on weekends, the entertainment industry's seven-day-a-week schedule. The Christian household that wants to keep Sabbath has to refuse not only Sunday work but also Sunday consumption, and the refusal is countercultural in ways the previous generation of American Christians did not have to address.

The third cause has been the assimilation of evangelical Christianity to the broader American achievement culture. The implicit theology of much American evangelicalism has been that Christian commitment should produce productive, successful, achievement-oriented lives. The Sabbath rest, which is structurally a refusal of the achievement orientation, has not fit comfortably within the implicit theology. The Sabbath has been quietly dropped in many evangelical households because the Sabbath did not match the broader theological framework the households were operating inside.

The fourth cause has been the failure of the church to teach Sabbath as a practice. Most American evangelical pastors I have talked with do not preach on Sabbath with any regularity. Most evangelical Sunday school curricula do not address Sabbath as a household practice. Most evangelical youth ministry does not transmit Sabbath observance as a normal feature of Christian life. The failure of transmission has produced a generation of evangelical Christians who have never been taught what Sabbath observance actually looks like in practice, and who consequently have not developed the practices their parents and grandparents may have had.

## What it actually looks like

I want to describe Sabbath practice concretely, because the abstract conversation does not produce practice in households.

A Sabbath of twenty-four hours. The historic practice is Friday sundown to Saturday sundown for the Jewish tradition. The Christian practice has typically been some twenty-four-hour period that begins Saturday evening and ends Sunday evening, or that runs Sunday morning to Monday morning. The specific timing matters less than the substance — a sustained twenty-four-hour period of rest from the patterns of work and consumption that dominate the rest of the week.

The cessation of work. The Christian household keeping Sabbath does not work during the Sabbath period. The cessation includes not only paid employment but also the various household labors that can wait — laundry, yard work, the running of errands. The cessation is concrete rather than abstract. The household notices that it is not doing what it would normally be doing.

The cessation of digital activity. The phone is put away or put in a drawer. The email is not checked. The social media is not engaged. The streaming services are not consumed in the way they are consumed on other days. The digital cessation is, for most contemporary Christians, the hardest part of Sabbath practice. The digital environment is the dominant cultural rhythm of our time, and refusing it for twenty-four hours produces a kind of withdrawal that many people have not experienced in years. The withdrawal is part of the practice. The withdrawal allows the body and the soul to encounter what twenty-four hours without the digital noise actually contains.

The presence to worship. The Sabbath day includes corporate worship — the gathering with the congregation for the proclamation of the word, the celebration of the sacraments, the singing of the church. The corporate worship is one of the central features of the Sabbath, and the household keeping Sabbath does not skip it for soccer games or for travel. The corporate worship is not the totality of Sabbath; the rest of the day is also Sabbath. The corporate worship is the center.

The presence to the household. The Sabbath day includes substantial unhurried time with the household. The shared meals are longer. The conversations are unrushed. The activities together are oriented toward presence rather than productivity. The household with children does Sabbath differently from the household without; the substance is similar, but the activities reflect the particular composition of the household. The presence to the household is part of how the Sabbath forms the family in a way that the rest of the week does not allow.

The presence to creation. The Sabbath day often includes time outdoors, time with the body in natural settings, time that reminds the soul that it inhabits a created world rather than a digital simulation. The walking, the gardening, the swimming, the sitting in a park — these are not the achievements of the rest of the week. They are the receiving of the world as gift, in a rhythm that allows the receiving to happen.

The presence to scripture and prayer. The Sabbath day allows for sustained time with scripture and with prayer that the busier days do not allow. The reading is slow. The prayer is unhurried. The household devotional practices are extended. The Christian who has been doing five minutes of devotional practice during the workweek can do longer, deeper, more contemplative practice on the Sabbath, and the deeper practice is part of how the Sabbath forms the broader rhythm.

## What the first months will feel like

I want to be honest about what beginning Sabbath practice feels like, because the literature has often presented it as immediately delightful and the immediate experience is often something else.

The first three months of consistent Sabbath practice produce, in most people I have walked with through it, more discomfort than rest. The body has been operating on the surrounding economy's rhythm for years. The body does not know what to do with twenty-four hours that are not optimized for productivity. The hands reach for the phone reflexively. The mind generates work-related anxieties that fill the space that work usually fills. The household, deprived of the entertainment and activity that usually fills its time, experiences a kind of low-grade restlessness that can be uncomfortable.

The discomfort is part of the practice. The discomfort is the body's withdrawal from the rhythm it has been operating on. The discomfort gradually decreases as the body adjusts to the new rhythm. By the end of the first year of consistent Sabbath practice, most people I have walked with describe the Sabbath day as one of the most life-giving features of their week. The transition takes longer than the literature suggests, but the transition does happen, and the transition produces a sustainable practice that becomes increasingly central to the household's life.

## The verdict

The American empire is not Pharaoh, but the American empire has its own demand for endless production. The forty-hour week has become the fifty-hour week, the sixty-hour week, the always-on week. The Sabbath that used to structure the week in American Protestantism has dissolved for most American Christians I know. The dissolution has been one of the most consequential losses of the late-Christendom period.

The recovery is concrete. Twenty-four hours a week. Phone off. Work off. Errands not done. Time for worship, for slow meals, for conversation, for reading, for prayer, for being with your household in a way that is not optimized for productivity.

The first three months will be hard. Most people I have walked with discover, during those three months, that they did not know how exhausted they were, because they had never given the body a chance to rest. The Sabbath is the chance.

The Sabbath also forms, in the people who keep it, a body that knows it is not the empire's slave. The knowing is what makes the rest of the political theology I have been writing about in this writing project actually livable. You cannot resist the empire's narrative if your body is running on the empire's rhythm. The body has to be retrained. The retraining is Sabbath.

I am writing this for the Christian reader who has been wanting to keep Sabbath and has not been able to figure out how. The figuring-out is what I have tried to describe in this essay. Begin this Saturday evening. Turn off the phone. Do not work. Pay attention to what you have not been paying attention to. The first week will be uncomfortable. The first year will be transformative. The rest of your life will be different because of what Sabbath does, in slow accumulation, to the soul that submits to it.
