The Hebrew word is *ger* — the resident alien, the one who lives among the people of Israel without belonging to the kinship networks that provide protection and standing. Not a visitor passing through. Not an enemy combatant. The *ger* is the person who has come to live among you, who has made a life in the community, who is present and vulnerable, who has no one at the city gate to advocate for him in the legal disputes that determined whether you kept your land, your freedom, and your family.

The law addresses the *ger* repeatedly and specifically. Leviticus 19:33-34 states: *When a foreigner resides among you in your land, do not mistreat them. The foreigner residing among you must be treated as your native-born. Love them as yourself, for you were foreigners in Egypt. I am the Lord your God.* This is not a general hospitality principle. It is a specific legal command with a theological rationale: you know what it means to be the stranger. You lived it in Egypt. That memory is the basis of the obligation.

Deuteronomy returns to this command so many times that it cannot be read as incidental. The tithe every third year includes provision for the *ger* alongside the widow and the orphan (Deuteronomy 14:29). The gleaning laws require that the edges of the field be left unharvested so that the *ger* can eat (Deuteronomy 24:19-21). The Sabbath rest applies to the *ger* as well as to Israel's own people (Deuteronomy 5:14). The stranger is woven into the fabric of the covenant community's economic and social life, not as an object of charity, but as a participant with legal standing and legal protections.

The rationale is always the same: *you were foreigners in Egypt.* The Exodus is not merely a theological event. It is the founding memory that shapes Israel's ethical obligations for the rest of her history. The people who were enslaved in a foreign land, who were vulnerable and powerless, who had no advocate at the gate — those people were liberated by God. And because God liberated them, they are obligated to never become the kind of community that treats the *ger* the way Egypt treated them.

This theological logic is among the most demanding in the entire Old Testament. It is not asking Israel to be generous to strangers out of benevolence. It is asking Israel to remember its own history with enough clarity that the sight of a vulnerable stranger activates the same obligation God demonstrated toward them when they were in that position. Empathy rooted in memory. Justice rooted in solidarity. The obligation flows from the story, and the story is not allowed to be forgotten.

The prophets condemn the violation of this obligation in language that leaves no room for exception. Ezekiel 22:7 indicts Jerusalem: *In you, the foreigner is mistreated.* Jeremiah 7:6 lists doing no wrong to the foreigner alongside shedding innocent blood as conditions of God's continued presence in the temple. Zechariah 7:10 places the *ger* alongside the widow, the orphan, and the poor as those who must not be oppressed. The God of Israel is watching the gate — watching what happens to the person who arrives without protection, without kinship, without the social capital that the established community takes for granted.

The New Testament does not retire this obligation. The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) takes it further: the obligation extends beyond the community's own categories, past the boundaries of kinship and ethnicity, to wherever a person is lying in need. And Revelation's vision of the renewed Jerusalem includes *every nation, tribe, people and language* gathered before the throne (Revelation 7:9). The stranger at the gate is not outside the story of redemption. She is, in some readings of the text, essential to it.

I have watched the church in America decide, in recent decades, that its response to the question of strangers and immigrants is primarily a political position rather than a biblical one. I have made that calculation myself — weighing the political complexity against the textual clarity and concluding that the complexity was reason enough to stay quiet. That is not neutrality. It is a choice, and it has a cost that I have not always been willing to name.

The text is not confused about the *ger*. The obligation is clear, the rationale is given, and the theological stakes are stated without softening.

The stranger is at the gate. That much has not changed.
