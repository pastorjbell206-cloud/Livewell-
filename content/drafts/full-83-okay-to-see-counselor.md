He will send you to a counselor. He knows the referral by heart — the gentle transition from pastoral conversation to clinical recommendation, the assurance that there is no shame in it, that professional help is wisdom not weakness, that the body of Christ uses the resources available to it. He has said these things to dozens of people. He believes them.

He has not seen a counselor himself.

The resistance that most pastors carry toward professional counseling is not primarily theological, though theology is sometimes used to rationalize it. It is a matter of shame — the deep, specific shame of being a man trained to bear others' burdens who cannot admit that his own burden has become too heavy for his private resources. The pastor understands suffering. He is the one who walks into it, names it, prays over it, accompanies it. He is supposed to be the one who helps others carry the weight, not the one who needs help carrying his own. To sit in a counselor's chair feels like stepping out of role, like admitting a failure that will damage the authority he needs to lead.

I have felt that shame. I have resisted help that I needed because receiving it would require acknowledging a need that my self-image as a pastor had no room for. I did not say it that way at the time. I said I didn't have time, that the right person wasn't available, that I was working through it on my own. These were rationalizations dressed in practical language.

The theological confusion that sometimes accompanies pastoral resistance goes something like this: if I really believed, I would not need this. Prayer should be sufficient. The Spirit is the Counselor (John 14:16), and to rely on a human one is to express some deficiency in my dependence on God. This is a misreading of Scripture serious enough to be dangerous. The God who created the human mind with its complex architecture of emotion, memory, and relationship did not design it to resolve its disorders through spiritual discipline alone. The same theological tradition that believes the Spirit heals also believes the Spirit works through created means — through surgeons, through teachers, through therapists who understand the mechanics of the human mind. The church has never argued otherwise when it comes to physical illness. A pastor with a broken leg does not refuse orthopedic care. The distinction we make for mental and emotional wounds is not theological. It is cultural.

The culture of evangelical masculinity has much to answer for here. The formation most male pastors received, explicitly or implicitly, was a formation that equated strength with self-sufficiency and help-seeking with weakness. This formation is not biblical. It is American — a particular version of frontier individualism that has been so thoroughly absorbed into the evangelical imagination that it feels like orthodoxy. The biblical witness runs in exactly the other direction. Moses had Jethro (Exodus 18). Elijah had Elisha. Paul had the churches he founded, and he told them so with explicit gratitude. The isolated leader who needs no one is not a New Testament figure. He is a modern mythology.

Counseling, done well, is not a substitute for prayer, community, or Scripture. It is something else: a structured and skilled engagement with the interior life of a person who has been formed, wounded, and confused by experiences that do not resolve themselves through good intentions or spiritual effort alone. The pastor who has carried years of congregational grief, who has absorbed trauma through proximity, who has maintained a public persona under relentless scrutiny — that man has specific interior work that requires a specific kind of help. Refusing it is not strength. It is stubbornness that costs too much.

See the counselor. Not as an alternative to your faith. As an expression of it.
